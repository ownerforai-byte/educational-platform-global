Introduction
Chemistry practical carries 25 marks in Higher Secondary Education
Board Examination. One should pass both the theory and pracücal
examinations separately. The pass marks for practical paper will be 40%
of the full marks. The practical marks are divided into for:
a) Experiments
b) Practical note book
c) Viva-voce.
Normally, practical examination period is of 3 hours and one should
complete two experiments within this period.
The practical chemistry involves two types of experimenW.
a) GQ neral experiments and quantitative analysis.
b) Qualitative analysis.
In general type of experiments, we perform the experiments related
to srmple laboratory techniques, mixture separation, preparation of gases
etc. In quantitative analysis, the experiments related to determination of
mass or volume are performed. Determinations of weight of a substance,
Percentage composition, volumetric analysis (titraåon) etc. are such
experiments.
In qualitative analysis, the related to identifying the
quality of a chemical substance are performed. Such experiments involve
the identification of acid and basic radicals in an inorganic salt, detecüon
Of hetero element (s) in an organic compound, identification of the
organic compound, detection of functional group in the organic
compound etc. The experiments related to qualitative analysis are
discussed in the part II of this book.2

Study of Bunsen burner
Bunsen burner is commonly used heating device in laboratory. It was
discovered by a German Scientist Robert Bunsen in 1855 . It can produce
a high temperature up to 35000C.
It mainly consists of the following three parts:
Base: It is made of cast iron. It is heavy and keeps the burner in
Upright position. It has a side tube that communicates inside with a
jet. The base is connected to the gas tap through a rubber tube.
Barrel or chimney: It is metallic tube having a pair of air holes at the
opposite sides near the lower end.
Air regulator or collar: It is fitted to the lower end of the barrel. It is
used to close or open the air holes.8
Practical Chemistry
The burner burns with a mixture of fuel gas and air. Depending
upon the quantity of air mixed, two types of flame can be seen.
1. With air holes closed: If the air holes are kept closed and the
burner is lighted, it gives a yellow and smoky flame, called the
luminous flame (or, reducing flame).
2. With air holes opened: If the air holes are slowly opened,
smoky flame gradually disappears and non-luminous (or
oxidising) flame is produced. This is the normal flame of the
burner. This flame is used for heating purposes. This flame
consists of two parts: inner blue part and outer hottest part.

Introduction
The mixing of two or more than two substances results in the impure
substance. Impure substances are also known as mixtures. Mixtures are
of homogeneous or heterogeneous types. There are various techniques
employed to separate the components of a mixture. The technique
depends on the nature of the components.

THEORY
The soluble and insoluble components of a mixture can be separated by
dissolving the mixture in water followed by filtration. Sand and common
salt are separated from their mixture by dissolving the mixture in water
followed by filtration.
Filtration can be defined as the method of separating the insoluble
COmponent and solution of the soluble component by means of a porous12
Practical Chemistry
medium like filter paper, glass wool etc. The insoluble component, here
sand, is obtained on the filter paper as residue. The solution of the soluble
component, here common salt, which passes through the filter paper- is
collected in the beaker. Thus received solution is called filtrate.
The purity of sand can be checked by treating sand-washed water
with few drops of silver nitrate solution. Presence of common salt in sand
is indicated by formation of a curdy white precipitate.

dyes in the ink) become separated in different coloured bands.

General Idea about the Preparation and Collection of Gases
in the Laboratory
Selection of apparatus
1.
a) The gases which are prepared by the application of heat are
prepared in a hard glass test tube or round bottom flask.
b) The gases which are prepared without the application of heat
are prepared either in a Woulfts bottle or in a conical flask.
Simple reactions are carried out in a Woulfs bottle and whereas
vigorous reactions are carried out in a conical flask.
c) Dropping funnel is used if the liquid has to be added in small
lots or if the reaction is vigorous, otherwise thistle funnel is used
to pour the liquid.
Collection of gases
2.
a) Water soluble gases are collected by displacement of air. If the
gas is heavier than air, it is collected by upward displacement of
air and if the gas is lighter than air then it is collected by
downward displacement of water.
b) Water insoluble gases are collected by downward displacement
of water. They are lighter than air.
Note: A gas is preferred to be collected in water because while collected in air,
it mixes with air to some extent.

Acidimetry and Alkalimetry
Acidimetry and alkalimetry is related with the measurement of strength
of an acid or an alkali in presence of each other.
Volumetric Analysis
It is a quantitative analysis in which the strength of an unknown solution
is determined by taking standard solution with the help of known
volumes of these two solutions.

into a known volume/ weight of solution/ solvent. A substance

standard substance, and should have following requisites:
It should be available in pure and dry state.

b)

behaviour. Similarly, it should not be excessively

Selection of an Indicator
For the selection of an indicator, required for a particular titration, a
curve is drawn by taking the volume of alkali along X-axis and pH value
along Y-axis. This curve is called as titration curve. As the alkali is added
to the acid taken in a conical flask, the pH of the solution increases and
different types of curves will be obtained in different acid-base titrations.
The steep rise in pH in the curve corresponds to the end point of titration.
The pH range at the end point is compared with the pH range of each
indicator and the selection of a suitable indicator is carried out.

Burette
The burette is a long cylindrical tube fitted with a stop cock at the lower
end. It is graduated in ml. It is clamped to the stand. Before using, it
should be thoroughly washed with distilled water and then rinsed with
small amounG of the solution which is placed in it.
Pipette
Pipettes are long narrow tubes having a bulb in the middle. The upper
stem of the pipette has a mark upto which it is to be filled with solution•
Pipettes are of different capacities i.e. 50, 25, 20, 10 and 5 ml. Before using'
the pipette should be thoroughly washed with distilled water and then
rinsed with the solution that is to be pipetted out. While introducing the
pipetted out solution into the titration flask, the pipette should not be
blown in.
Volumetric flask
A volumetric flask is a pear shaped vessel with a long narrow neck and
having a lid. A mark is made around the neck to indicate a definite
volume i.e. 1000, 500, 200 or 100 ml. This flask is used to prepare primary
standard solution and for diluting a solution to a definite volume. Before
using, the flask and the lid should be thoroughly washed distilled water.99

THEORY
The reactions in which oxidation and reduction go hand in hand is called
redox reaction. The reaction in which loss of electron occurs is called
oxidation and the reaction in which reduction occurs is called reduction.
The electrons lost by one species should be gained by another species.
Therefore, oxidation and reduction always go hand in hand.
The chemical substance whose oxidation state increases is called
reductant or reducing agent. It reduces other substances being itself
oxidised. The chemical substance whose oxidation state decreases is
called oxidant or oxidising agent. It oxidises other species being itself
reduced. Many redox reactions are known to occur as a result of some
visual changes.

THEORY
Hydrolysis of a salt is defined as the combination of ions of the salt with
hydrogen and hydroxyl ions of water to give acidic or basic solution.
Salts of strong acid and weak base (e.g. NH4Cl) are hydrolysed to give
weakly acidic solution whereas salts of weak acid and strong base (e.g.
Na2C03) are hydrolysed to give weakly alkaline solution. Salts of weak
acid and weak base (e.g. CH3COONH4) are hydrolysed to give almost
neutral solutions. Salts of strong acid and strong base (e.g. NaCl) are not
hydrolysed and therefore, their solutions are neutral. The acidic, alkaline
or neutral nature can be identified with the help of the indicators like
litmus papers, phenolphthalein solution, methyl orange solution etc.
Methyl orange solution produces pink colour with an acid whereas it
produces light yellow colour with a base. (Phenolphthalein gives a pink
colour with alkali and it has no colour with acid. Acid turns blue litmus
to red and base turns red litmus to blue).
