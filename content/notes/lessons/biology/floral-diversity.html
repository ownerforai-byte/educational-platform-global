Floral Diversity

Class 11BiologyLessons

The living world is astonishingly varied, and biology organises that variety into a rational system. This unit surveys the diversity of the plant-like world — from bacteria-level organisms to flowering plants — following the NEB Class 11 syllabus. It covers the three domains of life, Whittaker's five-kingdom classification, binomial nomenclature, and representative organisms from fungi (Mucor, yeast, mushrooms), lichens, algae (Spirogyra), bryophytes (Marchantia), pteridophytes (Dryopteris), gymnosperms (Pinus) and the economically important angiosperm families.

Classification: Domains and Kingdoms

Modern systematics recognises three domains — Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya — based on ribosomal RNA evidence. Carl Woese proposed this scheme, while R. H. Whittaker (1969) proposed the widely taught five-kingdom system. Binomial nomenclature, introduced by Carolus Linnaeus, gives every species a two-word scientific name (e.g., Mangifera indica), the first word being the genus and the second the specific epithet.

From Algae to Angiosperms

Plant evolution shows a clear progression: algae (aquatic thallophytes such as Spirogyra with spiral chloroplasts), bryophytes (Marchantia — amphibians of the plant kingdom needing water for fertilisation), pteridophytes (Dryopteris — first true vascular plants), gymnosperms (Pinus — naked seeds in cones), and angiosperms (seeds enclosed in fruits). Lichens, meanwhile, are dual organisms formed by a symbiosis of an alga and a fungus; the alga photosynthesises while the fungus provides shelter and mineral absorption. Among fungi, phycomycetes (coenocytic hyphae) include Mucor, ascomycetes include yeast and morels, basidiomycetes include rusts and mushrooms, and deuteromycetes — the "imperfect fungi" — include Alternaria and Penicillium-like forms lacking known sexual stages.

Key points

  • Binomial nomenclature follows Linnaeus; names are italicised (or underlined when handwritten) with the genus capitalised.
  • Whittaker's five kingdoms are Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
  • Fungal walls contain chitin; lichens combine an algal photobiont with a fungal mycobiont.
  • Bryophytes lack vascular tissue; pteridophytes have it but still need water for fertilisation.
  • The four syllabus families — Brassicaceae, Fabaceae, Solanaceae and Liliaceae — supply food crops (mustard, pea, potato, onion) and medicines.

Common mistakes

  • Placing fungi in Plantae: fungi are absorptive heterotrophs with chitin walls, not plants.
  • Writing a scientific name without italics or with both words in capitals.
  • Calling Marchantia a moss — it is a liverwort; mosses are a separate bryophyte group.
  • Saying gymnosperms bear fruits: their seeds lie naked on megasporophylls of cones.
  • Confusing deuteromycetes with basidiomycetes; "imperfect" refers only to the absent sexual stage, not to any basidium.
