Notes

  • <h2>What Is Reading Comprehension?</h2>
  • <p><b>Reading comprehension</b> is the ability to understand, interpret, and analyse written text. It involves more than decoding words — it requires extracting meaning, making inferences, evaluating arguments, and connecting the text to prior knowledge. In the NEB Class 11 English examination, comprehension questions typically carry 10-15 marks and include unseen passages with objective and subjective questions. Success in this section depends on systematic reading strategies and practice.</p>
  • <h2>Types of Comprehension Questions</h2>
  • <p>NEB comprehension questions generally fall into four categories: (1) <b>Factual questions</b> — ask for information explicitly stated in the passage (e.g., 'What did the protagonist decide?'). (2) <b>Inferential questions</b> — ask for meaning that is implied but not directly stated (e.g., 'Why did the character feel trapped?'). (3) <b>Vocabulary-in-context questions</b> — ask for the meaning of a word as used in the passage (e.g., 'What does the word "desolate" mean in line 5?'). (4) <b>Interpretive/evaluative questions</b> — ask for the reader's understanding of the passage's theme, tone, or author's purpose (e.g., 'What is the main idea of the passage?'). Each type requires a different reading strategy.</p>
  • <h2>Strategy 1: Skimming</h2>
  • <p><b>Skimming</b> is reading quickly to get the general idea or gist of a passage. To skim effectively: (1) Read the <b>title</b> (if given) — it often hints at the topic. (2) Read the <b>first and last sentences</b> of each paragraph — these often contain the main idea. (3) Read the <b>first and last paragraphs</b> of the passage — these usually contain the thesis and conclusion. (4) Note <b>key words</b> — names, dates, technical terms, and repeated ideas. Skimming should take 1-2 minutes for a standard passage. It helps you understand the overall structure before diving into details, which is essential for answering inferential and interpretive questions.</p>
  • <h2>Strategy 2: Scanning</h2>
  • <p><b>Scanning</b> is reading quickly to find specific information (a name, date, fact) without reading every word. To scan effectively: (1) Know what you are looking for before you start. (2) Move your eyes rapidly across the text, looking for keywords or numbers. (3) Stop when you find the relevant information and read that sentence carefully. Scanning is particularly useful for factual questions (e.g., 'What year was the bridge built?'). It is different from skimming — skimming gets the gist; scanning finds a specific detail. Practising both strategies significantly improves reading speed and accuracy in exams.</p>
  • <h2>Strategy 3: Close Reading</h2>
  • <p><b>Close reading</b> is a careful, detailed reading of the passage to understand its deeper meaning, structure, and language. After skimming for the general idea, close reading involves: (1) Identifying the <b>main idea</b> of each paragraph. (2) Noting <b>key terms</b> and their definitions. (3) Analysing <b>literary devices</b> (metaphor, simile, imagery). (4) Understanding the <b>author's tone and attitude</b>. (5) Making <b>inferences</b> about implicit meanings. Close reading takes 5-8 minutes for a standard passage but is essential for answering inferential and interpretive questions accurately.</p>
  • <h2>Understanding Vocabulary in Context</h2>
  • <p>When asked to explain a word's meaning in context, do not simply give the dictionary definition — explain how the word functions in the specific passage. Use these techniques: (1) <b>Read the surrounding sentences</b> — context clues often reveal the meaning. (2) <b>Look for synonyms or antonyms</b> in nearby text. (3) <b>Identify the word's part of speech</b> — is it a noun, verb, adjective? (4) <b>Consider the tone</b> — is the word used positively or negatively? For example, if the passage describes a 'desolate landscape', the word 'desolate' likely means 'empty, bleak, barren' — not just 'deserted'. Context-based vocabulary questions are common in NEB exams and require careful attention to surrounding text.</p>
  • <h2>Making Inferences</h2>
  • <p><b>Inference</b> is a logical conclusion drawn from evidence in the text combined with prior knowledge. To make inferences: (1) Look for <b>clues</b> — what does the author say indirectly? (2) Consider <b>character motivation</b> — why did they act this way? (3) Think about <b>implications</b> — what does this suggest about the theme? (4) Avoid <b>over-interpreting</b> — your inference must be supported by the text, not just your imagination. Example: If the passage says 'She stared at the unanswered letter, her fingers tracing the worn envelope,' a valid inference is that she is hesitant or anxious about opening it. An invalid inference would be that she is angry (no evidence for this). Inference questions test your ability to read between the lines.</p>
  • <h2>Identifying Tone and Attitude</h2>
  • <p>The <b>tone</b> of a passage is the author's attitude toward the subject, expressed through word choice, sentence structure, and imagery. Common tones in comprehension passages: <b>formal</b> (serious, academic), <b>informal</b> (conversational, friendly), <b>ironic</b> (saying the opposite of what is meant), <b>sarcastic</b> (mocking, bitter), <b>humorous</b> (amusing, light-hearted), <b>melancholy</b> (sad, reflective), <b>optimistic</b> (hopeful, positive), <b>pessimistic</b> (negative, doubtful), <b>critical</b> (disapproving, judgmental). To identify tone, examine the author's word choices — do they use positive or negative language? Are sentences formal or casual? Tone questions are frequently tested in NEB comprehension sections.</p>
  • <h2>Answering Comprehension Questions Effectively</h2>
  • <p>To maximise marks in comprehension: (1) <b>Read the questions first</b> — this tells you what to look for while reading. (2) <b>Underline key words</b> in both the passage and the questions. (3) <b>Quote directly</b> for factual questions — use exact words from the passage where possible. (4) <b>Paraphrase</b> for inferential questions — express the implied meaning in your own words. (5) <b>Keep answers concise</b> — answer only what is asked; do not add unnecessary information. (6) <b>Check your answers</b> — ensure each answer is supported by the passage. For long-answer questions (3-5 marks), provide a complete sentence with explanation and textual evidence.</p>

Confusion

  • Skimming vs. Scanning: Skimming is reading for the general idea/gist (fast, broad). Scanning is reading for specific information (fast, targeted). Skimming answers 'what is this passage about?'; scanning answers 'when did the event happen?' or 'who said this?'. Students often confuse these strategies, spending too much time on one when the other is needed.
  • Inference vs. Prediction: An inference is a conclusion about what is already in the text (past or present). A prediction is a guess about what will happen next (future). 'The character looked at the dark sky and gathered her umbrella' — inference: she expects rain. Prediction: she will get wet anyway. Inferences must be supported by text evidence; predictions are educated guesses about future events.
  • Main idea vs. Topic: The <b>topic</b> is what the passage is about (one word or phrase: 'environmental pollution'). The <b>main idea</b> is what the author says about the topic (a complete sentence: 'Environmental pollution is a growing crisis that requires immediate government action'). The topic is the subject; the main idea is the author's point about that subject. NEB exams sometimes ask for either, and students confuse the two.
  • Author's purpose vs. Author's tone: The <b>purpose</b> is why the author wrote the passage (to inform, to persuade, to entertain, to describe). The <b>tone</b> is the author's attitude toward the subject (formal, ironic, optimistic, critical). Purpose answers 'why?'; tone answers 'how does the author feel?'. A passage can have the purpose of informing with a critical tone, or the purpose of persuading with an optimistic tone.
  • Literal vs. Interpretive questions: Literal questions ask for information directly stated in the text (facts, details). Interpretive questions ask for meaning that requires thinking beyond the text (inferences, themes, author's purpose). Literal answers can be found by locating specific sentences; interpretive answers require synthesising information from across the passage.

Practice

  • Read this passage and answer the questions: 'The old man sat alone on the bench, his eyes fixed on the empty swing where his granddaughter used to play. The park was empty now, save for a few pigeons pecking at scattered crumbs. Autumn leaves drifted down around him like forgotten memories.' (a) What is the mood of the passage? (b) What can be inferred about the old man? (c) What literary device is used in 'like forgotten memories'? [Answer: (a) Melancholy/somber — the empty park, the old man alone, drifting leaves create a sad atmosphere. (b) He is lonely and misses his deceased or absent granddaughter. (c) Simile — comparison using 'like'.]
  • What does the word 'desolate' mean in this context? 'The desolate landscape stretched for miles, with no sign of life or civilization.' [Answer: In this context, 'desolate' means barren, empty, bleak — a landscape that is deserted and lifeless. The context clues ('no sign of life or civilization') support this meaning.] — Note: Dictionary meaning alone is insufficient; the answer must reflect the contextual usage.
  • Identify the tone of this passage: 'The new policy, announced with great fanfare, turned out to be little more than a rebranding of the old one. Citizens were promised change, but received the same old solutions dressed in new language.' [Answer: The tone is sarcastic/cynical. The author uses irony ('great fanfare' vs. 'little more than') and mockery ('dressed in new language') to express disappointment and disbelief in the policy's authenticity.] — Note: 'Sarcastic' is more precise than just 'negative' — the author is mocking, not merely critical.
  • What is the main idea of this passage? 'Urbanisation in Nepal has accelerated dramatically over the past two decades. Cities like Kathmandu and Pokhara have expanded rapidly, drawing migrants from rural areas in search of employment and education. However, this growth has outpaced infrastructure development, leading to traffic congestion, housing shortages, and environmental degradation.' [Answer: The main idea is: Rapid urbanisation in Nepal has created significant infrastructure and environmental challenges that have outpaced city growth.] — Note: The topic is 'urbanisation in Nepal'; the main idea is the author's point about its negative consequences.] — This is a complete sentence, not just a topic label.
  • What is the author's purpose in this passage? 'Vaccination has prevented an estimated 2-3 million deaths annually worldwide. Yet, vaccine hesitancy persists in many communities, fueled by misinformation and lack of awareness. Health authorities must prioritise education campaigns to address these concerns.' [Answer: The author's purpose is to persuade — to convince readers that vaccination is vital and that health authorities should prioritise education to combat vaccine hesitancy. The passage presents facts (prevention of deaths), identifies a problem (hesitancy), and proposes a solution (education campaigns).] — Note: Purpose is 'to persuade', not just 'to inform' — the author is advocating for action.
  • Make an inference: 'She carefully folded the letter, sealed it in an envelope, and placed it in the drawer where she kept her most precious possessions. Then she resumed her work, as if nothing had happened.' [Answer: Inference: The letter contains something deeply personal and emotionally significant to her — perhaps a love letter, a farewell note, or important news. She chose to hide it rather than send it, suggesting inner conflict or fear. Her return to work 'as if nothing had happened' indicates she is concealing her true feelings.] — Note: The inference must be supported by textual evidence (folding carefully, sealing, placing in a special drawer, resuming work).
  • What does the word 'benevolent' mean in this context? 'The benevolent donor visited the orphanage annually, bringing gifts and speaking kindly to the children, who gathered around him with obvious affection.' [Answer: In this context, 'benevolent' means kind, generous, charitable. The context clues ('bringing gifts', 'speaking kindly', 'obvious affection') indicate the donor is caring and generous toward the children.] — Note: The answer must be based on context, not just a dictionary definition.
  • Distinguish between literal and interpretive: For the passage 'The storm raged through the night, tearing branches from ancient oaks and flooding the lower streets,' (a) What happened during the night? (b) What might the storm symbolise? [Answer: (a) Literal: A severe storm with strong winds and flooding occurred during the night. (b) Interpretive: The storm could symbolise chaos, destruction, or upheaval in a character's life or in society. Symbolic interpretation depends on the broader context of the passage.] — Note: (a) is a factual recall; (b) requires thinking beyond the text.

Universal Facts

  • Reading comprehension is one of the most heavily weighted sections in the NEB English examination, typically carrying 10-15 marks. The passage is usually unseen (not from the textbook), testing students' ability to read and analyse unfamiliar texts — a skill essential for academic success across all subjects.
  • The four types of comprehension questions (factual, inferential, vocabulary-in-context, interpretive) reflect Bloom's Taxonomy of cognitive levels, from lowest (remembering facts) to highest (evaluating and interpreting). Preparing for all four types ensures comprehensive readiness for any comprehension passage.
  • Skimming and scanning are research skills developed by early 20th-century educators who recognised that not all reading needs to be slow and careful. These speed-reading techniques are now standard in academic and professional reading worldwide.
  • Inference-making is a higher-order thinking skill that distinguishes skilled readers from reluctant ones. Skilled readers actively construct meaning by connecting textual clues with their own knowledge, while reluctant readers tend to look for answers that are explicitly stated in the text.
  • The ability to identify tone is particularly important in Nepali students' reading comprehension, as Nepali literary texts often use subtler tone markers than English texts. Students trained to read Nepali literature closely are often better equipped to detect tone in English passages.
