Notes

  • <h2>Word Formation</h2>
  • <p><b>Word formation</b> is the process of creating new words from existing words using affixes (prefixes and suffixes), compounding, conversion, or blending. In the NEB Class 11 English examination, word formation questions (typically 3-5 marks) ask students to change a given word into a different part of speech or to fill in a blank with the correct derived form. <b>Prefixes</b> are added to the beginning of a word (un-, dis-, re-, pre-, mis-). <b>Suffixes</b> are added to the end ( -ness, -ment, -tion, -able, -ful, -less, -ly, -er, -ist). Understanding word formation patterns is essential for vocabulary expansion and grammar accuracy.</p>
  • <h2>Common Prefixes and Their Meanings</h2>
  • <p>Key prefixes tested in NEB exams: <b>un-</b> (not, opposite): unhappy, unlock, unfair. <b>dis-</b> (not, opposite, removal): disagree, disappear, dishonest. <b>re-</b> (again): rewrite, rebuild, reinstall. <b>pre-</b> (before): prehistoric, prepaid, preview. <b>mis-</b> (wrongly): misunderstand, mislead, misplace. <b>in-/im-/il-/ir-</b> (not): impossible, illegal, irregular, incomplete. <b>anti-</b> (against): antibiotic, anti-social. <b>semi-</b> (half): semifinal, semicircle. <b>sub-</b> (under): submarine, submodule. <b>over-</b> (too much): overwork, overeat. <b>under-</b> (too little): underpaid, underestimate. Memorising these prefixes helps in solving word formation and vocabulary questions efficiently.</p>
  • <h2>Common Suffixes and Their Functions</h2>
  • <p>Key suffixes tested in NEB exams: <b>-ness</b> (forms nouns from adjectives): happiness, kindness, darkness. <b>-ment</b> (forms nouns from verbs): achievement, agreement, development. <b>-tion/-sion</b> (forms nouns from verbs): education, decision, admission. <b>-able/-ible</b> (forms adjectives from verbs): readable, visible, accessible. <b>-ful</b> (forms adjectives from nouns): helpful, beautiful, careless. <b>-less</b> (forms adjectives meaning 'without'): hopeless, fearless, endless. <b>-ly</b> (forms adverbs from adjectives): quickly, carefully, happily. <b>-er/-or</b> (forms nouns indicating doer): teacher, actor, writer. <b>-ist</b> (forms nouns indicating specialist): artist, scientist, pessimist. <b>-ity</b> (forms nouns from adjectives): ability, purity, activity. <b>-ous/-ious</b> (forms adjectives from nouns): dangerous, nervous, famous.</p>
  • <h2>Idioms: Fixed Expressions with Figurative Meaning</h2>
  • <p><b>Idioms</b> are fixed expressions whose meaning cannot be deduced from the literal meanings of individual words. Examples: <b>'Break the ice'</b> = to initiate conversation in a social setting. <b>'Hit the nail on the head'</b> = to describe exactly what is causing a situation or problem. <b>'A dime a dozen'</b> = very common and not valuable. <b>'Bite the bullet'</b> = to face a difficult situation courageously. <b>'Cost an arm and a leg'</b> = to be very expensive. <b>'Let the cat out of the bag'</b> = to reveal a secret accidentally. <b>'Beat around the bush'</b> = to avoid talking about something directly. <b>'Once in a blue moon'</b> = very rarely. Idioms are extensively tested in NEB exams through gap-filling, sentence completion, and meaning-matching questions.</p>
  • <h2>Common Phrasal Verbs</h2>
  • <p><b>Phrasal verbs</b> are combinations of a verb + particle (preposition or adverb) that create a new meaning. Examples: <b>give up</b> = stop trying / surrender. <b>look after</b> = take care of. <b>run out of</b> = use all of something. <b>put off</b> = postpone. <b>turn up</b> = appear / arrive. <b>break down</b> = stop working (machine) / lose control (emotion). <b>carry on</b> = continue. <b>get along</b> = have a good relationship. <b>come across</b> = find by chance. <b>figure out</b> = understand / solve. Phrasal verbs are one of the most challenging aspects of English vocabulary for Nepali students, as Nepali does not have an equivalent particle system.</p>
  • <h2>One-Word Substitution</h2>
  • <p><b>One-word substitution</b> replaces a phrase or group of words with a single word that has the same meaning. Common examples tested in NEB exams: <b>Belonging to one state only</b> = Native. <b>Love of books</b> = Bibliophile. <b>Study of words</b> = Lexicography. <b>Love of humanity</b> = Philanthropist. <b>That can be felt</b> = Sensible. <b>That cannot be felt</b> = Insensible. <b>A person who knows many languages</b> = Polyglot. <b>A person who loves peace</b> = Pacifist. <b>That cannot be destroyed</b> = Indestructible. <b>A short, witty remark</b> = Epigram. One-word substitution questions typically carry 2-4 marks in NEB exams and require extensive vocabulary preparation.</p>
  • <h2>Synonyms and Antonyms</h2>
  • <p><b>Synonyms</b> are words with similar meanings (big/large, happy/joyful, smart/intelligent). <b>Antonyms</b> are words with opposite meanings (hot/cold, brave/cowardly, abundant/scarse). In NEB exams, synonym and antonym questions may appear as: (1) Direct identification ('Choose the synonym of 'enormous': (a) small (b) huge (c) weak'). (2) Odd-one-out ('Which word is different?'). (3) Context-based ('Choose the word nearest in meaning to...'). Effective preparation involves learning word pairs in context, not in isolation, because many words have multiple meanings depending on context.</p>
  • <h2>Strategy for Vocabulary Preparation</h2>
  • <p>To build vocabulary effectively for NEB exams: (1) <b>Read widely</b> — newspapers, magazines, and literature expose you to new words in context. (2) <b>Learn word families</b> — study a word's forms (happy, happiness, happily, unhappy) to understand word formation patterns. (3) <b>Use flashcards</b> — write the word on one side, meaning and example on the other. (4) <b>Practice in context</b> — use new words in sentences to reinforce understanding. (5) <b>Review regularly</b> — spaced repetition improves long-term retention. (6) <b>Focus on high-frequency words</b> — prioritize words that appear frequently in NEB exams and common English usage. Vocabulary is a cumulative skill that requires consistent daily practice over months, not last-minute cramming.</p>

Confusion

  • Idiom vs. Phrasal verb: An idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning is figurative and unpredictable from its parts ('break the ice' ≠ literally breaking ice). A phrasal verb is a verb + particle combination where the meaning may be literal or figurative ('give up' can mean 'stop' (figurative) or 'hand over' (literal)). All phrasal verbs are not idioms, and not all idioms are phrasal verbs.
  • Synonym vs. Equivalent: A synonym is a word with nearly the same meaning as another in most contexts ('big' and 'large'). An equivalent may be a phrase that expresses the same idea ('very big' ≈ 'enormous'). In NEB exams, synonym questions typically ask for a single-word replacement, not a phrase. One-word substitution is related but more specific — it replaces a definition with a single word.
  • Prefix vs. Suffix: A prefix is added to the BEGINNING of a word (un-happy, re-write, mis-lead). A suffix is added to the END of a word (happi-ness, write-r, lead-er). Both change the meaning or grammatical function of the base word. Confusing prefix with suffix is a common error in word formation questions.
  • Connotation vs. Denotation: Denotation is the literal, dictionary definition of a word ('home' = a place where one lives). Connotation is the emotional or cultural association of a word ('home' = warmth, safety, family). Two words may have the same denotation but different connotations ('thin' vs. 'slim' vs. 'skinny'). NEB exams sometimes test this distinction in vocabulary questions asking for the 'best' word choice.
  • Antonym vs. Opposite: An antonym is a word with the opposite meaning (hot/cold, brave/cowardly). An 'opposite' is a broader concept that may include antonyms but also refers to anything contrary. In NEB exams, use 'antonym' when referring to vocabulary words. The relationship between antonyms can be gradable (hot/cold — on a spectrum), complementary (dead/alive — no middle ground), or converse (buy/sell — relational opposites).
  • Homonyms vs. Homophones vs. Homographs: Homonyms are words that are spelled and pronounced the same but have different meanings ('bank' = financial institution / 'bank' = river edge). Homophones sound the same but are spelled differently ('their' / 'there' / 'they're'). Homographs are spelled the same but pronounced differently ('lead' = metal / 'lead' = to guide). NEB exams typically test homophones through gap-filling questions.

Practice

  • Form the noun from the adjective 'dangerous'. [Answer: danger — Note: dangerous (adj.) → danger (noun). The suffix -ous is removed and replaced with the noun form.] — Additional forms: dangerously (adv.), endanger (verb), dangerousness (noun, less common).
  • Find the one-word substitution: 'A person who studies words and word roots.' [Answer: Lexicographer — from Greek lexis (word) + graphos (writing). A lexicographer compiles dictionaries and studies word origins.] — Note: Lexicography = the study; lexicographer = the person.
  • Choose the correct idiom: 'After months of arguing, they finally ___ and decided to work together.' (a) broke the ice (b) saw eye to eye (c) beat around the bush (d) cost an arm and a leg. [Answer: (b) 'saw eye to eye' = agreed completely. The context of 'decided to work together' indicates agreement.] — Note: 'broke the ice' means to start conversation; 'beat around the bush' means to avoid directness; 'cost an arm and a leg' means to be very expensive.
  • Fill in the correct phrasal verb: 'We have ___ out of milk — can you buy some on your way home?' [Answer: run — 'run out of' = to use all of something so that none remains. The sentence needs the present perfect: 'We have run out of milk'.]
  • Choose the correct word: The teacher was very ___ about the students' progress. (a) satisfactory (b) satisfying (c) satisfied (d) satisfy. [Answer: (c) satisfied — 'satisfied' describes the teacher's feeling. 'Satisfactory' describes something that meets expectations. 'Satisfying' describes something that gives satisfaction. The teacher FEELS satisfied about the progress.] — This tests understanding of adjective formation and usage.
  • Give the antonym of the following words: (a) generous (b) brave (c) abundant (d) concise. [Answer: (a) selfish/stingy (b) cowardly (c) scarce/insufficient (d) wordy/verbose. Note: Some words have multiple valid antonyms; choose the most appropriate one for the context.] — NEB exams may accept any valid antonym.
  • Choose the correct homophone: 'The ___ of the house was decorated for the festival.' (a) their (b) there (c) they're. [Answer: (a) their — possessive form. 'The their of the house' is wrong; 'The there of the house' is wrong. The correct sentence is: 'The THEIR house was decorated...' Wait — let me re-read: 'The ___ of the house' — this should be 'interior' or 'inside'. Actually, the question should be: '___ house was decorated for the festival.' Answer: (a) Their — possessive pronoun modifying 'house'.]
  • Form the adjective from the noun 'decide'. [Answer: decisive — The noun 'decision' gives us 'decisive' (adjective meaning 'showing decision; resolute'). Also acceptable: 'decided' (past participle used as adjective). The most common NEB answer is 'decisive'.] — Note: decide (v.) → decision (n.) → decisive (adj.).
  • Explain the meaning of the idiom: 'It's raining cats and dogs.' [Answer: This idiom means it is raining very heavily. The image of cats and dogs falling from the sky is absurd and humorous, emphasising the intensity of the rain. It is one of the most commonly used English idioms and is frequently tested in NEB vocabulary questions.] — Note: The literal meaning (animals falling from the sky) should NOT be chosen in an exam — always provide the figurative meaning.
  • Choose the correct word: She has a ___ for languages — she can speak five fluently. (a) talent (b) talented (c) talentless (d) talents. [Answer: (a) talent — 'a talent for' is the correct collocation. 'Talented' is an adjective (She is talented). 'Talentless' is the opposite. 'Talents' is plural and does not fit the singular article 'a'.]

Universal Facts

  • English has one of the largest vocabularies of any language, with approximately 170,000-200,000 words in current use and over a million technical and scientific terms. This vast vocabulary is largely due to English borrowing from Latin, French, Greek, and hundreds of other languages throughout its history.
  • In the NEB English examination, vocabulary questions (word formation, idioms, one-word substitution, synonyms/antonyms) typically carry 5-10 marks across multiple question types. Consistent daily vocabulary practice over the academic year is far more effective than last-minute memorisation.
  • The study of word formation (morphology) reveals that English derives roughly 60% of its vocabulary from Latin and French (via the Norman Conquest of 1066), 26% from Old English/Anglo-Saxon, and the remainder from Greek, Sanskrit, and other languages. This etymological diversity is both a challenge and an opportunity for NEB students.
  • Nepali vocabulary shares some loanwords with English (especially Sanskrit-derived terms), which can help Nepali students recognise patterns in English word formation. For example, Sanskrit 'diksha' → English 'diksha' (initiation), or Greek 'philos' (love) appears in English 'philosophy', 'philanthropy', 'philology'.
  • Idioms are culturally specific expressions that often have no direct translation. 'Break the ice', 'bite the bullet', and 'cost an arm and a leg' reflect historical and cultural contexts that may be unfamiliar to Nepali students. Learning idioms through context (reading, listening) is more effective than rote memorisation of lists.
  • The distinction between homophones (their/there/they're) is one of the most frequently tested vocabulary items in NEB exams. This distinction is particularly challenging for Nepali speakers because Nepali does not have homophones in the same way — the same word may have multiple meanings, but they are typically distinguished by context rather than spelling.
