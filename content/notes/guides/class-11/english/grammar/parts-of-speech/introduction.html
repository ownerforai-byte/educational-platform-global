{ "title": "Parts of Speech", "unitSlug": "grammar", "topicSlug": "parts-of-speech", "topicTitle": "Parts of Speech", "relevance": 95, "notes": [ "<h2>What Are Parts of Speech?</h2>", "<p><b>Parts of Speech</b> are the categories into which words are classified based on their function in a sentence. English has eight traditional parts of speech: <b>noun</b>, <b>pronoun</b>, <b>adjective</b>, <b>verb</b>, <b>adverb</b>, <b>preposition</b>, <b>conjunction</b>, and <b>interjection</b>. name general items (e.g., <b>city</b>, <b>girl</b>, <b>country</b>). <b>Collective Nouns</b> refer to a group considered as a whole (e.g., <b>team</b>, <b>herd</b>, <b>committee</b>). <b>Material Nouns</b> name substances (e.g., <b>gold</b>, <b>water</b>, <b>wood</b>). <b>Abstract Nouns</b> name ideas or qualities (e.g., <b>honesty</b>, <b>bravery</b>, <b>freedom</b>). <b>Countable Nouns</b> can be counted and have plural forms; <b>Uncountable Nouns</b> cannot be counted and take singular verbs.</p>", "<h2>Pronoun: Types and Uses</h2>", "<p>A <b>pronoun</b> is a word that takes the place of a noun to avoid repetition. <b>Personal Pronouns</b> refer to specific people or things: I, you, he, she, it, we, they (and their objective forms: me, him, her, us, them). <b>Reflective Pronouns</b> end in -self/-selves and are used when the subject and object are the same (e.g., 'She taught herself'). <b>Relative Pronouns</b> (who, whom, whose, which, that) connect clauses to nouns. <b>Demonstrative Pronouns</b> (this, that, these, those) point to specific things. <b>Indefinite Pronouns</b> (some, any, none, all, each, either, neither) refer to non-specific entities. <b>Interrogative Pronouns</b> (who, whom, which, what) are used to ask questions.</p>", "<h2>Verb: Action and State</h2>", "<p>A <b>verb</b> is the most important part of speech — it expresses action or state of being. <b>Action Verbs</b> show physical or mental activity (e.g., <b>run</b>, <b>think</b>, <b>write</b>). <b>State Verbs</b> (also called stative verbs) describe conditions rather than actions (e.g., <b>be</b>, <b>seem</b>, <b>belong</b>, <b>know</b>). <b>Transitive Verbs</b> require a direct object (e.g., 'She <b>kicked</b> the ball'). <b>Intransitive Verbs</b> do not take an object (e.g., 'He <b>slept</b>'). <b>Linking Verbs</b> (be, seem, become, appear) connect the subject to a complement. <b>Auxiliary (Helping) Verbs</b> (be, do, have) combine with main verbs to form tenses, voices, and moods.</p>", "<h2>Adjective: Describing Nouns</h2>", "<p>An <b>adjective</b> modifies or describes a noun or pronoun, providing more information about its quality, quantity, or identity. <b>Descriptive Adjectives</b> describe qualities (e.g., <b>beautiful</b>, <b>tall</b>, <b>intelligent</b>). <b>Demonstrative Adjectives</b> (this, that, these, those) identify specific nouns. <b>Quantitative Adjectives</b> (some, many, few, little, enough) indicate quantity. <b>Numeral Adjectives</b> (one, first, double) indicate number or order. <b>Possessive Adjectives</b> (my, your, his, her, its, our, their) show ownership. <b>Interrogative Adjectives</b> (which, what, whose) modify nouns in questions. Adjectives generally precede the noun they modify ('a <b>red</b> car'), but some follow linking verbs ('The sky is <b>blue</b>').</p>", "<h2>Adverb: Modifying More Than Verbs</h2>", "<p>An <b>adverb</b> modifies verbs, adjectives, other adverbs, or even entire sentences. Many adverbs are formed by adding <b>-ly</b> to adjectives, but important exceptions exist: <b>fast</b> (not 'fastly'), <b>hard</b> vs. <b>hardly</b> (different meanings), <b>late</b> vs. <b>lately</b>.</p>", "<h2>Preposition: Showing Relationships</h2>", "<p>A <b>preposition</b> shows the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and another word in the sentence. Prepositions commonly indicate <b>place</b> (at, in, on, under, above, between), <b>time</b> (at, on, in, before, after, during), and <b>direction</b> (to, into, towards, through). Common prepositions include: <b>at</b> (specific time/place), <b>in</b> (enclosed spaces, months, years), <b>on</b> (surfaces, days), <b>by</b> (agent, near), <b>with</b> (accompaniment), <b>from</b> (origin), <b>about</b> (concerning). Use <b>a</b> before consonant <i>sounds</i> (not just letters): 'a university' (yoo-niversity), 'a one-way street' (won-way). Use <b>an</b> before vowel <i>sounds</i>: 'an hour' (the h is silent), 'an MBA degree'. Articles are one of the most frequently misused elements by NEB students, especially in written passages and error correction exercises.</p>", "<h2>Article: The Definite Article</h2>", "<p>The <b>definite article</b> <b>the</b> is used before nouns that are specific or already known to the reader. Use 'the' when: the noun has been mentioned before ('I saw a dog. <b>The</b> dog was black'); there is only one of something ('<b>The</b> sun rises in the east'); superlatives and ordinals ('the best', 'the first'); rivers, oceans, mountain ranges, plural country names ('the Himalaya', 'the USA'); and musical instruments ('play <b>the</b> guitar'). (2) Collective nouns (team, class, government) usually take singular verbs when the group acts as one unit, but plural when members act individually. (3) Subjects joined by <b>and</b> usually take plural verbs ('Ravi and Sita <b>are</b> here').
