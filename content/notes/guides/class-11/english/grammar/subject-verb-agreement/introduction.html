A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. This is one of the most frequently tested grammar rules in NEB English exams, appearing in gap-filling, error correction, and multiple-choice questions.</p>", "<h2>Basic Rule: Singular vs. Plural</h2>", "<p>The fundamental rule: <b>Singular subjects</b> take singular verbs (usually ending in -s in present tense). <b>Plural subjects</b> take plural verbs (no -s in present tense). Examples: 'The boy <b>runs</b>' (singular subject + singular verb). 'The boys <b>run</b>' (plural subject + plural verb). 'She <b>is</b> here' vs. 'They <b>are</b> here'. Note that the verb 'to be' is irregular: I am, you are, he/she/it is, we are, they are. This irregularity is a common source of errors.</p>", "<h2>Subjects Joined by 'And'</h2>", "<p>When two or more subjects are joined by <b>'and'</b>, they usually take a <b>plural</b> verb: 'Rama and Shyam <b>are</b> friends.' 'The teacher and the principal <b>are</b> coming.' Exception: When the two nouns joined by 'and' refer to the same person or thing, use a singular verb: 'The singer and songwriter <b>is</b> performing tonight' (one person who is both singer and songwriter). 'Bread and butter <b>is</b> my favourite snack' (a single combination).</p>", "<h2>Subjects Joined by 'Or' / 'Nor'</h2>", "<p>When subjects are joined by <b>'or'</b>, <b>'nor'</b>, <b>'either...or'</b>, or <b>'neither...nor'</b>, the verb agrees with the <b>nearer subject</b> (proximity rule). Examples: 'Neither the teacher nor the <b>students</b> were present.' (nearer subject 'students' is plural → plural verb). 'Either the students or the <b>teacher</b> is responsible.' (nearer subject 'teacher' is singular → singular verb). 'Neither Ram nor his friends <b>are</b> coming.' Examples: 'Each of the boys <b>has</b> a pen.' 'Nobody <b>knows</b> the answer.' 'Everyone <b>is</b> present.' However, 'both, few, several, many' take <b>plural</b> verbs: 'Both of them <b>are</b> coming.' 'Few of the students <b>have</b> passed.' 'Several of the books <b>are</b> missing.'</p>", "<h2>'Each' and 'Every': Singular Verbs</h2>", "<p>The words <b>'each'</b> and <b>'every'</b> always take singular verbs, even when followed by a plural noun in a prepositional phrase. 'Each of the students <b>has</b> a book.' (NOT 'have'). 'Every boy and girl <b>has</b> a seat.' (NOT 'have'). 'Each' emphasises individuality within a group; 'every' emphasises the group as a whole.
