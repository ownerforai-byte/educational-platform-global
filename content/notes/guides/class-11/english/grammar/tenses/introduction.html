1.

<h2>What Are Tenses?</h2>

2.

<p><b>Tense</b> is a verb form that shows the time of an action or state — past, present, or future. English has three main tenses: <b>Present</b>, <b>Past</b>, and <b>Future</b>. Each main tense has four aspects: <b>Simple</b>, <b>Continuous</b> (Progressive), <b>Perfect</b>, and <b>Perfect Continuous</b>. This gives English a total of twelve tense forms. Tense is one of the most important grammar topics in the NEB Class 11 English syllabus and appears frequently in both objective and descriptive questions.</p>

3.

<h2>Present Simple Tense</h2>

4.

<p>The <b>present simple</b> tense is used for: (1) <b>habits and routines</b> — 'I <b>wake</b> up at six every day.' (2) <b>General truths and facts</b> — 'Water <b>boils</b> at 100 degrees Celsius.' (3) <b>Permanent situations</b> — 'She <b>lives</b> in Pokhara.' (4) <b>Scheduled events</b> — 'The train <b>leaves</b> at 9 AM.' The form is: subject + base verb (add -s/-es for third person singular). Negative: do not/does not + base verb. Question: Do/Does + subject + base verb? Time markers: always, usually, often, sometimes, never, every day/week/year.</p>

5.

<h2>Present Continuous Tense</h2>

6.

<p>The <b>present continuous</b> tense describes actions happening right now or around the present time. Form: subject + am/is/are + verb-ing. Uses: (1) Actions happening at the moment of speaking — 'She <b>is writing</b> a letter.' (2) Temporary situations — 'I <b>am staying</b> with my cousin this week.' (3) Future arrangements — 'We <b>are meeting</b> them tomorrow.' (4) Changing situations — 'The climate <b>is getting</b> warmer.' Important: Stative verbs (know, believe, belong, love, hate, seem, understand) are generally NOT used in continuous forms. We say 'I know him', not 'I am knowing him'.</p>

7.

<h2>Present Perfect Tense</h2>

8.

<p>The <b>present perfect</b> tense connects the past with the present. Form: subject + has/have + past participle. Uses: (1) Actions completed at an unspecified time — 'I <b>have visited</b> Chitwan.' (2) Actions that started in the past and continue to the present — 'She <b>has lived</b> here for ten years.' (3) Recent actions with present results — 'I <b>have lost</b> my keys' (so I can't enter now). (4) Life experiences — 'He <b>has read</b> all of Shakespeare.' Time markers: already, yet, just, ever, never, recently, so far, up to now, since, for.</p>

9.

<h2>Present Perfect Continuous Tense</h2>

10.

<p>The <b>present perfect continuous</b> emphasises the duration of an action that started in the past and continues to the present, or has just finished with visible results. Form: subject + has/have + been + verb-ing. Example: 'I <b>have been studying</b> English since 2019.' Compare with present perfect: 'I <b>have studied</b> English for five years' (focus on completion/amount) vs. 'I <b>have been studying</b> English for five years' (focus on the ongoing activity). Both are grammatically correct but carry slightly different emphasis.</p>

11.

<h2>Past Simple Tense</h2>

12.

<p>The <b>past simple</b> tense describes completed actions at a specific time in the past. Form: subject + past form of verb (regular verbs add -ed; irregular verbs have unique past forms). Uses: (1) Completed actions — 'I <b>went</b> to Kathmandu last year.' (2) Series of completed actions — 'He <b>woke</b> up, <b>had</b> breakfast, and <b>left</b> for school.' (3) Past habits — 'I <b>played</b> cricket every evening when I was young.' Time markers: yesterday, last week/year, ago, in 2010, when I was a child. Regular past form: add -ed (walked, played). Irregular past forms must be memorised (go→went, see→saw, eat→ate).</p>

13.

<h2>Past Continuous Tense</h2>

14.

<p>The <b>past continuous</b> tense describes actions that were in progress at a specific time in the past. Form: subject + was/were + verb-ing. Uses: (1) An action in progress at a specific past time — 'At 8 PM yesterday, I <b>was watching</b> TV.' (2) A longer action interrupted by a shorter one — 'I <b>was sleeping</b> when the phone <b>rang</b>.' (The continuous tense for the longer action; simple past for the interruption.) (3) Two simultaneous ongoing actions — 'While I <b>was cooking</b>, she <b>was cleaning</b>.' Time markers: while, when, as, at 5 PM yesterday, all day yesterday.</p>

15.

<h2>Past Perfect Tense</h2>

16.

<p>The <b>past perfect</b> tense describes an action completed before another action or time in the past. It is the 'past before the past.' Form: subject + had + past participle. Example: 'When I reached the station, the train <b>had already left</b>.' The leaving happened before the reaching. Use 'had + past participle' for the earlier action and simple past for the later action. Time markers: already, just, never, before, by the time, until then. This tense is essential for narrative writing and is frequently tested in gap-filling exercises.</p>

17.

<h2>Past Perfect Continuous Tense</h2>

18.

<p>The <b>past perfect continuous</b> emphasises the duration of an action that was ongoing before another past action or time. Form: subject + had + been + verb-ing. Example: 'She <b>had been waiting</b> for two hours when the bus finally arrived.' This highlights that the waiting started before the bus arrived and continued up to that point. Compare: 'She had waited for two hours' (focus on completion) vs. 'She had been waiting for two hours' (focus on the prolonged activity). Use this tense when the duration is important to the meaning.</p>

19.

<h2>Future Simple Tense</h2>

20.

<p>The <b>future simple</b> tense describes actions that will happen after the present moment. Form: subject + will/shall + base verb. Uses: (1) Predictions — 'It <b>will rain</b> tomorrow.' (2) Spontaneous decisions — 'I <b>will help</b> you with your homework.' (3) Promises and offers — 'I <b>will never</b> forget this.' (4) Future facts — 'The sun <b>will rise</b> at 6 AM.' Note: 'Shall' is used with I and we in formal British English for future (I shall, we shall), though 'will' is acceptable everywhere. In American English, 'will' is used with all persons.</p>

21.

<h2>Future Continuous Tense</h2>

22.

<p>The <b>future continuous</b> tense describes actions that will be in progress at a specific time in the future. Form: subject + will be + verb-ing. Example: 'This time tomorrow, I <b>will be travelling</b> to Pokhara.' It can also describe parallel actions in the future: 'While you <b>will be sleeping</b>, I <b>will be studying</b>.' This tense can also be used for polite enquiries about someone's plans: 'Will you be using the car tonight?'</p>

23.

<h2>Future Perfect Tense</h2>

24.

<p>The <b>future perfect</b> tense describes an action that will be completed before a specific time in the future. Form: subject + will have + past participle. Example: 'By next year, I <b>will have graduated</b> from school.' The graduation will be completed before next year arrives. Time markers: by the time, by next year/month/day, before, when. This tense is commonly tested in gap-filling exercises where the time marker 'by + future time' signals its use.</p>

25.

<h2>Future Perfect Continuous Tense</h2>

26.

<p>The <b>future perfect continuous</b> emphasises the duration of an action up to a point in the future. Form: subject + will have been + verb-ing. Example: 'By December, I <b>will have been studying</b> English for five years.' This combines the future perfect (completion by a future point) with the continuous aspect (duration). Use this tense when you want to stress how long an action will have been going on by a particular future moment.</p>

27.

<h2>Overview Table of All Twelve Tenses</h2>

28.

<p>Present: Simple (I write), Continuous (I am writing), Perfect (I have written), Perfect Continuous (I have been writing). Past: Simple (I wrote), Continuous (I was writing), Perfect (I had written), Perfect Continuous (I had been writing). Future: Simple (I will write), Continuous (I will be writing), Perfect (I will have written), Perfect Continuous (I will have been writing). The key to mastering tenses is understanding <b>aspect</b>: simple (completed/habitual), continuous (ongoing/in progress), perfect (completed before a reference point), perfect continuous (duration up to a reference point).</p>

29.

<h2>Stative Verbs: Special Rules</h2>

30.

<p><b>Stative verbs</b> (also called state verbs) describe states rather than actions and are generally NOT used in continuous tenses. Categories include: <b>Thought</b> (know, believe, understand, remember, forget, suppose, mean). <b>Emotion</b> (love, like, hate, prefer, want, need, hope, wish). <b>Sense/perception</b> (see, hear, smell, taste, feel, recognize). <b>Possession</b> (have, possess, belong, own). <b>Being/state</b> (be, seem, appear, cost, weigh). Exception: Some stative verbs can be used in continuous form when they have a different meaning. 'I <b>am having</b> lunch' (eating = action) vs. 'I <b>have</b> a car' (possession = state). 'The soup <b>tastes</b> good' (perception) vs. 'She is <b>tasting</b> the soup' (action of sampling).</p>

31.

<h2>Sequence of Tenses in Reported Speech</h2>

32.

<p>When reporting speech, tense shifting (backshifting) usually occurs: Present simple → past simple ('I like apples' → She said she <b>liked</b> apples). Present continuous → past continuous. Present perfect → past perfect. Past simple → past perfect. Future (will) → conditional (would). However, backshifting is NOT done when: (1) The reported information is still true — 'The Earth revolves around the Sun' (reported as present simple). (2) The reporting verb is in present/future tense — 'He says he <b>is</b> tired.' (3) Using modal verbs that have no past form (ought to, might, could, would, should).</p>

33.

<h2>Tense Markers and Signal Words</h2>

34.

<p>Each tense has characteristic time markers that signal its use. <b>Present simple</b>: always, usually, often, sometimes, never, every day, on Mondays. <b>Present continuous</b>: now, at the moment, currently, today, this week. <b>Present perfect</b>: already, yet, just, ever, never, recently, so far, since, for. <b>Past simple</b>: yesterday, last week, ago, in 1999, when I was young. <b>Past perfect</b>: already, just, never, before, by the time. <b>Future simple</b>: tomorrow, next week, in 2030, soon. Recognising these markers quickly helps in gap-filling and tense conversion exercises in NEB exams.</p>

35.

<h2>Common Tense Errors by NEB Students</h2>

36.

<p>Common errors include: (1) Using present perfect with specific past time — 'I have seen him yesterday' (wrong) → 'I saw him yesterday' (right). (2) Using simple past instead of past perfect — 'When I reached, the train left' (wrong) → 'When I reached, the train had left' (right). (3) Using continuous with stative verbs — 'I am knowing him' (wrong) → 'I know him' (right). (4) Confusing present perfect with past simple — 'I have lost my key yesterday' (wrong) → 'I lost my key yesterday' (right). 39.
  • Present Perfect vs. Past Simple: Use past simple with a specific past time (I saw him yesterday). Use present perfect without a specific past time or with 'since/for' (I have seen him / I have known him for years). You CANNOT use present perfect with words like 'yesterday', 'last week', 'ago', 'in 1990'.
  • Past Simple vs. Past Perfect: Past perfect is used for the earlier of two past actions. 'When I arrived, the movie started' means they happened simultaneously. 'When I arrived, the movie had started' means the movie began before I arrived. Always use past perfect for the action that happened first when two past actions are described together.
  • Will vs. Shall: Traditionally, 'shall' is used with I and we for simple future (I shall go, we shall see). 'Will' is used with all persons. In modern English, 'will' is used with all persons in both British and American English. 'Shall' survives mainly in questions with I/we (Shall we dance? Shall I open the window?) and in formal/legal language.
  • Present Perfect Continuous vs. Present Perfect: Use present perfect simple for completed actions with present results (I have painted the room — it's done). Use present perfect continuous for ongoing actions emphasising duration (I have been painting the room — I may still be working on it). Some verbs (know, believe, have) are rarely used in continuous forms even for duration.
  • Past Simple vs. Past Continuous: Past simple describes completed actions (I read the book). Past continuous describes ongoing actions at a specific past time (I was reading when you called). When both appear in one sentence, the continuous form is the background action and the simple form is the interrupting action.
  • Will vs. Going to: 'Going to' is used for planned intentions and predictions with present evidence (Look at those clouds! It's going to rain). 'Will' is used for spontaneous decisions, predictions without evidence, promises, and offers (I will help you). In NEB exams, distinguishing these is tested through gap-filling and sentence transformation.
  • Since vs. For with Perfect Tenses: 'Since' marks the starting point of an action (since 2015, since Monday, since I was a child). 'For' marks the duration (for five years, for two hours, for a long time). Both are used with perfect tenses, but never interchangeably: 'I have lived here since 2015' vs. 'I have lived here for nine years'.

45.
  • English is unusual among European languages in having a rich tense-aspect system. Many languages (like Chinese and Japanese) do not have grammatical tense at all, expressing time through context and time words instead.
  • The English present perfect tense has no direct equivalent in many languages, making it one of the most difficult concepts for Nepali-speaking learners, since Nepali uses a single past tense (या/यो) that covers both simple past and present perfect meanings.
  • The distinction between simple and continuous aspects is present in nearly all Indo-European languages but is expressed differently. In English, the continuous aspect uses the auxiliary 'be' + '-ing', a pattern inherited from Old English.
  • In the NEB English examination, tense questions typically appear in gap-filling (3-5 marks), error correction (2-3 marks), and sentence transformation (2-3 marks), making this topic one of the highest-yield grammar areas for exam preparation.
  • The use of 'shall' with first person is increasingly rare in modern English, especially in American English. Most native speakers now use 'will' with all persons. However, NEB exams may still test traditional usage, so students should know both forms.
  • Stative verbs are a uniquely English challenge because many verbs that are stative in English can be used dynamically in other languages. For example, 'I am thinking about the answer' (process) vs. 'I think the answer is correct' (opinion/state).

Grammar

Notes
  • Tense (noun): a verb form indicating time. The word comes from Latin 'tempus' meaning time.
  • Aspect (noun): how an action extends over time — simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous.
  • Stative verb (noun phrase): a verb describing a state, not an action (e.g., know, love, belong). Generally not used in continuous forms.
  • Auxiliary verb (noun phrase): helping verb (be, have, do) used to form tenses.
  • Past participle (noun phrase): verb form used in perfect tenses (e.g., eaten, gone, written).
  • Time marker (noun phrase): a word indicating when an action occurs (e.g., yesterday, since, for).
Important Statements
  • In English grammar, 'tense' strictly means time reference (past/present/future), while 'aspect' means how the action is viewed (completed/ongoing). Many learners confuse these.
  • The present perfect tense connects past to present — it is NOT a past tense, despite describing past actions.
  • Stative verbs are a common error source: 'I am knowing' is incorrect; use 'I know'.
