Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Real Lenses Blur

    Ideal lenses focus every ray to one point; real lenses don't. Different colours focus at different distances (chromatic aberration), and wide beams never meet at a single point (spherical aberration). Understanding both defects is the first step to correcting them.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Chromatic aberration

    • Cause: dispersion — n depends on wavelength, so the lens maker's formula gives a different f for each colour: fv​<fr​ (violet, larger n, focuses closer).
    • Effect: fringes of colour around white-light images; blurred photographs.
    • Nature: a chromatic (colour) defect — absent for monochromatic light.
    • Remedies: achromatic doublets (Topic 82), long-focus + small-aperture designs, reflecting mirrors (no dispersion → no colour error).

    2.2 Spherical aberration

    • Cause: rays through the edge of a spherical surface refract more strongly than paraxial rays, meeting closer to the lens; paraxial rays focus farther out.
    • Effect: a single point object images as a blurred disc; the focal "point" is a small region.
    • Nature: a geometric defect — happens even for monochromatic light.
    • Remedies: stops/apertures that cut edge rays, parabolic mirrors, combinations of lenses sharing the aberration, meniscus lens shapes.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 80: chromatic aberration is dispersion applied to a lens (f_v < f_y < f_r).
    • To Topic 82: the achromatic combination cancels chromatic aberration by pairing dispersions.
    • To the lens maker's formula: f ∝ 1/(n − 1) links colour to focus.

  • Violet focuses closer (larger n → shorter f); red focuses farther.

Summary

Real lenses suffer two defects. Chromatic aberration comes from dispersion: since n varies with wavelength, the lens maker's formula gives each colour its own focal length — violet (largest n) focuses closest, red farthest — so white-light images show coloured fringes; it vanishes for monochromatic light and is cured by achromatic doublets or by mirrors. Spherical aberration is geometric: rays through the edge of a spherical surface bend more than paraxial rays and cross the axis nearer the lens, blurring the focus even in monochrome light; it is reduced by aperture stops, parabolic surfaces and matched lens combinations. Cause, colour-dependence and cure distinguish the pair.

Special Notes

  • Stopping down does not make glass non-dispersive.
  • Primary achromatism reduces selected colour separation but may leave secondary spectrum.

Important Statements

  • "Spherical aberration arises because marginal and paraxial rays of the same colour focus at different points."

Specific Conditions

  • Dispersion Definition: Dispersion is the separation of white light into its constituent colors (spectrum) due to the dependence of the refractive index n on the wavelength λ of light (n(λ)).
  • Cauchy's Equation: For many transparent materials, the refractive index follows Cauchy's equation n(λ)=A+B/λ2+C/λ4. This explains why blue light (λ smaller) is refracted more than red light (λ larger).
  • Prism Validity: The formula for dispersive power ω=(nv​−nr​)/(ny​−1) assumes small angle of deviation and that the prism is in air. For non-thin prisms or different mediums, angular dispersion depends on the geometry.

Exercises

1.

A lens brings paraxial rays to a focus 15.0 cm from the lens and marginal rays to 14.2 cm. Find the longitudinal spherical aberration.

Steps
  • Longitudinal spherical aberration is the gap between the two foci.
  • 15.0 − 14.2 = 0.80 cm.
  • Marginal rays focus nearer the lens.
  • A smaller aperture would reduce this gap.
  • This number is not the chromatic gap, which needs two colours.
Answer

0.80 cm.
