Notes

  • Type 2 — alternate treatment: this version derives all three equations of motion graphically (from the velocity–time graph) — the derivation style NEB asks for 'using a graphical method'.
  • Setup: a body starts with initial velocity u and accelerates uniformly at a; after time t its velocity is v. On a v–t graph this is a straight line from (0,u) to (t,v) — the straightness is exactly the meaning of uniform acceleration.
  • First equation (from the slope): slope of the line = acceleration a=tv−u​. Rearranging: v=u+at — 'final velocity = initial velocity + rate of change of velocity × time'.
  • Second equation (from the area): displacement = area under the v–t line = area of the trapezoid = area of rectangle ut + area of triangle 21​×t×(v−u). Since v−u=at: s=ut+21​at2.
  • Second equation, alternate area view: average velocity × time: s=(2u+v​)t — the trapezoid's mean-height form. Both views give the same result and both are accepted in exams.
  • Third equation (eliminating t): from t=av−u​ substitute into s=(2u+v​)t: s=2a(u+v)(v−u)​=2av2−u2​, giving v2=u2+2as — the time-free equation, ideal when t is neither given nor asked.
  • Reading the other graphs: on an x–t graph, constant a appears as a parabola (curvature fixed); the tangent slope at any point is v. On an a–t graph, constant a is a horizontal line and the area under it (a×t) gives the change in velocity v−u.
  • Displacement in the n-th second: sn​=u+2a​(2n−1) — obtained from areas: distance covered between t=n−1 and t=n. For free fall from rest this gives the odd-numbers rule 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 (Galileo).
  • NEB exam focus: derive v=u+at and s=ut+21​at2 with a labelled v–t graph; derive v2=u2+2as; state which equation to choose from what is given/asked; apply to braking, free fall and projected bodies.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'The area under the v–t graph gives acceleration.' ✅ Area gives DISPLACEMENT; the SLOPE gives acceleration. Swap-proof: slope of v–t = a (m/s²), area of v–t = s (m).
  • ❌ Using s=ut+21​at2 for the distance in the n-th second directly with t=n. ✅ That gives the total distance in n seconds. The n-th SECOND distance is sn​=u+2a​(2n−1), i.e. s(n)−s(n−1).
  • ❌ Applying v2=u2+2as with sign errors during braking. ✅ Take one positive direction and keep it: braking at 20 m/s with a=−5 m/s² gives 0=400+2(−5)s⇒s=40 m — the minus sign is essential.
  • ❌ 'The equations work if acceleration changes gradually.' ✅ All three equations REQUIRE constant acceleration; if a varies, use graphs/areas or calculus.
  • ❌ Treating u and v as interchangeable. ✅ u is the velocity at t=0; v is at time t. A body dropped from rest has u=0; a body thrown up has u=+20 m/s while a=−10 m/s².
  • ❌ Confusing distance with displacement when the body reverses. ✅ The equations give displacement (vector). For total path length through a reversal, split the motion at the v=0 instant and add the two parts.

Practice

  • Derive s=ut+21​at2 from the trapezoid area, stating every step and labelling the v–t graph.
  • A car brakes from 30 m/s to rest in 6 s. Using a v–t graph find the acceleration and stopping distance. — a=−5 m/s²; s=230×6​=90 m (triangle area).
  • A body starting at 4 m/s accelerates at 2 m/s². Find (a) v at 5 s, (b) s in 5 s, (c) s in the 5th second. — (a) 14 m/s; (b) 45 m; (c) 4+1(9)=13 m.
  • A stone dropped from a cliff hits the ground at 40 m/s (g=10 m/s²). Find the cliff height using v2=u2+2as and the fall time. — s=80 m; t=4 s.
  • A train travelling at 72 km/h applies brakes producing −0.5 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping, and how long does it take? — u=20 m/s; s=400 m; t=40 s.

Universal Facts

  • The velocity–time 'trapezoid' derivation appears in Galileo's reasoning (via inclined planes and odd-number distances) two centuries before formal calculus existed.
  • Air-force and driving-school 'stopping distance' charts are exactly the v2=u2+2as equation with v=0 — doubling speed quadruples braking distance.
  • Crash investigators reconstruct vehicle speeds from skid marks using u=2μgs​ — the same third equation rearranged with friction as the deceleration.
  • Physics simulators (games, driving sims) integrate v–t areas every frame — the trapezoid rule your textbook uses graphically is the numerical engine behind them.

Examples

  • Example 1 (slope): a cyclist's v–t line rises from 2 m/s to 10 m/s in 4 s → a=410−2​=2 m/s², read straight off the graph.
  • Example 2 (area): the same cyclist covers s=22+10​×4=24 m — the trapezoid area, no equation needed.
  • Example 3 (third equation): a car at 25 m/s needs to stop within 50 m. Required deceleration: 0=252+2a(50)⇒a=−6.25 m/s² — time never enters, which is why this equation fits.
  • Example 4 (two-stage motion): a train accelerates at 1 m/s² for 20 s then brakes at 2 m/s² to stop. Draw the v–t trapezoid; peak speed 20 m/s; total distance = area = 300 m.
  • Example 5 (drop with initial speed): a stone thrown down a well at 2 m/s strikes water 3 s later: s=2(3)+21​(10)(9)=51 m — the rectangle-plus-triangle areas directly.

Formulas

  • v=u+at (slope of v–t line)
  • s=ut+21​at2 (rectangle + triangle area)
  • s=(2u+v​)t (trapezoid mean-height form)
  • v2=u2+2as (time eliminated)
  • sn​=u+2a​(2n−1) (distance in the n-th second)

Key Points

  • Slope of v–t = acceleration; area under v–t = displacement.
  • The three equations hold ONLY for constant acceleration.
  • v2=u2+2as is the equation of choice when time is unknown.
  • s=(2u+v​)t is the graphical 'average velocity × time' form.
  • Sign convention decides deceleration: keep one positive direction throughout the problem.
  • Distance in the n-th second ≠ distance in n seconds — the classic NEB distinction.

Summary

All three equations of motion follow from the straight-line v–t graph of uniform acceleration: the slope gives v=u+at; the area (rectangle + triangle, or the trapezoid mean-height 2u+v​t) gives s=ut+21​at2; eliminating t yields v2=u2+2as. The a–t graph (area = change in velocity) and the x–t parabola complete the graphical picture. These derivations are standard NEB questions, and the equations themselves — with careful sign conventions — solve braking, free-fall and multi-stage motion numericals.

Special Notes

  • Note 1: Always draw and label the v–t graph in derivation questions — the diagram itself carries marks.
  • Note 2: For two-stage journeys (accelerate then brake), apply the equations to EACH stage and add the areas.
  • Note 3: Convert km/h to m/s before substituting (divide by 3.6) — the most common numerical slip.
  • Note 4: If the body reverses direction, compute each leg separately and add magnitudes for distance, not displacement.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: For uniformly accelerated motion, the velocity–time graph is a straight line whose slope equals the acceleration.
  • Statement 2: The displacement in uniformly accelerated motion equals the area of the trapezoid under the v–t line.
  • Statement 3: The three equations of motion are valid only when acceleration is constant in magnitude and direction.
  • Statement 4: The area under an acceleration–time graph equals the change in velocity over that interval.

Important Notes

  • Note 1: v2=u2+2as contains no time — use it whenever t is unknown or irrelevant.
  • Note 2: The mean-velocity form s=2u+v​t is valid only for constant acceleration (it is the trapezoid's average height).
  • Note 3: Take downward as negative or positive consistently — free-fall and upward-throw problems share the same equations with different signs.
  • Note 4: For the n-th second distance, subtract s(n−1) from s(n) — never substitute t=n alone.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Given/asked table — list u, v, a, t, s; the equation missing BOTH the unknown and the unwanted quantity is your equation.
  • Trick 2: Braking distance without time: s=u2/2∣a∣ — memorise this special case of the third equation.
  • Trick 3: Odd-number rule for drop from rest: distances in successive seconds are 1:3:5:7 (× g/2).
  • Trick 4: In two-stage problems, the v–t trapezoid area is usually faster than applying equations twice.
  • Trick 5: Sanity check: for braking, your a must be negative and s positive — if both come out negative, a sign slipped.

Exam Notes

  • Exam Tip 1: The graphical derivation of v=u+at and s=ut+21​at2 is a repeating NEB theory question — practise the labelled figure.
  • Exam Tip 2: In numericals, write the chosen equation first, then substitute — method marks live in the equation selection.
  • Exam Tip 3: Convert all units to SI (km/h ÷ 3.6) before any substitution, and state the sign convention used.
  • Exam Tip 4: Multi-stage questions (lift, train, rocket) reward a quick v–t sketch before any algebra.
