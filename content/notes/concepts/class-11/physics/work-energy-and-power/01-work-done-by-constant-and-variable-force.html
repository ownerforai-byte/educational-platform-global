Notes

  • Definition of work (constant force): W=Fscos heta, where F is the magnitude of the constant force, s is the displacement, and  heta is the angle between the force vector and displacement vector. Work is a scalar quantity - it has magnitude but no direction.
  • Sign of work: Work is POSITIVE when 0^\\circ \\le \ heta < 90^\\circ (force component along displacement). Work is ZERO when \ heta = 90^\\circ (force perpendicular to displacement, e.g., centripetal force, normal force on horizontal surface). Work is NEGATIVE when 90^\\circ < \ heta \\le 180^\\circ (force opposes displacement, e.g., kinetic friction, gravity when lifting).
  • Work by multiple forces: Total work is the sum of works by individual forces: W extnet​=sumWi​=sumFi​scos hetai​. This is equivalent to the work done by the net force: W extnet​=F extnet​scos heta extnet​.
  • Variable force definition: When force varies with position, work is the integral of force over the displacement: W=intxi​xf​​F(x),dx (for 1D motion). Graphically, this is the area under the F vs x curve between the initial and final positions.
  • Work by a spring force (Hooke's law): For an ideal spring, F=−kx. Work done BY the spring when moving from xi​ to xf​: W extspring​=intxi​xf​​(−kx)dx=\ rac12kxi2​−\ rac12kxf2​. Work done ON the spring (by external agent) is the negative of this.
  • Work by gravity (near Earth): Gravity is a constant force Fg​=mg downward. If an object moves vertically by Deltay (up positive), Wg​=−mgDeltay. If it moves horizontally, Wg​=0. For a path with vertical drop h, Wg​=+mgh (gravity does positive work).
  • Work by a constant force in 2D/3D: W=vecFcdotvecs=Fx​sx​+Fy​sy​+Fz​sz​. Only the component of force parallel to displacement contributes. This dot product form is frame-independent.
  • Work-energy connection (preview): The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: W extnet​=DeltaK=\ rac12mvf2​−\ rac12mvi2​. This is the work-energy theorem, derived from Newton's second law.
  • Path dependence for variable forces: For a variable force, work depends on the path taken. For conservative forces (gravity, spring), work is path-independent and equals −DeltaU. For non-conservative forces (friction), work depends on the actual path length.
  • Graphical interpretation: The area under the F-x curve (with sign) gives the work. Positive area = positive work; negative area = negative work. For a spring, the F-x graph is a straight line through origin with slope k, and the area is a triangle \ rac12kx2.
  • Work by a varying force in 2D: W=intC​vecFcdotdvecr=intC​(Fx​dx+Fy​dy). This is a line integral. For a force vecF(x,y), the work along path C depends on the specific curve C unless vecF is conservative ( abla imesvecF=0).
  • Constant force vs variable force summary: Constant force: W=Fscos heta (simple product). Variable force: W=intF(x)dx (area under curve). Both are special cases of the general definition W=intvecFcdotdvecr.
  • Example - work by gravity on an incline: Block slides down incline of angle  heta, length L. Vertical drop h=Lsin heta. Work by gravity: Wg​=mgh=mgLsin heta. This equals force component parallel to incline (mgsin heta) times distance L.
  • Example - work by external agent stretching a spring: Slowly stretch spring from 0 to x (quasi-static, acceleration approx0). Applied force F extapp​=+kx (matches spring force). Work done: W=int0x​kxdx=\ rac12kx2. This energy is STORED as elastic potential energy in the spring.

Confusion

  • Work is NOT force times distance in general - it is force component in the direction of displacement times distance. W=Fs only when force and displacement are parallel (heta=0). Many students forget the cos heta factor.
  • Work done BY a force vs work done ON an object: W extby​=−W exton​. The work-energy theorem uses W extnet​ (work done BY all forces ON the object). Work done BY the object on something else is the negative.
  • Holding a heavy object stationary: You exert a force, but displacement is zero, so W=0! You get tired because your muscles do internal biochemical work (energy conversion), not mechanical work on the object. This is a classic misconception.
  • Centripetal force does NO work: Fc​ is always perpendicular to velocity (displacement), so \\cos 90^\\circ = 0. It changes direction, not speed. A planet in circular orbit around the Sun has zero work done by gravity.
  • For a variable force, you CANNOT just multiply 'average force' by distance unless you know the average is the integral average. The correct method is always integration or graphical area. A common error: averaging endpoints \ racFi​+Ff​2 only works for linear forces.

Universal Facts

  • The concept of work was formalized by Gaspard-Gustave Coriolis in 1829. He defined 'work' as the product of force and distance - the word 'work' in physics has a precise mathematical meaning different from everyday usage.
  • Work is a SCALAR, not a vector. It has no direction, only magnitude and sign. Energy (which work changes) is also a scalar. This is why we can add works from different forces as simple numbers: W extnet​=W1​+W2​+dots
  • The work-energy theorem W extnet​=DeltaK is mathematically equivalent to Newton's second law F=ma. It's not a new law - it's a reformulation that is often easier to use when forces vary or the path is complex.
  • One joule (1 J) is the work done by a force of 1 newton moving an object 1 meter in the direction of the force. It's a small amount: lifting a 100 g apple by 1 m does about 1 J of work against gravity.
  • In thermodynamics, work is energy transfer across a system boundary due to macroscopic forces. The same definition W=intFdx applies, but the 'system' and 'surroundings' must be clearly defined. The sign convention often differs: work done BY the system on surroundings is POSITIVE in thermodynamics (opposite to mechanics convention for the system).

Grammar

Notes
  • Work (noun): energy transfer by force. Uncountable in physics context.
  • Work (verb): to exert force causing displacement.
  • Work done (passive): the work performed by a force.
Important Statements
  • In physics, 'work' has a precise mathematical definition: W=∫F⋅dr.
  • The everyday meaning of 'work' (effort, employment) differs from the physics definition.
