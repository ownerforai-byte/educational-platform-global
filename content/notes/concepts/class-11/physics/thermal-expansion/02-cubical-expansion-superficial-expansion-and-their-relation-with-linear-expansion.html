Notes

  • Linear Expansion: When a solid is heated, its length increases: ΔL = αL₀ΔT where α is coefficient of linear expansion (K⁻¹), L₀ original length, ΔT temperature rise.
  • Superficial (Area) Expansion: Area increases: ΔA = βA₀ΔT where β ≈ 2α is coefficient of superficial expansion. For isotropic solid, β = 2α.
  • Cubical (Volume) Expansion: Volume increases: ΔV = γV₀ΔT where γ ≈ 3α is coefficient of cubical expansion. For isotropic solid, γ = 3α.
  • Relations: α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3 for isotropic solids. β = 2α, γ = 3α.
  • Isotropic vs Anisotropic: Relations β = 2α, γ = 3α hold for isotropic materials. For anisotropic (crystals), expansion differs along different axes.
  • Liquid Expansion: Liquids have only cubical expansion (no fixed shape). Apparent expansion differs from real expansion due to container expansion.
  • Real vs Apparent Expansion of Liquids: γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_container. The container expands too, so apparent rise is less than real expansion.
  • Anomalous Expansion of Water: Water contracts from 0°C to 4°C (maximum density at 4°C), then expands above 4°C. Negative expansion coefficient below 4°C.

Specific Conditions

  • Isotropic Material: γ = 3α and β = 2α only for isotropic solids. Crystalline anisotropic materials have different α along different axes.
  • Small ΔT: Formulas assume constant α over temperature range. For large ΔT, α may vary.
  • Solid Container: For liquid expansion, container expansion must be accounted for to get real expansion.

Formulas

  • Linear: ΔL = αL₀ΔT
  • Superficial: ΔA = βA₀ΔT, β = 2α
  • Cubical: ΔV = γV₀ΔT, γ = 3α
  • Relation: α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3
  • New dimensions: L = L₀(1+αΔT), A = A₀(1+βΔT), V = V₀(1+γΔT)
  • Liquid real expansion: γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_container

Key Points

  • Linear: ΔL = αL₀ΔT; coefficient α (K⁻¹).
  • Superficial: ΔA = βA₀ΔT, β = 2α.
  • Cubical: ΔV = γV₀ΔT, γ = 3α.
  • Relation α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3 for isotropic solids.
  • Relations valid only for isotropic materials.
  • Liquids have only volume expansion; real = apparent + container.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Linear expansion ΔL = αL₀ΔT where α is coefficient of linear expansion.
  • Statement 2: Superficial expansion coefficient β = 2α for isotropic solids.
  • Statement 3: Cubical expansion coefficient γ = 3α for isotropic solids.
  • Statement 4: α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3 for isotropic solids.
  • Statement 5: For liquids, real expansion = apparent expansion + container expansion.

Important Notes

  • γ = 3α can be derived: V = L³ → ΔV/V ≈ 3ΔL/L → γ = 3α (neglecting higher order terms α², α³).
  • α values: steel ~12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, aluminum ~24×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, copper ~17×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, invar ~1.2×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹.
  • Higher order terms (α²ΔT², α³ΔT³) are negligible since α ~ 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁶.
  • Bimetallic strips use different α of two metals.

Exam Short Tricks

  • α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3 — memorize this ratio.
  • β = 2α, γ = 3α for isotropic solids.
  • New dimension = original × (1 + coefficient × ΔT).
  • Liquid real expansion: γ_r = γ_a + γ_c.

Exam Notes

  • NEB frequently asks for derivation of γ = 3α and numericals on linear/volume expansion.
  • Distinguish real vs apparent expansion of liquids.
  • Calculate change in length, area, or volume given coefficient and ΔT.
  • Bimetallic strip and thermal stress applications.

Examples

  • Steel rod L₀ = 2 m, α = 12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, ΔT = 50 K: ΔL = 12×10⁻⁶ × 2 × 50 = 1.2×10⁻³ m = 1.2 mm.
  • Cube side 10 cm, α = 24×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, ΔT = 100 K: ΔV = 3αV₀ΔT = 3×24×10⁻⁶ × 1000 × 100 = 0.72 cm³.
  • Mercury in glass: γ_real(Hg) = 1.8×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, γ_glass = 2.5×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. γ_apparent = 1.55×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
  • Water density maximum at 4°C — lakes freeze from top, protecting aquatic life.

Universal Facts

  • Railway tracks have expansion gaps to prevent buckling in summer.
  • Invar (Fe-Ni alloy) has near-zero α — used in precision instruments, pendulum rods.
  • Concrete and steel have similar α (~12×10⁻⁶) — steel reinforcement bonds well with concrete.
  • Glass cookware (Pyrex) has low α to resist thermal shock.

Confusion

  • Applying γ = 3α to anisotropic crystals: Only valid for isotropic materials.
  • Forgetting container expansion in liquid problems: Real expansion = apparent + container.
  • Confusing coefficient of linear expansion with fractional expansion: α = ΔL/(L₀ΔT).
  • Using Celsius vs Kelvin for ΔT: Only ΔT matters, same in °C or K.
  • Neglecting higher order terms: α²ΔT² is tiny (~10⁻¹⁰) and ignored.
