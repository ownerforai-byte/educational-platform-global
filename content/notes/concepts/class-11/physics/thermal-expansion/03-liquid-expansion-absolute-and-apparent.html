Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Liquids Take the Shape of the Question

    A heated liquid expands — but observed through a container, part of the expansion is swallowed by the expanding glass. Physics separates the apparent expansion (what you see) from the absolute expansion (what really happens): their difference is the container's expansion. And water, uniquely, breaks the rule between 0 °C and 4 °C.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Real (absolute) and apparent expansion

    When a liquid in a vessel of volume V0​ is heated by ΔT:

    • Absolute (real) expansion: the true volume increase of the liquid: ΔVreal​=γr​V0​ΔT, with γ_r the coefficient of absolute (real) cubical expansion.
    • Apparent expansion: the observed increase (liquid level in the vessel): ΔVapp​=γa​V0​ΔT, with γ_a the apparent coefficient.
    • The vessel's cavity expands too: ΔVvessel​=γg​V0​ΔT (γ_g = cubical coefficient of the vessel material).

    γr​=γa​+γg​ — the real expansion is the apparent expansion plus the vessel's expansion.

    2.2 Density change

    ρT​=1+γr​ΔTρ0​​≈ρ0​(1−γr​ΔT)

    2.3 Anomalous expansion of water (NEB 2024)

    Q (3m, 2024): What is the anomalous expansion of water? Explain its importance for aquatic life in cold climates.

    Answer: Between 0 °C and 4 °C, water contracts on heating (and expands on cooling) — the opposite of normal behaviour. Water has maximum density at 4 °C (999.97 kg/m³); below 4 °C it expands, so ice floats and 0 °C water sits above 4 °C water.

    Importance for aquatic life: in winter, a lake cools from the top; when surface water reaches 4 °C it sinks (densest), circulating until the whole lake is at 4 °C. Further cooling makes the surface less dense, so it stays on top and finally freezes. The ice layer insulates the water below, which remains at ~4 °C — fish and other aquatic life survive the winter in this denser, unfrozen bottom layer.

    2.4 Worked numericals

    Q (NEB-style): The apparent expansion of a liquid in a glass vessel is 0.9×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. If γ_glass = 2.5×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, find the real coefficient. γr​=γa​+γg​=9×10−5+2.5×10−5=1.15×10−4 K⁻¹.

    Q (NEB-style): A density bottle holds 100 g of liquid at 0 °C and 96 g at 100 °C (bottle calibrated). Find γ_r of the liquid. Mass expelled = 4 g from 100 g → ΔV/V = 4/96 = 0.0417 over 100 °C → γ_r = 4.17×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 47: γ_r and γ_a are cubical coefficients; the vessel's γ_g = 3α_glass.
    • To Topic 49: Dulong–Petit measures γ_r using the apparent expansion in two different vessels.
    • To density bottle / hydrometer: liquid expansion instruments.

Confusion

Summary

A heated liquid's true growth is its absolute (real) expansion ΔVr​=γr​V0​ΔT, but a vessel observer sees only the apparent expansion ΔVa​=γa​V0​ΔT, because the vessel's cavity expands too: γr​=γa​+γg​ (γ_g = 3α of the vessel material). Density falls as ρT​=ρ0​/(1+γr​ΔT). Water is anomalous: between 0 °C and 4 °C it contracts on heating, reaching maximum density at 4 °C — so winter lakes stratify with 4 °C water at the bottom, ice forms and floats on top as an insulating blanket, and aquatic life survives (NEB 2024, 3m).
