Notes

  • Linear Expansion: This topic covers the fundamental concepts of linear expansion in the physics curriculum.
  • Understanding linear expansion is essential for solving problems and building higher-level mathematical skills.
  • Key definitions and theorems related to linear expansion should be memorized and practiced regularly.
  • Linear expansion definition: When a solid is heated, its length increases. The fractional increase in length per unit rise in temperature is called the coefficient of linear expansion, α.
  • Formula: ΔL=L0​αΔT, where L0​ is the original length, α is the coefficient of linear expansion (unit: K⁻¹ or °C⁻¹), and ΔT is the temperature change.
  • New length: L=L0​(1+αΔT). For small αΔT, this is an excellent approximation. For large temperature ranges, α may vary and must be treated as a function of temperature.
  • Physical origin: Heating increases the amplitude of atomic vibrations in the lattice. Because the interatomic potential is asymmetric (steeper on the compression side), the average separation increases with temperature, causing expansion.
  • Coefficient of linear expansion for common materials: Steel: α≈12×10−6 K⁻¹. Aluminum: α≈24×10−6 K⁻¹. Copper: α≈17×10−6 K⁻¹. Invar (Fe-Ni alloy): α≈1.2×10−6 K⁻¹. Quartz: α≈0.5×10−6 K⁻¹.
  • Why metals expand more: Metals with weaker interatomic bonds (larger bond length, lower melting point) tend to have larger α. Lead (α≈29×10−6) expands more than steel because its atomic bonds are weaker.
  • Bimetallic strip: Two metals with different α bonded together bend when heated — the higher-α metal expands more, forcing the strip to curve. Used in thermostats, circuit breakers, and temperature indicators.
  • Railway tracks — expansion gaps: Steel rails expand by ΔL=L0​αΔT. For a 12 m rail and ΔT=40°C: ΔL=12×12×10−6×40=5.76 mm. Gaps are left between rails to accommodate this expansion, otherwise buckling occurs.
  • Measuring α — Searle's apparatus: A metal rod is heated uniformly. The expansion is measured by a micrometer screw gauge. α=L0​ΔTΔL​. The apparatus minimizes errors from thermal expansion of the frame.
  • Measuring α — Hoke's apparatus: Uses a glass tube filled with liquid. As the solid rod inside expands, it displaces liquid. The volume of displaced liquid gives ΔL indirectly. This is an elegant optical-mechanical method.
  • NEB exam focus: State the formula ΔL=L0​αΔT. Explain the bimetallic strip principle. Describe Searle's apparatus for measuring α. Solve numerical problems involving expansion of rods, bridges, and railways.
  • Effect on precision instruments: Clock pendulums expand with temperature, increasing their period and causing the clock to run slow. T=2πL/g​, so ΔT/T=21​αΔT. For steel (α=12×10−6), a 10°C rise causes the clock to lose about 5 seconds per day.
  • Composite rod problem: Two rods of different materials joined end-to-end: total expansion ΔL=L01​α1​ΔT+L02​α2​ΔT. The junction shifts by the expansion of the first rod.
  • Thermal stress: If a rod is constrained from expanding, thermal stress develops: σ=YαΔT, where Y is Young's modulus. For steel (Y=200 GPa, α=12×10−6), a 50°C constraint gives σ=120 MPa — significant structural stress.
  • Practical consequence: Bridges have expansion joints. Glass bottles crack when hot water is poured in because the inner surface expands faster than the outer. Telephone wires sag more in summer than in winter.

Confusion

  • Carefully distinguish between similar-looking concepts in linear expansion.
  • Pay attention to the conditions under which formulas and theorems apply.
  • Practice with multiple examples to solidify your understanding.
  • ❌ ΔL=L0​αΔT works for any temperature range. ✅ It is valid only for moderate ΔT where α is approximately constant. For very large temperature ranges, α varies and the formula becomes approximate.
  • ❌ All solids expand at the same rate. ✅ Different materials have very different α values. Invar (α≈1.2×10−6) expands 24 times less than aluminum (α≈24×10−6).
  • ❌ The coefficient α has units of temperature. ✅ α has units of K⁻¹ (or °C⁻¹). It is a fractional change per degree, not an absolute change.
  • ❌ A hole in a metal plate shrinks when heated. ✅ The hole EXPANDS — the material around it expands outward, making the hole larger. This is equivalent to a disk of the same material expanding.
  • ❌ Linear expansion applies to liquids. ✅ Liquids do not have a fixed shape, so linear expansion is meaningless for them. Only cubical (volume) expansion is defined for liquids.
  • ❌ The expansion gap for railway tracks should be as large as possible. ✅ The gap must be just large enough to prevent buckling. Too large a gap causes excessive noise and wear at the joints.
  • ❌ Thermal stress depends only on α and ΔT. ✅ Thermal stress is σ=YαΔT — it also depends on Young's modulus Y. A stiffer material develops more stress for the same constraint.

Practice

  • Solve at least 5 problems on linear expansion from your textbook.
  • Try to derive the key formulas from first principles.

  • A steel rail 10 m long at 20°C is heated to 60°C. Find the increase in length. αsteel​=12×10−6 K⁻¹. ΔL=10×12×10−6×40=4.8×10−3 m = 4.8 mm.
  • An aluminum rod is 2.000 m long at 20°C. What is its length at 100°C? αAl​=24×10−6 K⁻¹. L=2.000(1+24×10−6×80)=2.000(1+0.00192)=2.00384 m.
  • A brass pendulum clock keeps correct time at 20°C. How much does it lose per day at 40°C? αbrass​=19×10−6 K⁻¹. Fractional period change: ΔT/T=21​αΔT=21​×19×10−6×20=1.9×10−4. Loss per day: 1.9×10−4×86400=16.4 seconds.
  • A steel rod of length 1 m is rigidly fixed at both ends. If temperature rises by 50°C, find the thermal stress. σ=YαΔT=200×109×12×10−6×50=120×106 Pa = 120 MPa.
  • A bimetallic strip is made of brass (α=19×10−6) and iron (α=12×10−6). When heated, which metal is on the outer side of the curve? Brass expands more, so it is on the OUTER side of the curve (the convex side). The strip curves with brass on the outside.
  • A metal rod expands by 0.12% when heated from 0°C to 100°C. Find α. L0​ΔL​=0.0012=α×100. α=12×10−6 K⁻¹. This is typical of steel.

Universal Facts

  • This topic is a cornerstone of the physics curriculum and appears consistently in examinations.
  • Understanding linear expansion builds the foundation for more advanced topics in mathematics.
  • Real-world applications of linear expansion span engineering, physics, economics, and computer science.
  • The concept of thermal expansion was first quantified by René Antoine Ferchault de Réaumur in 1730, who showed that different materials expand by different amounts for the same temperature change.
  • The coefficient of linear expansion is related to the material's melting point — materials with higher melting points generally have smaller α because their atomic bonds are stronger.
  • Invar (Fe-36%Ni) was invented by Charles Édouard Guillaume in 1896 specifically for its near-zero expansion. He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1920 for this discovery.
  • Thermal expansion is exploited in "shrink fitting" — a metal ring is heated, placed over a shaft, and as it cools it contracts to form an extremely tight interference fit. This is used in bearing installation.
  • The Earth's crust experiences thermal expansion and contraction daily, contributing to weathering and rock fracturing. This is one of the most important physical weathering processes in geology.
