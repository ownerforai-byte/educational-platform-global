Notes

  • Superficial (area) expansion: When a solid sheet is heated, its area increases. ΔA=A0​βΔT, where β is the coefficient of superficial expansion.
  • Cubical (volume) expansion: When a solid is heated, its volume increases. ΔV=V0​γΔT, where γ is the coefficient of cubical expansion.
  • Relation for isotropic solids: α:β:γ=1:2:3. That is, β=2α and γ=3α. This holds for isotropic materials (same properties in all directions).
  • Derivation of β=2α: For a square of side L: A=L2=L02​(1+αΔT)2≈L02​(1+2αΔT) for small αΔT. Therefore β=2α.
  • Derivation of γ=3α: For a cube of side L: V=L3=L03​(1+αΔT)3≈L03​(1+3αΔT) for small αΔT. Therefore γ=3α.
  • Why the ratios hold: Each dimension (length, breadth, height) expands independently by the factor (1+αΔT). Area involves two dimensions → factor (1+αΔT)2≈1+2αΔT. Volume involves three → 1+3αΔT.
  • Anisotropic crystals: In crystals like graphite or calcite, expansion differs along different axes. The relation γ=α1​+α2​+α3​ still holds, where αi​ are the linear coefficients along the three principal axes.
  • Holes and cavities: A hole in a metal plate expands AS IF it were filled with the same material. The cavity behaves like a solid piece — its volume increases with γ. This is because the material around the hole expands outward.
  • Liquids have no α or β: Liquids cannot sustain shear stress, so they have no fixed shape. Only cubical (volume) expansion is defined for liquids. γliquid​ is typically much larger than γsolid​.
  • Glass vessels and liquids: When a liquid in a glass vessel is heated, BOTH expand. The liquid level may first fall (glass expands first) then rise (liquid expands more). The apparent expansion measures only the net effect.
  • NEB exam focus: Derive β=2α and γ=3α. Solve problems involving expansion of area and volume. Distinguish between absolute and apparent expansion of liquids.
  • Numerical example — area expansion: An aluminum plate has area 1.000 m² at 20°C. At 100°C: ΔA=1.000×2×24×10−6×80=3.84×10−3 m². New area = 1.00384 m².
  • Numerical example — volume expansion: A glass flask of 1000 cm³ is filled with mercury at 20°C. Both are heated to 100°C. γglass​=3αglass​=3×9×10−6=27×10−6. γHg​=182×10−6. Overflow = 1000×(182−27)×10−6×80=12.4 cm³.
  • Practical applications: (1) Mercury thermometers rely on the large γ of mercury vs. glass. (2) Expansion joints in pipelines accommodate volume changes. (3) Tight metal lids loosen when heated (the lid expands more than the jar).
  • Dimensional analysis: [α]=[β]=[γ]=[Θ−1]=[K−1]. All three coefficients have the same dimensions — they differ only in magnitude by factors of 2 and 3 for isotropic solids.

Confusion

  • ❌ The relation γ=3α holds for all materials including liquids. ✅ It holds only for ISOTROPIC SOLIDS. Liquids only have γ — they have no α or β because they have no fixed shape.
  • ❌ A hole in a metal plate gets smaller when heated. ✅ The hole EXPANDS. The material around it moves outward equally in all directions, so the hole gets larger.
  • ❌ α, β, and γ have different units. ✅ All three have the same units: K⁻¹ or °C⁻¹. They differ only in numerical value for isotropic solids (β=2α, γ=3α).
  • ❌ The expansion ratios β=2α and γ=3α are exact for all temperature ranges. ✅ They are approximations valid when αΔT≪1. For large ΔT, higher-order terms become significant.
  • ❌ Anisotropic crystals have γ=3α. ✅ For anisotropic crystals, γ=α1​+α2​+α3​, where the three αi​ may be different along different crystal axes.
  • ❌ Only solids expand when heated — liquids and gases do not. ✅ Liquids and gases expand significantly more than solids. Mercury in a thermometer rises because liquid expansion is much larger than glass expansion.
  • ❌ The volume of a cavity inside a solid does not change when heated. ✅ The cavity expands exactly as if it were filled with the solid material. The surrounding material expands outward, enlarging the cavity.

Practice

  • A square copper plate has side 0.500 m at 20°C. Find the increase in area when heated to 100°C. αCu​=17×10−6 K⁻¹. β=2α=34×10−6 K⁻¹. ΔA=0.250×34×10−6×80=6.8×10−4 m² = 6.8 cm².
  • A glass flask has a capacity of 500 cm³ at 15°C. How much mercury must be filled so that the empty space remains constant at all temperatures? γglass​=27×10−6, γHg​=182×10−6. Let V_Hg be the mercury volume. For constant empty space: VHg​γHg​=Vflask​γglass​. VHg​=500×27/182=74.2 cm³.
  • Show that for a sphere, ΔV/V0​=3Δr/r0​. From V=34​πr3: VΔV​=r3Δr​ (for small changes). Therefore γ=3α for a sphere, consistent with the general relation.
  • A steel ball has diameter 5.000 cm at 20°C. What is its volume at 100°C? αsteel​=12×10−6. Δr=2.500×12×10−6×80=2.4×10−3 cm. New radius = 2.5024 cm. V=34​π(2.5024)3=65.68 cm³ (vs. 65.45 at 20°C).
  • An iron ring has inner diameter 5.00 cm at 20°C. What temperature must it be heated to fit over a shaft of 5.02 cm? ΔD=0.02 cm. 0.02=5.00×12×10−6×ΔT. ΔT=333°C. Final temperature = 353°C.
  • The density of a liquid decreases when heated. By what fraction does density change if γ=10−3 K⁻¹ and ΔT=50 K? ρ=m/V. Δρ/ρ=−ΔV/V=−γΔT=−10−3×50=−0.05. Density decreases by 5%.

Universal Facts

  • The relation γ=3α was first derived rigorously by James Clerk Maxwell in his 1871 theory of heat. It follows from the geometry of three-dimensional expansion.
  • The anomalous expansion of water (maximum density at 4°C) means that ice floats and aquatic life survives winter. Without this anomaly, lakes would freeze from the bottom up, devastating ecosystems.
  • In engineering, the coefficient of cubical expansion is critical for designing fuel tanks, pipelines, and pressure vessels. A 10% volume increase in liquid fuel due to temperature can rupture a sealed tank.
  • The expansion ratio 1:2:3 (α:β:γ) is one of the simplest and most useful relationships in solid-state physics, connecting one-dimensional, two-dimensional, and three-dimensional thermal behavior.
  • Gemstones can crack when heated unevenly because they are anisotropic — different crystal axes expand by different amounts. This is why jewelers heat-set stones carefully.
