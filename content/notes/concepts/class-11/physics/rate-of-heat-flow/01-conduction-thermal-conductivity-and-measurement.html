Notes

  • Thermal Conduction: Heat transfer through a material without bulk motion of the material. Energy passes from particle to particle via molecular collisions and free electron movement (in metals).
  • Rate of Heat Flow: For steady-state conduction through a slab of area A, thickness d, thermal conductivity k, temperature difference ΔT: dQ/dt = kAΔT/d = kA(T₁ - T₂)/d. This is Fourier's law of heat conduction.
  • Thermal Conductivity (k): Material property measuring ability to conduct heat. SI unit: W/(m·K). Metals have high k (silver ~429, copper ~401, aluminum ~237). Insulators have low k (wood ~0.1, air ~0.026).
  • Temperature Gradient: dQ/dt = -kA(dT/dx) — heat flows from high to low temperature. Negative sign indicates heat flows in direction of decreasing temperature.
  • Thermal Resistance: R = d/(kA) analogous to electrical resistance. Heat current H = ΔT/R. Series slabs: R_total = ΣRᵢ; same heat current through each.
  • Composite Slabs: For layers in series, interface temperatures found from equal heat current: H = k₁A(T₁-Tᵢ)/d₁ = k₂A(Tᵢ-T₂)/d₂. Temperature drop is larger across poorer conductor.
  • Good vs Bad Conductors: Good conductors (metals) — free electrons transfer energy efficiently. Bad conductors/insulators (wood, air, plastics) — few free electrons, lattice vibrations less effective.
  • Applications: Cooking utensils (copper/aluminum base, wooden handle), insulation (fibreglass, styrofoam), clothing (trapped air), cooking pots (metal bottom for conduction, plastic handle for insulation).

Specific Conditions

  • Steady State: Fourier's law applies in steady state (temperature at each point constant in time). Transient state requires heat equation.
  • One-Dimensional: Heat flow perpendicular to slab faces, no heat loss from sides (insulated).
  • Isotropic Material: k is same in all directions — true for most polycrystalline materials.

Formulas

  • Fourier's law: dQ/dt = kAΔT/d
  • Heat current: H = ΔT/R where R = d/(kA)
  • Series resistance: R_total = Σ dᵢ/(kᵢA)
  • Thermal conductivity unit: W/(m·K)
  • Temperature gradient: dT/dx

Key Points

  • Conduction: heat transfer without material motion.
  • dQ/dt = kAΔT/d — Fourier's law of heat conduction.
  • k is material property; metals high, insulators low.
  • Thermal resistance R = d/(kA); series: R_total = ΣRᵢ.
  • Heat flows from hot to cold.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Rate of heat conduction through a slab: dQ/dt = kAΔT/d.
  • Statement 2: Thermal conductivity k is a material property (W/(m·K)).
  • Statement 3: Thermal resistance R = d/(kA) — analogous to electrical resistance.
  • Statement 4: For slabs in series, total thermal resistance is sum of individual resistances.
  • Statement 5: Metals are good conductors due to free electrons; insulators are poor conductors.

Important Notes

  • Steady state required — temperature at each point doesn't change with time.
  • One-dimensional flow assumed — sides insulated.
  • Thermal resistance concept simplifies composite slab problems.
  • Temperature drop is proportional to thermal resistance (larger drop across poorer conductor).

Exam Short Tricks

  • H = kAΔT/d — remember H = ΔT/R form too.
  • Series slabs: same H through each; total R = R₁ + R₂ + ...
  • Temperature drop ∝ thermal resistance.
  • k unit: W/(m·K); good conductors k ≈ 100-400; insulators k < 1.

Exam Notes

  • NEB frequently asks for Fourier's law derivation and numericals on composite slabs.
  • Calculate interface temperature in series slabs.
  • Compare thermal conductivities of materials.
  • Thermal resistance analogy to electrical resistance.

Examples

  • Copper slab A = 0.01 m², d = 0.005 m, ΔT = 100 K, k = 400 W/(m·K): dQ/dt = 400 × 0.01 × 100/0.005 = 80,000 W = 80 kW.
  • Two slabs: k₁ = 100, d₁ = 0.01, k₂ = 10, d₂ = 0.02, A = 0.1 m², ΔT_total = 90 K. R₁ = 0.01/(100×0.1) = 0.001 K/W; R₂ = 0.02/(10×0.1) = 0.02 K/W. H = 90/0.021 = 4286 W.
  • Air gap as insulator: k_air ≈ 0.026 W/(m·K) — double-pane windows use air gap to reduce conduction.

Universal Facts

  • Diamond has highest thermal conductivity (~2000 W/(m·K)) — used in high-power electronics cooling.
  • Air is an excellent insulator (k ≈ 0.026 W/(m·K)) — trapped air in clothing and building insulation provides warmth.
  • Silver is the best metallic conductor (k ≈ 429 W/(m·K)), followed closely by copper (~401).
  • The thermal resistance analogy allows using circuit techniques for heat flow problems.

Confusion

  • Confusing thermal conductivity k with heat capacity: k measures heat transfer rate; heat capacity measures heat needed to raise temperature.
  • Thinking temperature drop is equal across series slabs: ΔTᵢ = H·Rᵢ — larger drop across larger resistance.
  • Believing heat flows from cold to hot: Heat always flows hot → cold spontaneously.
  • Confusing conduction with convection: Conduction needs no material motion; convection involves fluid motion.
  • Forgetting steady-state assumption in Fourier's law.

1.

Rate of heat conduction is proportional to:

Options
  • ΔT/d
  • d/ΔT
  • A·d
  • ΔT·d
Answer

A

Explanation

dQ/dt = kAΔT/d — proportional to area and temperature difference, inversely proportional to thickness.

2.

Thermal conductivity of metals is:

Options
  • High
  • Low
  • Zero
  • Temperature independent
Answer

A

Explanation

Free electrons make metals excellent heat conductors.

3.

Thermal resistance R = d/(kA):

Options
  • Increases with k
  • Decreases with d
  • Increases with d
  • Independent of A
Answer

C

Explanation

R ∝ d — thicker slab = more resistance to heat flow.

4.

For slabs in series, total thermal resistance:

Options
  • 1/R_total = Σ1/Rᵢ
  • R_total = ΣRᵢ
  • R_total = R₁ × R₂
  • R_total = (R₁ + R₂)/2
Answer

B

Explanation

Thermal resistances add in series like electrical resistances.

5.

Heat flows from:

Options
  • Cold to hot
  • Hot to cold
  • Either direction
  • No flow at same temperature
Answer

B

Explanation

Second law — heat flows from higher to lower temperature.
