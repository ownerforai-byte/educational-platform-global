Radiation and Ideal Radiator

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Heat That Crosses Empty Space

    The Sun's energy crosses 150 million kilometres of vacuum to warm your face. That is radiation: heat carried by electromagnetic waves, needing no medium, travelling at the speed of light, and obeying surface rules — black surfaces absorb and emit best, shiny ones reflect.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts (NEB 2024)

    2.1 Nature of thermal radiation

    • Electromagnetic waves (mainly infrared for everyday temperatures) emitted by all bodies above 0 K.
    • Needs no material medium — travels through vacuum at c=3×108 m/s.
    • Travels in straight lines; can be reflected and refracted like light.
    • On hitting a surface: part reflected (r), part absorbed (a), part transmitted (t): r+a+t=1.

    2.2 Absorbing power, reflecting power and the ideal radiator

    • Absorbing power (a): fraction of incident radiation absorbed.
    • Reflecting power (r): fraction reflected.
    • A perfectly black body: absorbs all incident radiation (a=1, r=t=0). Kirchhoff's law: good absorbers are good emitters — so a perfect black body is also the ideal radiator.

    Everyday consequences (NEB 2024, 2m):

    • Cooking utensils are blackened at the bottom — to maximise absorption of heat from the flame (high a) and maximise emission to the food.
    • White clothes in summer — white reflects most incident radiation (high r, low a), keeping the wearer cooler; dark clothes absorb and warm.

    2.3 Emissive power

    Emissive power (E): energy radiated per unit area per unit time by a surface (W/m²). For a black body it depends only on temperature (→ Stefan–Boltzmann law); for real bodies E=e×Eblack​, where e = emissivity (0 < e ≤ 1; e = a by Kirchhoff).

    2.4 Worked reasoning

    Q (2m, 2024): Why are cooking utensils blackened at the bottom, and why do people prefer white clothes in summer? Blackened bottoms have high absorbing power — they absorb the flame's radiation efficiently (and emit well to the contents). White clothes have high reflecting power — they reflect most of the Sun's radiation, minimising absorption, so the wearer stays cool.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 59: the black body is the theoretical anchor of radiation physics.
    • To Topic 60: Stefan's law quantifies the ideal radiator's output.
    • To Prevost's theory: all bodies radiate continuously; net exchange decides heating or cooling.
    • To greenhouse effect: atmosphere transmits visible light but absorbs IR — radiative trapping.

Summary

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic energy (mainly infrared) emitted by every body above absolute zero — it needs no medium, travels at the speed of light, and splits at a surface into reflected, absorbed and transmitted parts (r+a+t=1). A perfectly black body absorbs everything (a=1) and, by Kirchhoff's law, is also the ideal radiator: good absorbers are good emitters. Hence blackened utensil bottoms absorb flame radiation efficiently, and white summer clothes reflect it (NEB 2024, 2m). Real bodies radiate E=eσT4 with emissivity e equal to their absorbing power — the physics behind thermos flasks, radiators and the greenhouse effect.
