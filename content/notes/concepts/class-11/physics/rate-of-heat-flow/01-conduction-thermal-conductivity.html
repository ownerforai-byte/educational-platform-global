Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Heat Through the Solid

    Hold a silver spoon in tea and the handle soon burns; hold a wooden spoon and nothing happens. Both conduct — but at rates differing by thousands of times. Thermal conductivity measures that difference, and Fourier's law turns it into an engineering quantity for walls, pipes and heat sinks.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts (NEB 2024)

    2.1 The three modes of heat transfer (NEB 2024)

    ModeMechanismMediumExample
    ConductionMolecular collisions / free-electron transport; matter does not moveSolids (chiefly), fluidsMetal spoon in hot tea
    ConvectionBulk motion of the heated fluidFluids onlyBoiling water circulation, sea breeze
    RadiationElectromagnetic waves; no medium neededNone (vacuum works)Sun's heat reaching Earth

    2.2 Fourier's law and thermal conductivity (NEB 2024)

    Statement: the rate of heat flow through a conductor is proportional to the cross-sectional area and the temperature gradient, and inversely proportional to... formally: dtdQ​=−kAdxdT​⇒tQ​=LkA(θ1​−θ2​)​ (for a uniform rod)

    • dQ/dt = rate of heat flow (W); A = cross-sectional area (m²); dT/dx = temperature gradient (K/m); k = thermal conductivity (W m⁻¹K⁻¹) — the heat flowing per second through 1 m² of cross-section per kelvin of gradient over 1 m.
    • The minus sign: heat flows down the temperature gradient.
    • Good conductors (metals): k = 10²–10³ (silver 406, copper 385 W/m·K — free electrons). Insulators: k ~ 0.1 (wood 0.12, air 0.024, wool trapped air).

    2.3 Measurement of k (Forbe's / Lee's disc method idea)

    For a good conductor: a bar heated at one end (steam chest), temperature gradients measured by thermometers/thermocouples at holes along the bar; the heat input is measured by the cooling of a steam jacket or by electrical power. For bad conductors: Lee's disc method — a thin disc of the material sandwiched between a steam-heated plate and a cooling disc; the heat flow rate is obtained from the cooling curve of the bottom disc at the steady state.

    2.4 Worked numerical (NEB 2024)

    Q (3m, 2024): A metal rod 0.5 m long with cross-section 4 cm² has its ends at 100 °C and 0 °C. If k = 400 W/m·K, find the rate of heat flow through the rod. tQ​=LkAΔθ​=0.5400×4×10−4×100​=32 W

    Q (NEB-style): A window glass pane 2 m², 4 mm thick, k = 0.8 W/m·K spans 20 °C inside vs 5 °C outside. Find the heat loss per hour. Q/t=0.8×2×15/0.004=6000 W → per hour: 2.16×10⁷ J.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Ohm's law analogy: thermal resistance L/kA mirrors electrical resistance; heat current mirrors electric current — series/parallel walls combine like resistors.
    • To Topic 57/58: conduction, convection and radiation partition the same heat flow.
    • To Topic 51: k and c combine in the diffusivity k/ρc that sets how fast temperature changes propagate.

    4.2 Common examination mistakes

    1. Forgetting to convert cm² → m² (4 cm² = 4×10⁻⁴ m²).
    2. Writing k's unit as W/m (it is W m⁻¹K⁻¹).
    3. Dropping the minus sign's meaning in Fourier's law.

Confusion

  • Conduction needs no bulk flow — energy passes by collisions and free electrons while the solid stays put.
  • Thermal resistance L/kA — thick, narrow, low-k walls resist heat like long thin wires resist current.

Practice

  • Why is a quilt warm even though it is mostly air?

Universal Facts

  • Still air (k = 0.024) is one of the best insulators — the principle behind double glazing and woollen clothing.

Summary

Heat travels by conduction (molecular collisions and free electrons, no bulk motion), convection (bulk fluid motion) and radiation (EM waves, no medium) (NEB 2024). Fourier's law quantifies conduction: dtdQ​=−kAdxdT​, or for a uniform rod tQ​=LkA(θ1​−θ2​)​, where the thermal conductivity k (W m⁻¹K⁻¹) is the heat per second through unit area per unit gradient — 385 for copper (free electrons) against 0.024 for still air. The analogy L/kA = thermal resistance solves composite walls like resistor networks. NEB 2024's rod (400 W/m·K, 4 cm², 0.5 m, 100 K) carries 32 W.
