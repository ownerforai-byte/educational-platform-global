Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Colour of Heat

    Heat a horseshoe and it glows dull red, then orange, then white. That colour sequence is black-body radiation — the universal spectrum that every hot object approaches, the spectrum that forced Planck to invent the quantum and so started modern physics.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 What is a black body?

    A perfect black body absorbs all incident radiation (a=1) regardless of wavelength or direction. By Kirchhoff's law it is also the best possible emitter at every wavelength. No perfect black body exists, but a cavity with a small hole approximates one: radiation entering the hole bounces internally until absorbed; the hole's emission is pure cavity radiation.

    2.2 Energy distribution (Wien and Rayleigh-Jeans limits)

    Plotting emissive power vs wavelength for a black body at temperature T:

    • The spectrum is continuous, rising from near-zero at short wavelengths, peaking, and falling at long wavelengths.
    • Wien's displacement law: the peak wavelength shifts inversely with temperature:

    λmax​T=b=2.9×10−3 m⋅K Hotter bodies peak at shorter wavelengths — hence red-hot → white-hot as T rises. (Sun: T ≈ 5800 K → λ_max ≈ 500 nm, visible green.)

    • Total area under the curve grows as T⁴ (Stefan–Boltzmann).
    • Classical (Rayleigh–Jeans) theory predicted infinite ultraviolet output — the "ultraviolet catastrophe"; Planck's quantum hypothesis (E = hν) fixed it, giving the correct spectrum and birthing quantum theory.

    2.3 Practical black bodies

    • Cavity radiator (hole in a hollow enclosure).
    • Lamp-black / soot-coated surfaces (a ≈ 0.96).
    • The Sun, stars and the cosmic microwave background (2.7 K) are near-perfect natural black bodies.

    2.4 Worked numerical

    Q (NEB-style): A black body peaks at 580 nm. Estimate its temperature. T=λmax​b​=580×10−92.9×10−3​≈5000 K

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 58: the black body is the ideal radiator defined there.
    • To Topic 60: Stefan's law is the integral of the black-body spectrum.
    • To modern physics: Planck's fix of the ultraviolet catastrophe launched quantum mechanics (1900).
    • To cosmology: the CMB at 2.7 K is the most perfect black body known.

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying a black body is necessarily black in colour — at high T it glows white.
    2. Mixing up Wien's law direction (hotter → shorter λ).
    3. Omitting the cavity-with-hole construction.

Summary

A perfect black body absorbs all incident radiation and, by Kirchhoff's law, emits the maximum possible at every wavelength; it is realised practically as a cavity with a tiny hole. Its spectrum is continuous, peaking at λmax​=2.9×10−3/T (Wien's displacement law) — hotter bodies glow at shorter wavelengths, from dull red to white-hot, and the Sun's 5800 K peak sits in the visible. The total emitted power grows as T⁴ (Stefan–Boltzmann). Classical physics predicted an infinite ultraviolet output — the ultraviolet catastrophe — until Planck's quantum hypothesis (1900) produced the correct spectrum and opened quantum physics. The cosmic microwave background at 2.7 K is nature's most perfect black body.

A black body is best realised as:

Options
  • A black-painted ball
  • A cavity with a small hole
  • A mirror
  • A lamp filament
Answer

B

Explanation

Multiple internal reflections absorb all entry radiation.

2.

Wien's law states:

Options
  • λ_max ∝ T
  • λ_max T = constant
  • λ_max ∝ T²
  • λ_max is fixed
Answer

B

Explanation

Hotter → shorter peak wavelength.

A body peaking at 580 nm has T ≈

Options
  • 2900 K
  • 5000 K
  • 5800 K
  • 10000 K
Answer

B

Explanation

2.9×10⁻³/580×10⁻⁹.

4.

As temperature rises, a black body's spectrum:

Options
  • Shrinks
  • Peaks at shorter λ and grows in area
  • Stays same
  • Becomes line spectrum
Answer

B

Explanation

Both Wien and Stefan effects.
