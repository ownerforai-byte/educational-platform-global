Notes

  • Stress and strain. Stress is the restoring force per unit area (F/A) and strain is the fractional deformation (ΔL/L); within the elastic limit, stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law).
  • Young's modulus. Y = longitudinal stress ÷ longitudinal strain = FL/AΔL; it measures a material's resistance to stretching or compression along its length.
  • Bulk modulus. K = −P ÷ (ΔV/V); the negative sign shows volume decreases under pressure, and its reciprocal is the compressibility of the material.
  • Shear modulus. G = shear stress ÷ shear strain = (F/A) ÷ (Δx/L); it measures resistance to change of shape at constant volume.
  • Poisson's ratio. σ = −(lateral strain ÷ longitudinal strain); for most stable materials it lies between 0 and 0.5, and elastic potential energy per unit volume is ½ stress × strain.
  • Young modulus is tensile stress over tensile strain and describes a wire. Bulk modulus describes a volume squeezed from all sides, and the minus sign makes B positive because the volume falls as the pressure rises. Shear modulus describes a shape change with no volume change.

Confusion

  • ❌ skip the key fact → ✅ stress = f/a, strain = δl/l (dimensionless). hooke's law (elastic limit): stress ∝ strain..
  • ❌ 'All three moduli have different units.' ✅ All three are measured in N/m² (Pa) — same units, different deformation types.
  • ❌ 'Bulk modulus applies only to liquids.' ✅ It applies to solids and gases too — any material under uniform pressure.
  • ❌ 'Shear strain is a length ratio.' ✅ Shear strain is the ANGLE of displacement θ (radians), not a length ratio.
  • ❌ 'Young's modulus and stiffness k are the same.' ✅ Y and k connect through geometry: E = Fl/(AΔl) while k = F/Δl, so E = kL/A.
  • ❌ 'Liquids have Young's modulus.' ✅ Liquids flow; they have NO shear modulus and no Young's modulus for stretching — only bulk modulus.
  • ❌ 'B increases when you compress a solid.' ✅ B is a constant MATERIAL property within the elastic range; it does not change with the applied pressure.

Universal Facts

  • Stress = F/A, strain = ΔL/L (dimensionless). Hooke's law (elastic limit): stress ∝ strain.
  • Elastic moduli: Young's Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L); Bulk K = −P/(ΔV/V); Shear G = (F/A)/(Δx/L).
  • Elastic potential energy U = ½FΔL = ½kx². Poisson's ratio σ = −(lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain) ≈ 0.25–0.35 for most metals.
  • Thomas Young established the concept of the modulus of elasticity in 1807 — his modulus E is one of the most fundamental material constants.
  • Rigidity (shear) modulus explains why liquids flow: their G = 0 — they cannot resist shear.
  • Diamond has the largest E of all materials (≈ 1.2×10¹² N/m²), which is why it is used for cutting tools.
  • Rubber has a low E but a very high elastic limit — it can stretch hundreds of percent before breaking.
  • Ultrasound techniques measure tissue Young's modulus for medical diagnosis (elastography).
  • Anisotropic Materials: In crystals and composites, elastic moduli depend on direction. Wood is much stronger along the grain than across it. This is described by the stiffness tensor with up to 21 independent constants for the most general anisotropic material.

Examples

  • Young: A 1 mm² wire, 2 m long, carrying 200 N extends 2 mm → E = (200/10⁻⁶)/(2×10⁻³/2) = 2×10¹¹ N/m².
  • Bulk: 3×10⁶ Pa pressure shrinks 1 m³ of steel by 1.9×10⁻⁵ m³ → B = 1.6×10¹¹ Pa.
  • Shear: A 2×10⁴ N force shears a 10⁻³ m² face by 2.5×10⁻³ rad → G = (2×10⁴/10⁻³)/2.5×10⁻³ = 8×10⁹ N/m².
  • Relation check: E = 2G(1+σ) for steel: 2×10¹¹ = 2×8×10¹⁰(1+σ) → σ = 0.25.
  • Compressibility: water k = 1/B = 1/2.2×10⁹ = 4.5×10⁻¹⁰ Pa⁻¹.

Formulas

  • Y = FL/(A ΔL)
  • K = −P/(ΔV/V)
  • U = ½kx²
  • Young's modulus: E = (F/A)/(Δl/l) = Fl/(AΔl)
  • Bulk modulus: B = −P/(ΔV/V)
  • Shear modulus: G = (F/A)/θ = τ/θ
  • Relation: E = 2G(1 + σ) = 3B(1 − 2σ)
  • Combined: E = 9BG/(3B + G); compressibility k = 1/B

Numericals

  • A wire of area 1.0 × 10^−6 m^2 and length 2.0 m stretches by 1.0 mm under 200 N. Find Young modulus.

Key Points

  • E, B, G all have units N/m² (Pa) — they differ by the type of deformation they describe.
  • E (stretching), B (volume), G (shape) measure the corresponding resistance of a material.
  • The three moduli are linked: E = 2G(1+σ) and E = 3B(1−2σ).
  • Liquids and gases have G = 0 (they cannot resist shear) — only solids have a shear modulus.
  • Compressibility is the reciprocal of B.

Summary

The three elastic moduli — Young's (E), bulk (B), and shear (G) — quantify how materials resist stretching, uniform compression, and shape change respectively. Each is stress/strain with units N/m². They are related by E = 2G(1+σ) = 3B(1−2σ). Solids possess all three; fluids have only bulk modulus.

  • unit memory: all three moduli are in N/m² (Pa).
  • For Young's modulus: rearrange E = Fl/(AΔl) to solve any one variable.
  • For bulk modulus: B = −P/(ΔV/V) — use the minus sign only once (final positive).
  • If you know E and G, find σ = E/(2G) − 1.

Notes
  • Young's modulus (E): the ratio of longitudinal stress to longitudinal strain: E = (F/A)/(Δl/l) = Fl/(AΔl). It measures resistance to stretching/compression. For steel, E ≈ 2×10¹¹ N/m²; for aluminium ≈ 7×10¹⁰ N/m²; for rubber ≈ 5×10⁶ N/m².
  • Bulk modulus (B): the ratio of volume stress (pressure) to volume strain: B = −P/(ΔV/V). It measures resistance to uniform compression. The minus sign keeps B positive (ΔV is negative under pressure). For water B ≈ 2.2×10⁹ Pa; steel B ≈ 1.6×10¹¹ Pa.
  • Shear modulus / modulus of rigidity (G or η): the ratio of shear stress to shear strain: G = τ/θ = (F/A)/θ. It measures resistance to change of shape without change of volume. For steel G ≈ 8×10¹⁰ N/m².
  • Key relation between moduli (for isotropic materials): E = 2G(1 + σ) and E = 3B(1 − 2σ), where σ is Poisson's ratio. Combining them: 9/B = 3/G + 1/E or E = 9BG/(3B + G). These links let you compute one modulus from the others.
  • Worked example — Young's modulus: a wire of length 2 m and area 1 mm² extends 1 mm under a 50 kg load. F = 490 N, σ = 490/10⁻⁶ = 4.9×10⁸ Pa, ε = 10⁻³/2 = 5×10⁻⁴, E = 4.9×10⁸/5×10⁻⁴ = 9.8×10¹¹ ≈ 10¹¹ N/m².
  • Worked example — bulk modulus: 10⁵ Pa pressure on 1 m³ of water changes its volume by 4.5×10⁻⁵ m³. B = 10⁵/(4.5×10⁻⁵) = 2.2×10⁹ Pa.
  • Compressibility: the reciprocal of bulk modulus: k = 1/B. Fluids with high B are hard to compress; gases have very low B.
  • Which modulus to use? stretching a wire → Young's modulus; squeezing a sphere → bulk modulus; twisting/sliding layers → shear modulus. Choose by the type of deformation.

Universal Facts
  • Thomas Young established the concept of the modulus of elasticity in 1807 — his modulus E is one of the most fundamental material constants.
  • Rigidity (shear) modulus explains why liquids flow: their G = 0 — they cannot resist shear.
  • Diamond has the largest E of all materials (≈ 1.2×10¹² N/m²), which is why it is used for cutting tools.
  • Rubber has a low E but a very high elastic limit — it can stretch hundreds of percent before breaking.
  • Ultrasound techniques measure tissue Young's modulus for medical diagnosis (elastography).
  • Anisotropic Materials: In crystals and composites, elastic moduli depend on direction. Wood is much stronger along the grain than across it. This is described by the stiffness tensor with up to 21 independent constants for the most general anisotropic material.
Examples
  • Young: A 1 mm² wire, 2 m long, carrying 200 N extends 2 mm → E = (200/10⁻⁶)/(2×10⁻³/2) = 2×10¹¹ N/m².
  • Bulk: 3×10⁶ Pa pressure shrinks 1 m³ of steel by 1.9×10⁻⁵ m³ → B = 1.6×10¹¹ Pa.
  • Shear: A 2×10⁴ N force shears a 10⁻³ m² face by 2.5×10⁻³ rad → G = (2×10⁴/10⁻³)/2.5×10⁻³ = 8×10⁹ N/m².
  • Relation check: E = 2G(1+σ) for steel: 2×10¹¹ = 2×8×10¹⁰(1+σ) → σ = 0.25.
  • Compressibility: water k = 1/B = 1/2.2×10⁹ = 4.5×10⁻¹⁰ Pa⁻¹.

Formulas
  • Young's modulus: E = (F/A)/(Δl/l) = Fl/(AΔl)
  • Bulk modulus: B = −P/(ΔV/V)
  • Shear modulus: G = (F/A)/θ = τ/θ
  • Relation: E = 2G(1 + σ) = 3B(1 − 2σ)
  • Combined: E = 9BG/(3B + G); compressibility k = 1/B
Key Points
  • E, B, G all have units N/m² (Pa) — they differ by the type of deformation they describe.
  • E (stretching), B (volume), G (shape) measure the corresponding resistance of a material.
  • The three moduli are linked: E = 2G(1+σ) and E = 3B(1−2σ).
  • Liquids and gases have G = 0 (they cannot resist shear) — only solids have a shear modulus.
  • Compressibility is the reciprocal of B.
Summary

The three elastic moduli — Young's (E), bulk (B), and shear (G) — quantify how materials resist stretching, uniform compression, and shape change respectively. Each is stress/strain with units N/m². They are related by E = 2G(1+σ) = 3B(1−2σ). Solids possess all three; fluids have only bulk modulus.

Special Notes
  • Choose the modulus by the type of deformation: stretch → E, squeeze → B, twist → G.
  • The sign in B = −P/(ΔV/V) makes the modulus positive since ΔV is negative under compression.
  • Convert areas to m² before computing stress (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m²).
  • The relation E = 2G(1+σ) lets you compute Poisson's ratio from E and G.
  • NEB commonly asks to compute one modulus from two others using the linking relations.
Important Statements
  • Young's modulus E = Fl/(AΔl) measures resistance to longitudinal deformation.
  • Bulk modulus B = −P/(ΔV/V) measures resistance to uniform compression.
  • Shear modulus G = τ/θ measures resistance to change of shape.
  • All three moduli have the units N/m² and are related by E = 2G(1+σ) = 3B(1−2σ).
  • Fluids have G = 0 and no Young's modulus — only bulk modulus.
Important Notes
  • Each modulus = stress/strain: E (longitudinal), B (volume), G (shear) — pick by the deformation type.
  • E, B and G are linked by Poisson's ratio σ: E = 2G(1+σ) = 3B(1−2σ).
  • Materials with low E (rubber) stretch easily; high E (diamond) resists deformation.
  • Fluids have no shear modulus — they flow in response to shear stress.
  • Compressibility k = 1/B is used to gauge how easily a substance is squeezed.
Exam Short Tricks
  • unit memory: all three moduli are in N/m² (Pa).
  • For Young's modulus: rearrange E = Fl/(AΔl) to solve any one variable.
  • For bulk modulus: B = −P/(ΔV/V) — use the minus sign only once (final positive).
  • If you know E and G, find σ = E/(2G) − 1.
  • Convert mm² → 10⁻⁶ m² and cm² → 10⁻⁴ m² before stress calculations.

Specific Conditions
  • Material State Determines Available Moduli: Solids have all three moduli (E,B,G). Fluids (liquids/gases) have only bulk modulus B — they cannot sustain shear stress (G=0) and have no defined Young's modulus for stretching.
  • Isotropic Material Relations: For isotropic solids, the three moduli are linked via Poisson's ratio σ: E=2G(1+σ)=3B(1−2σ). These relations do not hold for anisotropic materials (crystals, composites) where moduli depend on direction.
  • Geometry Separation: E is an intensive material property (Pa), while spring constant k=EA/L is extensive. Changing a wire's length or cross-section changes k but not E — this distinction is essential for connecting spring problems to material science.

Exercises

1.

A wire of area 1.0 × 10^−6 m^2 and length 2.0 m stretches by 1.0 mm under 200 N. Find Young modulus.

Steps
  • Stress = F/A = 200 / 1.0 × 10^−6 = 2.0 × 10^8 Pa.
  • Strain = 1.0 × 10^−3 / 2.0 = 5.0 × 10^−4.
  • Y = stress/strain = 2.0 × 10^8 / 5.0 × 10^−4.
  • Y = 4.0 × 10^11 Pa.
  • This is Young modulus, not the bulk modulus, because only the length was loaded.
Answer

4.0 × 10^11 Pa.
