Reflection at Curved Surface

Relevance

Notes

  • A spherical reflecting surface acts as a spherical mirror. For paraxial rays, a concave mirror converges parallel rays to a real focus; a convex mirror makes them diverge from a virtual focus.
  • The centre of curvature C is distance R from pole P; paraxial focus F is halfway between, so f=R/2. Wide-angle marginal rays do not meet this approximation exactly.
  • Using Cartesian distances positive to the right, a concave mirror has f=−15 cm. For u=−30 cm, 1/v=1/f−1/u=−1/15+1/30=−1/30, so v=−30 cm and m=−v/u=−1.
  • Construct an image with a ray parallel to the principal axis (reflected through F for a concave mirror) and a ray through C (retraces itself). Their intersection locates a real image; backward extensions locate a virtual image.
  • Spherical aberration occurs when marginal rays focus differently from paraxial rays. A smaller aperture reduces it by excluding marginal rays.
  • A spherical mirror's focal length is half its radius of curvature. Parallel rays close to the axis meet at that focus. Rays far from the axis do not, which is spherical aberration. The sign of f is negative for a concave mirror in the Cartesian convention.

Confusion

  • Do not substitute the thin-lens equation for the mirror equation.
  • f=R/2 assumes a spherical reflecting surface and small angles.
  • A symbol is not a number until the unit is named.
  • A proportional relation is not an equality until the constant is included.
  • A vector answer needs a direction as well as a magnitude.
  • A special case, such as a right angle or a steady temperature, has to be stated before it is used.

Specific Conditions

  • Mirror Equation Validity: The mirror formula 1/f=1/u+1/v (or 1/f=1/v−1/u for lenses) assumes paraxial rays (small angles, close to principal axis). For large apertures, spherical aberration occurs and the simple formula breaks down.
  • Sign Convention (Cartesian): Distances measured from pole: u (object) positive if object is on the incoming light side; v (image) positive if image is on the outgoing light side (real image for mirrors, virtual for lenses); f positive for concave mirrors/convex lenses, negative for convex mirrors/concave lenses.
  • Focal Length and Radius: For spherical mirrors, f=R/2. This comes from the geometry of reflection at a spherical surface. For lenses, 1/f=(n−1)(1/R1​−1/R2​) (lens maker's formula), which reduces to mirror-like behavior for reflective surfaces.

Exercises

1.

A concave mirror has radius 30 cm. Find the focal length and its Cartesian sign.

Steps
  • f = R/2 = 15 cm in magnitude.
  • The mirror is concave, so f = −15 cm.
  • Parallel rays near the axis meet 15 cm in front of the mirror.
  • The centre is 30 cm in front.
  • Edge rays do not share that exact focus.
Answer

f = −15 cm.
