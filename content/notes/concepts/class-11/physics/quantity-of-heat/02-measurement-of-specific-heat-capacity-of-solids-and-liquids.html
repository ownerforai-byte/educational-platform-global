  • 1. Topic Introduction — How Much Heat for One Degree?

    Water needs 4186 J to raise one kilogram by one kelvin; copper needs barely a tenth of that. Specific heat capacity quantifies this appetite for heat — and the principle of calorimetry (heat lost = heat gained) turns mixtures into measurement instruments.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts (NEB 2020, 2023, 2024)

    2.1 Definitions

    Quantity of heat: the energy transferred because of temperature difference; measured in joules.

    Specific heat capacity (c): the heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K: Q=mcΔTc in J kg−1K−1

    Thermal (heat) capacity: heat to raise a whole body by 1 K: C=mc (J/K).

    Water's c = 4186 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹ — exceptionally high (hydrogen bonding), which is why water is the coolant of engines and the climate buffer of coastal cities.

    2.2 Principle of calorimetry

    Heat lost by hot body=Heat gained by cold body m1​c1​(T1​−T)=m2​c2​(T−T2​) (neglecting losses) — the working equation of the method of mixtures.

    2.3 Measurement of specific heat — method of mixtures

    1. A known mass of the solid is heated in boiling water (to a known temperature T1​).
    2. It is quickly transferred into a calorimeter (known mass mc​, specific heat cc​) containing known mass mw​ of water at T2​.
    3. The final common temperature T is read.
    4. Energy balance (including the calorimeter):

    ms​cs​(T1​−T)=(mw​cw​+mc​cc​)(T−T2​)⇒cs​=ms​(T1​−T)(mw​cw​+mc​cc​)(T−T2​)​

    2.4 Worked numericals

    Q (3m, 2024): Define specific heat capacity. A 200 g block of aluminium is heated from 20 °C to 100 °C. How much heat is absorbed? (c = 0.897×10³ J kg⁻¹K⁻¹... as given: 0.897 J/g°C)

    • Definition as above. Q=mcΔT=0.2×897×80=1.44×104 J ≈ 14.4 kJ

    Q (3m, 2020): Calculate the heat required to raise the temperature of 500 g of water from 20 °C to 60 °C. (c = 4200 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹) Q=mcΔT=0.5×4200×40=8.4×104 J

    Q (NEB-style): A 100 g copper calorimeter (c = 385) contains 200 g water at 20 °C. A 50 g metal at 100 °C is dropped in; final temperature 22.5 °C. Find c of the metal.

    • Lost: 0.05 c (77.5). Gained: (0.2×4186 + 0.1×385)(2.5) = (837.2+38.5)(2.5) = 2194 J.
    • c=2194/(0.05×77.5)=566 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹.

    4.2 Common examination mistakes

    1. Mixing grams and kilograms in Q = mcΔT.
    2. Omitting the calorimeter's heat capacity in mixture problems.
    3. Writing c's unit as J/K (that is heat capacity C, not specific heat c).

Confusion

  • c vs C: specific heat c (J kg⁻¹K⁻¹) is per kilogram; heat capacity C = mc (J/K) is for the whole body.
  • Water's huge c makes it both a coolant and a climate stabiliser.

Summary

Specific heat capacity c is the heat per kilogram needed to raise temperature by one kelvin: Q=mcΔT (J kg⁻¹K⁻¹); the whole-body version is heat capacity C=mc. Water's 4186 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹ is exceptionally high, making it nature's coolant. The principle of calorimetry — heat lost equals heat gained — powers the method of mixtures: a hot solid dropped into a calorimeter of water yields cs​=ms​(T1​−T)(mw​cw​+mc​cc​)(T−T2​)​. NEB's numericals: 500 g of water over 40 °C needs 8.4×10⁴ J (2020); 200 g of aluminium over 80 °C absorbs 14.4 kJ (2024); definitions repeat (2023, 2024).

Exercises

1.

A 0.10 kg metal piece at 100°C is dropped into 0.20 kg of water at 20°C. The mixture reaches 25°C. Neglect the calorimeter. Take c_w = 4200 J kg^-1 K^-1. Find c of the metal.

Steps
  • Heat lost by the metal equals heat gained by the water.
  • 0.10 × c × (100 − 25) = 0.20 × 4200 × (25 − 20).
  • 0.10 × c × 75 = 0.20 × 4200 × 5.
  • 7.5 c = 4200.
  • c = 560 J kg^-1 K^-1.
Answer

560 J kg^-1 K^-1.
