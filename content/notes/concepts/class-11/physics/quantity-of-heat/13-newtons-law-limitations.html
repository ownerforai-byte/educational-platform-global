Notes

  • Newton's law of cooling states: dtdT​=−k(T−T0​), where T is body temperature, T0​ is surrounding temperature, and k is a positive constant. This law has several critical limitations that restrict its applicability.
  • Limitation 1 — Small temperature difference: The law holds accurately only when ∣T−T0​∣<30∘C (roughly). For larger differences, radiative heat loss dominates, which follows the Stefan-Boltzmann law: P=eσA(T4−T04​), not linear in ΔT.
  • The linear approximation: T4−T04​=(T−T0​)(T+T0​)(T2+T02​)≈4T03​(T−T0​) for small ΔT. The approximation breaks down when ΔT is large — radiative cooling is much faster than predicted.
  • Limitation 2 — Constant surrounding temperature T0​: T0​ must remain constant throughout cooling. If T0​ changes (e.g., cooling in a room that itself heats up), the differential equation changes, and k is no longer constant.
  • Limitation 3 — Same medium throughout cooling: The medium surrounding the body must be the same (same density, same composition). If the body moves between media (air to water), k changes discontinuously.
  • Limitation 4 — No phase change: The law assumes no latent heat effects. If the body undergoes phase change (water evaporating, ice melting), the temperature plateau absorbs/releases enormous heat, violating the single-c assumption.
  • Limitation 5 — Uniform temperature throughout body: Newton's law assumes the lumped capacitance model — internal thermal resistance is negligible, so T is uniform throughout. This requires the Biot number Bi=KhLc​​≪1, where Lc​ is characteristic length and K thermal conductivity.
  • For large objects (large Bi), the surface cools faster than the interior — temperature gradient exists. The body's effective cooling follows a sum of exponentials, not a single exponential. Newton's law describes only the first (dominant) mode.
  • Limitation 6 — No internal heat generation: The law assumes no heat is produced inside the body. Endothermic reactions, electrical heating, or nuclear decay would add to the heat balance, invalidating the simple model.
  • Limitation 7 — Constant k: The cooling constant k=mchA​ depends on heat transfer coefficient h, surface area A, mass m, and specific heat c. h itself depends on T, air velocity, and surface emissivity — not truly constant.
  • For cooling in still air: h≈5–25 W/m2K, dominated by natural convection. For forced convection (wind): h can be 25–250 W/m2K, making k velocity-dependent.
  • Mathematical derivation of limitations: Starting from Stefan-Boltzmann: dtdQ​=ϵσA(T4−T04​). With dQ=mcdT: dtdT​=mcϵσA​(T4−T04​). Linearization at T≈T0​: dtdT​=mc4ϵσAT03​​(T−T0​)=−k(T−T0​) with k=mc4ϵσAT03​​.
  • The correction factor for large ΔT: actual rate vs Newton prediction. For ΔT=100∘C with T0​=20∘C, the radiative rate is ~3× the linear prediction. Error grows as ∼3T02​(ΔT)2.
  • NEB exam focus: State the limitations, explain why T−T0​ must be small, draw the comparison between Newton's law and Stefan's law graphs, calculate Biot number to check applicability.
  • Practical implications: In forensic science (algor mortis), body cools from ~37°C to ambient (~20°C), ΔT≈17°C — within range. But for larger ΔT or outdoor exposure, corrections needed.
  • When Newton's law IS valid: Small ΔT, still air, same medium, uniform T inside body (small Bi), no phase change, no internal heat source. Laboratory cooling experiments with calorimeters are designed to satisfy these.
  • Definition of water equivalent W: The mass of water that would require the same amount of heat as a given body to raise its temperature by 1∘C (or 1 K). It is the thermal capacity of the body expressed in mass units of water.
  • Thermal capacity (heat capacity): C=mc — the heat required to raise the temperature of a body by 1∘C. Water equivalent W is defined so that W×cw​=m×c, therefore W=cw​m⋅c​, where cw​=4200 J/kg⋅K or 1 cal/g⋅°C.
  • Physical meaning: A calorimeter with water equivalent W=20 g absorbs as much heat when warming by 1∘C as 20 g of water. The calorimeter's own mass mcal​ and its specific heat ccal​ contribute: W=cw​mcal​ccal​​.
  • Derivation: For a body of mass m, specific heat c, raising temperature by ΔT: heat gained H=mcΔT. For W grams of water gaining same heat: H=W⋅cw​⋅ΔT. Equating: mcΔT=Wcw​ΔT⇒W=cw​mc​.
  • Units: Since c has units J/kg⋅K or cal/g⋅°C, and m has kg or g, W has units of kg or g (mass). Note: W is not a heat capacity itself — it is a mass equivalent.
  • For calorimeter: Wcal​=cw​mcal​ccal​​. For thin copper calorimeter: cCu​≈390 J/kg⋅K, cw​=4186 J/kg⋅K, so WCu​≈0.093mCu​. A 50 g copper calorimeter has W≈4.7 g.
  • Why water as reference? Water has the highest specific heat of common liquids (cw​=4186 J/kg⋅K at 20∘C). Using water as the reference makes W directly comparable to actual water mass — physical meaning is intuitive.
  • Effective heat capacity in calorimetry: When a calorimeter contains liquid of mass ml​, specific heat cl​, and water equivalent W, the total effective heat capacity is Ceff​=ml​cl​+Wcw​. This is used in both method of mixtures and method of cooling.
  • In method of cooling: Formula (m1​c1​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​c2​+Wcw​)/t2​ accounts for calorimeter heat capacity. If W is neglected, calculated c2​ has systematic error.
  • Finding W experimentally: (1) Cool known mass of water from T1​ to T2​, measure time t1​. (2) Cool same calorimeter with different mass of water, measure t2​. (3) Solve two equations: (m1​cw​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​cw​+Wcw​)/t2​. This eliminates k and gives W.
  • Dimensional analysis: [W]=[M]. [mc/cw​]=[Q][Θ]−1[M]⋅[Q][Θ]−1​=[M]. Dimensionally consistent.
  • NEB exam focus: Define W, derive W=mc/cw​, calculate W for given calorimeter, include W in calorimetry equations, distinguish between water equivalent and actual mass of calorimeter.
  • Connection to heat capacity: Heat capacity C=mc. Since cw​=1 cal/g⋅°C in CGS, W=mc numerically equals the heat capacity in calories per degree. For any body in SI: C=W×4.2 J/°C.
  • Applications: Calorimetry (all types),热水器 design (W concept used in thermal mass calculations), building thermal mass (W of walls/furniture affects room temperature stability), spacecraft thermal control.
  • Special cases: For ideal gas in bomb calorimeter (constant volume): CV​ used instead of c. For phase change calorimetry: effective W includes latent heat contributions. For food calorimetry: W of bomb calorimeter includes ignition wire, crucible.
  • The method of cooling experiment measures specific heat capacity by comparing the time taken for two liquids to cool through the same temperature range under identical conditions.
  • Newton's law: dtdT​=−k(T−T0​). For a body of mass m and specific heat c, heat loss rate is dtdQ​=mcdtdT​=−mck(T−T0​).
  • In the lab, we cannot measure dT/dt continuously with a stopwatch. Instead, we measure the time t for temperature to fall through a fixed interval ΔT (e.g., 60∘C→50∘C). Average cooling rate ≈tΔT​.
  • For two liquids in the same calorimeter (same k, same surface area, same surroundings), the heat loss rates are equal at the same temperature: m1​c1​t1​ΔT​=m2​c2​t2​ΔT​.
  • Since ΔT is identical, it cancels out, giving the practical formula: t1​m1​c1​​=t2​m2​c2​​ or c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​.
  • Apparatus: Calorimeter (thin copper cup) with stirrer and lid, thermometer (0ext−−100∘C, least count 0.5∘C or 0.1∘C), stopwatch, water (reference liquid), unknown liquid, burner/heater, stand.
  • Procedure: (1) Weigh empty calorimeter →mcal​. (2) Fill with known volume of water → find mass mw​. (3) Heat to ∼70∘C. (4) Place in constant-temperature enclosure (or still air). (5) Stir gently, record temperature every 30 s or 1 min. (6) Note time tw​ for 60∘C→50∘C. (7) Repeat with same volume of unknown liquid →ml​, tl​. (8) Calculate cl​.
  • Why same volume? Same volume → same liquid height → same surface area exposed → same k. If masses differ due to density, use m1​, m2​ in formula.
  • Stirring: Essential to maintain uniform temperature throughout liquid. Without stirring, surface cools faster than bulk → non-uniform T → Newton's law invalid.
  • Surroundings: Must be constant temperature. Best done in a double-walled vessel (water jacket) or a large room with stable temperature. Drafts cause forced convection → k changes.
  • Temperature range: Choose range where T−T0​<30∘C (e.g., 60→50, 50→40 if T0​≈25∘C). Ensures linear approximation of radiation holds.
  • Water equivalent of calorimeter: If calorimeter absorbs significant heat, effective thermal capacity is (ml​cl​+Wcw​). Formula becomes tw​mw​cw​+Wcw​​=tl​ml​cl​+Wcw​​. W found by separate experiment (cooling empty calorimeter + water).
  • Graphical analysis: Plot T vs t for both liquids. Draw tangents at same T to find (dT/dt)1​, (dT/dt)2​. Or plot ln(T−T0​) vs t — slope =−k. Verify k matches for both runs.
  • Sources of error: (1) Heat loss by radiation not purely Newtonian, (2) Convection currents in room, (3) Thermometer lag, (4) Incomplete stirring, (5) Evaporation (especially volatile liquids), (6) Calorimeter W not accounted, (7) Temperature range too large.
  • Accuracy: Typically ±5ext−−10%. For better accuracy: use oil bath for constant T0​, electrical heating for precise initial T, digital thermometer with data logging.
  • NEB practical exam: Common questions: 'Describe the method of cooling experiment.' 'Why same calorimeter?' 'Why stir?' 'Why small temperature range?' 'How to find water equivalent?' 'Plot cooling curve and find k.'
  • Alternative: Continuous cooling curve. Record T(t) for both liquids. Fit T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt. Extract k and Ti​. If k identical, ratio of initial slopes gives c ratio.
  • Purpose of Newton's law of cooling: The law provides a simple mathematical model to predict how the temperature of a body changes with time when it is placed in a surroundings of constant temperature.
  • Forensic science — estimating time of death: A corpse cools according to Newton's law after death (the "algor mortis" process). If body temperature is measured at time t, and the ambient temperature is known, the law estimates how long ago death occurred: θ(t)=θ0​+(θbody​−θ0​)e−K′t.
  • Engineering — cooling system design: Engineers use Newton's law to size radiators, heat sinks, and cooling fans. For a component dissipating power P, the steady-state temperature rise is ΔT=P/(kA), where k is the heat transfer coefficient.
  • Food industry — pasteurization and chilling: After pasteurization, milk must be cooled to below 4°C within a specified time to prevent bacterial growth. Newton's law calculates the required cooling time and the size of the heat exchanger.
  • Everyday life — coffee and tea: A hot cup of coffee cools faster when the room is cold and slower when the room is warm. The law explains why adding cold milk immediately (rather than waiting) keeps coffee hotter longer — the milk lowers the initial temperature, reducing the rate of heat loss.
  • Metallurgy — quenching and annealing: The rate of cooling a hot metal determines its microstructure and hardness. Quenching in water (large k) produces hard martensite; slow cooling in oil or air (smaller k) produces softer phases. Newton's law approximates the cooling curve.
  • Climate and building science — thermal mass: Walls and floors with high heat capacity (large mc) change temperature slowly. Newton's law predicts how quickly a building cools after the heating is turned off, helping design energy-efficient HVAC systems.
  • Weather forecasting — radiation fog: On clear nights, the ground loses heat by radiation. Newton's law approximates how quickly surface temperature drops. When it falls below the dew point, fog forms. This is used in agricultural frost warnings.
  • Electronic devices — CPU thermal management: A processor generates heat at rate P. Without cooling, its temperature rises until heat loss balances power input. With a heatsink, Newton's law predicts the equilibrium temperature: Teq​=Tambient​+P/k.
  • Calorimetry corrections — radiation loss: In precision calorimetry, heat loss to surroundings causes systematic errors. Newton's law allows correction: the heat lost during an experiment is ∫k(θ−θ0​)dt, which can be estimated from the cooling curve.
  • Medicine — therapeutic hypothermia: After cardiac arrest, patients are cooled to 32–34°C to reduce brain damage. Newton's law guides the cooling protocol, predicting how long ice packs or cooling blankets must be applied.
  • Photography — darkroom processing: Chemical baths in photography must be kept at a precise temperature. Newton's law predicts how quickly a fresh bath reaches the target temperature when moved from a warm storage area.
  • Automotive — engine warm-up and cooling: A car engine reaches operating temperature faster in cold weather (larger θ−θ0​) but the cooling system must dissipate more heat. Newton's law models both warm-up and cooling phases.
  • NEB exam applications: Identify real-world situations where Newton's law applies. Explain how the law is used in forensic time-of-death estimation and in finding unknown specific heats by the method of cooling.
  • Limitation reminder: Newton's law is an approximation. Real cooling involves radiation (∝T4−T04​), convection, and conduction simultaneously. The law is most accurate when temperature differences are moderate (20–50°C) and convection dominates.
  • Fact. 1st law (inertia): a body stays in uniform motion unless acted on by a net external force.
  • Fact. 2nd law: net force = rate of change of momentum = m·a (for constant m).
  • Fact. 3rd law: forces are mutual, equal and opposite, acting on different bodies (never cancel on the same body).
  • Fact. Impulse = ∫F dt = Δp; F = ma gives F = m·(Δv/Δt).

Confusion

  • Newton's law of cooling works for all temperature differences — It is valid only for ∣T−T0​∣<30∘C (approx). For large differences, radiative cooling dominates and follows T4−T04​, giving faster cooling than the linear law predicts.
  • The cooling constant k is always the same — k=hA/(mc) depends on heat transfer coefficient h which varies with temperature, air velocity, and surface conditions. k is only approximately constant within the small ΔT range.
  • A large body cools the same way as a small body — For large bodies, Biot number Bi=hLc​/K determines if lumped capacitance applies. Large Bi (metals, large solids) → significant temperature gradient inside body → Newton's law fails.
  • Surrounding temperature T0​ can change during cooling — T0​ must be constant. If surroundings heat up (sun, nearby heater), or if the body itself warms the air around it, k is not constant and the exponential solution T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt is invalid.
  • The formula T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt works for any body — This solution holds only when k is constant (small ΔT) and no phase change/internal heat. For radiation-dominated cooling, T(t) follows a different integral.
  • Evaporation doesn't affect cooling in a covered calorimeter — Evaporation removes heat (latent heat ≈2260 kJ/kg) and changes liquid composition (concentration). Even in covered calorimeters, some evaporation occurs. For volatile liquids, error is significant.
  • Newton's law applies to all states of matter — Valid for liquids and gases where convection homogenizes temperature. For solids without internal convection, the Fourier heat equation applies, not Newton's law.
  • If two bodies have the same mass and same c, they cool identically — Surface area A is in k. Two spheres of same mass but different radii have different A/m ratio → different k → different cooling rates. Shape matters.
  • The lumped capacitance assumption (uniform body temperature) is always valid for liquids — For liquids in still air, convection currents maintain uniformity. But in a viscous liquid cooling by conduction alone, internal gradients exist and Newton's law over-simplifies.
  • The law fails only at very high temperatures — The failure depends on ΔT relative to T0​, not absolute T. A body at 100∘C cooling in 25∘C air (ΔT=75∘C) fails the linear approximation even though absolute T is not 'high'.
  • Water equivalent W is the mass of water in the calorimeter — W is a calculated mass-equivalent, not actual water present. For a dry copper calorimeter of mass 50 g, W≈4.7 g. No water is inside.
  • Water equivalent and heat capacity are the same thing — Heat capacity C=mc has units J/°C. Water equivalent W=mc/cw​ has units of mass (g or kg). They are related by C=W×cw​, but numerically different in SI.
  • The water equivalent of a calorimeter is constant — W=mcal​ccal​/cw​. Since cw​ is nearly constant, W is constant for a given calorimeter. But cw​ itself varies with temperature (4.186→4.219 J/g⋅K), so W varies slightly.
  • A heavier calorimeter always has larger W — Not always. W∝m×ccal​. Aluminum (c≈900) has higher specific heat per unit mass than copper (c≈390). So an aluminum calorimeter has higher W per unit mass than copper.
  • Water equivalent is used only in method of cooling — W appears in all calorimetry calculations: method of mixtures, method of cooling, bomb calorimetry, and steam calorimetry. Any time a calorimeter absorbs heat, W must be included.
  • The formula W=mc/cw​ means the body behaves like mc/cw​ grams of water — Yes, this is exactly the physical meaning. A 100 g copper block (c=390) has W=100×390/4186=9.3 g — it behaves thermally like 9.3 g of water.
  • Water equivalent can be negative — W is always positive since m>0, c>0, cw​>0. However, in some contexts (endothermic reactions in calorimeter) the effective W from calibration includes correction factors.
  • All calorimeters have negligible W — Thin copper calorimeters have small W≈0.1m. But thick brass calorimeters, massive aluminum blocks, or calorimeters with large stirrers can have W comparable to liquid mass (∼20%–50% of liquid mass).
  • Water equivalent is independent of temperature — cw​ varies by ∼1% from 0∘C to 100∘C. For precise work, use cw​ at the average temperature of the experiment. In school physics, 4.2 J/g⋅K is standard.
  • Water equivalent is measured in liters — Since density of water ≈1 g/mL, 1000 g=1 L. But W is defined in mass units, not volume. A W=50 g means thermal capacity equivalent to 50 mL of water.
  • We measure cooling rate dT/dt directly with a stopwatch — We measure time t for a fixed ΔT, then average rate =ΔT/t. Instantaneous dT/dt requires continuous recording or tangent method.
  • The formula m1​c1​/t1​=m2​c2​/t2​ works for any temperature range — Only valid for small ΔT where Newton's law holds (T−T0​<30∘C). Large range → k not constant → formula fails.
  • Stirring is optional — Stirring is mandatory. Without it, temperature is not uniform, T in Newton's law is undefined (surface vs bulk), and cooling is non-exponential.
  • Water equivalent W is negligible for all calorimeters — For thin copper cups, W≈0.1mcal​ may be small. For thick brass/aluminum, W is significant. Always check if given in problem.
  • The method works for any two liquids — Liquids must not react with calorimeter, not evaporate significantly, and have similar viscosity (affects convection). Mercury, acids, volatile organics are problematic.
  • Initial temperature must be exactly the same for both runs — Temperature range (e.g., 60→50) must be same. Initial Ti​ can differ slightly if T0​ constant, but same Ti​ ensures identical k(T−T0​) profile.
  • Cooling time t is inversely proportional to specific heat c — From c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​, if m1​=m2​, c2​∝t2​. But this is for fixed ΔT, not total cooling time to room temperature.
  • The calorimeter's water equivalent is the same as its mass — W=mcal​ccal​/cw​. For copper (ccal​≈390 J/kg⋅K), W≈0.093mcal​. For aluminum (ccal​≈900), W≈0.21mcal​.
  • ❌ Newton's law of cooling tells you the exact time of death. ✅ It gives an ESTIMATE. The model ignores many factors (clothing, body size, air movement, humidity) that affect real cooling rates.
  • ❌ The law works equally well for very large temperature differences (e.g., a red-hot iron in air). ✅ For large ΔT, radiation (∝T4) dominates over convection, and Newton's linear law becomes inaccurate.
  • ❌ A larger object always cools faster. ✅ Larger objects have larger heat capacity (mc), which slows cooling. The cooling RATE depends on the ratio k/(mc), not just on size.
  • ❌ Newton's law of cooling is the same as the Stefan-Boltzmann law. ✅ Newton's law is linear (dθ/dt∝θ−θ0​). Stefan-Boltzmann is quartic (∝T4−T04​). Newton's law is an approximation valid for small ΔT.
  • ❌ The cooling constant k is a property of the material. ✅ k depends on the SURFACE properties (area, texture, air flow), not the material inside. Two objects of the same material but different shapes have different k.
  • ❌ Once a body reaches room temperature, it stays there forever. ✅ It reaches thermal equilibrium asymptotically — it gets arbitrarily close but never exactly reaches θ0​ in finite time (though for practical purposes it appears to).
  • ❌ The method of cooling (comparing cooling rates) gives the exact specific heat. ✅ It gives an approximate value. Errors arise from non-identical conditions, radiation losses, and the approximation that k is constant.
  • ❌ skip the key fact → ✅ 1st law (inertia): a body stays in uniform motion unless acted on by a net external force..

Universal Facts

  • Historical: Isaac Newton described his law of cooling in Principia Mathematica (1701): 'The rates of cooling are proportional to the excess temperature above ambient.' He observed candle flames cooling and noted the exponential relationship, without calculus — he used geometric methods.
  • The law is an empirical fit, not a fundamental law of physics — Unlike Newton's laws of motion or universal gravitation, this is an engineering correlation. The underlying physics is heat transfer: conduction, convection, radiation, each with its own T-dependence.
  • NEB exam tip: The Stefan-Boltzmann law P=ϵσAT4 (for radiation) and the Newton's law linear approximation are often compared in NEB. Draw both curves on the same graph: P∝(T−T0​) (straight line) vs P∝(T4−T04​) (steep curve). Show where they diverge.
  • Real-world application: Engine cooling systems (car radiators) use Newton's law for preliminary design. But actual cooling involves forced convection (fan), coolant flow, radiator geometry — full heat transfer equations needed for precision.
  • The Biot number (Bi=hLc​/K) is the key criterion: Bi<0.1 for lumped capacitance (Newton valid); Bi>40 (very large body): surface temperature ≈ ambient; 0.1<Bi<40: distributed system requiring Fourier equation.
  • Forensic science: Algor mortis (body cooling after death) uses a modified Newton's law: T(t)=T0​+(Tinitial​−T0​)e−kt. But corrections needed for clothing, body fat, humidity, wind. Police use Henssge nomogram for time-of-death estimation.
  • Connection to differential equations: Newton's law dtdT​=−k(T−T0​) is separable: T−T0​dT​=−kdt. Integrating gives ln∣T−T0​∣=−kt+C, or T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt. The time constant τ=1/k is the time to reduce ΔT to 1/e of initial.
  • Advanced: The Dulong-Petit law for cooling rate of metal spheres (dtdT​∝A(T−T0​)/m) was historically used before Newton's law. Both are forms of convective cooling with different assumed convection regimes (forced vs natural).
  • Historical: The concept of water equivalent was introduced by Joseph Black (1760s), who coined 'latent heat' and made precise calorimetry measurements. Black's water equivalent was the basis for the calorie definition — 1 calorie = heat to raise 1 g of water by 1∘C.
  • The calorie (cal) is defined relative to water: 1 cal = heat to raise 1 g of water by 1∘C at 14.5∘C. This makes cw​=1 cal/g⋅°C by definition. In SI, 1 cal=4.184 J (thermochemical), 4.1868 J (international).
  • NEB exam tip: Many calorimetry problems in NEB give W directly. If not given and calorimeter is mentioned, assume W is negligible unless specified otherwise, or estimate W≈0.1mcal​ for thin copper calorimeter.
  • Real-world: Solar thermal systems use water equivalent to size thermal storage tanks. The thermal mass of a building (walls, furniture, water) is expressed as 'liters of water equivalent' — how many liters of water would provide the same temperature stability.
  • Connection to specific heat: Materials with high specific heat (water, concrete, brick) have large W per unit mass. This is why coastal climates are milder — water's high c moderates temperature. Desert sand has low c≈800 J/kg⋅K, causing extreme day/night swings.
  • Calorimeter materials: Copper (c≈390) has low W per unit mass — ideal for sensitive calorimetry. Aluminum (c≈900) has moderate W. Glass (c≈800) has higher W. Stainless steel (c≈500) is common for lab calorimeters.
  • Advanced: Mixing calorimeters use the method of mixtures with W correction. Bomb calorimeters (constant volume) use CV​ instead of cp​. Adiabatic calorimeters minimize heat loss (effectively W of surroundings = 0). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measures W and phase changes precisely.
  • NEB practical tip: In the calorimetry practical, always tabulate: mass of empty calorimeter (m1​), mass with water (m2​), mass of water (mw​=m2​−m1​), mass of unknown solid/liquid, initial temperatures, final temperature. Calculate heat gained and lost, then c.
  • Historical: The method of cooling was first used by Joseph Black (1728–1799) in the 1760s to measure specific heats of liquids. Black also discovered latent heat of fusion and vaporization, founding quantitative thermal physics.
  • The method is a null method in principle — it compares ratios without measuring absolute heat. This cancels systematic errors in thermometer calibration, heat losses to surroundings (if identical), and timer calibration.
  • NEB practical exam: This experiment appears in the Physics Practical syllabus for Class 11. Typical marks distribution: Procedure (3), Observation table (4), Calculation (3), Graph (2), Precautions (2), Viva (1). Know the precautions by heart.
  • Connection to thermal conductivity: The cooling constant k=mcdKA​ for conduction through calorimeter wall (thickness d, conductivity K), plus convection/radiation terms. Same calorimeter → same k is the core assumption.
  • Real-world application: Food industry uses cooling curve analysis to determine thermal properties of liquid foods (soups, sauces, dairy). Pharmaceuticals use it for quality control of syrups. Forensic science uses body cooling (algor mortis) with similar principles.
  • Exam tip: If water equivalent W is not given in a numerical problem, assume W=0 and state it. If W is given, always include it in both sides of the equation. Missing W is the most common error.
  • Advanced technique: Regnault-Pfaundler method uses three cooling curves — empty calorimeter, calorimeter+water, calorimeter+liquid — to simultaneously eliminate k and W without needing to know either. Used in precision thermometry.
  • Limitation: Method fails for liquids that evaporate rapidly (alcohol, ether) — evaporative cooling adds non-Newtonian heat loss. Also fails for viscous liquids (glycerin) where internal convection is suppressed, altering k.
  • Newton's law of cooling is an example of a "linear first-order differential equation" — the same mathematical form appears in RC circuits (dQ/dt=−Q/RC), radioactive decay (dN/dt=−λN), and chemical reaction kinetics.
  • The time constant τ=mc/k has units of time and appears in countless physical, chemical, and biological processes. It characterizes how "inertial" a thermal system is — large τ means slow response.
  • Forensic use of Newton's law for time-of-death estimation was described by Poisson in 1829. Modern forensic science combines it with more sophisticated models that account for body composition, clothing, and air movement.
  • The law is named after Isaac Newton, who first published it in 1701 in "Scala Graduum Caloris". However, the principle was likely known to earlier natural philosophers and was studied independently by several scientists.
  • In electronics, the thermal time constant of a CPU heatsink is typically 10–100 seconds. This means it takes about 50–500 seconds (1–8 minutes) for the CPU to reach near its steady-state temperature after a sudden power increase.
  • 1st law (inertia): a body stays in uniform motion unless acted on by a net external force.
  • 2nd law: net force = rate of change of momentum = m·a (for constant m).
  • 3rd law: forces are mutual, equal and opposite, acting on different bodies (never cancel on the same body).

Formulas

  • Newton's law of cooling holds while T−Ts​≪Ts​, with the temperature difference decaying as ΔT=ΔT0​e−kt.

Key Points

  • The law fails at large temperature differences: radiation then dominates and the body's temperature follows the T⁴ Stefan law, not a straight line.
  • It also fails when the surroundings are not at a fixed temperature, when cooling is forced (fan, wind), or when the body changes phase while cooling.
