  • A body at 80∘C cools in surroundings at 20∘C. Using Newton's law, time to reach 50∘C is 10 min. How much time does Newton's law predict from 50∘C to 30∘C? Realistically, which will be faster? [Solution: Newton's law: T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt. 50=20+60e−10k⇒e−10k=0.5⇒k=0.0693 min−1. For 30∘C: 30=20+60e−kt⇒e−kt=1/6⇒kt=ln6=1.791⇒t=25.8 min. Realistically, radiative cooling accelerates at higher T; actual time will be shorter than predicted for the 80→50 step.]
  • A metal sphere (radius 2 cm, K=400 W/m⋅K, c=400 J/kg⋅K, ρ=8000 kg/m3) cools in still air (h=20 W/m2K). Check if lumped capacitance model (Newton's law) is valid. [Solution: Lc​=V/A=r/3=0.667 cm. Bi=hLc​/K=20×0.00667/400=0.00033≪1. Lumped capacitance valid; Newton's law applies.]
  • A large concrete slab (ρ=2300 kg/m3, c=750 J/kg⋅K, K=1.7 W/m⋅K, thickness 30 cm) cools from 60∘C to 30∘C in still air (h=10 W/m2K). Can Newton's law be applied? [Solution: Lc​=d/2=0.15 m. Bi=10×0.15/1.7=0.88. Since Bi∼1, significant temperature gradient inside. Newton's law (lumped) gives only approximate results; Fourier conduction equation needed for accuracy.]
  • At what ΔT does radiative cooling deviate by more than 20% from Newton's linear law? [Solution: Stefan-Boltzmann: P∝T4−T04​=T04​[((T−T0​)/T0​+1)4−1]. Newton: PN​∝(T−T0​). Ratio R=[(1+x)4−1]/(4x) where x=ΔT/T0​. R=1+x+x2/3+x3/16+.... For R>1.2, need x+x2/3>0.2⇒x≈0.15. For T0​=300 K, ΔT>45 K≈45∘C.]
  • State three limitations of Newton's law of cooling and explain each with a physical reason. [Solution: (1) Small ΔT: Radiation follows T4−T04​, linear only for small differences. (2) Constant T0​: If surroundings change, differential equation changes; k not constant. (3) Lumped capacitance: Bi≪1 required; for large bodies, internal thermal gradients make single-T model invalid. (4) No phase change: Latent heat violates single-c assumption. (5) No internal heat generation: Adds to energy balance.]
  • A cup of hot coffee (∼80∘C) in a room (∼25∘C) cools. Which stages follow Newton's law approximately? [Solution: 80→70: ΔT=55∘C, some radiation error. 70→50: ΔT=25→45∘C, good approximation. 50→30: ΔT=5→25∘∘C, excellent. Evaporation dominates throughout, especially near boiling. Newton's law only a rough guide for coffee.]
  • A body cools from 100∘C to 50∘C in still air (T0​=20∘C). How does the actual cooling time compare to Newton's law prediction? [Solution: Newton's law (linear): T(t)=20+80e−kt. If t100→50​=30 min, then k=0.0231 min−1. Stefan-Boltzmann: dtdT​∝T4−T04​. At T=100∘C=373 K, rate ∝3734−2934=1.936×1010−0.736×1010=1.2×1010. At mean T=75∘C=348 K, rate ∝3484−2934=1.465−0.736=0.729×1010. Newton's rate at mean ΔT=55∘C: normalized to 1.2 at ΔT=80. At ΔT=55: 55/80=0.6875 of max rate. Actual mean rate =0.729. So radiative rate is ~6% higher. Actual time slightly shorter than predicted.]
  • Why is Newton's law not applicable to cooling of a lake overnight? [Solution: Lake has enormous thermal capacity, significant solar absorption during day, evaporation, wind mixing, density anomaly of water (4∘C maximum density), possible freezing. Temperature gradient from surface to bottom. Not a lumped system; also, T0​ (air temperature) changes significantly overnight.]
  • The Biot number for a thermometer (glass bulb, radius 2 mm, K=1 W/m⋅K, h=50 W/m2K) is very small. What does this imply about its time constant? [Solution: Bi=hLc​/K=50×0.002/1=0.1. Small Bi → lumped capacitance valid. Time constant τ=mc/(hA). For fast response, we want small τ → small thermal mass mc, large surface A → small bulb. Indeed, thermometer bulbs are small for quick response.]
  • In a Newton's law of cooling experiment, the student uses a temperature range of 80∘C to 40∘C (surroundings 25∘C). Is this valid? What range would you recommend? [Solution: At T=80∘C, ΔT=55∘C; at T=40∘C, ΔT=15∘C. Mean ΔT=35∘C — borderline. Mean actual radiative rate vs linear: ~15-20% error. Recommended range: 50→35∘C (ΔT from 25 to 10, mean 17.5∘C — within 30∘C limit with minimal error).
  • A copper calorimeter weighs 60 g. Specific heat of copper =390 J/kg⋅K. Find its water equivalent. If filled with 150 g of water, what is total effective thermal capacity? [Solution: Wcal​=mcal​ccal​/cw​=0.06×390/4186=0.00559 kg=5.59 g. Total Ceff​=mw​cw​+Wcw​=(150+5.59)×4.186=651.3 J/°C or Ceff​=ml​cl​+Wcal​cw​=150×4.186+5.59×4.186=651.3 J/°C.]
  • A calorimeter of water equivalent 12 g contains 80 g of water at 20∘C. A hot metal piece (mass 50 g, c=400 J/kg⋅K) at 100∘C is dropped in. Find final equilibrium temperature. [Solution: Heat lost by metal = heat gained by water + calorimeter. 50×0.4×(100−T)=(80+12)×4.2×(T−20). 20(100−T)=92×4.2×(T−20). 2000−20T=386.4T−7728. 406.4T=9728⇒T=23.94∘C.]
  • Two calorimeters A and B have water equivalents WA​=8 g and WB​=15 g. Both contain 100 g of water at 25∘C. Equal masses (50 g) of a hot substance (c=2000 J/kg⋅K) at 100∘C are added to each. Will final temperatures differ? [Solution: A: effective mass =100+8=108 g. B: 100+15=115 g. 50×2(100−TA​)=108×4.2×(TA​−25)⇒TA​=36.5∘C. B: 100−TB​=115×2.1×(TB​−25)/50⇒TB​=35.8∘∘C. Yes, B has slightly lower final T because larger W absorbs more heat.]
  • How would you determine the water equivalent of a calorimeter experimentally using the method of cooling? [Solution: (1) Fill calorimeter with mass m1​ of water, cool from T1​ to T2​, record time t1​. (2) Repeat with mass m2​ of water, same T1​→T2​, record t2​. (3) Method of cooling: (m1​cw​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​cw​+Wcw​)/t2​. (4) Solve: W=cw​(m2​t1​−m1​t2​)/(t1​−t2​).]
  • A calorimeter of mass 100 g and c=900 J/kg⋅K is used. Calculate W. Compare with a copper calorimeter of same mass (c=390 J/kg⋅K). [Solution: Aluminum: WAl​=100×900/4186=21.5 g. Copper: WCu​=100×390/4186=9.3 g. Same mass, aluminum has W 2.3× larger because cAl​>cCu​.]
  • In a method of cooling experiment, the formula t1​(m1​+W)cw​​=t2​(m2​+W)cw​​ is used. If m1​=100 g, m2​=120 g, t1​=5 min, t2​=4 min, find W. [Solution: (100+W)/5=(120+W)/4⇒4(100+W)=5(120+W)⇒400+4W=600+5W⇒W=−200 g. Negative W is physically impossible — indicates experimental error, non-Newtonian cooling, or wrong assumptions. Recheck measurements.]
  • Steam at 100∘C is passed into a calorimeter of water equivalent 15 g containing 200 g of water at 20∘C. The mass of condensed steam is 5 g. Find final temperature. (Latent heat of steam L=540 cal/g.) [Solution: Heat from condensing steam: 5×540=2700 cal. Heat from cooling condensed water (100→T): 5×(100−T) cal. Heat gained by water + calorimeter: (200+15)(T−20) cal. 2700+5(100−T)=215(T−20)⇒2700+500−5T=215T−4300⇒220T=7500⇒T=34.1∘C.]
  • A calorimeter with stirrer (W=10 g) contains 150 g of water at 15∘C. When 100 g of a substance at 80∘C (c=800 J/kg⋅K) is added, equilibrium is 25∘C. Find W again using this data. [Solution: Heat lost by substance: 0.1×800×55=4400 J. Heat gained by water+calorimeter: (0.15×4200+W×4200)(10)=(630+4200W)×10=6300+42000W J. 4400=6300+42000W⇒W=−0.0452 kg=−45.2 g. Negative! Suggests heat loss to surroundings, incomplete mixing, or wrong c.]
  • State the physical meaning of water equivalent. A calorimeter of mass 80 g has water equivalent 7.5 g. Find specific heat of calorimeter material. [Solution: Physical meaning: The mass of water that would absorb the same heat as the calorimeter for the same temperature rise. W=mcal​ccal​/cw​⇒7.5=80×ccal​/4.2⇒ccal​=7.5×4.2/80=0.394 J/g⋅K=394 J/kg⋅K. Material is likely copper or brass.]
  • Two calorimeters of water equivalents W1​=10 g and W2​=20 g contain equal masses of water. The same hot solid is added to each. Show that the temperature rise is inversely proportional to (m+W). [Solution: Heat lost by solid Q=ms​cs​(Ts​−T). Heat gained: Q=(mw​+W)cw​(T−Ti​). T−Ti​=cw​(mw​+W)ms​cs​​(Ts​−T). Since ms​,cs​,Ts​,T are same for both, (T−Ti​)(mw​+W)=constant. Thus (T−Ti​)∝1/(mw​+W). Larger W → smaller ΔT.]
  • In a method of cooling experiment, 100 g of water in a calorimeter (water equivalent 10 g) takes 4 minutes to cool from 60∘C to 50∘C. When replaced with 120 g of an unknown liquid, the time is 3 minutes. Find specific heat of liquid. [Solution: (mw​cw​+Wcw​)/tw​=(ml​cl​+Wcw​)/tl​. (100×4.2+10×4.2)/4=(120×cl​+10×4.2)/3⇒115.5=40cl​+14⇒cl​=2.5375 J/g⋅K=2538 J/kg⋅K]
  • A student performs the method of cooling experiment. Water (mass 80 g, cw​=4.2 J/g⋅K) cools from 65∘C to 55∘C in 180 s. The same calorimeter with 100 g of oil cools through the same range in 120 s. Water equivalent of calorimeter is 5 g. Find c of oil. [Solution: (80×4.2+5×4.2)/180=(100×c+5×4.2)/120⇒1.9833=(100c+21)/120⇒238=100c+21⇒c=2.17 J/g⋅K=2170 J/kg⋅K]
  • Equal volumes (100 mL) of water and an unknown liquid are used. Density of water =1 g/mL, density of liquid =0.8 g/mL. Water cools 60→50∘C in 5 min; liquid in 3 min. Water equivalent of calorimeter =8 g. Find c of liquid. [Solution: mw​=100 g, ml​=80 g. (100×4.2+8×4.2)/5=(80×c+8×4.2)/3⇒90.72=(80c+33.6)/3⇒272.16=80c+33.6⇒c=2.982 J/g⋅K=2982 J/kg⋅K]
  • Why must the calorimeter be the same for both liquids? [Solution: Cooling constant k=hA/(mc) depends on surface area A, heat transfer coefficient h, and total thermal capacity mc. Same calorimeter → same A, same h (same surface), same surroundings → same k. Different calorimeters → different k → formula invalid.]
  • A student forgets to stir the liquid during cooling. How does this affect the result? [Solution: Without stirring, temperature gradient develops — surface cools faster than bulk. Measured T (thermometer at center) lags behind surface T. Cooling appears slower → measured t larger → calculated c larger than true value. Also, cooling curve deviates from exponential.]
  • In an experiment, the cooling curve (T vs t) for water is plotted. The tangent at 55∘C gives slope −0.5∘C/min. For the same calorimeter with unknown liquid, tangent at 55∘C gives slope −0.8∘C/min. Masses: water 100 g, liquid 120 g. Water equivalent 5 g. Find c of liquid. [Solution: At same T, (mw​cw​+Wcw​)(dT/dt)w​=(ml​cl​+Wcw​)(dT/dt)l​. (100×4.2+5×4.2)(0.5)=(120cl​+5×4.2)(0.8)⇒220.5=96cl​+16.8⇒cl​=2.122 J/g⋅K]
  • Explain how to determine the water equivalent W of a calorimeter experimentally. [Solution: (1) Cool calorimeter with known mass of water m1​ through range ΔT, measure t1​. (2) Cool calorimeter with different mass of water m2​ through same ΔT, measure t2​. (3) (m1​cw​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​cw​+Wcw​)/t2​. Solve for W. Or: cool empty calorimeter (mass mcal​, known ccal​) — not practical as ccal​ may be unknown.]
  • The temperature of surroundings T0​=25∘C. A liquid cools from 70∘C to 60∘C in 2 min. Is Newton's law applicable? [Solution: Average T−T0​=65−25=40∘C>30∘C. Newton's law not strictly applicable. Radiation ∝T4−T04​ is non-linear. Error ∼10ext−−15%. Better range: 55→45∘C (avg T−T0​=25∘C).]
  • Two liquids A and B have cooling times tA​=4 min, tB​=3 min for same ΔT. Same masses, same calorimeter. If cA​=3000 J/kg⋅K, find cB​. [Solution: mcA​/tA​=mcB​/tB​⇒cB​=cA​(tB​/tA​)=3000×(3/4)=2250 J/kg⋅K]
  • Derive the formula for specific heat by method of cooling starting from dQ/dt=−kA(T−T0​) and dQ=mcdT. [Solution: mcdtdT​=−kA(T−T0​). For same calorimeter, kA same. At same T: m1​c1​(dT/dt)1​=m2​c2​(dT/dt)2​. Average over ΔT: m1​c1​t1​ΔT​=m2​c2​t2​ΔT​⇒c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​.]
  • A body cools from 80°C to 50°C in 3 min in a 20°C room. How long to cool from 50°C to 35°C? K′=31​ln3060​=3ln2​=0.231 min−1. Time 50→35: t=K′1​ln1530​=0.231ln2​=3 min. Same time because the ratio of excesses is the same (2:1).
  • A cup of tea at 95°C cools to 75°C in 4 min in a 20°C room. How long until it reaches 40°C (drinkable)? K′=41​ln5575​=4ln1.364​=0.0783 min−1. Time to 40°C: t=0.07831​ln2075​=12.77×1.322=16.9 min from start.
  • Why does adding cold milk to coffee keep it warmer longer? Without milk: coffee at 85°C, excess = 65°C. With milk added immediately: coffee at 70°C, excess = 50°C. The rate of cooling is proportional to excess, so the milk-added coffee cools at a slower RATE from the start, and stays above any given temperature longer.
  • A forge-cooled CPU has k=0.5 W/K and dissipates 65 W. If ambient is 25°C, what is the steady-state temperature? At equilibrium, heat generated = heat lost: P=k(Teq​−T0​). 65=0.5(Teq​−25). Teq​=25+130=155°C. This is dangerously hot — a larger heatsink (larger k) is needed.
  • A metal sphere and a metal cube of the same material and same mass are heated to the same temperature and allowed to cool. Which cools faster? The sphere has the smallest surface area for a given volume. The cube has more surface area. Since K′=k/(mc) and k∝ surface area, the cube cools faster (larger A → larger k).
  • In a calorimetry experiment, a hot metal sample is dropped into water. Heat loss to the room causes the measured equilibrium temperature to be lower than the true value. How does Newton's law help correct this? By recording the temperature before and after mixing, you can estimate the heat lost: Qlost​=∫k(θ−θ0​)dt≈k(θavg​−θ0​)Δt. Adding this to the energy balance gives a more accurate specific heat.
