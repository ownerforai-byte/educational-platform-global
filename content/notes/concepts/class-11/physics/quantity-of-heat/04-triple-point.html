Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Where Three Phases Meet

    At exactly 273.16 K and 611.7 Pa, water coexists as ice, liquid and vapour simultaneously — the triple point. It is not a laboratory curiosity: it defines the kelvin, anchoring every temperature measurement on Earth.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition

    The triple point of a substance is the unique temperature and pressure at which its solid, liquid and gaseous phases coexist in equilibrium.

    For water: Ttp​=273.16 K(0.01 °C),Ptp​=611.7 Pa(0.006 atm)

    2.2 Phase diagram context

    On a P–T diagram the three phase-boundary curves (fusion, vaporization, sublimation) meet at the triple point:

    • Fusion curve: melting point vs pressure (for water, slopes backward — ice melts under pressure).
    • Vaporization curve: boiling point vs pressure, ending at the critical point (647 K, 218 atm for water) where liquid and gas become indistinguishable.
    • Sublimation curve: solid ↔ vapour below the triple point (frost, freeze-drying).

    Below Ptp​, liquid water cannot exist — ice sublimes directly to vapour (why ice sublimates at high altitude / in a vacuum).

    2.3 Role in thermometry

    The kelvin is defined by fixing the water triple point: Ttp​≡273.16 K exactly (pre-2019 SI), making the triple point the primary reproducible fixed point of thermometry — any laboratory can realise it in a triple-point cell to calibrate thermometers. (Since 2019 the kelvin is defined via the Boltzmann constant, but the triple point remains the practical calibration anchor.)

    2.4 Comparison with other substances

    SubstanceT_tp (K)P_tp (Pa)
    Water273.16611.7
    Carbon dioxide216.65.18×10⁵ (≈5.1 atm — why CO₂ "ice" sublimes at 1 atm)
    Mercury234.31.65×10⁻⁷

    2.5 Worked reasoning

    Q (NEB-style): Why does dry ice (solid CO₂) sublime rather than melt at atmospheric pressure? Because 1 atm (1.01×10⁵ Pa) is below CO₂'s triple-point pressure (5.18×10⁵ Pa) — the liquid phase is unstable there, so solid CO₂ passes straight to gas at −78.5 °C.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 45: fixed points calibrate thermometers; the triple point is the most reproducible one.
    • To ideal gas (Unit 13): the old kelvin definition used ideal-gas thermometry matched at the triple point.
    • To latent heat: at the triple point, all three latent heats can be exchanged simultaneously.

Summary

The triple point of a substance is the unique temperature and pressure where solid, liquid and gas coexist in equilibrium — for water 273.16 K at 611.7 Pa. On the P–T phase diagram the fusion, vaporization and sublimation curves meet there; below the triple pressure the liquid phase cannot exist, which is why dry ice sublimes (CO₂'s P_tp = 5.18×10⁵ Pa exceeds 1 atm) and why freeze-drying works. The water triple point anchored the kelvin (Ttp​≡273.16 K exactly, pre-2019 SI) and remains the most reproducible calibration point for thermometers — a cell of pure water at its triple point reads the same temperature in every laboratory on Earth.

Special Notes

  • Do not quote 0°C and 1 atm as the triple-point conditions.
  • The definition applies to one pure substance in equilibrium.

Important Statements

  • "The triple point of water is the temperature and pressure at which ice, liquid water and water vapour coexist in equilibrium."

Exercises

1.

Explain why a kelvin temperature can be defined from the water triple point, and state that temperature.

Steps
  • The triple point occurs at one temperature and one pressure.
  • It can be reproduced in a sealed cell of pure water.
  • That temperature is defined as 273.16 K.
  • A degree kelvin is then 1/273.16 of that temperature.
  • The ice point, 0°C, is slightly lower, at 273.15 K, because it is not the triple point.
Answer

273.16 K.
