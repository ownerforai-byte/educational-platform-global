Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Force That Opposes Every Push

    Friction is the contact force opposing relative (or attempted) motion between surfaces. It is indispensable — walking, brakes, nail grips — and costly — worn engines, wasted fuel. The syllabus asks for the laws of solid friction and their experimental verifications, plus incline numericals.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    TermDefinition
    Static frictionOpposes the start of motion; self-adjusting up to a maximum
    Limiting frictionMaximum static friction just before sliding begins: fsmax​=μs​N
    Kinetic frictionOpposes motion once sliding: fk​=μk​N, roughly constant and slightly less than limiting friction
    Normal reactionThe surface's perpendicular push: N=mg on level ground, mgcosθ on an incline
    Coefficient of friction (μ)Ratio of friction to normal reaction — dimensionless, typically 0.05–1.5

    2.1 Laws of solid friction

    1. Friction acts opposite to the direction of attempted/actual relative motion, along the surfaces in contact.
    2. Friction is proportional to the normal reaction: f∝N.
    3. Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact (for a given normal reaction).
    4. Kinetic friction is independent of the relative velocity (at ordinary speeds).
    5. Kinetic friction is slightly less than limiting static friction.

    2.2 Experimental verifications

    • Proportional to N: a block on a table is pulled by a spring balance at constant speed; repeat with weights stacked on the block — the reading scales with total weight; a graph of f against N is a straight line whose slope is μ.
    • Independent of area: repeat with the block on different faces (different contact areas) — the balance reading is unchanged.
    • Independent of speed: pull at different steady speeds — the reading stays (approximately) constant.
    • Angle of repose: a block on an adjustable incline just slides at angle θ_r; resolving gives μ=tanθr​ — a direct measurement.

    2.3 Incline relations

    QuantityFormula
    On the point of sliding downμs​=tanθr​ (angle of repose)
    Sliding down with frictiona=g(sinθ−μcosθ)
    Force to pull up the incline at constant speedF=mg(sinθ+μcosθ)

  • 3. Worked Numericals

    Q1 (3m, 2023): A block of mass 5 kg rests on a horizontal plane. The coefficient of static friction is 0.4. What horizontal force is needed to start the block moving? (g = 10 m/s²) N=mg=50 N; flimiting​=μs​N=0.4×50=20 N. A force just over 20 N starts the motion.

    Q2 (3m, 2021): A block of mass 10 kg rests on a rough inclined plane of angle 30°. If the coefficient of friction is 0.2, find the frictional force (and the acceleration). (g = 10 m/s²) N=mgcos30°=100×0.866=86.6 N; f=μN=0.2×86.6=17.3 N. Driving component: mgsin30°=50 N > 17.3 N, so it slides: a=g(sin30°−μcos30°)=10(0.5−0.173)=3.27 m/s². Friction ≈ 17.3 N up the plane; a ≈ 3.3 m/s² down the plane.

    Q3 (3m, 2024): A block just begins to slide down a rough incline when the angle of inclination is 30°. Calculate the coefficient of static friction. At the point of sliding, gravity's component equals limiting friction: mgsinθ=μmgcosθ→μ=tanθ=tan30°=0.577. μ ≈ 0.58.

  • 4. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 13: friction is a contact force entering Newton's-law applications; the incline formulas combine the second law with friction.
    • To Work/Energy (Unit 5): friction dissipates mechanical energy as heat — the reason slides stop.
    • To Circular Motion: friction supplies the centripetal force for cornering vehicles (banking topic).
    • To real life: tyres, brakes, belts and nails need friction; engines and skates fight it with lubrication (replacing solid with fluid friction).

Summary

Friction opposes attempted or actual relative motion along surfaces. Static friction self-adjusts up to the limiting value μ_sN; kinetic friction runs at μ_kN, slightly smaller and nearly speed-independent. The laws: friction opposes relative motion, is proportional to normal reaction, and ignores contact area — verified by spring-balance pulls with added weights (straight-line f–N graph), face-swaps (same reading) and the angle of repose, where μ = tan θ_r. Incline numericals flow from N=mgcosθ: a 5 kg block with μ_s = 0.4 needs just over 20 N to start (NEB 2023); a 10 kg block on 30° with μ = 0.2 feels 17.3 N of friction and slides at 3.3 m/s² (NEB 2021); a block just sliding at 30° fixes μ = tan 30° ≈ 0.58 (NEB 2024).

Special Notes
  • Static friction is NOT always μ_sN — it is a variable force that matches the applied force up to that maximum.
  • The normal force is NOT always mg — on a slope it is mg cos θ; with pulling/pushing at an angle it changes.
  • Friction can act in the direction of motion — static friction pushes you forward when walking.
  • The angle of repose depends only on μ_s, not on the mass of the body.
  • NEB frequently asks: 'Does the body slide? If so, find the acceleration' — check tan θ vs μ_s first.

Original Content

Notes
  • What friction is: a contact force that opposes relative motion (or its tendency) between two surfaces in contact. It acts parallel to the contact surface. Static friction (no sliding) and kinetic friction (sliding) are the two main types.
  • Laws of friction: (1) Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact (to a good approximation). (2) Maximum static friction and kinetic friction are both proportional to the normal reaction: f_s(max) = μ_sN, f_k = μ_kN. (3) μ_k < μ_s — it is harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.
  • Static friction is a variable force: it matches the applied force up to a maximum: f_s ≤ μ_sN. If you push with 30 N and the object doesn't move, f_s = 30 N (not μ_sN). Only at the point of slipping does f_s = μ_sN.
  • Angle of friction (λ): the angle between the normal reaction and the resultant reaction (normal + friction) when slipping is about to occur. tan λ = μ_s. The resultant reaction makes angle λ with the normal.
  • Angle of repose (θ_r): the steepest angle of a slope at which a body just remains at rest. At this angle, mg sin θ_r = μ_s mg cos θ_r → tan θ_r = μ_s. So θ_r = λ — the angle of repose equals the angle of friction.
  • Worked example — body on a slope: a 10 kg block on a 25° slope (μ_s = 0.5). Does it slide? tan 25° = 0.466 < 0.5 → no, it stays. Static friction = mg sin 25° = 41.5 N (less than μ_sN = 0.5 × 92.4 = 46.2 N).
  • Worked example — pulling a block: a 5 kg block on a horizontal surface (μ_k = 0.3). Pulled by a 20 N force at 30° above horizontal. Normal N = mg − F sin 30° = 49 − 10 = 39 N. Friction = 0.3 × 39 = 11.7 N. Net force = 20 cos 30° − 11.7 = 5.6 N. a = 5.6/5 = 1.12 m/s².
  • NEB exam focus: distinguish static vs kinetic friction; solve slope problems (does it slide? find acceleration); angle of repose and its equality with angle of friction; pulling/pushing at an angle (changes the normal, hence friction).
Confusion
  • ❌ 'Friction always opposes motion.' ✅ Friction opposes RELATIVE motion (or its tendency). Static friction can act IN the direction of motion — e.g., the static friction on your feet pushes you forward when walking.
  • ❌ 'Friction depends on the area of contact.' ✅ For a given normal force, friction is (approximately) independent of area. A wide tyre and a narrow tyre with the same load have the same maximum friction (in the simple model).
  • ❌ 'Static friction is always μ_sN.' ✅ Static friction is VARIABLE: it matches the applied force up to μ_sN. Only at the point of slipping does f_s = μ_sN.
  • ❌ 'μ_k > μ_s because sliding is faster.' ✅ μ_k < μ_s — it is harder to start sliding than to keep sliding. This is why a heavy box jerks when you first push it.
  • ❌ 'The angle of repose depends on the mass of the body.' ✅ tan θ_r = μ_s — mass cancels out. All bodies with the same μ_s have the same angle of repose.
  • ❌ 'Pulling a block at an angle reduces friction because the force is smaller.' ✅ Pulling upward reduces the normal (N = mg − F sin θ), which reduces friction. Pushing downward increases the normal and friction.

Universal Facts
  • Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact — a counterintuitive but experimentally verified law.
  • μ_k < μ_s for most surface pairs — this is why objects jerk when starting to slide.
  • The angle of repose equals the angle of friction: θ_r = λ = tan⁻¹μ_s — a beautiful geometric connection.
  • Friction can act in the direction of motion — static friction is the force that accelerates you forward when walking.
  • Pulling a block at an angle reduces friction (by reducing N); pushing at an angle increases it (by increasing N).
Examples
  • Slope: A 5 kg block on a 30° slope (μ_s = 0.4). tan 30° = 0.577 > 0.4 → slides. a = g(sin30° − μ_k cos30°) = 9.8(0.5 − 0.4×0.866) = 1.51 m/s².
  • Horizontal pull: A 10 kg block (μ_k = 0.25) pulled by 40 N at 20° above horizontal. N = 98 − 40sin20° = 84.3 N; f = 21.1 N; F_net = 40cos20° − 21.1 = 16.5 N; a = 1.65 m/s².
  • Angle of repose: A block slides at constant speed down a 35° slope. μ_k = tan 35° = 0.70. μ_s is slightly higher (≈ 0.75).
  • Static friction variable: A 20 kg box (μ_s = 0.5). Push with 40 N — box doesn't move, f_s = 40 N. Push with 120 N — box moves (max f_s = 98 N).
  • Pulling vs pushing: A 15 kg block (μ_k = 0.3). Pull at 25°: N = 147 − Fsin25°. Push at 25°: N = 147 + Fsin25°. Pulling gives less friction.

  • A 5 kg block slides down a 40° slope (μ_k = 0.25). Find the acceleration.
  • A 10 kg block is pulled by a 50 N force at 30° above horizontal (μ_k = 0.2). Find the acceleration.
  • A block remains at rest on a slope up to 35°. Find μ_s. If the slope is increased to 40°, find the acceleration (μ_k = 0.6).
  • Explain why it is harder to start sliding a heavy box than to keep it sliding.
Formulas
  • Maximum static friction: f_s(max) = μ_sN (acts just before slipping)
  • Kinetic friction: f_k = μ_kN (acts during sliding; μ_k < μ_s)
  • Slope sliding condition: body slides when tan θ > μ_s; a = g(sin θ − μ_k cos θ)
  • Angle of friction: tan λ = μ_s (angle between normal and resultant reaction)
  • Angle of repose: tan θ_r = μ_s → θ_r = λ (steepest angle for rest)
Key Points
  • Static friction is VARIABLE: it matches the applied force up to μ_sN.
  • Kinetic friction is approximately CONSTANT: f_k = μ_kN during sliding.
  • μ_k < μ_s — harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.
  • Friction is (approximately) independent of the apparent area of contact.
  • Pulling at an angle reduces N and friction; pushing at an angle increases both.

Exam Short Tricks
  • Slope check: compare tan θ with μ_s — if tan θ > μ_s, the body slides; otherwise it stays.
  • Normal force shortcut: N = mg (horizontal), N = mg cos θ (slope), N = mg − F sin θ (pulling up), N = mg + F sin θ (pushing down).
  • Friction direction: it opposes relative motion — for a block being pulled, friction acts opposite to the pull.
  • Angle of repose shortcut: θ_r = tan⁻¹μ_s — just take the inverse tangent of the coefficient.
  • Static vs kinetic: use μ_s for 'just about to slip' problems; use μ_k for 'already sliding' problems.

Specific Conditions
  • Static vs. Kinetic Threshold: The maximum static friction fsmax​=μs​N is the upper bound for static friction. Static friction is a variable force that matches the applied force up to this limit. Kinetic friction fk​=μk​N applies only when sliding occurs, with μk​<μs​.
  • Normal Force Dependency: Friction is proportional to the normal reaction, not the weight. Any vertical force component (pulling up, pushing down, incline) changes N, which directly changes the friction force. Always compute N first.
  • Area Independence (Ideal Model): The laws of friction assume friction is independent of the apparent contact area (Coulomb's model). This holds well for dry, rigid surfaces but breaks down for soft materials, lubricated contacts, or adhesive forces where real contact area matters.

    • Pull up at constant speed: F = mg(sin θ + μ cos θ)

Key Points

  • Laws: opposes relative motion; f ∝ N; independent of contact area; kinetic friction independent of speed; μ_k < μ_s.
  • Angle of repose: μ = tan θ_r.
  • Incline: N = mg cos θ; sliding a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ); pull up at constant speed F = mg(sin θ + μ cos θ).

Special Notes

  • Static friction is NOT always μ_sN — it is a variable force that matches the applied force up to that maximum.
  • The normal force is NOT always mg — on a slope it is mg cos θ; with pulling/pushing at an angle it changes.
  • Friction can act in the direction of motion — static friction pushes you forward when walking.
  • The angle of repose depends only on μ_s, not on the mass of the body.

Summary

Friction opposes attempted or actual relative motion along surfaces. Static friction self-adjusts up to the limiting value μ_sN; kinetic friction runs at μ_kN, slightly smaller and nearly speed-independent. The laws: friction opposes relative motion, is proportional to normal reaction, and ignores contact area — verified by spring-balance pulls with added weights (straight-line f–N graph), face-swaps (same reading) and the angle of repose, where μ = tan θ_r. Incline numericals flow from N=mgcosθ: a 5 kg block with μ_s = 0.4 needs just over 20 N to start (NEB 2023); a 10 kg block on 30° with μ = 0.2 feels 17.3 N of friction and slides at 3.3 m/s² (NEB 2021); a block just sliding at 30° fixes μ = tan 30° ≈ 0.58 (NEB 2024).
