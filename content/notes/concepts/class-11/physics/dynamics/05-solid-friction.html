Enriched Content

Notes
  • What friction is: a contact force that opposes relative motion (or its tendency) between two surfaces in contact. It acts parallel to the contact surface. Static friction (no sliding) and kinetic friction (sliding) are the two main types.
  • Laws of friction: (1) Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact (to a good approximation). (2) Maximum static friction and kinetic friction are both proportional to the normal reaction: f_s(max) = μ_sN, f_k = μ_kN. (3) μ_k < μ_s — it is harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.
  • Static friction is a variable force: it matches the applied force up to a maximum: f_s ≤ μ_sN. If you push with 30 N and the object doesn't move, f_s = 30 N (not μ_sN). Only at the point of slipping does f_s = μ_sN.
  • Angle of friction (λ): the angle between the normal reaction and the resultant reaction (normal + friction) when slipping is about to occur. tan λ = μ_s. The resultant reaction makes angle λ with the normal.
  • Angle of repose (θ_r): the steepest angle of a slope at which a body just remains at rest. At this angle, mg sin θ_r = μ_s mg cos θ_r → tan θ_r = μ_s. So θ_r = λ — the angle of repose equals the angle of friction.
  • Worked example — body on a slope: a 10 kg block on a 25° slope (μ_s = 0.5). Does it slide? tan 25° = 0.466 < 0.5 → no, it stays. Static friction = mg sin 25° = 41.5 N (less than μ_sN = 0.5 × 92.4 = 46.2 N).
  • Worked example — pulling a block: a 5 kg block on a horizontal surface (μ_k = 0.3). Pulled by a 20 N force at 30° above horizontal. Normal N = mg − F sin 30° = 49 − 10 = 39 N. Friction = 0.3 × 39 = 11.7 N. Net force = 20 cos 30° − 11.7 = 5.6 N. a = 5.6/5 = 1.12 m/s².
  • NEB exam focus: distinguish static vs kinetic friction; solve slope problems (does it slide? find acceleration); angle of repose and its equality with angle of friction; pulling/pushing at an angle (changes the normal, hence friction).
Confusion
  • ❌ 'Friction always opposes motion.' ✅ Friction opposes RELATIVE motion (or its tendency). Static friction can act IN the direction of motion — e.g., the static friction on your feet pushes you forward when walking.
  • ❌ 'Friction depends on the area of contact.' ✅ For a given normal force, friction is (approximately) independent of area. A wide tyre and a narrow tyre with the same load have the same maximum friction (in the simple model).
  • ❌ 'Static friction is always μ_sN.' ✅ Static friction is VARIABLE: it matches the applied force up to μ_sN. Only at the point of slipping does f_s = μ_sN.
  • ❌ 'μ_k > μ_s because sliding is faster.' ✅ μ_k < μ_s — it is harder to start sliding than to keep sliding. This is why a heavy box jerks when you first push it.
  • ❌ 'The angle of repose depends on the mass of the body.' ✅ tan θ_r = μ_s — mass cancels out. All bodies with the same μ_s have the same angle of repose.
  • ❌ 'Pulling a block at an angle reduces friction because the force is smaller.' ✅ Pulling upward reduces the normal (N = mg − F sin θ), which reduces friction. Pushing downward increases the normal and friction.
Practice
  • Solve 5 slope problems: determine if a body slides, and if so, find the acceleration.
  • Solve 5 horizontal-surface problems with pulling/pushing at an angle (find normal, friction, acceleration).
  • Solve 3 angle-of-repose problems showing θ_r = λ = tan⁻¹μ_s.
  • Solve 3 problems comparing static and kinetic friction (e.g., a block given an initial push).
  • Solve 2 problems where friction acts in the direction of motion (e.g., a block on a moving conveyor, walking).
Universal Facts
  • Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact — a counterintuitive but experimentally verified law.
  • μ_k < μ_s for most surface pairs — this is why objects jerk when starting to slide.
  • The angle of repose equals the angle of friction: θ_r = λ = tan⁻¹μ_s — a beautiful geometric connection.
  • Friction can act in the direction of motion — static friction is the force that accelerates you forward when walking.
  • Pulling a block at an angle reduces friction (by reducing N); pushing at an angle increases it (by increasing N).
Examples
  • Slope: A 5 kg block on a 30° slope (μ_s = 0.4). tan 30° = 0.577 > 0.4 → slides. a = g(sin30° − μ_k cos30°) = 9.8(0.5 − 0.4×0.866) = 1.51 m/s².
  • Horizontal pull: A 10 kg block (μ_k = 0.25) pulled by 40 N at 20° above horizontal. N = 98 − 40sin20° = 84.3 N; f = 21.1 N; F_net = 40cos20° − 21.1 = 16.5 N; a = 1.65 m/s².
  • Angle of repose: A block slides at constant speed down a 35° slope. μ_k = tan 35° = 0.70. μ_s is slightly higher (≈ 0.75).
  • Static friction variable: A 20 kg box (μ_s = 0.5). Push with 40 N — box doesn't move, f_s = 40 N. Push with 120 N — box moves (max f_s = 98 N).
  • Pulling vs pushing: A 15 kg block (μ_k = 0.3). Pull at 25°: N = 147 − Fsin25°. Push at 25°: N = 147 + Fsin25°. Pulling gives less friction.

Formulas
  • Maximum static friction: f_s(max) = μ_sN (acts just before slipping)
  • Kinetic friction: f_k = μ_kN (acts during sliding; μ_k < μ_s)
  • Slope sliding condition: body slides when tan θ > μ_s; a = g(sin θ − μ_k cos θ)
  • Angle of friction: tan λ = μ_s (angle between normal and resultant reaction)
  • Angle of repose: tan θ_r = μ_s → θ_r = λ (steepest angle for rest)

Summary

Friction is a contact force opposing relative motion. Static friction (variable, up to μ_sN) acts before sliding; kinetic friction (constant, μ_kN) acts during sliding, with μ_k < μ_s. The angle of repose equals the angle of friction (tan⁻¹μ_s). Slope problems, pulling/pushing at angles, and the distinction between static and kinetic friction are the key NEB topics.

  • The angle of repose depends only on μ_s, not on the mass of the body.
  • NEB frequently asks: 'Does the body slide? If so, find the acceleration' — check tan θ vs μ_s first.

Specific Conditions
  • Static vs. Kinetic Threshold: The maximum static friction fsmax​=μs​N is the upper bound for static friction. Static friction is a variable force that matches the applied force up to this limit. Kinetic friction fk​=μk​N applies only when sliding occurs, with μk​<μs​.
  • Normal Force Dependency: Friction is proportional to the normal reaction, not the weight. Any vertical force component (pulling up, pushing down, incline) changes N, which directly changes the friction force. Always compute N first.
  • Area Independence (Ideal Model): The laws of friction assume friction is independent of the apparent contact area (Coulomb's model). This holds well for dry, rigid surfaces but breaks down for soft materials, lubricated contacts, or adhesive forces where real contact area matters.

  • Friction: the force that opposes relative motion (or impending motion) between two surfaces in contact. It arises from microscopic irregularities and molecular adhesion between surfaces.
  • Coefficient of friction μ: μ=RF​, where F is the limiting frictional force and R is the normal reaction. μ is dimensionless — it is a ratio of two forces.
  • Laws of friction: (1) Friction acts opposite to the direction of impending motion. (2) Limiting friction Fs​=μs​R, proportional to the normal reaction. (3) Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact (for a given normal force). (4) Friction depends on the nature and condition of the surfaces in contact.
  • Static vs kinetic friction: static friction adjusts up to a maximum Fs,max​=μs​R; kinetic friction is approximately constant at Fk​=μk​R, where typically μs​>μk​. This is why it is harder to start moving an object than to keep it moving.
  • Angle of friction λ: the angle between the resultant contact force and the normal reaction when limiting friction is reached. tanλ=μs​. When the incline angle equals the angle of friction, the body is on the point of sliding.
  • Angle of repose: the maximum angle of an incline at which a body just rests on the plane without sliding. It equals the angle of friction: θrepose​=λ=tan−1μs​.
  • Verification of area independence: experiments show that for a given normal force, doubling the contact area halves the pressure but leaves the frictional force unchanged because the increased number of molecular interactions compensates.
  • Rolling friction: much smaller than sliding friction because rolling deforms surfaces less and the contact time per point is shorter. Ball bearings use this principle to reduce friction.
  • Units check: μ=F/R — both numerator and denominator are forces, so μ is dimensionless. Friction force F=μR has units of newtons.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'Friction always opposes motion.' ✅ Friction opposes RELATIVE motion (or its tendency). Static friction can act IN the direction of motion — e.g., the static friction on your feet pushes you forward when walking.
  • ❌ 'Friction depends on the area of contact.' ✅ For a given normal force, friction is (approximately) independent of area. A wide tyre and a narrow tyre with the same load have the same maximum friction (in the simple model).
  • ❌ 'Static friction is always μ_sN.' ✅ Static friction is VARIABLE: it matches the applied force up to μ_sN. Only at the point of slipping does f_s = μ_sN.
  • ❌ 'μ_k > μ_s because sliding is faster.' ✅ μ_k < μ_s — it is harder to start sliding than to keep sliding. This is why a heavy box jerks when you first push it.
  • ❌ 'The angle of repose depends on the mass of the body.' ✅ tan θ_r = μ_s — mass cancels out. All bodies with the same μ_s have the same angle of repose.
  • ❌ 'Pulling a block at an angle reduces friction because the force is smaller.' ✅ Pulling upward reduces the normal (N = mg − F sin θ), which reduces friction. Pushing downward increases the normal and friction.
  • Friction is NOT always opposite to the direction of motion — it is opposite to the direction of relative motion (or impending relative motion) between the two surfaces. For example, when you walk, friction on your foot points forward (in the direction of motion), preventing your foot from slipping backward.
  • The friction force is NOT always μR. This formula gives the LIMITING (maximum static) friction. For static friction, fs​≤μs​R, and the actual value adjusts to match the applied force up to the maximum.
  • Friction being independent of area is an approximation that holds for rigid bodies with moderate contact pressures. For very soft materials (like rubber tyres) or very high pressures, the relationship becomes more complex.
  • μs​>μk​ is generally true but not universal. Some materials (like Teflon on Teflon) have nearly equal coefficients, and certain combinations can even have μk​>μs​ under specific conditions.
  • The normal reaction R is NOT always equal to mg. On an inclined plane, R=mgcosθ. If an additional vertical force is applied, R changes accordingly. Always determine R from force equilibrium in the direction perpendicular to the surface.

Universal Facts

  • Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact — a counterintuitive but experimentally verified law.
  • μ_k < μ_s for most surface pairs — this is why objects jerk when starting to slide.
  • The angle of repose equals the angle of friction: θ_r = λ = tan⁻¹μ_s — a beautiful geometric connection.
  • Friction can act in the direction of motion — static friction is the force that accelerates you forward when walking.
  • Pulling a block at an angle reduces friction (by reducing N); pushing at an angle increases it (by increasing N).
  • answer pattern for "Solid friction: laws of solid friction and their verifica…": define → method → result. Structure earns the marks on 6–10 mark questions.
  • The laws of friction were first systematically studied by Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519), but they were rediscovered and formalized by Guillaume Amontons in 1699 and further refined by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in 1785.
  • The coefficient of friction for rubber on dry concrete is about 1.0, while for ice on steel it is only about 0.03 — a factor of 33 difference, explaining why ice skating is possible.
  • Friction generates heat: the work done against friction is converted into thermal energy. This is why brakes get hot and why meteoroids burn up in the atmosphere.
  • In ideal mathematical models, friction is treated as independent of contact area and sliding speed. In reality, both factors can affect friction, especially for rubber, polymers, and at very high or very low speeds.
  • The friction force can do positive, negative, or zero work depending on the reference frame. In the frame of the surface, friction on a sliding object does negative work; in the frame of a moving conveyor belt, friction can do positive work on an object placed on it.
