Summary

Solid friction opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact. The laws state that friction is independent of apparent area, proportional to normal reaction, and depends on surface nature. Static friction exceeds kinetic friction. Verified experimentally using inclined plane and horizontal surface methods.

Notes

  • Definition of Solid Friction: Force opposing relative motion (or tendency of motion) between two solid surfaces in contact. Acts tangentially at the interface. Always opposes actual or impending motion.
  • Types of Solid Friction: (1) Static friction — acts when bodies are at rest relative to each other; self-adjusting up to a maximum value f_s(max) = μ_s N. (2) Kinetic (dynamic) friction — acts during sliding; constant magnitude f_k = μ_k N. (3) Rolling friction — much smaller than sliding friction.
  • Law 1 — Independence of Apparent Area: The force of friction is independent of the apparent area of contact, provided the normal reaction remains the same. A brick lying flat or on its edge experiences the same frictional force if weight is unchanged.
  • Law 2 — Proportionality to Normal Reaction: The limiting frictional force is directly proportional to the normal reaction between the surfaces: f_max = μN, where μ is the coefficient of friction. This defines μ as the ratio f_max/N.
  • Law 3 — Dependence on Nature of Surfaces: Friction depends on the materials and roughness of the contacting surfaces. Smooth glass-on-glass has low μ; rubber-on-concrete has high μ. Lubrication reduces μ dramatically.
  • Law 4 — Static vs Kinetic: Limiting static friction is greater than kinetic friction (μ_s > μ_k). Once motion begins, less force is needed to maintain it. This explains why starting a heavy object requires more effort than keeping it moving.
  • Law 5 — Direction of Friction: Friction always acts opposite to the direction of relative motion (kinetic) or impending motion (static). It is a passive force — it arises only in response to an applied force.
  • Verification by Horizontal Surface Method: Place a block on a horizontal table. Attach a spring balance horizontally. Pull gradually until the block just starts moving. Record the reading (= limiting friction f_max). Repeat with different weights on the block. Plot f_max vs N — straight line through origin confirms f ∝ N. Slope gives μ.
  • Verification by Inclined Plane Method: Place a block on an adjustable inclined plane. Gradually increase the angle θ until the block just begins to slide. At this critical angle (angle of repose), tan θ = μ. Measure θ for different surface combinations. Confirms μ depends on surface nature, not area or mass.
  • Coefficient of Friction Values: Steel on steel (dry): μ ≈ 0.6; wood on wood: μ ≈ 0.3–0.5; ice on ice: μ ≈ 0.03; rubber on dry concrete: μ ≈ 0.8; lubricated metal: μ ≈ 0.05.

Specific Conditions

  • Normal Reaction Must Be Constant: Laws hold only when comparing surfaces under the same normal load. Changing N changes f proportionally but μ remains constant for given materials.
  • Dry Surfaces Assumed: Standard laws apply to dry (Coulomb) friction. Wet or lubricated surfaces follow fluid friction rules where viscosity matters.
  • Low Speeds: Kinetic friction is approximately constant at low speeds. At very high speeds, heating and surface deformation change μ.
  • Angle of Repose Equals Friction Angle: On an inclined plane, the angle at which sliding begins equals arctan(μ). This provides a direct experimental measure of μ without measuring forces.

  • Kinetic friction: fk​=μk​N
  • Coefficient of friction: μ=f/N

Key Points

  • Friction is independent of apparent contact area.
  • Limiting friction ∝ normal reaction (f_max = μN).
  • μ depends only on material pair and surface condition.
  • Static friction > kinetic friction (μ_s > μ_k).
  • Angle of repose θ satisfies tan θ = μ.
  • Friction always opposes relative or impending motion.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Limiting friction is directly proportional to the normal reaction between surfaces.
  • Statement 2: Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact for a given normal load.
  • Statement 3: Coefficient of friction depends solely on the nature and roughness of contacting surfaces.
  • Statement 4: Limiting static friction exceeds kinetic friction for the same pair of surfaces.
  • Statement 5: Angle of repose equals the angle whose tangent is the coefficient of static friction.

Important Notes

  • μ is dimensionless — it is a ratio of two forces.
  • Real contact area is microscopic; friction arises from molecular adhesion and interlocking asperities.
  • Rolling friction coefficient is typically 100× smaller than sliding friction.
  • In NEB exams, verification experiments (horizontal pull and inclined plane) are frequently asked as long-answer questions.

Exam Short Tricks

  • f ∝ N → plot f vs N → slope = μ.
  • tan(angle of repose) = μ — no force measurement needed.
  • Area doesn't matter — doubling area with same weight gives same friction.
  • μ_s > μ_k — harder to start than to keep moving.
  • On incline: component of weight parallel = mg sin θ drives motion; friction = μ mg cos θ resists.

Exam Notes

  • NEB asks to describe verification of laws using either horizontal or inclined plane method with diagram.
  • Numerical problems typically give μ and ask for acceleration, stopping distance, or minimum force.
  • Distinguish clearly between static (self-adjusting ≤ μ_s N) and kinetic (constant = μ_k N) friction.
  • Always draw free-body diagram showing normal reaction, weight components, applied force, and friction direction.

Examples

  • Block (5 kg) on horizontal table, μ = 0.4. Limiting friction = 0.4 × 5 × 9.8 = 19.6 N. Applied force of 15 N produces no motion (static friction adjusts to 15 N). Applied force of 25 N causes acceleration: a = (25 − 0.4×5×9.8)/5 = (25−19.6)/5 = 1.08 m/s².
  • Inclined plane: Block slides at θ = 30°. μ = tan 30° = 0.577. Independent of block mass.
  • Car braking: μ = 0.7 on dry road. Maximum deceleration = μg = 0.7 × 9.8 ≈ 6.86 m/s². Stopping distance from 20 m/s: v²/(2μg) = 400/(2×6.86) ≈ 29 m.

Universal Facts

  • Amontons formulated the first two laws of friction in 1699; Coulomb added the distinction between static and kinetic friction in 1785.
  • Friction converts mechanical energy into heat — brakes get hot because of this.
  • Without friction, walking, driving, and holding objects would be impossible.
  • Gecko feet use van der Waals forces (not Coulomb friction) to climb smooth vertical surfaces.

Confusion

  • Thinking friction depends on contact area: It does NOT for dry friction — only normal reaction matters.
  • Confusing μ_s and μ_k: μ_s applies to stationary/impending motion; μ_k applies during sliding. μ_s ≥ μ_k always.
  • Believing friction always opposes motion: Static friction can act in the direction of motion (e.g., car tire pushing forward on road).
  • Assuming μ > 1 is impossible: μ can exceed 1 for very sticky surfaces (e.g., silicone rubber).
  • Forgetting friction is passive: It only exists in response to an applied force or tendency to move.
