Notes

  • Gauss's Law: Total electric flux through any closed Gaussian surface equals enclosed charge divided by ε0​: ΦE​=∮E⋅dA=Qenc​/ε0​.
  • Charged Spherical Shell (conducting): Outside (r>R): E=kQ/r2 (as if all charge at center). Inside (r<R): E=0. On surface (r=R): E=kQ/R2 (maximum).
  • Solid Insulating Sphere (uniform charge density): Outside (r>R): E=kQ/r2. Inside (r<R): E=kQr/R3=ρr/(3ε0​) (linear in r, zero at center).
  • Infinite Line Charge: Linear charge density λ. Cylindrical Gaussian surface gives E=λ/(2πε0​r)=2kλ/r. Field falls as 1/r, directed radially outward for λ>0.
  • Infinite Plane Sheet of Charge: Surface charge density σ. Pillbox Gaussian surface gives E=σ/(2ε0​) on each side — uniform, independent of distance.
  • Charged Conducting Plane/Surface: Just outside conductor surface: E=σ/ε0​ (twice the sheet value because charge resides on both surfaces of isolated sheet, but conductor has charge only on outer surface). Inside conductor: E=0.
  • Gaussian Surface Selection: Choose surface where E is either parallel or perpendicular to dA and ∣E∣ is constant on flux-contributing parts.

Specific Conditions

  • Symmetry Required: Gauss's law is always true but practically useful only for spherical, cylindrical, or planar symmetry where E is constant over the Gaussian surface.
  • Conductor vs Insulator: For conducting sphere, charge resides on surface → E = 0 inside. For insulating sphere with uniform volume charge, E grows linearly from center.
  • Infinite Approximation: Line charge formula E=λ/(2πε0​r) assumes infinite length — valid near middle of long wire, not near ends.

Formulas

  • Gauss's law: ∮E⋅dA=Qenc​/ε0​
  • Spherical shell outside: E=4πε0​1​r2Q​
  • Spherical shell inside: E=0
  • Solid insulating sphere inside: E=3ε0​ρr​=4πε0​1​R3Qr​
  • Solid insulating sphere outside: E=4πε0​1​r2Q​
  • Infinite line charge: E=2πε0​rλ​=r2kλ​
  • Infinite plane sheet: E=2ε0​σ​
  • Conductor surface: E=ε0​σ​

Key Points

  • Gauss's law: flux through closed surface = Qenc​/ε0​.
  • Charged conducting shell: E=0 inside, E=kQ/r2 outside.
  • Solid insulating sphere: E∝r inside, E∝1/r2 outside.
  • Infinite line charge: E∝1/r (not 1/r2).
  • Infinite plane sheet: E=σ/(2ε0​) — uniform, independent of distance.
  • Conductor surface just outside: E=σ/ε0​.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Gauss's law states ∮E⋅dA=Qenc​/ε0​ for any closed surface.
  • Statement 2: For a charged conducting spherical shell, electric field is zero inside and kQ/r2 outside.
  • Statement 3: For a uniformly charged solid insulating sphere, field inside varies linearly with distance from center.
  • Statement 4: Electric field due to infinite line charge falls as 1/r: E=λ/(2πε0​r).
  • Statement 5: Electric field due to infinite plane sheet is uniform: E=σ/(2ε0​), independent of distance.

Important Notes

  • Gauss's law is always valid but computationally useful only with high symmetry (spherical, cylindrical, planar).
  • For conductor, all excess charge moves to outer surface; E = 0 inside conducting material.
  • The 1/r dependence for line charge differs from point charge's 1/r2 — dimensionality matters.
  • Plane sheet field is constant regardless of distance — an idealization for infinite sheets.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Conducting shell: E = 0 inside, kQ/r² outside — treat as point charge at center outside.
  • Line charge: E ∝ 1/r (cylindrical symmetry), not 1/r².
  • Plane sheet: E is constant — same near or far from sheet.
  • Solid sphere inside: E = kQr/R³ — linear, zero at center, max at surface.
  • Gaussian surface must match symmetry of charge distribution.

Exam Notes

  • NEB frequently asks to derive E for spherical shell, solid sphere, line charge, and plane sheet using Gauss's law.
  • Distinguish conducting vs insulating sphere — crucial difference in field inside.
  • Draw Gaussian surface and show flux calculation clearly for derivation questions.
  • Remember: field just outside conductor surface is σ/ε0​, not σ/(2ε0​).

Examples

  • A conducting sphere of radius 10 cm carries charge +5μC. Field at 20 cm from center: E=kQ/r2=(9×109)(5×10−6)/0.04=1.125×106 N/C. Inside: E = 0.
  • A long wire has λ=10μC/m. Field at 5 cm from wire: E=λ/(2πε0​r)=2kλ/r=2(9×109)(10×10−6)/0.05=3.6×106 N/C.
  • A large plane sheet has σ=5μC/m2. Field near sheet: E=σ/(2ε0​)=(5×10−6)/(2×8.85×10−12)=2.82×105 N/C.
  • Solid insulating sphere R = 15 cm, uniform charge Q = 10μC. Field at r = 5 cm (inside): E=kQr/R3=(9×109)(10×10−6)(0.05)/(0.15)3=1.33×105 N/C.

Universal Facts

  • Gauss's law is one of Maxwell's four equations — fundamental to classical electromagnetism.
  • The fact that E = 0 inside a conductor enables electrostatic shielding (Faraday cage).
  • The inverse-square law for point charges is mathematically equivalent to Gauss's law — if Coulomb's law were not exactly 1/r², Gauss's law would fail.
  • Gravitational field obeys an analogous Gauss's law: ∮g​⋅dA=−4πGMenc​.
  • Gauss's law works for any closed surface regardless of shape — only enclosed charge matters for total flux.

Confusion

  • Thinking E = 0 inside shell means no field anywhere inside: E = 0 only inside the conducting material and hollow cavity; field exists outside.
  • Believing line charge field follows 1/r² like point charge: Line charge has cylindrical symmetry → E ∝ 1/r.
  • Confusing plane sheet (σ/2ε0​) with conductor surface (σ/ε0​): Factor of 2 difference due to charge distribution on one vs both surfaces.
  • Assuming Gauss's law can always be used to find E: Only works with sufficient symmetry to pull E outside the integral.
  • Thinking enclosed charge determines local E at surface: Total flux depends on Q_enc, but local E includes contributions from all charges.

Summary

Gauss's law (∮E⋅dA=Qenc​/ε0​) gives electric field for symmetric charge distributions: conducting shell (E = 0 inside, kQ/r² outside), solid insulating sphere (E ∝ r inside), infinite line charge (E ∝ 1/r), and infinite plane sheet (E constant). Conducting surface field is σ/ε0​.
