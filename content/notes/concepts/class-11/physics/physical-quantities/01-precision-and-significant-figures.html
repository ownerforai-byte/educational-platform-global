Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Measurement Is Where Physics Begins

    Physics is a quantitative science: every law is a statement about measured quantities. This topic fixes the vocabulary of measurement — SI base units, accuracy vs precision, significant figures, scientific notation, and the errors that limit every measurement. NEB asks it as short definitional questions and small numericals.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    TermDefinition
    Physical quantityA property that can be measured, expressed as a number times a unit
    Fundamental (base) quantitiesSeven independent quantities: length (m), mass (kg), time (s), electric current (A), thermodynamic temperature (K), amount of substance (mol), luminous intensity (cd)
    Derived quantitiesBuilt from base quantities: velocity (m/s), force (kg·m/s² = N), etc.
    AccuracyHow close a measurement is to the true value
    PrecisionHow close repeated measurements are to each other (and the smallest step the instrument resolves)
    ErrorThe difference between the measured value and the true value

    Accuracy vs precision (NEB 2024): a shooter hitting near the bull's-eye (accurate) is different from hitting the same off-centre spot every time (precise but inaccurate). Good measurement needs both.

  • 3. Significant Figures — The Rules

    Significant figures are the digits in a number that carry meaning — all certain digits plus one uncertain digit.

    RuleExample
    All non-zero digits are significant247.5 → 4 s.f.
    Zeros between non-zero digits are significant2004 → 4 s.f.
    Leading zeros are not significant0.00320 → 3 s.f. (3, 2, 0)
    Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant2.300 → 4 s.f.
    Trailing zeros without a decimal point are ambiguous — use scientific notation1200 → write 1.2×103 (2 s.f.) or 1.200×103 (4 s.f.)
    Exact counts (2 apples, 100 in a definition) have unlimited s.f.—

    NEB 2014: 0.00320 m has 3 significant figures — the leading zeros only locate the decimal point; the trailing zero after the 2 is significant.

    3.1 Rounding rules

    • Digit to drop < 5 → drop (2.34 → 2.3); > 5 → round up (2.36 → 2.4); exactly 5 → round so the last kept digit is even (2.35 → 2.4, 2.45 → 2.4).

    3.2 Arithmetic with significant figures

    OperationRule
    Multiplication / divisionResult keeps the fewest significant figures of any factor: 4.2×1.31=5.5 (2 s.f.)
    Addition / subtractionResult keeps the fewest decimal places: 12.11+1.2=13.3

  • 4. Errors of Measurement

    Error typeDefinitionExpression
    Absolute errorDifference between a measurement and the mean (or true) valueΔa=∣ai​−aˉ∣
    Relative errorAbsolute error divided by the mean valueΔa/aˉ
    Percentage errorRelative error × 100(Δa/aˉ)×100%
    Systematic errorRepeats in one direction — faulty zero, calibration, parallaxReduce by calibration and better technique
    Random errorScatters both ways — fluctuationsReduce by repeating and averaging
    Least count errorHalf the smallest division of the instrumentInstrument-limited

    4.1 Propagation of errors

    OperationError rule
    Sum/difference Z=A±BΔZ=ΔA+ΔB (absolute errors add)
    Product Z=ABΔZ/Z=ΔA/A+ΔB/B (relative errors add)
    Quotient Z=A/Bsame as product
    Power Z=AnΔZ/Z=nΔA/A

    Worked numerical (NEB 2022 style): How many seconds in a leap year (366 days)? 366×24×3600=3.16×107 s. (366 × 24 = 8,784 h; 8,784 × 3,600 = 31,622,400 s ≈ 3.16×107 s.)

    Worked numerical: The length of a rod is measured as 25.0 cm with least count 0.1 cm. Express the percentage error. Percentage error = (0.1/25.0) × 100% = 0.4%; the length is recorded as 25.0 ± 0.1 cm.

    6.2 Model answers

    Q1 (2m, 2024) — Accuracy vs precision with example. Answer: Accuracy is how close a measurement lies to the true value; precision is how closely repeated measurements agree with one another (and how fine the instrument's least count is). Example: if the true length of a rod is 25.00 cm, readings of 24.98, 24.99 and 25.01 cm are both accurate and precise; readings of 26.10, 26.11 and 26.09 cm are precise but not accurate (a systematic error); readings of 24.5, 26.0 and 25.4 cm are neither.

    Q2 (2m, 2014) — Significant figures; count in 0.00320 m. Answer: Significant figures are the digits in a measured quantity that are reliably known — all certain digits plus one uncertain (estimated) digit. In 0.00320 m the leading zeros only fix the decimal point and are not significant; the digits 3, 2 and the trailing 0 are significant — so it has 3 significant figures.

    Q3 (1m, 2022) — Seconds in a leap year. Answer: 366 days × 24 h/day × 3,600 s/h = 31,622,400 s ≈ 3.16×107 s.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Counting leading zeros as significant (0.00320 has 3, not 5).
    2. Confusing accuracy with precision — accuracy needs the true value; precision needs only repeatability.
    3. Keeping too many digits in a numerical answer — round to the least precise factor.
    4. Writing percentage error without units of the quantity or without the ± notation.
    5. Mixing up systematic (one-directional) and random (scattering) errors.

Confusion

  • Accuracy vs precision: accuracy compares with the true value; precision compares repeated measurements with each other — a precise instrument can be inaccurate if mis-calibrated.
  • Least count vs error: the least count is the smallest step the instrument can read; the least-count error is half of it.

Practice

  • Why do we repeat measurements and average them?

Universal Facts

  • The kilogram was the last SI base unit defined by a physical object (the platinum-iridium cylinder in Paris) until 2019, when it was redefined via Planck's constant.

Practice Questions

  • Numerical (2m): A wire's diameter is measured as 0.52 mm with least count 0.01 mm. Express the result with its percentage error. — Diameter = 0.52 ± 0.01 mm; percentage error = (0.01/0.52) × 100% ≈ 1.9%. The uncertainty comes from the least count of the instrument, and the answer keeps two significant figures consistent with the measurement.

Formulas

    • Percentage error = (absolute error / mean value) × 100%
    • Product/quotient: ΔZ/Z = ΔA/A + ΔB/B
    • Power: Z = Aⁿ → ΔZ/Z = n·ΔA/A
    • Sum/difference: ΔZ = ΔA + ΔB

Key Points

  • Seven SI base units: m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd; derived units built from them.
  • Significant figures: leading zeros never significant; trailing zeros after a decimal point are; 0.00320 m → 3 s.f. (NEB 2014).
  • Arithmetic rules: ×/÷ keeps fewest s.f.; +/− keeps fewest decimal places.
  • Errors: absolute, relative, percentage; systematic vs random; propagation — powers multiply the relative error by n.

Summary

Measurement is the foundation of physics: seven SI base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd) generate every derived unit. Accuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is agreement among repeats — an instrument can be precise yet inaccurate. Significant figures carry the reliability of a number: leading zeros never count (0.00320 m has 3 s.f.), trailing zeros after a decimal point do; multiplication keeps the fewest significant figures, addition the fewest decimal places. Errors come as absolute, relative and percentage, split into systematic (one-directional, fixable by calibration) and random (scattering, reduced by averaging), and propagate through calculations — powers multiply relative error by the exponent. Every measured answer must end rounded, united and honest about its uncertainty.

Special Notes

  • Note 1: Pure numbers (2, π, counted quantities) have unlimited significant figures and never limit the result.
  • Note 2: In NEB numericals, always carry one extra guard digit through the working and round only the FINAL answer.
  • Note 3: Angular measure in radians is dimensionless but its precision still limits results in formulas like s=rθ.
  • Note 4: Instruments: vernier callipers (0.01 cm) and screw gauge (0.001 cm) reduce least count — the practical way to gain significant figures.

Important Statements

  • "A measurement without an uncertainty is an opinion with a decimal point."

Important Concepts

  • Every measurement carries an uncertainty: stating 25.0 ± 0.1 cm is more honest and more examinable than 25 cm alone.

Important Tasks

  • Task 1: Measure the diameter of a coin 5 times with vernier callipers and report it as d ± Δd with the correct significant figures.
  • Task 2: Using stop-watch timing of 20 oscillations, compute g = 4π²L/T² and its percentage uncertainty.
  • Task 3: Convert the following to 3 significant figures in scientific notation: 0.004560, 123,456, 7,500.
  • Task 4: Solve the past-year NEB question: 'The percentage errors in mass and velocity are 2% and 3%. Find the maximum percentage error in kinetic energy.' (Answer: 2% + 2×3% = 8%.)
  • Task 5: Write a one-page summary comparing accuracy, precision, least count and resolution with one instrument example for each.

Specific Conditions

  • Intermediate Calculations: Keep at least one extra 'guard' digit during intermediate calculation steps. Only round the final answer to the required number of significant figures to avoid compounding rounding errors.
  • Constants & Conversion Factors: Treat mathematical constants (e.g., π, √2) and exact conversion factors (e.g., 100 cm = 1 m) as having infinite significant figures. They should never limit the precision of the measured data.
  • Parallax Error: For analog measurements (metre rules, thermometers), ensure the line of sight is perpendicular to the scale; this is a necessary physical condition that must be met before determining the significant figure precision of the measurement.
