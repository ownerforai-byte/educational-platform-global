Notes

  • Accuracy vs Precision: Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other. A watch running 5 minutes fast can still be precise (consistent) but inaccurate. In physics we must report BOTH.
  • Significant figures are the digits that carry meaning about precision. Rules: (1) All non-zero digits are significant — 24.7 has 3 s.f. (2) Zeros between non-zero digits are significant — 2005 has 4 s.f. (3) Leading zeros are NOT significant — 0.0052 has 2 s.f. (4) Trailing zeros AFTER a decimal point ARE significant — 2.300 has 4 s.f. (5) Trailing zeros in a whole number without a decimal point are ambiguous — 500 may have 1, 2 or 3 s.f.; write 5.0×102 to remove ambiguity.
  • Why significant figures matter: every measurement has an uncertainty limited by the instrument's least count. Reporting T=2.034567 s measured with a stopwatch (least count 0.01 s) falsely claims precision. Round only to figures the instrument can justify.
  • Rounding rules: digit dropped < 5 → leave last digit (2.34 → 2.3); digit dropped > 5, or 5 followed by non-zeros → round up (2.36 → 2.4; 2.351 → 2.4); digit dropped exactly 5 with nothing after → round to the nearest EVEN digit (2.45 → 2.4, 2.35 → 2.4), which prevents systematic bias.
  • Multiplication and division: the result has as many significant figures as the factor with the FEWEST significant figures. Example: 0.6212.5×3.0​ → 3.0 has 2 s.f. → answer 60 (2 s.f.), not 60.48.
  • Addition and subtraction: the result is rounded to the same number of DECIMAL PLACES as the term with the fewest decimal places. Example: 12.11+1.2+0.452=13.762→13.8.
  • Scientific notation and prefixes: write measurements as a×10n with 1≤a<10; SI prefixes: nano (10−9), micro (10−6), milli (10−3), centi (10−2), kilo (103), mega (106), giga (109). 4.50×103 m unambiguously has 3 s.f.
  • Least count and uncertainty: metre rule 0.1 cm, vernier callipers 0.01 cm, screw gauge 0.001 cm. Quote readings to the least count; uncertainty is usually ± half the least count. Percentage uncertainty = xΔx​×100%.
  • Propagation of uncertainty: Z=A+B: ΔZ=ΔA+ΔB. Z=AB or A/B: ZΔZ​=AΔA​+BΔB​. Z=An: ZΔZ​=nAΔA​ — powers amplify relative error, which is why L must be measured carefully in T=2πL/g​.
  • NEB exam focus: count significant figures; round products/quotients/sums correctly; explain why trailing zeros matter; compute percentage error of a derived quantity; distinguish accuracy from precision using a target-shooting example.

  • Precision and significant figures

    1. Introduction

    Every physics result is a number with a unit — and every number carries the fingerprint of the instrument that produced it. A metre ruler cannot give millimetres; a vernier caliper reads to 0.01 cm; a screw gauge reaches 0.001 cm. Precision is how finely an instrument divides its scale; significant figures are how honestly we report what it told us. Writing extra digits is not extra knowledge — it is fiction.

    Nepal's labs live this daily: a +2 practical asks the diameter of a wire, and the examiner checks not just the digits but whether the zero error was subtracted and the answer rounded to the instrument's least count. A mistimed stopwatch (0.01 s) cannot justify g = 9.81 m/s² from a 1-second swing — the topic teaches exactly where the honesty boundary lies.

    What you will learn: (i) physical quantities, units and the SI base set; (ii) precision vs accuracy; (iii) least count of vernier caliper and screw gauge — derived, not memorised; (iv) zero errors and corrections; (v) significant figures: rules, rounding, arithmetic; (vi) errors: absolute, relative, percentage and their combination.

    Learning outcomes. You can: take a vernier and a screw-gauge reading with zero correction; derive both least-count formulae; round any result to correct significant figures; and combine errors through +, −, ×, ÷ and powers.

  • 2. Concepts and laws

    Physical quantities, units and SI

    1. Physical quantity = number × unit (e.g., 5.20 cm: magnitude 5.20, unit cm). A bare number is not a measurement.
    2. Fundamental vs derived. Length, mass, time, current, temperature, amount, luminous intensity are fundamental (SI base units: m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd — redefined 2019 via exact constants: c, h, e, N_A, k_B); everything else (velocity, force, energy) is derived from them.
    3. Assumptions box (used all topic). Scales are linear and calibrated; temperature effects on instruments ignored unless stated; zero errors are systematic (same sign every trial) while random errors scatter.

    Precision vs accuracy

    1. Precision = fineness/reproducibility of readings (small least count, clustered repeats). Accuracy = closeness to the true value.
    2. The target analogy. Four darts clustered off-centre: precise but inaccurate (systematic error — e.g., uncorrected zero error). Scattered around bullseye: accurate on average but imprecise. Clustered on bullseye: both — the only acceptable physics result.
    3. Validity condition. High precision never cures systematic error: a biased instrument repeats its lie faithfully. Accuracy needs calibration; precision needs a fine scale.

    Least count — derived, with zero error

    1. Least count (LC) = the smallest value an instrument measures = 1 main-scale division ÷ divisions on the vernier/circular scale.
    2. Derivation (vernier). Let 1 MSD = 1 mm and n VSD coincide with (n−1) MSD. Then 1 VSD = (n−1)/n MSD, so LC = 1 MSD − 1 VSD = MSD/n. For n = 10: LC = 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm. Reading = MSR + n_coincide × LC.
    3. Derivation (screw gauge). Pitch = distance moved per full rotation (usually 1 mm). With N circular divisions, LC = pitch/N (N = 100 → LC = 0.01 mm = 0.001 cm). Reading = PSR + HSR × LC ± zero correction.
    4. Zero error. Vernier: positive if vernier zero lies right of main zero when closed (add +x×LC? subtract: correction = −error). Screw gauge: positive if circular zero lies below the reference line (subtract it); negative if above (add it). Rule: observed − zero error (with sign).
    5. Significant figures. All certain digits + one doubtful digit. Rules: non-zero digits count; trapped zeros count; leading zeros never count; trailing zeros count only after a decimal point (or with explicit bar/notation). Exact counts (12 eggs, π in a definition) have infinite sig figs.
    6. Rounding. Look at the first dropped digit: >5 round up; <5 drop; =5 followed by non-zero round up; =5 exactly → round the keeper to even (banker's rule — NEB accepts "round up on 5" in school answers; state your convention).
    7. Arithmetic. Addition/subtraction: result to the least decimal places of any term (12.5 + 3.42 = 15.9). Multiplication/division: result to the least significant figures of any factor (4.0 × 3.142 = 12.6). Powers/roots keep the operand's sig figs.
    8. Errors. Absolute Δx (same unit), relative Δx/x, percentage (Δx/x)×100%. Combinations: for x = a±b add absolute (Δx = Δa+Δb); for x = ab or a/b add fractional (Δx/x = Δa/a+Δb/b); for x = a^n multiply by |n| (Δx/x = n·Δa/a). Derived: percentage error of density ρ = m/(πr²l) sums all four fractional errors, radius counted twice.

  • 5. Worked numericals (Given → Formula → Substitution → Answer with unit)

    1. Vernier reading. MSR = 2.4 cm, 7th vernier division coincides, LC = 0.01 cm, zero error nil. Reading = 2.4 + 7×0.01 = 2.47 cm (3 sig figs — the instrument's honesty limit).
    2. Screw gauge with zero error. PSR = 3.0 mm, HSR = 35, LC = 0.01 mm; circular zero is 4 divisions below the line (+0.04 mm error). Observed = 3.0 + 35×0.01 = 3.35 mm. Corrected = 3.35 − 0.04 = 3.31 mm.
    3. Negative zero error. Observed 5.62 mm, zero error −0.03 mm (zero above line). Corrected = 5.62 − (−0.03) = 5.65 mm. Sign discipline is the whole mark.
    4. Sig-fig arithmetic. (a) 12.5 + 3.42 + 0.625 = 16.545 → 16.5 (one decimal place). (b) 4.0 × 3.142 = 12.568 → 12.6 (two sig figs — the 4.0 rules).
    5. Density with error propagation. m = (50.0 ± 0.1) g, r = (2.00 ± 0.01) cm, l = (10.0 ± 0.1) cm; ρ = m/(πr²l). Δρ/ρ = 0.1/50 + 2×(0.01/2.00) + 0.1/10 = 0.002+0.01+0.01 = 0.022 → ±2.2%; radius dominates because of the square — always attack the power term first.
    6. Pendulum g (preview of error honesty). l = (98.4 ± 0.1) cm, T = (1.99 ± 0.01) s (average of 50 swings); g = 4π²l/T² = 4×9.8696×0.984/3.9601 = 9.80 m/s² (3 sig figs); Δg/g = 0.1/98.4 + 2×(0.01/1.99) ≈ 0.011 → ±1.1%. A 1-second single swing could never justify this — averaging is precision engineering.

  • 6. Worked examples and applications

    1. Buying wire in Kalimati. A shopkeeper's screw gauge reads 3.31 mm after zero correction — the difference between 3.31 and 3.35 is the difference between honest trade and dispute.
    2. NEB practical. Diameter of a bob by vernier (2 readings) and by screw gauge (3 readings): the screw gauge wins on precision; the report must state both LCs and the zero corrections.
    3. Stopwatch averaging. Timing 50 oscillations instead of 1 divides the reaction-time error by 50 — the cheapest precision upgrade in physics.
    4. Why calculators lie. 4.0 × 3.142 shows 12.568; physics writes 12.6. Every unrounded calculator display in an answer book is a lost mark.
    5. Parallax. Reading a burette or ammeter from the side shifts the value — eye normal to the scale is a precision habit, not fussiness.
    6. Significant money. A budget of Rs 12.5 lakh + Rs 3.42 lakh is reported as Rs 15.9 lakh — the addition rule runs national accounts too.

    7. Key terms, signs and traps

    • Physical quantity = magnitude × unit; no unit, no measurement.
    • Least count = 1 MSD/n (vernier); pitch/N (screw gauge).
    • Zero error is systematic: corrected reading = observed − error (with sign).
    • Precision (clustered, fine) vs accuracy (true); precision without calibration is organised fiction.
    • Significant figures = certain digits + one doubtful digit.
    • Absolute / relative / percentage error; combinations: ± adds absolute, ×/÷ adds fractional, powers multiply by n.
    • Traps: trailing zeros without a decimal point don't count (100 cm is ambiguous — write 1.00×10²); exact counts have infinite sig figs; backlash (never un-turn a screw gauge mid-reading); forgetting the factor 2 on r² in error sums.

    Common mistakes. Adding zero error instead of subtracting (or dropping its sign) · reporting calculator digits · counting leading zeros as significant · using backlash-affected readings · confusing precision with accuracy in theory answers.

Confusion

  • ❌ '0.00250 has 6 significant figures because it has 6 digits.' ✅ Leading zeros only locate the decimal point — 0.00250 has 3 s.f. (the 2, the 5, and the trailing zero after the decimal point).
  • ❌ '500 has 3 significant figures.' ✅ Without a decimal point, trailing zeros are ambiguous — 500 could be 1, 2 or 3 s.f. Write 5.00×102 if you mean 3 s.f.
  • ❌ Rounding 2.45 to 2.5 'because 5 always rounds up'. ✅ For an exact 5 with nothing after, round to the nearest EVEN digit: 2.45 → 2.4 (but 2.4501 → 2.5).
  • ❌ 'A precise measurement is always accurate.' ✅ Precision (repeatability) says nothing about correctness — a wrongly calibrated balance gives precise but systematically wrong (inaccurate) masses.
  • ❌ Keeping decimal places in multiplication: 2.0×3.00=6.00. ✅ In multiplication count significant figures (2 s.f. here) → 6.0; decimal-place counting applies only to addition/subtraction.
  • ❌ Reporting 12.11+1.2 as 13.31. ✅ The term 1.2 has only 1 decimal place → 13.3; the hundredths digit is already meaningless.
  • Zero error is subtracted WITH sign: minus a negative error adds.
  • Calculator digits are not significant figures: round before inking the answer.
  • Precision (clustered) without accuracy (true) is systematic error, not quality.

Universal Facts

  • The SI has seven base quantities: length (metre), mass (kilogram), time (second), electric current (ampere), temperature (kelvin), luminous intensity (candela) and amount of substance (mole). Since 2019 every base unit is defined by fixing a fundamental constant.
  • 'Significant' means reliable/meaningful, not mathematically important — instrument precision, not the calculator display, decides how many digits you may report.
  • The round-to-even convention is the same rule specified by the IEEE 754 floating-point standard used in all modern computers.
  • Laser ranging against reflectors left on the Moon by Apollo astronauts measures the Earth–Moon distance (384,400 km) to millimetre precision — one part in 1011 — achievable only through strict uncertainty discipline.

Examples

  • Example 1: A measured current is 0.0450 A — this has 3 s.f.; the trailing zero after 5 is significant because it is after a decimal point, telling us the ammeter resolved to 0.0001 A.
  • Example 2: Mass of a coin measured 5 times: 5.42 g, 5.40 g, 5.41 g, 5.42 g, 5.40 g — highly precise (spread only 0.02 g); if the true mass is 5.00 g the readings are precise but inaccurate (zero error).
  • Example 3: Area of a plate with sides 12.5 cm and 4.0 cm: A=50.0 cm² full value, but 4.0 has 2 s.f. → report A=50 cm² (2 s.f.).
  • Example 4: Time for 10 oscillations of a pendulum measured as 20.2 s with a stopwatch of least count 0.1 s → T=2.02 s; quoting T = 2.020 s would falsely claim precision beyond the stopwatch.
  • Example 5: Density from ρ=m/V with m=(7.58±0.01) g and V=(2.7±0.1) cm³: ρ=2.81 g/cm³, ρΔρ​=7.580.01​+2.70.1​≈3.8% → ρ≈(2.8±0.1) g/cm³.

  • Timing 50 swings divides reaction-time error by 50.
  • 12.5+3.42 lakh budget reported as 15.9 lakh — the addition rule in accounts.

Formulas

  • Percentage uncertainty: %uncertainty=xΔx​×100%
  • Sum/difference: ΔZ=ΔA+ΔB (absolute errors add)
  • Product/quotient: ZΔZ​=AΔA​+BΔB​ (relative errors add)
  • Power rule: Z=An⇒ZΔZ​=nAΔA​
  • Pendulum example: g=T24π2L​⇒gΔg​=LΔL​+2TΔT​

Key Points

  • Significant figures express the precision of a MEASUREMENT, not of a pure number — a counted quantity like '12 students' is exact.
  • Multiplication/division → fewest significant figures; addition/subtraction → fewest decimal places.
  • Leading zeros never count; captive zeros always count; trailing zeros count only after a decimal point.
  • Uncertainty of a reading is normally ± half the least count of the instrument.
  • Relative errors add in products and are multiplied by the power for An — highest-power quantities dominate error.
  • Scientific notation (a×10n, 1≤a<10) removes ambiguity about trailing zeros.
  • LC: vernier MSD/n; screw gauge pitch/N. Corrected = observed − zero error.
  • Significant figures: +/− by decimal places, ×/÷ by digit counts, one doubtful digit always.
  • Errors: ± adds absolute, ×/÷ adds fractional, powers multiply by n (r² counts twice).

Summary

Physics reports magnitude × unit, honestly. Precision (fine, repeatable) differs from accuracy (true); systematic zero errors are subtracted with sign. Vernier LC = MSD/n (typically 0.01 cm); screw-gauge LC = pitch/N (typically 0.001 cm). Significant figures keep one doubtful digit: addition follows decimal places, multiplication follows digit counts. Errors combine by adding absolute (±), fractional (×/÷), n-times for powers — the r² term usually dominates (density ±2.2% worked). Special Notes

  • Note 1: Pure numbers (2, π, counted quantities) have unlimited significant figures and never limit the result.
  • Note 2: In NEB numericals, always carry one extra guard digit through the working and round only the FINAL answer.
  • Note 3: Angular measure in radians is dimensionless but its precision still limits results in formulas like s=rθ.
  • Note 4: Instruments: vernier callipers (0.01 cm) and screw gauge (0.001 cm) reduce least count — the practical way to gain significant figures.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: A measurement is meaningful only when stated with its uncertainty — a bare number is an incomplete experimental result.
  • Statement 2: In multiplication and division, the result cannot have more significant figures than the least precise factor.
  • Statement 3: In addition and subtraction, the result cannot be more precise (more decimal places) than the least precise term.
  • Statement 4: Systematic errors shift all readings the same way (affecting accuracy); random errors scatter readings (affecting precision).

Important Notes

  • Note 1: Counted quantities and defined constants (π, 2, 100 cm = 1 m) are exact — they never limit significant figures.
  • Note 2: The uncertainty of a single reading is conventionally ± half the least count of the instrument.
  • Note 3: Round only at the end of a calculation; rounding intermediate steps accumulates error.
  • Note 4: Raising a quantity to the power n multiplies its percentage uncertainty by n — the reason T is timed over 10+ oscillations when measuring g.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Underline the significant figures of EVERY given value before starting a numerical — the smallest count sets your answer's s.f.
  • Trick 2: For 'which reading is more precise' MCQs, compare least counts, not decimal places: 0.01 cm (vernier) beats 0.1 cm (rule).
  • Trick 3: In error propagation MCQs, remember powers: if a question has v2 or T2, its error contribution is doubled instantly.
  • Trick 4: Convert ambiguous numbers to scientific notation first (500→5.0×102) — then counting s.f. becomes trivial.
  • Trick 5: Round-half-to-even only matters when the dropped digit is exactly 5 with nothing after; otherwise use the ordinary >5 up / <5 down rule.

Exam Notes

  • Exam Tip 1: NEB frequently asks 1-mark questions on counting significant figures and 2-3 mark questions on percentage error propagation.
  • Exam Tip 2: In practical exams, quoting a result beyond the instrument's least count loses marks — always match digits to the instrument.
  • Exam Tip 3: When a question gives data like (2.0 ± 0.1) and (3.45 ± 0.02), compute the value first, then add relative errors for products.
  • Exam Tip 4: Show the unrounded working and the rounded final answer separately — method marks are awarded for the guard digits.

Important Concepts

  • Concept 1: Accuracy = closeness to true value; precision = closeness of repeated readings to each other.
  • Concept 2: Least count = smallest interval an instrument can measure; it fixes the last significant figure of any reading.
  • Concept 3: Absolute error (Δx), relative error (Δx/x) and percentage error (Δx/x × 100%) are three views of the same uncertainty.
  • Concept 4: Error propagation rules: absolute errors add for sums/differences, relative errors add for products/quotients and powers.
  • Concept 5: Scientific notation encodes both magnitude and precision simultaneously — the standard form for final answers.

Important Tasks

Specific Conditions

  • Intermediate Calculations: Keep at least one extra 'guard' digit during intermediate calculation steps. Only round the final answer to the required number of significant figures to avoid compounding rounding errors.
  • Constants & Conversion Factors: Treat mathematical constants (e.g., π, √2) and exact conversion factors (e.g., 100 cm = 1 m) as having infinite significant figures. They should never limit the precision of the measured data.
  • Parallax Error: For analog measurements (metre rules, thermometers), ensure the line of sight is perpendicular to the scale; this is a necessary physical condition that must be met before determining the significant figure precision of the measurement.
