Notes

  • Particle creation (pair production): A high-energy photon (γ-ray) with energy hν ≥ 2mc² can create a particle-antiparticle pair. For electron-positron: γ → e⁺ + e⁻. The minimum photon energy is 2m_e c² = 1.022 MeV. Both rest mass energy (2mc²) and momentum must be conserved, requiring a nearby nucleus to absorb recoil.
  • Pair production threshold: For a photon to create a particle of mass m at rest, the photon must have energy at least E_min = 2mc² (for pair production). In the center-of-mass frame, the threshold is exactly 2mc². At rest, the created particles have zero kinetic energy.
  • Annihilation: When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate, converting their entire rest mass into energy. e⁺ + e⁻ → γ + γ (two photons to conserve momentum). Total energy released = 2m_e c² = 1.022 MeV.
  • Nuclear fission: A heavy nucleus splits into two (or more) lighter nuclei plus neutrons, releasing large amounts of energy. Example: ²³⁵U + n → ⁹²Kr + ¹⁴¹Ba + 3n + 200 MeV. The neutrons released trigger a chain reaction.
  • Chain reaction and criticality: In ²³⁵U fission, an average of 2-3 neutrons are released per fission. If, on average, exactly one neutron causes another fission, the reaction is critical. More than one → supercritical (exponential growth). Less than one → subcritical (reaction dies out).
  • Nuclear fusion: Light nuclei combine to form heavier nuclei, releasing energy when the product has higher binding energy per nucleon. Example: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV. Fusion powers stars (proton-proton chain, CNO cycle) and hydrogen bombs.
  • Coulomb barrier in fusion: Positively charged nuclei repel each other (Coulomb force). To overcome this and fuse, particles need high kinetic energy (high temperature) to approach within the nuclear force range (< 10⁻¹⁵ m). This requires temperatures of 10⁷-10⁸ K.
  • Deuterium-tritium (D-T) fusion: The easiest fusion reaction to achieve on Earth. ²H + ³H → ⁴He (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV) + 17.6 MeV total. The neutron can be captured to generate heat for electricity. Requires ~100 million K.
  • Proton-proton chain (solar fusion): The dominant fusion process in stars like the Sun. 4p → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν_e + 26.7 MeV. Involves proton to neutron conversion via weak interaction (beta-plus decay).
  • Fusion vs. fission energy per event: A single U-235 fission releases ~200 MeV. A D-T fusion event releases ~17.6 MeV. However, fusion releases about 3-4 times more energy PER UNIT MASS because lighter nuclei have more nucleons.
  • Controlled vs. uncontrolled reactions: Nuclear reactors use control rods (boron, cadmium) and moderators (water, graphite) to absorb excess neutrons and control the reaction. Nuclear weapons allow uncontrolled supercritical chain reactions producing massive energy release.
  • Binding energy perspective: Fusion increases binding energy (releases energy) for nuclei lighter than iron (~8.8 MeV/nucleon). Fission increases binding energy for nuclei heavier than iron. Iron itself is the most stable — neither fission nor fusion releases energy.

Confusion

  • Pair production requires a nucleus to conserve momentum — a photon alone cannot produce a particle-antiparticle pair even if it has enough energy, because momentum conservation would be violated if the particles are at rest.
  • Fusion does NOT occur in ordinary stars like the Sun because the 'fusion' of everyday materials involves atomic (electron) interactions, not nuclear. Fusion requires overcoming the Coulomb barrier at 10⁷-10⁸ K, far hotter than any chemical reaction.
  • Nuclear fission is NOT the same as radioactive decay. Fission requires a neutron to trigger the split; decay is spontaneous. Fission releases ∼200 MeV; decay releases typically 1-5 MeV.
  • The energy in nuclear reactions comes from the binding energy difference (mass defect), NOT from the rest mass of the fuel itself being 'converted.' Only Δm (the mass difference between reactants and products) converts, not the entire fuel mass.
  • Antimatter annihilation produces energy (E = mc²), but creating antimatter requires MORE energy than it produces. It is never an energy source on Earth — antimatter is produced in particle accelerators at enormous energy cost.
  • Nuclear fusion and fission are NOT just 'bigger bombs' — fusion is the opposite of fission in terms of the binding energy curve. Fusion moves nuclei toward the peak (iron); fission moves heavy nuclei toward the peak too. Both release energy by moving toward greater stability.

Practice

  • A 1 kg block of uranium contains ³²³²U (natural uranium). How much energy is released if 0.1% of it undergoes fission, with each fission releasing 200 MeV? Solution: Number of U atoms = (1000 g/mol)/(238 g/mol) × 6.02×10²³ = 2.53×10²¹ atoms. 0.1% undergoing fission = 2.53×10¹⁸ atoms. Total energy = 2.53×10¹⁸ × 200 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ = 8.11×10⁷ J ≈ 81 MJ (enough to power a 100 W bulb for 2.3 years).
  • A particle accelerator produces a photon of energy 3.0 MeV. Can this photon create an electron-positron pair? Solution: Threshold for e⁺e⁻ pair production is 2m_e c² = 2 × 0.511 MeV = 1.022 MeV. Since 3.0 MeV > 1.022 MeV, YES it can. The excess energy (3.0 - 1.022 = 1.978 MeV) becomes kinetic energy of the e⁺ and e⁻.
  • The Sun emits energy at 3.8 × 10²⁶ W. If this comes from fusion of hydrogen into helium (4H → He + 2ν + 26.7 MeV), how many kg of hydrogen are converted to energy per second? Solution: Energy per fusion = 6.7 MeV = 6.7 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ = 1.073×10⁻¹² J. Number of fusions per second = 3.8×10²⁶ / 1.073×10⁻¹² ≈ 3.54×10³⁸. Mass per fusion = 4 × 1.67×10⁻²⁷ = 6.68×10⁻²⁷ kg. Mass to energy per second = 3.54×10³⁸ × 6.68×10⁻²⁷ = 2.37 kg. About 600 million tons of hydrogen fuse per second in the Sun.
  • A D-T fusion reactor produces 1 GW (10⁹ W) of power. How much fuel (in grams/year) is needed if the reactor is 50% efficient? Each D-T fusion releases 17.6 MeV. Solution: Each fusion = 17.6 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ = 2.82×10⁻¹² J. Fusions needed per second = 10⁹/(0.5 × 2.82×10⁻¹²) = 7.09×10²⁰. Mass of D+T = 2+3 = 5 u = 5×1.66×10⁻²⁷ = 8.3×10⁻²⁷ kg. Mass per second = 7.09×10²⁰ × 8.3×10⁻²⁷ = 5.88×10⁻⁶ kg/s. Per year: 5.88×10⁻⁶ × 365 × 24 × 3600 = 185 g/year.
  • A typical nuclear reactor uses 100 tons of uranium enriched to 3.5% U-235. How much energy is released from 1 year of full operation at 2000 MW thermal? Solution: If all 100 tons (0.035×100 = 3.5 tons of U-235) fissions: Number = 3500/238 × 6.022×10²³ = 8.83×10²⁴ atoms. Energy = 8.83×10²⁴ × 200 MeV = 8.83×10²⁴ × 200 × 1.602×10⁻¹³ J = 2.83×10¹⁴ J. Power = energy/year = 2.83×10¹⁴/(365×24×3600) ≈ 8.96×10⁶ W ≈ 9 MW (reactor does not fission all fuel in one year — fuel stays in core for years).

Universal Facts

  • Einstein's E = mc² is a special case of the full equation E² = (mc²)² + (pc)², which applies to moving particles. At rest (p=0), this reduces to E = mc².
  • The binding energy curve peaks at iron-56. This is why stars stop producing energy via fusion once they form iron — fusing iron consumes energy instead of releasing it, which is the trigger for stellar collapse and supernova.
  • The Q-value of a nuclear reaction equals the difference in total rest mass energy (binding energy) of reactants and products: Q = (Σm_reactants - Σm_products)c². This is ALWAYS computed using the full relativistic mass-energy relation.
  • Pair production is not just a laboratory curiosity — it is how high-energy photons (gamma rays) interact with matter. Medical X-ray and PET imaging relies on understanding these particle creation and annihilation processes.
  • Both fission and fusion convert mass-energy (Δmc²) to kinetic energy of products. The energy density of nuclear fuel is millions of times greater than chemical fuel precisely because nuclear binding energies (MeV) dwarf chemical energies (eV).
