Notes

  • Atomic mass unit (u): 1, extu=1.660539 imes10−27, extkg. Defined as exactly 1/12 the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state. All nuclear masses are expressed in u for convenience.
  • Exact mass vs mass number: The mass number A is the integer count of nucleons. The exact mass in u differs slightly from A due to mass defect. For example, 12 extC has exact mass 12, extu by definition, but 1 extH has exact mass 1.007825, extu, not 1, extu.
  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element (Z same) with different mass numbers (A different). They have identical chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different nuclear masses, stabilities, and decay modes. Example: 35 extCl (75.78) and 37 extCl (24.22) are chlorine isotopes.
  • Mass defect Deltam: Deltam=Zmp​+Nmn​−M extnucleus​, where mp​approx1.007276, extu is the proton mass, mn​approx1.008665, extu is the neutron mass, and M extnucleus​ is the nuclear mass in u. The mass defect is always positive - the nucleus is lighter than its separated parts.
  • Packing fraction: f=(M−A)/A, where M is the exact atomic mass in u and A is the mass number. f measures the fractional deviation from A. Negative f (mass less than mass number) indicates strong binding; positive f indicates weaker binding. 56 extFe has the most negative f, making it the most stable nuclide.
  • Binding energy: BE=Deltamc2. The energy equivalent of the mass defect. In MeV, BE ext(MeV)approx931.494 imesDeltam ext(u). For 56 extFe, BE/Aapprox8.8, extMeV, the peak of the binding-energy curve.
  • Binding energy per nucleon: BE/A. Rises from sim1, extMeV for light hydrogen to sim8.8, extMeV near iron (Aapprox56), then slowly declines to sim7.6, extMeV for uranium. This curve explains why light nuclei release energy via fusion and heavy nuclei via fission.
  • Carbon-14 dating: 14 extC is a radioactive isotope with half-life 5730, extyears. While alive, living organisms maintain 14 extC/12 extCapprox1.1 imes10−12. After death, 14 extC decays via \eta−; measuring the remaining 14 extC gives the time since death (archaeological dating up to sim50, extkyr).
  • Isotopic abundance: The natural abundance of each isotope (given as a percentage) determines the standard atomic weight listed in the periodic table. For chlorine, \arA=0.7578 imes34.96885+0.2422 imes36.96590approx35.45, extu, matching the tabulated value.
  • Mass spectrometry: A device that separates ions by their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Ions are accelerated through electric fields and deflected by magnetic fields; lighter ions deflect more. The resulting spectrum shows peaks at different m/z values corresponding to different isotopes, with peak heights proportional to abundance.
  • Stable vs. radioactive isotopes: Only sim254 isotopes are stable; over 3000 are known to be radioactive. Stable isotopes have balanced neutron-proton ratios; too many or too few neutrons leads to instability and radioactive decay.
  • Nuclide notation: A nuclide is written as ZA​ extX, where A is the mass number (protons + neutrons), Z is the atomic number (protons), and  extX is the element symbol. Example: 92238​ extU has 92 protons and 238−92=146 neutrons.
  • Rutherford's gold foil experiment (1911): α particles (⁴He²⁺) were fired at a thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, but some were scattered at large angles. Rutherford concluded atoms have a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the center.
  • Unexpected result: Rutherford expected, based on J.J. Thomson's 'plum pudding' model, that α particles would experience only slight deflection. The large-angle scattering (some α particles bounced back nearly 180°) proved the positive charge (and most of the mass) was concentrated in a tiny, dense region — the nucleus.
  • Size of nucleus: The nucleus is roughly 10⁴ times smaller in radius than the atom. For a gold atom (radius ∼125 pm), the nucleus radius is ∼7 fm (femtometers = 10⁻¹⁵ m). The nucleus occupies a volume of about 10⁻¹² of the atom's volume.
  • Nuclear density: The density of nuclear matter is nearly constant for all nuclei. This can be derived from R = R₀A^(1/3), where A is the mass number. Since m_nucleus ≈ Am_u (atomic mass unit), density ρ = M_nucleus/V_nucleus = Am_u / (4/3 π R₀³A) = 3m_u/(4πR₀³). The A's cancel out!
  • Nuclear density value: Using R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm and m_u ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, the nuclear density ρ_nuc ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³. This is about 2.3 × 10¹⁴ times denser than ordinary matter — a sugar-cube-sized amount of neutron star material would weigh about 100 million tons on Earth.
  • Mass number (A): A = Z + N, the total number of protons (Z) plus neutrons (N) in the nucleus. Written as a superscript before the element name: ⁵⁶Fe, ²³⁵U. It approximates the atomic mass in atomic mass units (though the actual mass is slightly less due to mass defect).
  • Atomic number (Z): The number of protons in the nucleus, which determines the chemical element. All atoms of hydrogen have Z = 1, all helium atoms have Z = 2, all carbon atoms have Z = 6. Z also equals the number of orbiting electrons in a neutral atom.
  • Neutron number (N): N = A − Z, the number of neutrons in the nucleus. Neutrons contribute to the strong nuclear force that holds the nucleus together but do not affect the chemical properties of the element. They also add mass without adding Coulomb repulsion.
  • Isotopes: Atoms of the same element (same Z) but different neutron numbers (different N, different A). Example: hydrogen has three isotopes — protium (¹H, N=0), deuterium (²H, N=1), and tritium (³H, N=2). They have the same chemistry but different nuclear properties.
  • Nuclear radius formula: R = R₀A^(1/3), where R₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m is a constant. This shows that nuclear radius scales as the cube root of mass number — a consequence of the constant density. The nuclear force is short-range (1-2 fm), so adding a nucleon adds it to the surface.
  • Rutherford scattering formula: The number of α particles scattered at angle θ is proportional to 1/sin⁴(θ/2). This sharp angular dependence confirmed the concentrated-charge, small-radius nature of the nucleus. The formula matched Rutherford's theoretical predictions precisely.
  • Discovery of neutron (1932): James Chadwick discovered the neutron by bombarding beryllium with α particles. The neutral, massive particles (neutrons) could knock out protons from paraffin wax. This confirmed the proton-neutron model of the nucleus.
  • Nuclear composition: Nuclei contain Z protons (positively charged) and N neutrons (neutral), held together by the strong nuclear force. The strong force is short-range (fewer than 2-3 fm) but extremely powerful — overcoming the electrostatic repulsion between protons.
  • Density calculation example: For a carbon-12 nucleus (A = 12): R = 1.2 × 12^(1/3) = 1.2 × 2.29 = 2.75 fm. Mass = 12 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.99 × 10⁻²⁶ kg. Volume = 4/3 π(2.75 × 10⁻¹⁵)³ = 8.72 × 10⁻⁴⁴ m³. Density = 1.99×10⁻²⁶/8.72×10⁻⁴⁴ = 2.28 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³. Same as uranium — confirming constant density.

Confusion

  • Misconception - Mass number A is an integer count of nucleons; exact atomic mass in u is not exactly A. The difference is the mass defect, which is small but measurable. Mass spectrometry separates isotopes by their exact masses, not their mass numbers.
  • Misconception - Packing fraction f=(M−A)/A can be negative because the bound nucleus weighs less than its constituent nucleons. Students expect M>A by adding individual proton and neutron masses, but binding energy release makes M<Zmp​+Nmn​.
  • Misconception - Isotopes share the same Z but have different A; isobars share the same A but have different Z; isotones share the same N but have different A. These three distinctions are common exam distractors.
  • Misconception - Standard atomic weight (the periodic table value) is the weighted-average of all naturally occurring isotopic masses, not the mass of any single atom. A single chlorine atom has mass sim35,extu or sim37,extu, never 35.45,extu.
  • Misconception - Binding energy per nucleon BE/A peaks at iron (Asim56) at sim8.8, extMeV, while uranium has BE/Aapprox7.6, extMeV. Fission energy release depends on the difference between parent and daughter nuclei, not a fixed value.
  • Misconception - 1, extu of mass defect is equivalent to 931.5, extMeV of energy. This conversion factor is essential for all nuclear binding-energy calculations; omitting it produces results that are off by a factor of sim108.
  • Misconception - In eta^- decay, a neutron converts to a proton, electron, and antineutrino: n ightarrowp+e−+\ar ue​. The emitted electron originates from the nucleus, not from the orbital electron cloud.
  • Misconception - Half-life is the time required for a radioactive sample to decay to half its original amount. After n half-lives, the fraction remaining is (1/2)n, not 1/(2n).
  • The nucleus is NOT the same as the atom — the nucleus is the tiny dense central part (protons and neutrons), while the atom includes the orbiting electrons. A nucleus is about 10,000 times smaller in radius than the atom it belongs to.
  • Nuclear density is NOT the same as atomic density. Atomic density (mass/volume of the entire atom) is about 10³ kg/m³ (like water or solid materials). Nuclear density (mass/volume of just the nucleus) is about 10¹⁷ kg/m³ — unimaginably denser.
  • Mass number A approximates atomic mass in atomic mass units, but is NOT exactly equal. For example, ¹²C has A = 12 and atomic mass exactly 12 u (by definition), but most nuclei have actual mass slightly less than A due to binding energy (mass defect).
  • The nuclear radius formula R = R₀A^(1/3) shows that doubling the mass number (e.g., from helium A=4 to beryllium A=8) does NOT double the radius — it increases it by 2^(1/3) ≈ 1.26. This cubic-root scaling confirms the constant density hypothesis.
  • Atomic number Z determines the element, but ISOTOPES of the same element have the same Z but different A. You cannot tell isotopes apart chemically, but they differ greatly in their nuclear stability (e.g., ¹H is stable; ³H is radioactive with a 12-year half-life).

  • Calculate the radius, diameter, and density of a carbon-12 nucleus (A = 12). Use R₀ = 1.2 fm. Solution: R = 1.2 × 12^(1/3) = 1.2 × 2.289 = 2.747 fm. Diameter = 2R = 5.494 fm. Mass = 12 × 1.66×10⁻²⁷ = 1.993×10⁻²⁶ kg. Volume = 4/3 πR³ = 4/3 π(2.747×10⁻¹⁵)³ = 8.67×10⁻⁴⁴ m³. Density = 1.993×10⁻²⁶/8.67×10⁻⁴⁴ = 2.30×10¹⁷ kg/m³.
  • A uranium-238 nucleus (A = 238, radius ≈ 7.4 fm) has density equal to a carbon-12 nucleus (A = 12, radius ≈ 2.7 fm). Prove this mathematically using R = R₀A^(1/3). Solution: ρ_C = M_C/V_C = (12m_u)/(4/3 π( R₀12^(1/3))³) = 12m_u/(4/3 π R₀³ × 12) = m_u/(4/3 π R₀³). ρ_U = 238m_u/(4/3 π( R₀238^(1/3))³) = 238m_u/(4/3 π R₀³ × 238) = m_u/(4/3 π R₀³). They are EQUAL — confirming constant density.
  • The radius of a gold nucleus (A = 197) is measured at 7.4 fm. What is the value of R₀? Solution: R = R₀A^(1/3) → R₀ = R/A^(1/3) = 7.4/197^(1/3). 197^(1/3) = 5.82. R₀ = 7.4/5.82 = 1.27 fm. This is close to the standard value of 1.2 fm, with differences due to experimental measurement uncertainties.
  • A copper sample contains two stable isotopes: ⁶³Cu (69.3% abundant, A=63) and ⁶⁵Cu (30.7% abundant, A=65). Find the average atomic mass in u and the average nuclear radius. Solution: Average mass = 0.693×63 + 0.307×65 = 43.66 + 20.0 = 63.7 u. Average radius = 1.2 × 63.7^(1/3) = 1.2 × 3.99 = 4.79 fm. (Note: isotopes have same Z=29 but slightly different nuclear radii due to different neutron count.)
  • If a nucleus has 50 protons and 75 neutrons, what is its isotope, and what is its radius? Use R₀ = 1.2 fm. Solution: Z = 50 → element is tin (Sn). A = 50 + 75 = 125 → isotope is ¹²⁵Sn. Radius R = 1.2 × 125^(1/3) = 1.2 × 5.0 = 6.0 fm. Density = 125m_u/(4/3 π R³) ≈ 2.3×10¹⁷ kg/m³ (same for all nuclei).

Universal Facts

  • Fact (Historical): Frederick Soddy proposed the concept of isotopes in 1913, discovering that different radioactive elements could occupy the same position in the periodic table. He received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1921 for this work.
  • Fact (Application): Mass spectrometry is used in forensic science to analyze trace evidence, in environmental science to track pollution sources, in pharmacology to verify drug composition, and in astronomy to determine stellar composition.
  • Fact (Exam tip): Remember the conversion 1, extu=931.5, extMeV/c2. For mass defect problems, always compute Deltam first in u, then multiply by 931.5 to get binding energy in MeV. This avoids errors from using SI units.
  • Fact (Connection): The periodic table atomic weight of an element varies slightly depending on geographic source. For example, boron from different mines has different isotopic compositions, giving atomic weights between 10.81 and 10.82.
  • Fact (Everyday): Carbon-14 dating is used to verify the age of ancient manuscripts, authenticate artwork, date archaeological artifacts, and even verify the vintage of wine and spirits by measuring 14 extC levels.
  • Fact (Conservation): In all nuclear reactions, both mass number A (total nucleons) and atomic number Z (total charge) are conserved. These two conservation laws are the first checks students should perform when balancing nuclear equations.
  • Fact (Scale): Converting 1, extg of matter entirely to energy releases E=mc2=1 imes10−3 imes(3 imes108)2=9 imes1013, extJ, equivalent to about 21, extkilotons of TNT-the yield of the Hiroshima bomb.
  • Fact (Nuclear stability): The most stable nuclide is 56 extFe with a binding energy per nucleon of 8.79, extMeV. Nuclides lighter than iron release energy through fusion; those heavier than iron release energy through fission.
  • The density of nuclear matter (∼2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³) is so extreme that a single teaspoon of it would weigh about 6 billion tons on Earth. This is the density of neutron stars — objects with the mass of the Sun compressed into a city-sized sphere.
  • Rutherford's gold foil experiment took place in Geiger and Marsden's lab at the University of Manchester in 1909. Only about 1 in 8000 α particles was scattered at angles greater than 90° — but that one in 8000 was the key evidence.
  • The nuclear radius scales as A^(1/3) — this is a key piece of evidence that the strong nuclear force has a short range (about 1-2 femtometers). If the force were longer range, nuclei would be smaller and denser.
  • Atomic number Z determines all chemical properties (because chemistry is governed by electron configuration), while the number of neutrons N affects only nuclear stability and decay modes (isotopes differ in this).
  • All atoms of an element have identically sized nuclei only if they are the same isotope. Isotopes of the same element have slightly different nuclear radii because the additional neutrons increase A and thus R = R₀A^(1/3).

Specific Conditions

  • Rutherford Scattering Experiment: Alpha particles were fired at a thin gold foil. Most passed through with little deflection, but a few scattered at large angles (>90°). This disproved the Thomson plum-pudding model and established the nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most of the atom's mass.
  • Nuclear Size vs Atom Size: The nucleus is ~10⁻¹⁵ m (1-10 fm) while the atom is ~10⁻¹⁰ m (1 Å). The nucleus occupies ~10⁻¹⁵ of the atomic volume but contains >99.9% of the mass. The rest is empty space where electrons orbit.
  • Nuclear Density: Nearly constant for all nuclei: ρ = mass/volume ≈ A·1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg / (4/3 π (1.2×10⁻¹⁵ A¹ᐟ³)³) ≈ 2.3×10¹⁷ kg/m³. This constancy supports the liquid-drop model and the idea that nucleons are closely packed.
