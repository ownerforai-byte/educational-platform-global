Notes

  • Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden directed alpha particles at thin gold foil. Most passed almost straight through; a small fraction deflected sharply and very few returned. These observations could not be explained by positive charge spread uniformly through the atom.
  • Rutherford inferred a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing nearly all atomic mass, surrounded by electrons; most atomic volume is empty space. Nuclear radius is about 10⁻¹⁵ m versus atomic radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • For a head-on alpha particle, its kinetic energy converts to electrostatic potential energy at closest approach: Eα=(1/4πε₀)(2Ze²/rmin). Thus rmin=(1/4πε₀)(2Ze²/Eα), under the Coulomb-scattering model.
  • Nuclear notation ᴬ_ZX gives proton number Z, nucleon number A and neutron number N=A−Z. Isotopes share Z but have different A; isobars share A but have different Z.
  • Example: uranium-235 has Z=92, A=235 and N=143. Its nuclear charge is +92e.
  • Rutherford's foil experiment showed that the atom is mostly empty. Nearly every alpha particle went straight on, and a small number were turned through large angles. That is possible only if the positive charge and most of the mass sit in a nucleus far smaller than the atom.
  • The working relations are Most alpha particles go almost straight through the foil and A few bounce back from a tiny positive nucleus. Name each symbol, convert the data into SI units, and only then substitute.

Confusion

  • Do not claim the experiment established a modern planetary electron orbit.
  • Closest-approach estimates assume Coulomb interaction and head-on motion.
  • A symbol is not a number until the unit is named.
  • A proportional relation is not an equality until the constant is included.
  • A vector answer needs a direction as well as a magnitude.
  • A special case, such as a right angle or a steady temperature, has to be stated before it is used.

Specific Conditions

  • Rutherford Scattering Experiment: Alpha particles were fired at a thin gold foil. Most passed through with little deflection, but a few scattered at large angles (>90°). This disproved the Thomson plum-pudding model and established the nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most of the atom's mass.
  • Nuclear Size vs Atom Size: The nucleus is ~10⁻¹⁵ m (1-10 fm) while the atom is ~10⁻¹⁰ m (1 Å). The nucleus occupies ~10⁻¹⁵ of the atomic volume but contains >99.9% of the mass. The rest is empty space where electrons orbit.
  • Nuclear Density: Nearly constant for all nuclei: ρ = mass/volume ≈ A·1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg / (4/3 π (1.2×10⁻¹⁵ A¹ᐟ³)³) ≈ 2.3×10¹⁷ kg/m³. This constancy supports the liquid-drop model and the idea that nucleons are closely packed.

Exercises

1.

An atom is about 10^−10 m across and a nucleus is about 10^−15 m across. Find the ratio of the nuclear volume to the atomic volume.

Steps
  • Volume scales as the cube of the radius.
  • The radius ratio is 10^−15 / 10^−10 = 10^−5.
  • The volume ratio is (10^−5)^3 = 10^−15.
  • The nucleus occupies a tiny fraction of the atom.
  • The large-angle scatters come from that small charged core.
Answer

10^−15.
