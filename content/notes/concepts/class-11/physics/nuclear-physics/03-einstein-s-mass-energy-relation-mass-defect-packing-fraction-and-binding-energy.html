Notes

  • Atomic mass unit (u): 1, extu=1.660539 imes10−27, extkg. Defined as exactly 1/12 the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state. All nuclear masses are expressed in u for convenience.
  • Exact mass vs mass number: The mass number A is the integer count of nucleons. The exact mass in u differs slightly from A due to mass defect. For example, 12 extC has exact mass 12, extu by definition, but 1 extH has exact mass 1.007825, extu, not 1, extu.
  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element (Z same) with different mass numbers (A different). They have identical chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different nuclear masses, stabilities, and decay modes. Example: 35 extCl (75.78) and 37 extCl (24.22) are chlorine isotopes.
  • Mass defect Deltam: Deltam=Zmp​+Nmn​−M extnucleus​, where mp​approx1.007276, extu is the proton mass, mn​approx1.008665, extu is the neutron mass, and M extnucleus​ is the nuclear mass in u. The mass defect is always positive - the nucleus is lighter than its separated parts.
  • Packing fraction: f=(M−A)/A, where M is the exact atomic mass in u and A is the mass number. f measures the fractional deviation from A. Negative f (mass less than mass number) indicates strong binding; positive f indicates weaker binding. 56 extFe has the most negative f, making it the most stable nuclide.
  • Binding energy: BE=Deltamc2. The energy equivalent of the mass defect. In MeV, BE ext(MeV)approx931.494 imesDeltam ext(u). For 56 extFe, BE/Aapprox8.8, extMeV, the peak of the binding-energy curve.
  • Binding energy per nucleon: BE/A. Rises from sim1, extMeV for light hydrogen to sim8.8, extMeV near iron (Aapprox56), then slowly declines to sim7.6, extMeV for uranium. This curve explains why light nuclei release energy via fusion and heavy nuclei via fission.
  • Carbon-14 dating: 14 extC is a radioactive isotope with half-life 5730, extyears. While alive, living organisms maintain 14 extC/12 extCapprox1.1 imes10−12. After death, 14 extC decays via \eta−; measuring the remaining 14 extC gives the time since death (archaeological dating up to sim50, extkyr).
  • Isotopic abundance: The natural abundance of each isotope (given as a percentage) determines the standard atomic weight listed in the periodic table. For chlorine, \arA=0.7578 imes34.96885+0.2422 imes36.96590approx35.45, extu, matching the tabulated value.
  • Mass spectrometry: A device that separates ions by their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Ions are accelerated through electric fields and deflected by magnetic fields; lighter ions deflect more. The resulting spectrum shows peaks at different m/z values corresponding to different isotopes, with peak heights proportional to abundance.
  • Stable vs. radioactive isotopes: Only sim254 isotopes are stable; over 3000 are known to be radioactive. Stable isotopes have balanced neutron-proton ratios; too many or too few neutrons leads to instability and radioactive decay.
  • Nuclide notation: A nuclide is written as ZA​ extX, where A is the mass number (protons + neutrons), Z is the atomic number (protons), and  extX is the element symbol. Example: 92238​ extU has 92 protons and 238−92=146 neutrons.
  • Einstein's mass-energy relation (1905): E = mc². Mass and energy are equivalent — a change in mass corresponds to a change in energy by ΔE = Δmc². The speed of light c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s, so c² ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s².
  • Rest energy: The energy associated with an object's mass at rest is E_0 = m_0c². This is the minimum energy the mass can have. Even stationary mass has this enormous energy content.
  • Mass defect: When nucleons (protons and neutrons) form a nucleus, the measured nuclear mass M_A is slightly less than the sum of the individual nucleon masses: (Zm_p + Nm_n). The difference Δm = (Zm_p + Nm_n) − M_A is called the mass defect. It is always positive.
  • Origin of mass defect: The missing mass Δm converts to binding energy via E = Δmc². The nucleus is formed in an exothermic process, releasing energy. This energy holds the nucleus together — it is the binding energy.
  • Binding energy (BE): BE = Δm × c². It is the energy required to completely disassemble a nucleus into its individual protons and neutrons at rest. Also equals the energy released when the nucleus forms from its constituent nucleons.
  • Binding energy per nucleon (BE/A): BE divided by mass number A. This normalizes for nucleus size. The BE/A curve peaks at iron-56 (≈ 8.8 MeV per nucleon). For nuclei lighter than iron, fusion increases BE/A (releases energy). For nuclei heavier than iron, fission increases BE/A (releases energy).
  • Packing fraction: f = (M - A)/A, where M is the actual atomic mass (in u) and A is the mass number. Introduced by Aston. Negative packing fraction means the nucleus is more stable than the average for that mass number. Positive means less stable. This led to the discovery of isotopes and mass defects.
  • Mass-energy equivalence in chemical reactions: Chemical bonds have binding energies too, but they are much smaller (a few eV per bond) compared to nuclear binding (∼10⁶ eV). The mass defect in chemical bonds is negligible but measurable with precision instruments.
  • Energy units in nuclear physics: 1 eV (electron volt) = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The binding energy of nucleons is typically 1-8 MeV per nucleon, while chemical bond energies are 1-10 eV per atom — nuclear energies are ∼10⁶ times larger.
  • Q-value of nuclear reactions: Q = (mass of reactants - mass of products) × c². If Q > 0, the reaction is exothermic (releases energy). If Q < 0, it is endothermic (absorbs energy). Q equals the kinetic energy released or absorbed.
  • Stability and binding: The most stable nuclides have the highest binding energy per nucleon. Iron-56, nickel-62, and iron-58 are the most tightly bound. Very light and very heavy nuclei are less stable.
  • Einstein's famous equation derivation: From the special theory of relativity, the relativistic energy-momentum relation E² = (mc²)² + (pc)² simplifies to E = mc² when p = 0 (particle at rest). This holds for ALL particles, not just nuclear ones.

Confusion

  • Misconception - Mass number A is an integer count of nucleons; exact atomic mass in u is not exactly A. The difference is the mass defect, which is small but measurable. Mass spectrometry separates isotopes by their exact masses, not their mass numbers.
  • Misconception - Packing fraction f=(M−A)/A can be negative because the bound nucleus weighs less than its constituent nucleons. Students expect M>A by adding individual proton and neutron masses, but binding energy release makes M<Zmp​+Nmn​.
  • Misconception - Isotopes share the same Z but have different A; isobars share the same A but have different Z; isotones share the same N but have different A. These three distinctions are common exam distractors.
  • Misconception - Standard atomic weight (the periodic table value) is the weighted-average of all naturally occurring isotopic masses, not the mass of any single atom. A single chlorine atom has mass sim35,extu or sim37,extu, never 35.45,extu.
  • Misconception - Binding energy per nucleon BE/A peaks at iron (Asim56) at sim8.8, extMeV, while uranium has BE/Aapprox7.6, extMeV. Fission energy release depends on the difference between parent and daughter nuclei, not a fixed value.
  • Misconception - 1, extu of mass defect is equivalent to 931.5, extMeV of energy. This conversion factor is essential for all nuclear binding-energy calculations; omitting it produces results that are off by a factor of sim108.
  • Misconception - In eta^- decay, a neutron converts to a proton, electron, and antineutrino: n ightarrowp+e−+\ar ue​. The emitted electron originates from the nucleus, not from the orbital electron cloud.
  • Misconception - Half-life is the time required for a radioactive sample to decay to half its original amount. After n half-lives, the fraction remaining is (1/2)n, not 1/(2n).
  • E = mc² means mass and energy are CONSERVATED TOGETHER, not that mass can be freely converted to energy in any process. The mass-energy of the entire system is conserved. In nuclear reactions, the total mass-energy of all particles before equals after.
  • The mass defect is NOT the binding energy — it is the mass DEFICIT that, when multiplied by c², gives the binding energy. Δm = BE/c², not BE = Δm. BE = Δm × c².
  • Mass defect arises because binding energy is negative potential energy. When nucleons come together, potential energy decreases (becomes more negative), so mass decreases (since energy decreased). The system is more stable at lower mass-energy.
  • Binding energy per nucleon is NOT the same as binding energy. BE/A peaks at iron. Even though a heavy nucleus has a large total BE (it is very bound), BE/A can be low. This is why heavy nuclei can undergo fission to release energy.
  • The formula E = Δmc² does NOT mean you can convert any arbitrary mass m into energy. Only the mass associated with the binding energy (the 'defect') can be released in nuclear reactions. The rest mass of a stable nucleus cannot be converted to energy without destroying the nucleus.
  • Chemical energy does NOT involve mass-energy equivalence — chemical reactions involve rearrangement of electrons, and the mass changes are too tiny to measure. Nuclear physics deals with changes in the nucleus itself.

  • The binding energy of a helium-4 nucleus is 28.2 MeV. If the masses of protons and neutrons that form it have a total mass of 4.03282 u, find (a) the mass of the ⁴He nucleus, (b) the mass defect, (c) the binding energy per nucleon. (1 u = 931.5 MeV/c²). Solution: (a) Mass of He-4 = sum of constituents minus mass equivalent of BE. BE = 28.2 MeV, Δm = BE/c² = 28.2/931.5 = 0.03027 u. Mass of He-4 = 4.03282 - 0.03027 = 4.00255 u. (b) Mass defect = 0.03027 u. (c) BE/A = 28.2/4 = 7.05 MeV/nucleon.
  • Calculate the binding energy per nucleon for lithium-7. Given: m(7Li) = 7.01600 u, m(p) = 1.00783 u, m(n) = 1.00867 u. Solution: 3 protons + 4 neutrons. Mass of constituents = 3×1.00783 + 4×1.00867 = 3.02349 + 4.03468 = 7.05817 u. Mass defect = 7.05817 - 7.01600 = 0.04217 u. BE = 0.04217 × 931.5 = 39.28 MeV. BE/A = 39.28/7 = 5.61 MeV/nucleon.
  • In a nuclear reaction: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n. Given masses: 2.01410 u, 3.01605 u, 4.00260 u, 1.00867 u. Find Q-value. Solution: Mass of reactants = 2.01410 + 3.01605 = 5.03015 u. Mass of products = 4.00260 + 1.00867 = 5.01127 u. Δm = 5.03015 - 5.01127 = 0.01888 u. Q = 0.01888 × 931.5 = 17.59 MeV. Positive Q means energy is released (fusion).
  • How much mass is converted to energy per year by a 1000 MW coal power plant if it operates 80% of the time, assuming 35% efficiency? Solution: Annual output energy = 1000×10⁶ W × 0.8 × 365 × 24 × 3600 = 2.52 × 10¹⁶ J. This is 35% of the thermal energy. Thermal energy = 2.52×10¹⁶/0.35 = 7.2 × 10¹⁶ J. Δm = E/c² = 7.2×10¹⁶/(3×10⁸)² = 8.0 × 10⁻¹ kg = 0.8 kg per year.
  • Using the semi-empirical mass formula (Weizsäcker), the binding energy per nucleon for uranium-238 is about 7.5 MeV and for krypton-92 is 8.7 MeV. If uranium-238 fissions into krypton-92 + barium-141 + 3 neutrons, estimate Q-value. Solution: BE of U-238 = 7.5 × 238 = 1785 MeV. BE of products = 8.7(92) + 8.5(141) + 2(3) = 786.4 + 1200 + 6 = 1992 MeV. Q = 1992 - 1785 = 207 MeV (energy released).

Universal Facts

  • Fact (Historical): Frederick Soddy proposed the concept of isotopes in 1913, discovering that different radioactive elements could occupy the same position in the periodic table. He received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1921 for this work.
  • Fact (Application): Mass spectrometry is used in forensic science to analyze trace evidence, in environmental science to track pollution sources, in pharmacology to verify drug composition, and in astronomy to determine stellar composition.
  • Fact (Exam tip): Remember the conversion 1, extu=931.5, extMeV/c2. For mass defect problems, always compute Deltam first in u, then multiply by 931.5 to get binding energy in MeV. This avoids errors from using SI units.
  • Fact (Connection): The periodic table atomic weight of an element varies slightly depending on geographic source. For example, boron from different mines has different isotopic compositions, giving atomic weights between 10.81 and 10.82.
  • Fact (Everyday): Carbon-14 dating is used to verify the age of ancient manuscripts, authenticate artwork, date archaeological artifacts, and even verify the vintage of wine and spirits by measuring 14 extC levels.
  • Fact (Conservation): In all nuclear reactions, both mass number A (total nucleons) and atomic number Z (total charge) are conserved. These two conservation laws are the first checks students should perform when balancing nuclear equations.
  • Fact (Scale): Converting 1, extg of matter entirely to energy releases E=mc2=1 imes10−3 imes(3 imes108)2=9 imes1013, extJ, equivalent to about 21, extkilotons of TNT-the yield of the Hiroshima bomb.
  • Fact (Nuclear stability): The most stable nuclide is 56 extFe with a binding energy per nucleon of 8.79, extMeV. Nuclides lighter than iron release energy through fusion; those heavier than iron release energy through fission.
  • E = mc² is valid for ALL energy, not just nuclear — chemical, electrical, even mechanical. However, the mass changes are usually too small to detect (e.g., a compressed spring gains mass by about 10⁻¹⁶ kg).
  • The binding energy curve explains why fusion releases energy for elements lighter than iron and fission releases energy for elements heavier than iron. Iron is the most stable nucleus — its formation (in stars) is the end of fusion as an energy source.
  • The mass of a proton or neutron (about 1.008 u) is not exactly the sum of quark masses — most of the nucleon's mass comes from the strong interaction energy (E = mc² applied to the binding within the nucleon itself).
  • Annihilation (particle + antiparticle → photons) converts ALL rest mass to energy: E = 2m_p c² for electron-positron annihilation. But matter-antimatter annihilation is not used as an energy source because antimatter is extremely rare and difficult to produce.
  • The packing fraction method discovered the mass defect and led to the development of the Liquid Drop Model (Bethe-Weizsäcker formula) which quantitatively predicts nuclear binding energies, crucial for understanding fission and fusion.

Specific Conditions

  • E=mc² and Binding Energy: Einstein's relation E=mc2 underpins nuclear binding energy. The mass defect Δm corresponds to binding energy BE=Δm⋅c2. In nuclear reactions, the total mass-energy (rest mass + kinetic) is conserved.
  • Packing Fraction Significance: Packing fraction f=(M−A)/A measures how much the actual atomic mass M deviates from the mass number A. Negative f means the nucleus is bound (mass less than sum of parts). Most stable nuclei have the most negative f (e.g., 56Fe).
  • Binding Energy Curve and Stability: BE/A peaks at ~8.8 MeV for 56Fe. Nuclei lighter than iron gain stability through fusion (increasing BE/A). Nuclei heavier than iron gain stability through fission (increasing BE/A). This curve is the master key to nuclear energy release.
