Summary

Semiconductors have electrical conductivity between conductors and insulators. Intrinsic semiconductors are pure (e.g., Si, Ge) with equal electron and hole concentrations generated thermally across the band gap. Extrinsic semiconductors are doped with impurities: n-type adds donor electrons (group V), p-type adds acceptor holes (group III). Doping dramatically increases conductivity and enables diode/transistor action.

Notes

  • Band Theory Basis: Semiconductors have a small forbidden energy gap E_g between valence band (full at 0 K) and conduction band (empty at 0 K). For Si: E_g = 1.12 eV; Ge: E_g = 0.67 eV. Thermal energy kT ≈ 0.026 eV at 300 K allows some electrons to jump the gap.
  • Intrinsic Semiconductor: Pure crystal with no impurities. At T > 0 K, thermal excitation creates electron-hole pairs: n_i = p_i = √(N_c N_v) exp(-E_g/2kT). Conductivity σ = n_i e (μ_e + μ_h). For Si at 300 K: n_i ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³.
  • n-Type Semiconductor: Doped with pentavalent (group V) atoms (P, As, Sb) having 5 valence electrons. Four bond with Si; fifth is loosely bound (donor level E_d just below E_c). Ionization energy ~0.05 eV → nearly all donors ionized at room T. Majority carriers: electrons; minority: holes. n_n ≈ N_d ≫ p_n.
  • p-Type Semiconductor: Doped with trivalent (group III) atoms (B, Al, Ga) having 3 valence electrons. Creates vacancy (acceptor level E_a just above E_v). Electrons from valence band fill acceptor levels, creating mobile holes. Majority carriers: holes; minority: electrons. p_p ≈ N_a ≫ n_p.
  • Mass Action Law: In thermal equilibrium, np = n_i² regardless of doping type or level. Increasing majority carrier concentration suppresses minority carriers via recombination. Fundamental relation for device analysis.
  • Fermi Level Position: Intrinsic: E_F lies near mid-gap. n-type: E_F shifts toward conduction band (closer to E_c). p-type: E_F shifts toward valence band (closer to E_v). Heavy doping can push E_F into bands (degenerate semiconductor).
  • Conductivity Control: σ = e(nμ_e + pμ_h). Doping adjusts n or p over many orders of magnitude (10¹⁰ to 10¹⁹ cm⁻³). Mobility μ decreases at high doping due to impurity scattering. Optimal doping balances carrier density and mobility.
  • Temperature Dependence: Low T: freeze-out region (carriers not ionized). Medium T: extrinsic region (all dopants ionized, n ≈ N_d or p ≈ N_a). High T: intrinsic region (thermally generated pairs dominate over dopants). Device operation typically in extrinsic region.
  • Applications: p-n junctions (diodes, LEDs, solar cells), bipolar transistors (npn/pnp), MOSFETs (CMOS logic), integrated circuits. Modern electronics relies entirely on controlled doping of silicon.

Specific Conditions

  • Thermal Equilibrium Assumed: Mass action law np = n_i² and Fermi level definitions apply only at equilibrium. Non-equilibrium (illumination, bias) requires quasi-Fermi levels.
  • Non-Degenerate Doping: Standard formulas assume Boltzmann statistics (E_F at least 3kT from band edges). Heavy doping (>10¹⁸ cm⁻³) requires Fermi-Dirac statistics.
  • Complete Ionization at Room T: Shallow donors/acceptors fully ionized above ~100 K. Deep-level impurities may remain partially ionized and act as recombination centers.
  • Single Crystal Required: Polycrystalline or amorphous materials have grain boundary states that trap carriers and reduce effective mobility. Device-grade semiconductors are single crystals.

Key Points

  • Intrinsic: pure semiconductor, n = p = n_i, conductivity limited by E_g.
  • n-type: group V dopants donate electrons; n ≫ p; E_F near E_c.
  • p-type: group III dopants create holes; p ≫ n; E_F near E_v.
  • Mass action law np = n_i² holds always at equilibrium.
  • Doping controls conductivity over 9+ orders of magnitude.
  • Device function depends on spatial variation of doping profiles.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Intrinsic semiconductors have equal electron and hole concentrations determined solely by temperature and band gap.
  • Statement 2: n-type semiconductors use pentavalent dopants to increase free electron concentration above intrinsic level.
  • Statement 3: p-type semiconductors use trivalent dopants to increase hole concentration above intrinsic level.
  • Statement 4: The product of electron and hole concentrations remains constant (n_i²) at thermal equilibrium regardless of doping.
  • Statement 5: Electrical conductivity of semiconductors can be precisely engineered through controlled doping concentrations.

Important Notes

  • Silicon dominates industry due to stable native oxide (SiO₂) enabling MOS technology; GaAs used for high-frequency/optoelectronic applications.
  • Typical doping: 10¹⁵–10¹⁸ cm⁻³ (one impurity per 10⁶–10⁹ host atoms). Extremely precise control achieved via ion implantation.
  • Mobility values for Si at 300 K: μ_e ≈ 1350 cm²/V·s, μ_h ≈ 480 cm²/V·s. Electrons ~3× more mobile than holes.
  • NEB frequently asks distinction between intrinsic/extrinsic, n-type/p-type, and explanation of mass action law.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Intrinsic: pure, n=p, mid-gap E_F.
  • n-type: donor (group V), electrons major, E_F↑.
  • p-type: acceptor (group III), holes major, E_F↓.
  • np = n_i² always at equilibrium.
  • More doping → higher σ but lower μ at very high levels.

Exam Notes

  • Draw energy band diagrams showing E_c, E_v, E_F, E_d/E_a for each type.
  • Calculate carrier concentrations given doping level and n_i.
  • Explain why conductivity increases with doping but mobility eventually decreases.
  • Describe how p-n junction forms from joining p-type and n-type regions.

Examples

  • Si doped with P at N_d = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³: n ≈ 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, p = (1.5×10¹⁰)²/10¹⁶ = 2.25×10⁴ cm⁻³. σ = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁶ × 1350 ≈ 2.16 (Ω·cm)⁻¹.
  • Ge at 300 K: n_i ≈ 2.4×10¹³ cm⁻³ (much higher than Si due to smaller E_g). More sensitive to temperature.
  • Compensated semiconductor: N_d = 10¹⁶, N_a = 10¹⁵ → net n-type with n ≈ 9×10¹⁵ cm⁻³. Net doping determines carrier type.
  • Degenerate Si: N_d > 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ → E_F enters conduction band. Behaves like metal; used for ohmic contacts.

Universal Facts

  • First transistor (point-contact) invented at Bell Labs in 1947 by Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley (Nobel 1956).
  • Modern CPUs contain >50 billion transistors on single chip, each with gate length <5 nm.
  • Silicon wafer purity: 99.9999999% ('nine nines'). One impurity per billion atoms still matters.
  • Photovoltaic effect in p-n junctions converts sunlight directly to electricity; efficiency record >47% for multi-junction cells.

Confusion

  • Thinking n-type means negative charge: Material is electrically neutral; 'n' refers to negative charge carriers (electrons), not net charge.
  • Believing holes are real particles: Holes are absence of electrons behaving as positive quasiparticles with effective mass.
  • Assuming doping adds free carriers without limit: Compensation, solubility limits, and defect formation constrain achievable doping.
  • Confusing intrinsic carrier density with doping level: n_i depends only on T and E_g; doping sets n or p independently.
  • Thinking higher doping always improves devices: Excessive doping causes tunneling, reduced lifetime, and contact degradation.
