Notes

  • Metals – band overlap or partial filling: In metals, the valence band is either partially filled (alkali metals like Na, K have one electron per atom in an s-band) OR it overlaps with the conduction band (alkaline earth metals like Mg). In both cases, the Fermi level EF​ lies within a band, so electrons have empty states immediately available at EF​. Even a small electric field accelerates electrons, giving high conductivity.
  • Insulators – wide band gap: In insulators (diamond, SiO2​, NaCl), the valence band is completely filled and the conduction band is completely empty at 0 K. The band gap Eg​>3eV (typically 5–10 eV). At room temperature, kB​T≈0.026eV≪Eg​, so thermal excitation across the gap is negligible. No carriers → no conduction.
  • Semiconductors – small band gap: In semiconductors (Si, Ge, GaAs), the band gap is small (Eg​≈0.5–3eV). At room temperature, a small but significant number of electrons are thermally excited from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving holes in the valence band. Both electrons (conduction band) and holes (valence band) contribute to conduction.
  • Temperature coefficient: In metals, resistivity increases with temperature (ho∝T) because increased lattice vibrations (phonons) scatter electrons more. In semiconductors, resistivity decreases with temperature (ho∝eEg​/2kB​T) because more electron-hole pairs are thermally generated. This opposite trend is a hallmark distinction.
  • Band gap values: Silicon Eg​=1.12eV (indirect). Germanium Eg​=0.67eV (indirect). Gallium arsenide Eg​=1.43eV (direct). Diamond Eg​=5.5eV (insulator but wide-gap semiconductor when doped). InSb Eg​=0.17eV (narrow-gap semiconductor).
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration: For an intrinsic semiconductor, ni2​=Nc​Nv​exp(−Eg​/kB​T), where Nc​, Nv​ are the effective density of states in the conduction and valence bands. At 300K: ni​(Si)≈1.5×1016m−3, ni​(Ge)≈2.4×1019m−3, ni​(GaAs)≈2.1×1012m−3.
  • Conduction mechanism: In metals, only electrons conduct. In semiconductors, both electrons (negative charge, mobility μn​) and holes (positive charge, mobility μp​) conduct. Total conductivity σ=neμn​+peμp​, where n,p are electron and hole concentrations.
  • Hole conduction: A hole is the absence of an electron in a nearly-full valence band. When an electric field is applied, electrons move to fill the hole, making the hole appear to move opposite to the electron drift. Holes have positive effective charge +e and positive effective mass mp∗​.

Confusion

  • Confusion: Students often think insulators and semiconductors are fundamentally different materials — they are the SAME band structure, just with different Eg​ values. Diamond (5.5eV) is an insulator, silicon (1.12eV) is a semiconductor; both have the diamond crystal structure.
  • Confusion: Metals do NOT have a band gap. The Fermi level lies inside a band. There is no "forbidden gap" separating valence from conduction band — they are the same band or overlapping bands.
  • Confusion: In metals, conductivity decreases with temperature because more phonons scatter electrons. In semiconductors, conductivity increases because more carriers are generated. Students mix up the direction of temperature dependence.
  • Confusion: The Fermi level in an intrinsic semiconductor lies near the MIDDLE of the band gap, not at the bottom of the conduction band. EF​≈Eg​/2+(kB​T/2)ln(Nv​/Nc​). This is crucial for understanding doping effects.
  • Confusion: Holes are NOT real particles. They are quasiparticles representing collective motion of electrons in a nearly-full band. They have positive charge, but they are not positrons (antimatter).
  • Confusion: A partially filled valence band and a completely filled valence band with overlap to a conduction band are both metallic. The distinction is subtle — in the first case, there is one band partially filled; in the second, two bands overlap, so electrons can move freely into the higher band.
  • Confusion: Germanium has higher intrinsic carrier concentration than silicon at room temperature because its band gap is smaller (0.67eV vs 1.12eV), so thermal excitation is more probable. But Si is more widely used because it forms a stable native oxide (SiO2​).
  • Confusion: The band gap of a semiconductor is temperature dependent. Eg​ decreases as temperature increases (lattice expansion, electron-phonon coupling). This means the temperature coefficient of resistance has contributions from both carrier generation and gap narrowing.

Practice

  • Problem: A semiconductor has Eg​=1.1eV. At T=300K, kB​T=0.0259eV. Calculate the ratio of thermally excited electrons in the conduction band to total valence electrons, assuming Nc​=Nv​=1025m−3 and valence electron density =1028m−3. [Solution: ni​=Nc​exp(−Eg​/2kB​T)=1025exp(−1.1/(2×0.0259))≈1025×e−21.2≈1025×6.8×10−10≈6.8×1015m−3. Ratio =6.8×1015/1028≈6.8×10−13.]
  • Problem: Copper has conductivity σ=5.9×107S/m and electron density n=8.5×1028m−3. Find the electron mobility μ. [Solution: σ=neμ⇒μ=σ/(ne)=5.9×107/(8.5×1028×1.602×10−19)≈4.3×10−3m2V−1s−1=43cm2V−1s−1.]
  • Problem: An intrinsic semiconductor has n=p=1016m−3 at 300K. The electron and hole mobilities are μn​=0.14m2V−1s−1 and μp​=0.05m2V−1s−1. Calculate the conductivity and resistivity. [Solution: σ=en(μn​+μp​)=1.602×10−19×1016×0.19=3.04×10−4S/m. ρ=1/σ≈3.3kΩ⋅m.]
  • Problem: In a metal, the resistivity at T1​=273K is ρ1​=1.7×10−8Ω⋅m. If the temperature coefficient of resistivity is α=0.0039K−1, what is the resistivity at T2​=373K? [Solution: ρ2​=ρ1​[1+α(T2​−T1​)]=1.7×10−8[1+0.0039(100)]=1.7×10−8×1.39≈2.36×10−8Ω⋅m.]
  • Problem: Why does intrinsic silicon conductivity increase exponentially with temperature, while copper conductivity decreases linearly? [Solution: Si: more electron-hole pairs thermally generated ∝exp(−Eg​/2kB​T). Cu: more phonon scattering of existing conduction electrons ∝T. Different physics: carrier generation vs carrier scattering.]

Universal Facts

  • Fact (Historical): The band theory explanation of metals, insulators, and semiconductors was developed by Felix Bloch (1928), Alan Wilson (1931), and Rudolf Peierls. It unified the electrical properties of solids under a single quantum-mechanical framework.
  • Fact (Application): The temperature dependence of semiconductor resistance is used in thermistors (temperature sensors). NTC (negative temperature coefficient) thermistors use semiconductors; PTC (positive) thermistors use metals or special ceramics.
  • Fact (Exam tip): NEB often asks: "Why does semiconductor conductivity increase with temperature?" Answer: Thermal energy excites more electrons across the band gap, creating more electron-hole pairs. Carrier concentration increases exponentially, overwhelming the mobility decrease.
  • Fact (Connection): The band gap Eg​ determines the color of light a semiconductor absorbs. CdS (Eg​≈2.4eV) absorbs blue/green light (yellow appearance). Si (1.12eV) absorbs near-IR and visible (grey appearance). GaAs (1.43eV) is opaque to visible light.
  • Fact (Everyday): Your phone's screen uses InGaN/GaN LEDs (direct gap, blue/green emission) and amorphous silicon TFTs (backplane). The camera uses a silicon CCD/CMOS sensor (indirect gap, good for detecting visible light). Different semiconductors for different functions.
  • Fact (Scale): Copper conductivity ∼107S/m. Intrinsic silicon conductivity ∼10−4S/m. That's an 11-order-of-magnitude difference! Doping silicon can increase conductivity to ∼104S/m — still 3 orders below copper, but enough for switching.
  • Fact (Origin): In a perfect intrinsic semiconductor at 0 K, the valence band is completely full and the conduction band is completely empty. There are NO free carriers. The resistance is infinite. At finite T, thermal energy creates carriers, giving finite conductivity — the essence of semiconductor behavior.
