Notes

  • Superposition principle. The force on any one charge due to several others equals the vector sum of the individual pairwise forces, each calculated as if the rest were absent.
  • Method. Draw each Coulomb force on the chosen charge, resolve into components along convenient axes, sum the components, then combine for the net force.
  • Collinear charges. For three charges on a line the middle charge feels opposing forces; the net force points toward the weaker side and vanishes only in a carefully balanced position.
  • Link to electric field. The net force can be written F = qE, where E is the total field produced at the charge's position by all the other charges.
  • System energy. The potential energy of a charge system is the sum of the pairwise terms kqᵢqⱼ/rᵢⱼ — counted once per pair, never twice.
  • Three charges are handled one pair at a time. The net force on one charge is the vector sum of the forces from the others. Equal perpendicular forces of magnitude F give a resultant F√2 at 45°, not 2F.

Universal Facts

  • Coulomb: F = (1/4πε₀)·q₁q₂/r². Electric field E = F/q = kq/r²; for a line/dipole use superposition.
  • Gauss's law: ∮E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀ — powerful for spherical/cylindrical/planar symmetry.
  • Potential V = kq/r; ΔV = W/q; U of a charge in potential = qV. Equipotential surfaces are ⟂ to field lines.

Summary

Exercises

1.

A 1.0 μC charge at the origin feels 3.0 × 10^−3 N east from one charge and 4.0 × 10^−3 N north from another. Find the net force.

Steps
  • The forces are perpendicular.
  • R = √(3.0^2 + 4.0^2) × 10^−3 = 5.0 × 10^−3 N.
  • tanθ = 4/3, so θ = 53° north of east.
  • Do not add 3 and 4 to get 7.
  • The third charge, if it sat on the test charge, is not a separate force on itself.
Answer

5.0 × 10^−3 N at 53° north of east.
