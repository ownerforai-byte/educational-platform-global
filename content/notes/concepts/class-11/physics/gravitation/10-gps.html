Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Gravity as Navigation

    The Global Positioning System puts 24+ satellites in precisely known orbits and asks a simple question: how long does a radio signal take to reach me? Time-of-flight gives distance; distance from four satellites pins down position. Newton's gravitation determines the orbits; relativity corrects the clocks.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 How GPS works

    1. Constellation: at least 24 satellites in six orbital planes, ~20 200 km altitude, period ≈ 12 hours — visible from any point on Earth at any time (4+ satellites overhead).
    2. Each satellite broadcasts its identity, exact orbital position and an atomic-clock timestamp.
    3. Receiver measures signal travel time t; distance = c×t (pseudorange).
    4. Trilateration: one satellite puts you on a sphere; two → a circle; three → two points (one discarded); the fourth satellite resolves the receiver's clock error — hence 4 satellites minimum.

    2.2 The gravitational connection

    • Satellite orbits follow v=GM/r​ and T=2πr3/GM​ — the constellation's geometry is Newtonian gravitation in action.
    • Relativistic clock corrections: at 20 200 km, satellite clocks run faster than ground clocks by ~38 μs/day (special relativity slows them 7 μs; general relativity's weaker gravity speeds them 45 μs). Uncorrected, GPS positions would drift by ~10 km per day.

    2.3 Accuracy and error sources

    Error sourceEffect
    Ionosphere/troposphere delaymetres — modelled or dual-frequency corrected
    Multipath reflectionmetres near buildings
    Satellite clock/orbit errorcontrolled by ground segment
    With corrections (DGPS)sub-metre

    2.4 Applications

    Navigation (roads, aviation, maritime), surveying and mapping, geodesy (plate motion, earthquake displacement), time synchronization for networks and power grids, agriculture and logistics.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 33: GPS orbits obey the same T2∝r3 law — 12-hour period → ~20 200 km radius.
    • To Topic 36: geostationary satellites serve communication; GPS deliberately uses faster, multi-plane orbits for global coverage and accuracy.
    • To Topic 31: the gravitational redshift part of the clock correction is g-field physics at scale.

Summary

GPS places 24+ satellites in six orbital planes at ~20 200 km with 12-hour periods — orbits computed from the same gravitation as any satellite: v=GM/r​, T2∝r3. Each satellite broadcasts an atomic-clock timestamp; the receiver multiplies travel time by c to get pseudoranges and trilaterates: three satellites fix a position, the fourth solves the receiver's clock error. Gravity enters twice — fixing the orbits and demanding a +38 μs/day relativistic clock correction (without it, errors grow ~10 km/day). Applications span navigation, surveying, geodesy, and the time synchronization underpinning networks and power grids.
