Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — A Weight That Depends on Where

    Step up a mountain and you weigh a little less; dive into a mine and you weigh less too — but for different reasons. Altitude thins the inverse-square pull by moving you away from Earth's centre; depth weakens the pull because part of Earth stops pulling you net-ward.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 At the surface

    g0​=R2GM​

    2.2 At height h (altitude)

    Distance from centre becomes R+h: gh​=(R+h)2GM​=g0​(1+Rh​)−2≈g0​(1−R2h​)(h≪R) g decreases with altitude; for h ≪ R the decrease is linear at rate 2g0​/R≈3.1 m/s² per km... (i.e. g drops ≈ 0.31 % per 10 km).

    2.3 At depth d

    Only the mass inside radius (R−d) pulls (shell theorem — outer shells cancel): gd​=(R−d)2G34​π(R−d)3ρ​=34​πGρ(R−d)=g0​(1−Rd​) g decreases linearly with depth, reaching zero at the centre.

    2.4 Comparison

    LocationFormulaBehaviour
    Height hgh​=g0​(1−2h/R) approxQuadratic → linear decrease
    Depth dgd​=g0​(1−d/R)Exactly linear decrease
    Centre0Zero

    2.5 Worked numericals

    Q (3m, 2021): The mass of a planet is 8 times that of the earth and its radius is twice that of the earth. Find the acceleration due to gravity on the surface of the planet. (g = 9.8 m/s²) gp​=Rp2​GMp​​=(2R)2G(8M)​=2R2GM​=2×9.8=19.6 m/s2

    Q (NEB-style): Find g at depth 3.2 km (R = 6400 km). gd​=9.8(1−3.2/6400)=9.8×0.9995=9.795 m/s².

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 29: these are evaluations of g=GM/r2 at different radii.
    • To density: depth formula assumes uniform density — real Earth's core is denser, so actual g stays nearly constant in the upper mantle.
    • To satellites: weightlessness in orbit is not zero gravity — the field is ~89% of surface value at ISS height; free fall removes the sensation of weight.

    1. Show that g decreases linearly with depth (4m).
    2. At what height does g become half its surface value? (3m — h = R(√2 − 1) ≈ 2650 km)

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Using (1−h/R) for altitude — the altitude factor is (1+h/R)−2.
    2. Forgetting the shell theorem in the depth derivation.
    3. Saying g is zero in orbit — it is the apparent weight that vanishes in free fall.

Specific Conditions

  • Uniform Density Assumption: The depth formula gd​=g(1−d/R) assumes Earth has uniform density. Real Earth has varying density, so actual variation is more complex.
  • Small Altitude Approximation: gh​≈g(1−2h/R) is valid only for h≪R. For large heights, use full inverse-square formula.
  • Latitude Effect: Rotation effect on g depends on latitude — zero at poles, maximum at equator.

Formulas

    • g_h = GM/(R+h)² ≈ g₀(1 − 2h/R)
    • g_d = g₀(1 − d/R)
    • Planet: g = GM/R²

Key Points

  • gh​=g0​(1+h/R)−2≈g0​(1−2h/R); gd​=g0​(1−d/R); g = 0 at the centre.

Important Statements

  • "The value of g decreases with altitude quadratically and with depth linearly, vanishing at the centre."

Important Notes

  • Verified PYQ: planet surface gravity (2021, 3m).

Exam Short Tricks

  • "Planet scaling: g_p/g = (M_p/M)(R/R_p)²."

Exam Notes

  • Master the ratio method — the 2021 question is pure ratio, no G needed.

Examples

  • NEB 2021 — planet 8M, 2R: g_p = (8/4)g = 19.6 m/s².

Practice Questions

  • Numerical (3m): At what height above Earth's surface does g become half its surface value? — GM/(R+h)² = GM/2R² → (R+h)² = 2R² → h = R(√2−1) = 6400×0.414 = 2650 km.

Practice

  • Where is g larger: 3.2 km up or 3.2 km down?

Universal Facts

  • At the ISS (~400 km) g ≈ 8.7 m/s² — astronauts float because they fall with their spacecraft, not because gravity vanished.

Confusion

  • Altitude vs depth: both decrease g, but altitude does so quadratically (≈ 2h/R) while depth is exactly linear (d/R).
  • Weightlessness ≠ zero gravity: in orbit g is still ~90% of surface value; free fall removes apparent weight.

Mcs

1.

Acceleration due to gravity at height h (h << R) is approximately:

Options
  • g(1 + 2h/R)
  • g(1 - 2h/R)
  • g(1 - h/R)
  • g(1 + h/R)
Answer

B

Explanation

gh​=g(R/(R+h))2≈g(1−2h/R) using binomial expansion.

2.

Acceleration due to gravity at depth d below surface:

Options
  • g(1 + d/R)
  • g(1 - d/R)
  • gd/R
  • g(1 - d²/R²)
Answer

B

Explanation

Assuming uniform density, gd​=g(1−d/R) — decreases linearly with depth.

3.

At Earth's center, acceleration due to gravity is:

Options
  • Maximum
  • Zero
  • Equal to surface value
  • Twice surface value
Answer

B

Explanation

At center, mass is symmetrically distributed — net gravitational pull is zero.

4.

For equal small height h and depth d, the decrease in g is:

Options
  • Same at height and depth
  • Greater at depth
  • Greater at height
  • Zero at both
Answer

C

Explanation

Height: Δg ≈ -2gh/R; Depth: Δg = -gd/R. Height decrease is twice depth decrease.

5.

g is maximum at:

Options
  • Equator
  • Poles
  • Same everywhere
  • 45° latitude
Answer

B

Explanation

Poles: closer to Earth's center (oblateness) + no centrifugal effect from rotation.

Summary

Surface gravity g0​=GM/R2 is not constant. At height h, gh​=GM/(R+h)2≈g0​(1−2h/R) — a quadratic-then-linear decrease. At depth d, the shell theorem leaves only the inner sphere pulling, giving gd​=g0​(1−d/R) — exactly linear, zero at the centre. The ratio method solves planet problems instantly: a planet with 8× Earth's mass and 2× its radius has gp​=(8/4)×9.8=19.6 m/s² (NEB 2021). Astronauts float not because gravity is absent at 400 km (it is ~8.7 m/s²) but because they and their spacecraft fall together.
