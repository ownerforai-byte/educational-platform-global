Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Thin-Prism Shortcut

    For a slim prism with a small refracting angle, the full prism formula collapses to one tidy line: δ = (n − 1)A. It depends only on the prism angle and the refractive index — the reason thin prisms are so easy to design into optical instruments.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts (NEB 2024)

    2.1 Derivation of δ = (n − 1)A (NEB 2024, 2m)

    For a small-angled prism at near-normal incidence, all angles are small, so Snell's law linearises (sin θ ≈ θ in radians):

    • At the first face: i≈nr1​
    • At the second face: e≈nr2​

    Total deviation: δ=(i−r1​)+(e−r2​)=(nr1​−r1​)+(nr2​−r2​)=(n−1)(r1​+r2​) and since r1​+r2​=A: δ=(n−1)A​

    2.2 Features of the result

    • δ depends only on n and A — not on the angle of incidence (for small i).
    • The deviating power of a small prism is quoted as (n − 1)A.
    • Valid for small angles in radians; typical thin prisms have A ≈ 1–10°.

    2.3 Worked numerical

    Q (NEB-style): A thin prism of angle 4° is made of glass with n = 1.5. Find the deviation. δ=(n−1)A=0.5×4°=2°

    Q (NEB-style): A thin prism produces a deviation of 1.5° at n = 1.5. Find its angle. A=n−1δ​=0.51.5°​=3°.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 75: δ = (n − 1)A is the small-angle limit of the prism formula.
    • To dispersion: δ_violet − δ_red = (n_v − n_r)A — thin prisms separate colours gently.
    • To ophthalmic prisms: optometrists specify prism power in prism dioptres using exactly this relation.

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    3. Applying the formula to large-angle prisms (A = 60°) where it fails.

Specific Conditions

  • Small Angle Requirement: A must be small (< 10°) and angles small for sin θ ≈ θ approximation.
  • Near Minimum Deviation: Formula derived at minimum deviation but approximately valid for nearby incidence angles.
  • Monochromatic Light: μ depends on wavelength — formula applies to one color at a time.

Formulas

    • δ = (n − 1)A
    • i ≈ n r₁; e ≈ n r₂ (small angles)
    • r₁ + r₂ = A

Key Points

  • δ = (n − 1)A for small-angle prisms.

Important Statements

  • "For a small-angled prism the deviation is independent of the angle of incidence and equals (n − 1)A."

Important Notes

  • Verified PYQ: derivation of δ = (n − 1)A (2024, 2m).

Exam Short Tricks

  • "δ = 0.5 × A for crown glass (n = 1.5)."

Exam Notes

  • Quote the validity conditions (small A, small i, radians) for full marks.

Examples

  • A = 4°, n = 1.5 → δ = 2°.

Practice Questions

  • Numerical (2m): A thin prism of A = 6° has n = 1.52. Find δ. — δ = (1.52 − 1) × 6° = 0.52 × 6° = 3.12°.

Practice

  • Why do opticians use thin prisms rather than thick ones?

Universal Facts

  • Optometrists prescribe prism correction in prism dioptres — the same (n − 1)A physics.

Confusion

  • Radians, not degrees: sin θ ≈ θ underpins the derivation — the final δ, however, comes out in whatever unit A is quoted in.
  • δ = (n − 1)A is independent of the angle of incidence — for small i.

Summary

For a small-angled prism at near-normal incidence, Snell's law linearises (sin θ ≈ θ, radians): i ≈ nr₁ and e ≈ nr₂. The total deviation then collapses to δ=(n−1)(r1​+r2​)=(n−1)A — δ = (n − 1)A (NEB 2024, 2m). The result is independent of the angle of incidence, scales with the prism angle and with (n − 1), and defines a thin prism's deviating power. A 4° prism of n = 1.5 deviates by 2°; reversing the formula recovers A from a measured δ. Validity: small A, small i, radians.
