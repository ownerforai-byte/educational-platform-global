Notes

  • The ideal-gas equation combines Boyle’s, Charles’s and Avogadro’s laws: PV=nRT. P is absolute pressure, V volume, n amount in moles, T absolute temperature and R=8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Always convert Celsius using T(K)=t(°C)+273.15 and use consistent units. For 1.00 mol at 300 K in 24.6 L, P≈(8.314×300)/0.0246=1.01×10⁵ Pa.
  • For fixed amount n, PV/T stays constant between equilibrium states: P1V1/T1=P2V2/T2. In a sealed rigid vessel heated from 300 K to 450 K, pressure rises by the factor 450/300=1.5.
  • The model assumes molecules have negligible volume and exert no long-range intermolecular forces except during elastic collisions. Real gases approach ideal behaviour at low pressure and high temperature.
  • Use absolute rather than gauge pressure in the equation. If a gauge pressure is given, add atmospheric pressure before substituting.
  • The ideal-gas equation links pressure, volume, amount and temperature. The temperature must be in kelvin. A celsius value used in P V = n R T is the usual error, and it matters most near the ice point where 0°C is 273 K, not a small correction.

Specific Conditions

  • Ideal Gas Assumptions: The equation PV=nRT assumes (1) molecules are point particles with zero volume, (2) no intermolecular forces except during elastic collisions, (3) motion is random and obeys Newton's laws. These hold best at high temperature and low pressure; real gases deviate near condensation.

Exercises

1.

0.50 mol of ideal gas is at 300 K in a volume of 0.012 m^3. Take R = 8.3 J/mol K. Find the pressure.

Steps
  • P = n R T / V.
  • n R T = 0.50 × 8.3 × 300 = 1245.
  • P = 1245 / 0.012 = 1.04 × 10^5 Pa.
  • 300 K is 27°C, and the kelvin value is the one in the formula.
  • The gas is ideal, so there is no volume correction.
Answer

1.04 × 10^5 Pa.
