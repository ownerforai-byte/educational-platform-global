Summary

Gases have two principal specific heats: C_v at constant volume and C_p at constant pressure. For ideal gases, C_p − C_v = R (Mayer's relation). The ratio γ = C_p/C_v depends on molecular degrees of freedom: monatomic γ = 5/3, diatomic γ = 7/5. These values govern adiabatic processes, sound speed, and thermodynamic cycles.

Notes

  • Specific Heat Definition: Amount of heat required to raise temperature of unit mass by 1 K. For gases, value depends on process conditions because gases expand significantly when heated.
  • C_v (Constant Volume): Heat added increases only internal energy (no work done since dV = 0). Q = nC_vΔT = ΔU. For monatomic ideal gas: C_v = (3/2)R. Diatomic at room T: C_v = (5/2)R.
  • C_p (Constant Pressure): Heat added increases internal energy AND does expansion work against external pressure. Q = nC_pΔT = ΔU + PΔV. Always C_p > C_v because extra energy goes into work.
  • Mayer's Relation: C_p − C_v = R for one mole of ideal gas. Derived from H = U + PV and ideal gas law. Per unit mass: c_p − c_v = R/M where M is molar mass.
  • Adiabatic Index γ: γ = C_p/C_v. Monatomic: γ = 5/3 ≈ 1.67. Diatomic (room T): γ = 7/5 = 1.40. Polyatomic: γ ≈ 1.33. Determines adiabatic process behavior: PV^γ = constant.
  • Degrees of Freedom f: Each quadratic DOF contributes (1/2)R to C_v per mole. Monatomic: f = 3 (translational) → C_v = 3R/2. Diatomic: f = 5 (3 trans + 2 rot) → C_v = 5R/2. Vibrational modes add R each (kinetic + potential) but activate only at high T.
  • Temperature Dependence: At very low T, rotational/vibrational modes freeze out (quantum effect). C_v decreases below classical prediction. At high T, vibrational modes activate, increasing C_v toward equipartition limit.
  • Experimental Measurement: C_v measured in bomb calorimeter (fixed volume). C_p measured in flow calorimeter or derived from C_v + R. Adiabatic expansion methods (Clement-Desormes) determine γ directly.
  • Applications: γ determines speed of sound v = √(γRT/M), adiabatic lapse rate in atmosphere, efficiency of Carnot and Otto cycles, and shock wave properties.

Specific Conditions

  • Ideal Gas Assumption: Mayer's relation C_p − C_v = R holds exactly only for ideal gases. Real gases show deviations at high P or low T.
  • Temperature Range Matters: Degrees of freedom activated depend on T. Room-temperature diatomic values assume rotation active but vibration frozen.
  • Per Mole vs Per Mass: Molar heat capacities use R; specific (mass-based) use R/M. Always check units in problems.
  • Quasi-static Processes: Definitions assume reversible, quasi-static heating. Rapid non-equilibrium heating gives different effective heat capacities.

Key Points

  • C_p > C_v because constant-pressure heating includes expansion work.
  • Mayer's relation: C_p − C_v = R for ideal gases.
  • γ = C_p/C_v determines adiabatic behavior and sound speed.
  • Degrees of freedom dictate heat capacity values via equipartition.
  • Monatomic γ = 5/3; diatomic γ = 7/5 at ordinary temperatures.
  • Vibrational modes increase C_v at high temperatures.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Molar heat capacity at constant pressure exceeds that at constant volume by the gas constant R.
  • Statement 2: The ratio γ = C_p/C_v is always greater than unity for any gas.
  • Statement 3: Heat capacity values reflect the number of active molecular degrees of freedom.
  • Statement 4: Adiabatic processes obey PV^γ = constant where γ depends on molecular structure.
  • Statement 5: Speed of sound in a gas is proportional to √γ, linking thermodynamics to acoustics.

Important Notes

  • For air (mostly N₂ + O₂), γ ≈ 1.40 at STP — matches diatomic prediction.
  • Steam (H₂O, polyatomic) has γ ≈ 1.33; helium (monatomic) has γ ≈ 1.67.
  • At cryogenic temperatures, H₂ behaves as monatomic (rotation frozen): γ → 5/3.
  • NEB frequently asks derivation of C_p − C_v = R and calculation of γ for given molecular types.

Exam Short Tricks

  • C_p − C_v = R: memorize as 'difference equals gas constant'.
  • γ = 1 + 2/f: quick formula from DOF count.
  • Monatomic: f=3 → γ=5/3; Diatomic: f=5 → γ=7/5.
  • Higher γ → steeper adiabat → faster sound speed.
  • If given C_v, get C_p = C_v + R; if given γ and C_v, get C_p = γC_v.

Examples

  • Argon (monatomic): C_v = 12.47 J/(mol·K), C_p = 20.79 J/(mol·K), γ = 1.67. Difference = 8.32 ≈ R.
  • Nitrogen at 300 K: C_v = 20.8 J/(mol·K) ≈ 5R/2, C_p = 29.1 J/(mol·K) ≈ 7R/2, γ = 1.40.
  • Speed of sound in air at 20°C: v = √(1.4 × 8.314 × 293 / 0.029) ≈ 343 m/s.
  • Adiabatic compression: Air compressed from 1 atm to 8 atm. T₂/T₁ = (P₂/P₁)^((γ−1)/γ) = 8^(0.286) ≈ 1.81. If T₁ = 300 K, T₂ ≈ 543 K.

Universal Facts

  • Julius Robert von Mayer first stated C_p − C_v = R in 1841, contributing to energy conservation principle.
  • Einstein and Debye models explain low-temperature heat capacity anomalies using quantum statistics.
  • Atmospheric adiabatic lapse rate (~9.8 K/km dry) derives directly from γ and gravitational acceleration.
  • Supersonic flight creates shock waves whose strength depends on γ of the surrounding gas.

Confusion

  • Thinking C_p and C_v are material constants independent of process: They are defined FOR specific processes (constant P or V).
  • Using R without checking molar vs mass basis: Molar uses R = 8.314; specific uses R/M.
  • Assuming γ is always 1.4: Only true for diatomic gases near room temperature. Monatomic and polyatomic differ.
  • Believing vibrational modes always contribute: They activate only above characteristic temperatures (e.g., ~2000 K for N₂).
  • Confusing heat capacity with thermal conductivity: Heat capacity stores energy; conductivity transfers it. Different physical quantities.
