Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Matter Made of Molecules

    Everything around you is a swarm of molecules: in solids they vibrate in place, in liquids they slide past each other, in gases they fly freely. Molecular properties — size, number, spacing, intermolecular forces — explain the three states of matter from one picture.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Molecular picture of matter

    • Matter consists of a very large number of molecules (NA​=6.022×1023 per mole) separated by spaces.
    • Molecules attract each other with forces that vary strongly with separation: negligible far apart, strong at close range (attractive, then strongly repulsive below ~10⁻¹⁰ m).
    • Molecules are in ceaseless random motion; the motion's character defines the state.

    2.2 The three states compared

    PropertySolidLiquidGas
    Molecular spacingTightest (~10⁻¹⁰ m)Intermediate~10× larger
    MotionVibration about fixed positionsVibration + translation (sliding)Rapid random translation
    ForcesStrong (lattice)ModerateNegligible
    Shape/VolumeFixed/fixedFixed volume, takes container shapeFills any container
    CompressibilityNegligibleVery smallLarge

    2.3 Molecular size and number (orders of magnitude)

    • A water molecule: ~3×10⁻¹⁰ m across; one drop of water holds ~10²¹ molecules.
    • Avogadro's number NA​=6.022×1023 /mol links microscopic and macroscopic: n=N/NA​.
    • Mean free path of air molecules at STP: ~10⁻⁷ m — a molecule travels ~100 molecular diameters between collisions.

    2.4 Worked numerical

    Q (NEB-style): How many molecules are in 2 g of hydrogen (H₂, M = 2 g/mol)? n=1 mol → N=nNA​=6.022×1023 molecules.

    Q (NEB-style): Estimate the number of molecules in 1 cm³ of an ideal gas at STP (Loschmidt number). n=RTPV​=8.31×2731.013×105×10−6​=4.47×10−8 mol → N=2.7×1019 molecules/cm³.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 63: the kinetic model quantifies the molecular motion into pressure and temperature.
    • To Elasticity (Unit 8): interatomic forces' linear region is Hooke's law.
    • To latent heat (Unit 11): phase changes are changes in intermolecular potential energy.

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Forgetting to halve the molar mass for diatomic molecules (H₂, O₂).
    2. Confusing Avogadro's number (per mole) with the number of molecules in a sample.
    3. Claiming gas molecules exert large forces on each other — they are negligible except in collisions.

Confusion

  • Moles vs molecules: divide mass by molar mass to get moles; multiply by N_A to get molecules.
  • Diatomic masses: H₂ is 2 g/mol, O₂ is 32 g/mol — count both atoms.

Specific Conditions

  • State-Dependent Molecular Spacing: In gases, intermolecular spacing ≫ molecular diameter (∼10×); in liquids, spacing ≈ molecular diameter; in solids, molecules are locked in a lattice. This spacing determines the dominant energy (KE in gases, PE in solids) and compressibility.
  • Intermolecular vs Intramolecular Forces: Intermolecular forces (van der Waals, H-bonding, dipole-dipole) are weak (∼1−100 kJ/mol) and govern phase changes. Intramolecular (chemical) bonds are strong (∼100−1000 kJ/mol) and govern molecular structure. Phase transitions break intermolecular bonds, not intramolecular ones.
  • Mean Free Path λ∝1/(nσ): The average distance between collisions λ=1/(2​nσ) decreases with number density n and collision cross-section σ. At STP for air, λ≈68 nm; at low pressure (vacuum), λ can reach kilometers, enabling ballistic transport.

Summary

Matter is a swarm of molecules: ~10²³ of them per mole (NA​=6.022×1023), each ~10⁻¹⁰ m across, attracting strongly at close range and repelling fiercely below that. The state of matter is set by spacing and force balance: solids vibrate about fixed lattice sites (fixed shape), liquids slide past neighbours (fixed volume, fluid shape), gases fly nearly freely through ~10× wider spaces (compressible, container-filling). A drop of water holds ~10²¹ molecules; a cubic centimetre of air at STP holds 2.7×10¹⁹. The kinetic theory of the next topics turns this picture into pressure and temperature.
