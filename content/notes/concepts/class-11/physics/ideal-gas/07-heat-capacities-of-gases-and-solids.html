Notes

  • Boltzmann constant: kB​=R/NA​=1.38 imes10−23 J/K. It connects microscopic and macroscopic thermodynamics. Named after Ludwig Boltzmann (1844-1906).
  • RMS speed: vrms​=sqrt3RT/M, where M is molar mass in kg/mol. At STP (273 K), vrms​ for air is about 479 m/s. At 300 K, about 484 m/s. Lighter gases move faster on average.
  • Most probable speed: vp​=sqrt2RT/M. This is the speed at which the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution peaks. At 300 K for air: vp​approx364 m/s. At 300 K for H₂: vp​approx1650 m/s.
  • Mean speed: vmean​=sqrt8RT/piM. The average of all molecular speeds. At 300 K for air: vmean​approx464 m/s. At 300 K for H₂: vmean​approx1760 m/s.
  • Heat capacities - monatomic gases: CV​=\ rac32R, CP​=\ rac52R. The ratio gamma=CP​/CV​=5/3approx1.67. Monatomic gases (He, Ne, Ar) only have translational degrees of freedom.
  • Heat capacities - diatomic gases: CV​=\ rac52R, CP​=\ rac72R at ordinary temperatures (gamma=7/5=1.4). Includes 3 translational + 2 rotational DOF. At high temperature, vibration adds more, so CV​ ightarrow4R, CP​ ightarrow5R, gamma ightarrow9/7approx1.29.
  • Dulong-Petit law for solids: Molar heat capacity of most solid elements at room temperature approx3Rapprox25 J/(mol·K). This comes from 3 quadratic degrees of freedom per atom (one for each spatial axis), each contributing R per mole of atoms.
  • Why heat capacities differ: The ratio CP​/CV​=gamma matters for adiabatic processes (PV^\\gamma = \ ext{const}). This affects everything from engine cycles to atmospheric science. gammaapprox1.4 for air is crucial in meteorology.
  • Experimental verification: Heat capacities were measured by Regnault and others in the 19th century. The agreement with kinetic theory predictions was one of the great successes of molecular physics.
  • Units: kB​=1.38 imes10−23 J/K. CP​,CV​ have units J/(mol·K). Gas constant R has the same units.
  • Connection to energy: Each degree of freedom contributes \ rac12R per mole or \ rac12kB​ per molecule. 3 translational DOF ightarrow3 imes\ rac12R=\ rac32R for CV​
  • Heat capacity definition: The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by 1 K (or 1°C). It depends on the amount of substance and the conditions (constant volume or constant pressure).
  • Specific heat capacity (c): Heat required to raise 1 kg of substance by 1 K. Units: J/(kg·K). Water has c = 4186 J/(kg·K) at 20°C. This is the highest of any common substance.
  • Molar heat capacity (C): Heat required to raise 1 mole by 1 K. Units: J/(mol·K). For water (M = 0.018 kg/mol): C=4186×0.018=75.3 J/(mol·K).
  • Constant volume molar heat capacity CV​: For an ideal gas, CV​=2f​R, where f is the number of active degrees of freedom. Monatomic: CV​=23​R=12.5 J/(mol·K). Diatomic: CV​=25​R=20.8 J/(mol·K).
  • Constant pressure molar heat capacity CP​: CP​=CV​+R. This extra R is the work the gas does expanding against external pressure when heated at constant pressure. Monatomic: CP​=25​R=20.8 J/(mol·K). Diatomic: CP​=27​R=29.1 J/(mol·K).
  • Mayer's relation: CP​−CV​=R. This fundamental relation holds for all ideal gases, independent of molecular structure. Derived from the first law of thermodynamics and the ideal gas law.
  • Heat capacity ratio γ=CP​/CV​: For monatomic ideal gas: γ=5/3≈1.67. For diatomic (room temp): γ=7/5=1.4. For triatomic (nonlinear): γ=8/6=4/3≈1.33.
  • Degrees of freedom and CV​: Each quadratic degree of freedom contributes 21​R to CV​. Monatomic (3 trans): CV​=3×21​R=23​R. Linear diatomic (3 trans + 2 rot): CV​=5×21​R=25​R. Non-linear triatomic (3 trans + 3 rot): CV​=6×21​R=3R.
  • Vibrational modes at high temperature: Each vibrational mode contributes 2 degrees of freedom (kinetic + potential), so 2×21​R=R per mode. At room temperature, vibration is usually frozen out. At high T, CV​ increases further.
  • Dulong-Petit law for solids: At room temperature, the molar heat capacity of most solid elements is approximately C=3R≈25 J/(mol·K). This comes from 3 quadratic DOF per atom: 23​kB​ per atom ×NA​=23​R per mole, but since each atom can vibrate in 3D (kinetic + potential), total is 3R.
  • Why Dulong-Petit law works: At high temperature, all degrees of freedom are excited according to equipartition. Each atom has 3 vibrational modes (x, y, z), each with kinetic + potential, giving 6 DOF. So C=6×21​R=3R.
  • Failure of Dulong-Petit at low T: At low temperatures, heat capacity drops toward zero. This was explained by Einstein (1907) using quantum mechanics: vibrational energy is quantized, and at low T, most oscillators are in the ground state, so they don't contribute to heat capacity.
  • Debye model: Peter Debye (1912) refined Einstein's model by considering the spectrum of vibrational frequencies (phonons) in a solid. The Debye model gives C∝T3 at very low temperatures, which matches experiment much better than Einstein's model.
  • Applications: Heat capacities determine how much a gas heats up for a given heat input. In isochoric heating (V constant): Q=nCV​ΔT. In isobaric heating (P constant): Q=nCP​ΔT. The difference is important for engines operating at constant pressure.

Confusion

  • The ratio gamma=CP​/CV​ is NOT always 5/3 or 7/5. It depends on temperature and molecular structure. At very high temperatures, vibrational modes activate, changing gamma.
  • The formula CV​=\ rac32R applies ONLY to monatomic ideal gases. For diatomic or polyatomic gases, rotational (and sometimes vibrational) modes contribute.
  • Dulong's law (Capprox3R) for solids works well at room temperature but fails at very low temperatures (quantum effects freeze out degrees of freedom). Einstein and Debye developed low-temperature models.
  • The Boltzmann constant kB​ is NOT the same as the gas constant R. kB​=1.38 imes10−23 J/K per molecule. R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) per mole. R=NA​kB​.
  • Most probable speed is NOT the average speed. For the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution: vp​cdotsqrt2<vmean​<vrms​. The order is always vp​<vmean​<vrms​ at the same temperature.
  • Heat capacity is NOT the same as specific heat. Heat capacity C is for the whole object; specific heat c is per unit mass. Molar heat capacity Cm​ is per mole. They are related: C=mc for uniform objects.
  • CP​=CV​. Many students think the heat capacity is the same regardless of conditions. At constant pressure, the gas expands and does work, requiring more heat input to achieve the same temperature rise. CP​>CV​ always.
  • The Dulong-Petit law (C≈3R) works only at sufficiently high temperatures (well above Debye temperature). Below that, quantum effects dominate and heat capacity drops. For diamond, this 'high temperature' is over 2000 K!
  • Degrees of freedom 'freeze out' at different temperatures. At room temperature: translational (always active), rotational (active for most molecules), vibrational (usually frozen). The textbook CV​ values assume rotational but not vibrational modes are active.
  • Heat capacity depends on temperature for real substances. The values CV​=25​R for diatomic gases are approximate room-temperature values. Over a wide temperature range, CV​ can change significantly.

Practice

  • Calculate the root-mean-square speed of nitrogen molecules (N₂, M = 0.028 kg/mol) at 350 K. Solution: vrms​=sqrt3RT/M=sqrt3 imes8.314 imes350/0.028=sqrt3471.8/0.028=sqrt123,993=352 m/s.
  • Find the most probable speed of helium atoms (He, M = 0.004 kg/mol) at 400 K. Solution: vp​=sqrt2RT/M=sqrt2 imes8.314 imes400/0.004=sqrt6651.2/0.004=sqrt1662800=1290 m/s.
  • Calculate the mean speed of oxygen molecules (O₂, M = 0.032 kg/mol) at 273 K. Solution: vmean​=sqrt8RT/piM=sqrt8 imes8.314 imes273/(pi imes0.032)=sqrt18136/0.1005=sqrt180,458=425 m/s.
  • If the rms speed of helium at 300 K is 1250 m/s, what is the rms speed at 600 K? Solution: vrms​proptosqrtT. vrms​(600)=vrms​(300) imessqrt600/300=1250 imessqrt2=1768 m/s.
  • A container has 1 mol of gas at 300 K. What is the total internal energy for (a) monatomic gas? Solution: U=\ rac32nRT=\ rac32 imes1 imes8.314 imes300=3741 J.
  • Find the heat capacity ratio gamma for a diatomic gas at room temperature. Solution: gamma=CP​/CV​=(7/2R)/(5/2R)=7/5=1.4.
  • Calculate the heat required to raise the temperature of 2 mol of helium (monatomic) by 50 K at (a) constant volume, (b) constant pressure. Solution: (a) Q=nCV​ΔT=2×(3/2×8.314)×50=2×12.47×50=1247 J. (b) Q=nCP​ΔT=2×(5/2×8.314)×50=2×20.79×50=2079 J. Note: extra heat goes into expansion work.
  • Find the molar heat capacity at constant volume for CO₂ (linear triatomic). At room temperature, ignore vibration. Solution: Linear molecule has 3 translational + 2 rotational = 5 DOF. CV​=25​R=20.8 J/(mol·K). CP​=CV​+R=29.1 J/(mol·K). γ=29.1/20.8=1.4.
  • A 1 kg iron block (c = 449 J/(kg·K)) is heated from 20°C to 80°C. Find the heat absorbed. Solution: Q=mcΔT=1×449×(80−20)=449×60=26,940 J ≈27 kJ.
  • Show that for an ideal gas, CP​−CV​=R. Solution: At constant volume: QV​=nCV​ΔT=ΔU (no PV work). At constant pressure: QP​=nCP​ΔT=ΔU+PΔV. For ideal gas: PΔV=nRΔT. So nCP​ΔT=nCV​ΔT+nRΔT→CP​−CV​=R.
  • Compare the heat required to raise 1 mol of H₂ vs 1 mol of Xe by 10 K at constant volume. Solution: Both are diatomic (H₂) or monatomic (Xe). For H₂ (diatomic, CV​=5/2R): Q=1×20.8×10=208 J. For Xe (monatomic, CV​=3/2R): Q=1×12.5×10=125 J. More complex molecules require more heat.

Universal Facts

  • The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution was first derived by James Clerk Maxwell in 1860 and later generalized by Ludwig Boltzmann. It describes the distribution of speeds in an ideal gas and is one of the most important results of kinetic theory.
  • At 300 K, the three speed measures for nitrogen (M=28 g/mol) are: most probable vp​approx422 m/s, mean speed vmean​approx477 m/s, and rms speed vrms​approx517 m/s. The ratios are always vp​:vmean​:vrms​=sqrt2:sqrt8/pi:sqrt3approx1.414:1.596:1.732.
  • The Boltzmann constant kB​ was first introduced by Max Planck in his derivation of the blackbody radiation formula (1900). He used it to connect the energy of oscillators to temperature: E=kB​T.
  • Dulong and Petit's law (Capprox3R for solids) was discovered in 1819. It took nearly 150 years to understand why it works - the kinetic theory's 3 degrees of freedom per atom (1859-1870s) provided the explanation.
  • The ratio gamma=CP​/CV​ determines whether sound speed is faster in an adiabatic process: vsound​=sqrtgammaRT/M. For air (gammaapprox1.4), vsound​approx343 m/s at 300 K. For helium (gamma=5/3approx1.67), vsound​approx965 m/s at 300 K.
  • The law of Dulong and Petit (1819) states that the specific heat of solids is inversely proportional to atomic weight. This was an important empirical law before atomic theory was fully accepted.
  • Maxwell's equipartition theorem (1860s) explained why heat capacity should be proportional to the number of degrees of freedom. Each quadratic term in the energy contributes 21​kB​T per molecule.
  • The failure of classical equipartition at low temperatures was one of the first major problems solved by quantum mechanics. Einstein's 1907 paper on specific heats was one of the first successful applications of Planck's quantum hypothesis.
  • The Debye model (1912) treated vibrations in a solid as sound waves (phonons) with a range of frequencies. It correctly predicted C∝T3 at very low temperatures, for which Debye received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1936.
  • In calorimetry, water's high specific heat (4186 J/(kg·K)) makes it an excellent coolant and heat storage medium. This is why coastal areas have milder climates than inland — the ocean absorbs/releases enormous amounts of heat with small temperature changes.
