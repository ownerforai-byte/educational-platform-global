Current-Voltage Relations: Ohmic and Non-Ohmic Resistance

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Straight Lines and Curves

    Plot current against voltage: a metal wire gives a straight line, a diode gives a kinked curve, a bulb's filament bends downward. The shape of the I–V graph sorts conductors into ohmic and non-ohmic — and reveals where Ohm's law ends.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Ohmic conductors

    An ohmic conductor obeys Ohm's law: I ∝ V at constant temperature, giving a straight I–V line through the origin. R = V/I is constant. Examples: metals at steady temperature, manganin, constantan (alloys with tiny temperature coefficients).

    2.2 Non-ohmic conductors

    Non-ohmic conductors show I–V curves — R = V/I varies with V:

    • Filament lamp: current heats the filament; resistance rises with temperature — the curve flattens at high V.
    • Semiconductor diode: conducts strongly only in forward bias beyond ~0.7 V; nearly blocking in reverse — an asymmetric curve.
    • Electrolytes and thermistors: non-linear, often with falling R as current heats them.

    2.3 Distinguishing feature

    The test is the I–V graph: straight line through the origin ⇒ ohmic; any curvature or asymmetry ⇒ non-ohmic. Resistance "at a point" on a curve is often quoted as dynamic resistance dV/dI.

    2.4 Worked reasoning

    Q (NEB-style): Why does the resistance of a filament lamp increase as it glows? Current heats the tungsten filament to ~2500 K; lattice vibrations intensify, impeding electron drift more strongly. Since R grows with temperature, the I–V curve bends — the lamp is non-ohmic while glowing (though nearly ohmic when cold and at low current).

    Q (NEB-style): Sketch and compare the I–V characteristics of a metal wire and a diode. Metal: straight line through the origin in both directions. Diode: essentially zero reverse current; sharp forward rise beyond the knee voltage — a one-way valve.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 99: ohmic behaviour is the validity condition of Ohm's law.
    • To Topic 98: temperature changes the drift-lattice collision rate — the microscopic cause of curvature.
    • To thermistors: falling-R devices enable temperature sensing.

Specific Conditions

  • Ohm's Law Validity: Ohm's law V=IR holds only for ohmic materials at constant temperature and other physical conditions. It is NOT a fundamental law of nature but an empirical observation valid for many conductors.
  • Dynamic vs Static Resistance: For non-ohmic devices, static resistance R=V/I (at a point) differs from dynamic resistance rd​=dV/dI (slope of V-I curve). The dynamic resistance is what matters for small-signal AC analysis.
  • Temperature Must Be Constant: Ohm's law assumes temperature stays constant. As current flows, Joule heating (I2R) raises temperature, changing resistance — this is why filament lamps are non-ohmic.

Formulas

    • Ohmic: I = V/R (R constant)
    • Dynamic resistance: r = dV/dI
    • Filament: R ∝ T (roughly)

Key Points

  • Ohmic: I–V straight line, R constant. Non-ohmic: lamp, diode, thermistor.

Ohm's law is valid for:

Options
  • All materials
  • Only ohmic materials
  • Only semiconductors
  • Only insulators
Answer

Explanation

Ohm's law is an empirical law valid only for ohmic conductors at constant temperature.

2.

The V-I graph of a non-ohmic conductor is:

Options
  • A straight line through origin
  • A curve
  • A horizontal line
  • A vertical line
Answer

B

Explanation

Non-ohmic conductors have non-linear V-I characteristics.

3.

Resistivity ρ depends on:

Options
  • Length of conductor
  • Area of cross-section
  • Material and temperature
  • Applied voltage
Answer

C

Explanation

Resistivity is an intrinsic property of the material and varies with temperature, not dimensions.

4.

A filament lamp is non-ohmic because:

Options
  • Its temperature changes with current
  • It has no resistance
  • It obeys Ohm's law perfectly
  • It is a semiconductor
Answer

A

Explanation

As current increases, the filament heats up, changing its resistance — so V/I is not constant.

5.

Dynamic resistance rd​ is defined as:

Options
  • V/I
  • dV/dI
  • I/V
  • V²/I
Answer

B

Explanation

Dynamic resistance is the slope of the V-I curve at a point: rd​=dV/dI.

Summary

Ohmic conductors — metals at steady temperature, manganin, constantan — give a straight I–V line through the origin: R = V/I constant. Non-ohmic conductors bend the graph: a filament lamp's resistance rises as current heats it; a diode conducts only in forward bias beyond a knee; thermistors' resistance falls with temperature. The graph is the test, and self-heating is the usual cause of curvature. Dynamic resistance dV/dI replaces the simple ratio on curved characteristics. Recognising where Ohm's law applies — and where it silently fails — is the point of this topic.
