Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Tapping a Fraction of a Voltage

    Two resistors in series across a battery form a voltage tap: the junction sits at a fixed fraction of the supply. Slide the tap (a rheostat) and you have a volume control, a dimmer, a sensor readout — the potential divider is electronics' most humble workhorse.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The divider formula

    Two resistors R₁ and R₂ in series across a source V. The same current I = V/(R₁+R₂) flows through both; the voltage across R₂ is: Vout​=V×R1​+R2​R2​​ — the output is a fixed fraction of the input, set purely by the resistance ratio.

    2.2 The potentiometer (variable divider)

    Replace the pair with a rheostat/potentiometer — a resistive track with a sliding contact:

    • The slider divides the track resistance continuously → V_out sweeps smoothly from 0 to V.
    • Volume knobs, lamp dimmers and joystick axes are all potentiometers.

    2.3 The potentiometer as an instrument (NEB 2024, 3m)

    The potentiometer measures emf by null deflection: for a uniform wire carrying steady current, the potential drop per unit length is constant — the balancing length is proportional to emf. Each cell in turn is balanced against the wire (no galvanometer current): E2​E1​​=l2​l1​​ Because readings are taken at null (the cell supplies no current), the true emf — not the terminal voltage — is compared. This is the divider principle made exact.

    2.4 Worked numerical

    Q (NEB-style): A 12 V supply feeds R₁ = 4 kΩ and R₂ = 2 kΩ in series. Find V_out across R₂. Vout​=12×4+22​=4 V

    Q (NEB-style): In a potentiometer, a cell balances at 150 cm; a standard 1.5 V cell balances at 100 cm. Find the unknown emf. E=1.5×100150​=2.25 V.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 101: the divider is a series pair; the current is common.
    • To Topic 103: the potentiometer beats a voltmeter because it draws no current — no internal-resistance error.
    • To sensors: LDRs and thermistors in a divider convert light and heat into voltage.

Summary

A potential divider — two series resistors across a source — delivers an output fraction of the input: Vout​=VR1​+R2​R2​​; a potentiometer makes the fraction continuously adjustable (volume controls, dimmers). As an instrument, the potentiometer exploits a uniform wire's constant drop per unit length: balancing each cell against the wire at null deflection compares emfs by lengths, E1​/E2​=l1​/l2​ (NEB 2024, 3m) — accurate because at null the cell supplies no current, so no internal-resistance drop corrupts the reading. Dividers also translate sensor resistance into voltage, the front door of every measurement chain.

Specific Conditions

  • No-Load Assumption: The simple voltage divider formula Vout​=Vin​R1​+R2​R2​​ assumes the output current is zero (infinite load resistance). Any load drawing current changes the effective resistance of the lower branch.
  • Loading Effect Formalism: When a load RL​ is connected across R2​, the effective resistance becomes R2′​=R2​∥RL​=R2​+RL​R2​RL​​. The new output voltage is Vout​=Vin​R1​+R2′​R2′​​.
  • Design Trade-off: To minimize loading effects, the divider resistance (R1​+R2​) should be much smaller than RL​. However, this increases power dissipation (P=Vin2​/(R1​+R2​)). The designer must balance voltage stability against power efficiency.
