Notes

  • Ohmic conductor: obeys Ohm's law. The I-V graph is a straight line through the origin. Resistance is constant.
  • Non-ohmic conductor: does not obey Ohm's law. The I-V graph is curved. Resistance changes with voltage or current.
  • Examples of non-ohmic: diode (conducts easily in one direction only), filament lamp (resistance increases with temperature as current increases), thermistor (resistance decreases with temperature).
  • Why filament lamp is non-ohmic: as current increases, the filament heats up, increasing its resistivity. The I-V curve bends because R is not constant.
  • Dynamic resistance: Rd​=dV/dI at a point on the I-V curve. For non-ohmic devices, this is the useful measure of resistance at a specific operating point.
  • Forward bias diode: current flows easily once the voltage exceeds the threshold (approx0.7 V for silicon, approx0.3 V for germanium). Below threshold, very little current flows.
  • Reverse bias diode: almost no current flows (except a tiny leakage current). At the breakdown voltage, current increases sharply and the diode may be damaged.
  • Thermistor: a semiconductor device whose resistance decreases sharply with temperature (NTC type). Used as temperature sensors and inrush current limiters.
  • Zener diode: operates in reverse breakdown at a precise voltage. It maintains a nearly constant voltage across it, used as a voltage regulator.
  • Filament lamp I-V curve: starts linearly at low voltage (cool filament, low resistance). At higher voltages, the curve bends toward the voltage axis as the filament heats and resistance increases.
  • Temperature coefficient of filament: tungsten has alphaapprox0.0045 circC−1. A 100 W, 230 V bulb has cold resistance of about 50,Omega but hot resistance of 529,Omega.

Confusion

  • Ohm's law V=IR is a definition of resistance, not a law that all materials obey. Only ohmic conductors have a linear I-V relationship. For non-ohmic devices, R=V/I is still defined but varies.
  • A filament lamp becomes a better conductor (lower resistance) when cold. This is why bulbs often burn out at the moment they are switched on - the initial current surge is 10 times the steady-state current.
  • The dynamic resistance dV/dI is different from the static resistance V/I. For a non-ohmic device, the static resistance is V/I while the dynamic resistance is the slope of the I-V curve at that point.
  • A diode is not simply a 'one-way valve' for current. In reverse bias, a tiny leakage current (nA to muA range) still flows. In forward bias, the current is exponentially related to voltage, not linearly.
  • Thermistors are not simply temperature-dependent resistors. Their resistance changes exponentially with temperature: R=R0​e\eta(1/T−1/T0​), which makes them highly sensitive to small temperature changes.

Practice

  • A filament lamp has a resistance of 20,Omega when cold and 240,Omega when glowing at full brightness. If connected to a 12 V supply, find the current when cold and when glowing. Solution: Cold: I=V/R=12/20=0.6 A. Glowing: I=V/R=12/240=0.05 A. The inrush current is 12 times the steady-state current.
  • A silicon diode has a forward voltage drop of 0.7 V at a current of 10 mA. It is connected in series with a 500 Omega resistor and a 5 V battery. Find the current. Solution: The diode drops 0.7 V, so the resistor sees 5−0.7=4.3 V. I=4.3/500=8.6 mA.
  • A thermistor has resistance R0​=10, extkOmega at 25circC and R=2, extkOmega at 50circC. Find the resistance at 0circC assuming a linear temperature coefficient. Solution: alpha=(R0​−R)/(RcdotDeltaT)=(10000−2000)/(2000 imes25)=0.16 circC−1. At 0circC: R=R0​/(1+alphacdot25)=10000/(1+4)=2000,Omega.
  • A I-V graph of a device shows that at V=3 V, the current is 0.15 A, and the tangent at that point has slope dI/dV=0.02 A/V. Find the static resistance and dynamic resistance. Solution: Static resistance Rs​=V/I=3/0.15=20,Omega. Dynamic resistance Rd​=1/(dI/dV)=1/0.02=50,Omega.
  • A Zener diode with breakdown voltage 6 V is connected in parallel with a load resistor of 1 kOmega across a 10 V battery with series resistor of 500 Omega. Find the current through the Zener diode. Solution: Voltage across load: 6 V (clamped by Zener). Current through 500 Omega: I=(10−6)/500=8 mA. Current through load: IL​=6/1000=6 mA. Current through Zener: IZ​=8−6=2 mA.

Universal Facts

  • A tungsten filament lamp has a resistance that changes by a factor of 10-15 between cold and hot, making it a classic example of non-ohmic behavior due to temperature-dependent resistivity.
  • The threshold voltage of a silicon diode is approximately 0.7 V because this is the energy needed to overcome the potential barrier of the p-n junction, corresponding to the band gap energy of silicon.
  • Semiconductors have a negative temperature coefficient of resistance (NTC thermistors) because more charge carriers are thermally excited across the band gap, increasing conductivity.
  • Ohmic materials include most metals at constant temperature. Non-ohmic materials include all semiconductors, electrolytes, and any device where temperature or other conditions change with current.
  • The I-V characteristic of a diode is exponential: I=I0​(eeV/kT−1), where I0​ is the reverse saturation current, k is Boltzmann's constant, and T is temperature.

Specific Conditions

  • Series Connection Constraints: In a series circuit, the same current flows through all resistors. The equivalent resistance is the sum: Req​=R1​+R2​+…. The voltage divides proportionally to resistance: Vi​=IRi​.
  • Parallel Connection Constraints: In a parallel circuit, the same voltage appears across all resistors. The equivalent conductance is the sum of conductances: 1/Req​=1/R1​+1/R2​+…. The current divides inversely to resistance: Ii​=V/Ri​.
  • Ideal Wire Assumption: These formulas assume ideal wires with zero resistance connecting the components. Real wire resistance must be included as additional series resistors if significant.
