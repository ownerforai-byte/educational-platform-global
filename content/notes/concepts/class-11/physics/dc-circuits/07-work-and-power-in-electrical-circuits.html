Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Watts in the Wire

    Every appliance is a bargain between voltage, current and power. P = VI and its two disguises — I²R and V²/R — decide the bill, the fuse, the brightness and the burn. Electrical heating is the same physics with intent.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Work, power and the three formulas

    Moving charge q through potential difference V does work W = qV. Power is the rate: P=VI=I2R=RV2​W=VItunit: watt (W) = J/s

    • Series circuit: same I everywhere → compare power with I²R (the larger resistor dissipates more).
    • Parallel circuit: same V across branches → compare with V²/R (the smaller resistor dissipates more).

    2.2 Electrical heating (Joule's law)

    Current through a resistance converts electrical energy to heat: H=I2Rt(joules) — the basis of toasters, kettles, fuses and electric heating; the bane of transmission cables and hot ICs.

    2.3 Practical units

    • Kilowatt-hour (kWh): the energy delivered by 1 kW in 1 hour = 3.6×10⁶ J — the electricity meter's unit.
    • Bulb ratings: a "60 W, 220 V" bulb draws 60/220 ≈ 0.27 A and has R = V²/P ≈ 807 Ω when hot.

    2.4 Worked numericals

    Q (NEB-style): A 220 V, 100 W bulb: find its resistance and the current drawn.

    • I = P/V = 100/220 ≈ 0.45 A
    • R = V²/P = 220²/100 = 484 Ω

    Q (NEB-style): An electric kettle of 1500 W boils 1 kg of water from 25 °C in 4 minutes (c = 4200 J/kg·K). Find the efficiency.

    • Needed: mcΔT = 1 × 4200 × 75 = 3.15×10⁵ J
    • Supplied: 1500 × 240 = 3.6×10⁵ J
    • η = 3.15/3.6 ≈ 87.5%

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 99: P = V²/R compares resistances at fixed voltage; P = I²R at fixed current.
    • To Topic 103: the internal drop Ir dissipates I²r inside the battery — power "lost".
    • To heat (Units 9–11): Joule heating feeds the same calorimetry equations.

  • 4. Exam Orientation and Previous Year Questions (NEB)

    4.1 Verified PYQs from the solved-paper bank

    No verified PYQ for this exact topic in the bank. Practise the three power formulas, bulb-rating numericals and kWh conversions as NEB-style practice (2–3m).

    4.2 Likely question forms

    1. Derive P = VI and state the two other forms (3m).
    2. Bulb rating numerical (2–3m).
    3. Why do transmission cables use high voltage? (2m)

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Using the wrong form for the circuit: I²R for series comparisons, V²/R for parallel.
    2. Forgetting 1 kWh = 3.6×10⁶ J.
    3. Mixing the bulb's hot resistance with its cold resistance.

Confusion

  • Series → compare with I²R; parallel → compare with V²/R — pick the formula whose variable is common.
  • 1 kWh = 3.6×10⁶ J — the meter's unit, not a power.

Practice

  • Why do kettles need thick short elements while transmission lines want thin long ones... or do they?

Universal Facts

  • Grids transmit at hundreds of kV because P_loss = I²R shrinks as I falls for the same delivered power.

Specific Conditions

  • Power Formula Context: P=I2R is used when current is known (series circuits). P=V2/R is used when voltage is known (parallel circuits). Both are derived from P=VI and Ohm's law; using the wrong one gives incorrect results because V and I are coupled through R.
  • Joule Heating is Irreversible: The power P=I2R dissipated in a resistor represents an irreversible conversion of electrical energy to heat. It cannot be recovered within the circuit. This is distinct from energy stored in inductors/capacitors or converted to mechanical work.
  • Energy Units: Electrical energy is measured in joules (SI) or kilowatt-hours (practical). 1extkWh=3.6imes106extJ. Power (watts) is the rate of energy transfer, not energy itself.

Summary

Electrical work W = qV becomes power P=VI=I2R=V2/R (watt). Choose the form by what's common: in series compare I²R (bigger resistor dissipates more); in parallel compare V²/R (smaller resistor wins). Joule heating H=I2Rt converts electrical energy to heat — toasters by design, transmission losses by accident (hence high-voltage grids). Bulb ratings decode as I = P/V and R = V²/P (a 220 V, 100 W bulb draws 0.45 A through 484 Ω hot). The kilowatt-hour, 3.6×10⁶ J, is the commercial unit on every electricity bill.
