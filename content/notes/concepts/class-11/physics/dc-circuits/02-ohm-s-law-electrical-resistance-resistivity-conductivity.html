Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Law of the Linear Circuit

    For a metal wire at steady temperature, doubling the voltage doubles the current. That proportionality — Ohm's law — and its material constants (resistivity, conductivity) turn circuit design into arithmetic.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Ohm's law

    Statement: at constant temperature, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it: V∝I⇒V=IR

    • R, the electrical resistance, is the constant of proportionality (unit: ohm, Ω = V/A).
    • Valid for metallic conductors at constant temperature — not for diodes, electrolytes or lamps while heating.

    2.2 Resistance, resistivity, conductivity

    • Resistance of a wire: R=ρAl​ — longer is harder, fatter is easier.
    • Resistivity ρ (Ω·m): a material property, independent of size; copper ≈ 1.7×10⁻⁸ Ω·m.
    • Conductivity σ = 1/ρ (S/m): the ease of conduction.
    • Temperature: metals' resistance rises with T (more lattice vibration); semiconductors' falls.

    2.3 Factors affecting resistance

    Length l (∝), cross-section A (∝ 1/A), material (ρ), and temperature. Geometry affects R through l/A; the material sets ρ.

    2.4 Worked numericals

    Q (NEB-style): A wire of length 2 m, area 0.5 mm², ρ = 2×10⁻⁷ Ω·m. Find R. R=Aρl​=0.5×10−62×10−7×2​=0.8 Ω

    Q (NEB-style): A wire is stretched to double its length. Find the new resistance. Volume fixed: A halves. R′=ρA/22l​=4R — four times.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Topic 98: Ohm's law emerges from drift: v_d = μE gives I ∝ E ∝ V.
    • To Topic 100: Ohmic conductors keep R constant; non-ohmic ones don't.
    • To series/parallel (Topic 101): R values combine via the rules.

  • 4. Exam Orientation and Previous Year Questions (NEB)

    4.1 Verified PYQs from the solved-paper bank

    No verified PYQ for this exact topic in the bank. Practise the statement, the R = ρl/A numericals and the stretching problem as NEB-style practice (2–3m).

    4.2 Likely question forms

    1. State Ohm's law and its limitations (3m).
    2. R = ρl/A numerical (2–3m).
    3. Stretched-wire ratio problem (2m).

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Applying Ohm's law to non-ohmic devices without comment.
    2. Forgetting mm² → m² conversion (10⁻⁶).
    3. Missing the ×4 in the stretched-wire problem (both l and 1/A change).

Confusion

  • Ohm's law is conditional: constant temperature — a glowing filament defies it.
  • Resistivity belongs to the material; resistance to the object.

Universal Facts

  • Superconductors drop to exactly zero resistance below a critical temperature — ohm's law with R = 0.

Specific Conditions

  • Ohm's Law Validity: Ohm's law (V=IR) applies strictly to ohmic conductors at constant temperature. For metals, resistance increases with temperature, so the law holds only if temperature is held constant (isothermal conditions).
  • Resistivity vs. Resistance: Resistivity (ho) is an intensive material property (independent of geometry), while resistance (R=hoL/A) is an extensive property depending on the conductor's length and cross-section. Temperature changes affect ho, which in turn changes R.
  • Conductivity Definition: Conductivity (σ=1/ho) is the proportionality constant in the microscopic Ohm's law J=σE, where J is current density and E is the electric field. This form is valid for homogeneous, isotropic materials.

Summary

Ohm's law: at constant temperature, V ∝ I in a metallic conductor, or V = IR with R the resistance (ohm). Resistance grows with length and shrinks with area: R=ρl/A, where resistivity ρ (Ω·m) is a material property (copper 1.7×10⁻⁸) and conductivity σ = 1/ρ its inverse. Temperature raises metals' resistance and lowers semiconductors'. Stretching a wire to double its length halves its area — resistance quadruples. The law's limits (non-ohmic devices, heating filaments) matter as much as its statement.
