Notes

  • Definition of Potential Gradient: The potential gradient at a point is the rate of change of electric potential with distance measured in a specified direction, in the limit as the displacement approaches zero. Mathematically, for a uniform field along the x-axis, E=−dxdV​. More generally, the magnitude of the potential gradient equals the magnitude of the electric field: ∣E∣=∣∇V∣. The SI unit of potential gradient is volt per metre (V/m), which is dimensionally identical to N/C since 1 V/m = 1 (J/C)/m = 1 N/C.
  • Vector Form of Potential Gradient: The complete relationship between field and potential is E=−∇V, where ∇=i^∂x∂​+j^​∂y∂​+k^∂z∂​ is the gradient (del) operator. The negative sign is physically essential: the electric field always points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential, exactly as a ball rolls downhill. Written in Cartesian components: Ex​=−∂x∂V​, Ey​=−∂y∂V​, Ez​=−∂z∂V​.
  • Gradient for Standard Charge Distributions: (1) Point charge: V=kQ/r⇒E=−drd​(rkQ​)=r2kQ​, recovering the inverse-square law exactly; (2) Infinite line charge: V=−2πε0​λ​lnr0​r​⇒E=2πε0​rλ​, recovering the 1/r dependence; (3) Infinite plane sheet: V=−2ε0​σ​x⇒E=2ε0​σ​, a constant gradient matching the uniform field; (4) Charged spherical shell (outside): V=rkQ​⇒E=r2kQ​, identical to a point charge at the centre.
  • Graphical Interpretation of Potential Gradient: A graph of potential V against distance r along a field line has slope equal to minus the potential gradient. The graph is a straight line for a uniform field, a hyperbola for a point charge or dipole, and a logarithmic curve for a line charge. Where the graph is steep, the field is strong; where it is flat, the field is weak. At the point of inflection in the potential curve of a point charge's neighbourhood, the gradient and hence the field attain their maximum value.
  • Practical Determination of Potential Gradient: The potential gradient along a conductor or a wire carrying a steady current is measured by the potential drop per unit length, K=lV​, and equals the electric field E in that conductor. In a uniform current-carrying wire of length l and cross-sectional area A carrying current I and having resistivity ρ, K=AreaIR​=AIρ​, a result obtained by combining Ohm's law with E=V/l. This is the standard method for determining the resistivity of a material, and it is also the basis of the potentiometric and Kelvin bridge methods of measurement.
  • The electric field is the negative gradient of the potential. If potential falls as you walk in a certain direction, the field points that way. A uniform field between plates is simply the potential difference divided by the separation, from the positive plate toward the negative plate.

Confusion

  • Omitting the negative sign in E = -dV/dx: The minus sign indicates that the field points toward decreasing potential. Writing E = dV/dx gives the wrong direction whenever V decreases along the chosen axis.
  • Assuming the potential gradient is a vector while differentiating only with respect to one variable: For a general three-dimensional field the gradient is a vector; the scalar expression dV/dx is valid only along a chosen axis where the field is known to be uniform or one-dimensional.
  • Confusing the potential gradient with the gradient of a scalar function in general mathematics: In electrostatics the potential gradient carries a minus sign and is identical in magnitude to the electric field, but a generic mathematical gradient is always positive in magnitude and has no such sign convention.
  • A symbol is not a number until the unit is named.
  • A proportional relation is not an equality until the constant is included.
  • A vector answer needs a direction as well as a magnitude.
  • A special case, such as a right angle or a steady temperature, has to be stated before it is used.

(b) Find the resistivity of the material. Solution: (a) K=lV​=2.01.5​=0.75V/m, which equals the electric field E. (b) From Ohm's law, R=IV​=0.51.5​=3.0Ω. The resistivity is ρ=lRA​=2.0(3.0)(1.0×10−6)​=1.5×10−6Ω⋅m, a value typical of a good conductor such as graphite or a semiconductor.

Universal Facts

  • The steep potential gradient across a neuron axon membrane, roughly 10⁵ V per metre during an action potential, is what causes Na⁺ and K⁺ ions to rush through their channel proteins at 10⁻⁹ second timescales.
  • In an electron microscope the accelerating field of about 10⁵ V/m, obtained as a uniform potential gradient between the cathode and anode, shapes the electron beam into a fine probe capable of resolving atomic-scale structure.

Examples

  • High-voltage transmission lines: Insulators are designed so the potential gradient along their surface stays below the corona-discharge threshold, preventing leakage currents and audible corona noise at transmission voltages of hundreds of kilovolts.
  • Resistivity measurement in the laboratory: Measuring the potential gradient along a current-carrying wire of known length and area yields its resistivity, the standard four-probe technique used in semiconductor doping analysis.
  • Electron beam lithography and cathode ray tubes: A large, uniform potential gradient between accelerating electrodes sets the beam energy (in electron volts) and therefore the focal length and resolution of the instrument.

Key Points

  • Potential gradient is the rate of change of potential with distance, measured in V/m (identical to N/C).
  • Electric field equals the negative of the potential gradient: E = -dV/dx.
  • The field always points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential.
  • Differentiating V = kQ/r reproduces the inverse-square law E = kQ/r² exactly.
  • Slope of the potential–distance graph is minus the electric field at that point.

Summary

The potential gradient is the rate of change of potential with distance, K = dV/dl, and equals the electric field in magnitude with opposite sign: E = -dV/dx or in vector form E = -∇V. The negative sign shows the field always points toward decreasing potential. Differentiating the standard potentials (point charge kQ/r, line charge -λ/2πε₀ ln r, plane sheet -σx/2ε₀) recovers the corresponding field expressions exactly. Practically, the potential gradient along a current-carrying wire equals Iρ/A and is the basis of resistivity measurement.

Special Notes

  • The relation E = -∇V holds only for electrostatic fields. In a time-varying magnetic field, the generalized relation is E = -∇V - dA/dt, so potential alone no longer determines the field (Faraday's law).
  • Wherever the potential is constant, the potential gradient and hence the electric field are exactly zero. This is why the interior of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is field-free even though its potential is finite.

Important Statements

  • The electric field is the negative gradient of the potential. If potential falls as you walk in a certain direction, the field points that way. A uniform field between plates is simply the potential difference divided by the separation, from the positive plate toward the negative plate.
  • The working relations are E = − dV/dr and The field points toward falling potential. Name each symbol, convert the data into SI units, and only then substitute.
  • The potential falls by 20 V over 5.0 cm in the direction of the field. Find E. The fall is along the field, so the magnitude is ΔV/Δx. Δx = 0.050 m.

Important Notes

  • The gradient operator ∇V always points in the direction of maximum increase of V; the electric field is its negative, so E points toward maximum decrease.
  • Do not skip the unit conversion.
  • Match the formula to the situation drawn, not to a neighbouring topic.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Field recovery trick: differentiate the known V(r) and put a minus sign to obtain E(r). E.g. V = kQ/r gives E = kQ/r², and V = -(λ/2πε₀)ln r gives E = λ/(2πε₀r).
  • Unit trick: 1 V/m = 1 (J/C)/m = 1 N/C = 1 (kg·m/s²)/C, so volt per metre and newton per coulomb are interchangeable.
  • Resistivity shortcut: K = V/l = Iρ/A, so ρ = (V/l)(A/I).
