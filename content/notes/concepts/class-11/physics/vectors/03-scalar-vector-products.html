Notes

  • Scalars and vectors: a scalar has only magnitude (mass, time, speed, energy, temperature); a vector has magnitude AND direction (displacement, velocity, force, momentum, electric field). Vectors are written A or bold A, with magnitude ∣A∣=A.
  • The three addition laws answer one question — 'what is the single vector that has the same effect as several together?' (1) Triangle law (two vectors): place them head-to-tail; the resultant runs from the free tail to the free head. (2) Parallelogram law (two vectors from one point): draw both from a common origin; the diagonal through that origin is the resultant. (3) Polygon law (three or more): place all head-to-tail in order; the closing side taken in reverse is the resultant.
  • The master formula (obtained from either the triangle or parallelogram derivation): R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​ with direction α=tan−1(P+QcosθQsinθ​) from P. Every special case follows from it.
  • Special cases from the master formula: θ=0°: Rmax​=P+Q; θ=90°: R=P2+Q2​ (Pythagoras); θ=180°: Rmin​=∣P−Q∣; P=Q: R=2Pcos(θ/2) and the resultant bisects the angle; P=Q, θ=120°: R=P — three equal forces at 120° are in equilibrium.
  • Equilibrium: vectors placed head-to-tail that close into a polygon give R=0 — the body is in equilibrium. For three concurrent forces this is the triangle of forces; quantitatively, Lami's theorem: sinαF1​​=sinβF2​​=sinγF3​​ (each force over the sine of the angle between the other two).
  • Resultant vs equilibrant: the resultant R replaces all vectors; the equilibrant E=−R balances them. Equal magnitude, opposite direction — a favourite 2-mark definition pair in NEB.
  • Resolution connects the laws: for many vectors the fastest route is resolving each into x and y components, then Rx​=∑Fix​, Ry​=∑Fiy​, R=Rx2​+Ry2​​. The polygon law is the graphical twin of this algebraic method.
  • Bounds on the resultant: ∣P−Q∣≤R≤P+Q for any angle — a quick sanity check and a frequent MCQ. For three vectors, R lies between the sum and appropriate differences; a closed triangle guarantees R=0.
  • Choosing a method: (1) two vectors with a known angle → master formula; (2) perpendicular components → Pythagoras; (3) three or more vectors, roughly at right angles → component method; (4) checking equilibrium → closed polygon / Lami's theorem; (5) graphical answer required → scale drawing (parallelogram or polygon).
  • NEB exam focus: state and prove the triangle/parallelogram law; derive R and α; solve numericals on resultant and equilibrium; state Lami's theorem; define equilibrant; find the angle between two equal vectors whose resultant equals either vector (120°).

Confusion

  • ❌ 'The triangle law and parallelogram law give different resultants.' ✅ They are mathematically EQUIVALENT — the triangle is half the parallelogram, so the same diagonal/resultant appears in both.
  • ❌ Measuring the angle between the two sides INSIDE the parallelogram. ✅ θ is the angle between the vectors placed TAIL-TO-TAIL at the common origin — not the parallelogram's obtuse interior angle.
  • ❌ 'The resultant of three vectors is always bigger than each.' ✅ Not necessarily — vectors can partially or fully cancel; a closed polygon gives R=0, smaller than every individual magnitude.
  • ❌ 'The equilibrant is another name for the resultant.' ✅ They are OPPOSITE: equilibrant E=−R. Same magnitude, opposite direction — the force that balances the system.
  • ❌ Applying Lami's theorem to any three forces. ✅ Lami's theorem applies ONLY to three CONCURRENT forces IN EQUILIBRIUM; each force is divided by the sine of the angle between the OTHER two.
  • ❌ 'Adding vectors means adding magnitudes.' ✅ ∣P+Q​∣=P+Q in general; only the cross-term 2PQcosθ distinguishes vector addition from arithmetic addition.

Practice

  • Two forces 5 N and 12 N act at right angles. Find the resultant (magnitude and direction). — R=25+144​=13 N; α=tan−1(12/5)≈67.4° from the 5 N force.
  • Two equal forces of 10 N have a resultant of 10 N. Find the angle between them. — R=2Pcos(θ/2)⇒10=20cos(θ/2)⇒θ=120°.
  • Forces of 3 N, 4 N and 5 N keep a particle in equilibrium. Verify with a closed 3-4-5 force triangle and find the angle between the 3 N and 4 N forces. — Since 3² + 4² = 5², the triangle of forces is right-angled: the 5 N force (the equilibrant direction) balances 3 N and 4 N acting at 90° to each other.
  • A 100 N weight is hung from two strings making 30° and 60° with the horizontal (on opposite sides). Find the tension in each using Lami's theorem. — Angles between the forces: between T1​ and T2​ is 90°, between T1​ and W is 150°, between T2​ and W is 120°. sin120°T1​​=sin150°T2​​=sin90°100​ → T1​=86.6 N, T2​=50 N (string closer to vertical carries more).
  • Graphically, add three displacements: 6 km east, 8 km north, 10 km at 37° south of west, using the polygon method; verify with components that the resultant closes near zero if the data is designed so.

Universal Facts

  • The parallelogram of forces was stated by Isaac Newton in the Principia (1687); Simon Stevin had already used the triangle of forces for inclined-plane problems a century earlier.
  • Surveyors' 'traverse closure' is the polygon law in action: after measuring a circuit of angles and distances, the failure of the final polygon to close (closure error) quantifies the survey's accuracy.
  • Structural engineers analyse every truss joint with the polygon law (method of joints) — if the force polygon at a joint doesn't close, the structure fails.
  • Three mutually perpendicular unit vectors i^,j^​,k^ plus the polygon law let any 3D force system be reduced to three scalar equations — the basis of all statics software.

Examples

  • Example 1 (Pythagoras case): a boat is rowed at 4 m/s across a river flowing at 3 m/s — resultant speed =42+32​=5 m/s at tan−1(3/4)≈36.9° from the boat's heading.
  • Example 2 (equal vectors): two ropes each pull with 200 N at 60° apart — resultant =2(200)cos30°=346 N along the bisector; this is why wide angles waste pulling force.
  • Example 3 (equilibrium): a picture hung on a nail by two strings — each string tension found via the triangle of forces; as the strings become horizontal, tension grows without limit (T=W/2cosθ).
  • Example 4 (120° result): three equal forces F acting at 120° around a point give zero resultant — the geometry behind symmetric three-leg stands and tripod stability.
  • Example 5 (component check): forces 10 N east, 10 N north, 102​ N south-west: Rx​=10−10=0, Ry​=10−10=0 → equilibrium confirmed by the closed polygon.

Practice Questions

  • Q2. Show that the parallelogram law and the triangle law are equivalent.
  • Q3. Two forces of 7 N and 5 N act at 60°. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant.

  • Q5. Define the equilibrant of a system of forces and distinguish it from the resultant.

Formulas

  • Master formula: R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​
  • Direction: α=tan−1(P+QcosθQsinθ​) from P
  • Equal vectors: R=2Pcos(θ/2); R=P when θ=120°
  • Bounds: ∣P−Q∣≤R≤P+Q
  • Lami's theorem (equilibrium of 3 concurrent forces): sinαF1​​=sinβF2​​=sinγF3​​
  • Equilibrant: E=−R

Key Points

  • Triangle, parallelogram and polygon laws are three views of the same addition rule — use whichever suits the number of vectors.
  • The cross-term 2PQcosθ is what makes vector addition different from ordinary addition.
  • A closed polygon of vectors ⇒ zero resultant ⇒ equilibrium.
  • Lami's theorem works only for three concurrent forces in equilibrium.
  • The equilibrant equals the resultant in magnitude but points the opposite way.
  • For three or more vectors, the component method is usually faster than the graphical polygon.

Summary

The triangle, parallelogram and polygon laws are the graphical and analytical foundations of vector addition. All three lead to the master resultant formula R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​ with direction α=tan−1(Qsinθ/(P+Qcosθ)). Special cases (0°, 90°, 180°, equal vectors, 120°) cover most NEB numericals, while equilibrium problems use the closed polygon and Lami's theorem. The equilibrant (−R) balances the system. These laws are the working tools behind statics, navigation, and force analysis throughout physics.

Special Notes

  • Note 1: Always draw the vectors tail-to-tail when using the parallelogram law — the wrong angle is the #1 exam error.
  • Note 2: Memorise the three equilibrium fingerprints: closed polygon, three equal forces at 120°, and Lami's theorem.
  • Note 3: The resultant of two vectors is maximum at 0° and minimum at 180°; no configuration gives R outside [∣P−Q∣,P+Q].
  • Note 4: In proofs, the step sin2θ+cos2θ=1 must appear — NEB awards the derivation mark for it.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Two vectors are added by the triangle law; the resultant runs from the free tail to the free head in the head-to-tail construction.
  • Statement 2: The diagonal of the parallelogram through the common origin represents the resultant of two vectors drawn from that origin.
  • Statement 3: If the vector polygon closes, the resultant is zero and the body is in equilibrium.
  • Statement 4: The magnitude of the resultant of two vectors lies between their difference and their sum for every angle.

Important Notes

  • Note 1: The triangle and parallelogram laws are equivalent — one derivation serves both.
  • Note 2: Vector addition is commutative and associative, so polygon order doesn't change the resultant.
  • Note 3: Lami's theorem requires three concurrent forces in equilibrium — not merely any three forces.
  • Note 4: For P=Q, the resultant 2Pcos(θ/2) bisects the angle; at 120° it equals each vector.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: 'Resultant equals either vector' + 'equal vectors' ⇒ angle 120° instantly.
  • Trick 2: For max/min resultant MCQs: Rmax​=P+Q at 0°, Rmin​=∣P−Q∣ at 180°.
  • Trick 3: Perpendicular vectors ⇒ drop straight to Pythagoras; don't write the full formula.
  • Trick 4: In Lami's theorem problems, compute the three angles as 180° minus the geometry angles first — the sine values then fall out.
  • Trick 5: To test equilibrium quickly, resolve into components: both ∑Fx​=0 and ∑Fy​=0 must hold.

Exam Notes

  • Exam Tip 1: The parallelogram-law derivation (with figure, perpendicular drop, and the sin2+cos2=1 step) is a guaranteed derivation question — rehearse it in full written form.
  • Exam Tip 2: Numericals often combine resolution with the polygon law: resolve first, add components, only then quote R and α.
  • Exam Tip 3: In 'show that the system is in equilibrium' questions, demonstrate the closed polygon OR both component sums equal to zero.
  • Exam Tip 4: Always state the direction of the resultant (angle with a named vector) — magnitude alone loses a mark.

Important Concepts

  • Concept 1: Triangle law — head-to-tail addition; resultant is the closing vector.
  • Concept 2: Parallelogram law — diagonal through the common origin; analytically identical to the triangle law.
  • Concept 3: Polygon law — generalisation to any number of coplanar vectors; closure ⇒ equilibrium.
  • Concept 4: Resultant formula and its special cases (0°, 90°, 180°, equal vectors, 120°).
  • Concept 5: Lami's theorem and the equilibrant — the quantitative and graphical language of equilibrium.
