Notes

  • Physical quantities are classified by whether they require direction for a complete description. Scalar quantities have only magnitude and obey the ordinary rules of algebra (e.g., distance, speed, mass, energy). Vector quantities have both magnitude and direction and obey vector algebra (e.g., displacement, velocity, force, acceleration).
  • Scalar quantity: a physical quantity specified completely by its magnitude alone. Examples include mass (m=5kg), temperature (T=300K), speed (v=60km/h), and energy (E=500J). Scalars add like ordinary numbers.
  • Vector quantity: a physical quantity that requires both magnitude and direction for complete specification, and which obeys the triangle law of vector addition. Examples: displacement s, velocity v, acceleration a, force F. Vectors are written with an arrow, e.g. A.
  • A vector is represented graphically by a directed line segment — the length of the arrow represents the magnitude and the arrowhead indicates the direction. The magnitude (or modulus) of A is denoted ∣A∣ or simply A, and is always a non-negative scalar.
  • Key distinction: speed is a scalar (magnitude only), while velocity is a vector (magnitude + direction). You can travel at 60km/h in any direction, but a velocity of 60km/h east is fundamentally different from 60km/h north.
  • Position vector: the vector from the origin O to a point P(x,y,z) is r=xi^+yj^​+zk^. It specifies the location of point P relative to the origin and is fundamental in describing motion.
  • Displacement vs distance: distance is the total path length traveled (scalar), while displacement is the straight-line change in position from initial to final point (vector). For a round trip, distance >0 but displacement =0.
  • A free vector can be translated parallel to itself without changing its physical meaning — only magnitude and direction matter. A bound vector (e.g., the velocity of a specific particle) is tied to a particular point in space.
  • Two vectors are equal if and only if they have the same magnitude and the same direction. Thus A=B implies ∣A∣=∣B∣ and their directions coincide, regardless of where they are positioned.
  • Scalars follow standard algebraic rules: a+b=b+a and (a+b)+c=a+(b+c). Vectors also follow these commutative and associative laws of addition, but vector multiplication has two distinct types: dot product and cross product.
  • Dimensional formula of a vector matches its physical quantity: velocity vector [v]=LT−1, force vector [F]=MLT−2. The direction component is dimensionless.
  • In physics, vectors are not optional notation — they encode information that scalars cannot. Force applied in the wrong direction produces a completely different effect, even if the magnitude is the same.
  • The negative of a vector A is −A: it has the same magnitude as A but points in the opposite direction. If A points east, −A points west.
  • Scalars can be zero (e.g., mass =0 is unphysical but temperature =0K is the absolute zero). Vectors can be zero vectors 0 (null vector) — a vector with zero magnitude and indeterminate direction.
  • Vectors were formalized in the 19th century by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside, who separated vector analysis from quaternion calculus, making it the standard language of physics and engineering.
  • When adding scalars: 5kg+3kg=8kg. When adding vectors, direction matters: a 5N force east plus a 3N force west gives 2N east, not 8N.
  • Physical significance check: any quantity that is added according to the triangle/parallelogram law of vectors is a vector quantity. Electric current, despite having direction, is a scalar because it does not obey vector addition rules.
  • Invariance under coordinate rotation: a scalar is invariant under rotation of axes — its value doesn't change. A vector's components change, but its magnitude and physical direction remain the same. This is the fundamental difference: scalars are rank-0 tensors, vectors are rank-1 tensors.
  • Temperature vs temperature gradient: temperature is a scalar, but the temperature gradient ∇T is a vector pointing in the direction of greatest temperature increase. This illustrates how spatial variation of a scalar field produces a vector field.
  • Work is a scalar from vector dot product: work W=F⋅d is a scalar because the dot product of two vectors is a scalar. The angle between force and displacement determines the sign — positive work when force aids motion, negative when it opposes.
  • Power is scalar, force is vector: power P=F⋅v is the rate of doing work — a scalar. It depends on the component of force along velocity. A force perpendicular to velocity does zero work and delivers zero power.
  • Area is a vector in 3D: an oriented surface area is represented by a vector A=An^ perpendicular to the surface. This is essential for flux calculations: electric flux ΦE​=E⋅A, magnetic flux ΦB​=B⋅A.
  • Linear vs angular quantities: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum are polar vectors (true vectors). Angular displacement, angular velocity, angular acceleration, torque, angular momentum are axial vectors (pseudovectors) — they behave differently under reflection.
  • Density vs mass: mass is a scalar (extensive property). Density is a scalar (intensive property). Neither has direction. Mass flow rate m˙ is a scalar, but mass flux j​m​=ρv is a vector.
  • Pressure vs stress: pressure is a scalar (normal force per unit area, same in all directions). Stress is a tensor (has 9 components in 3D). Normal stress is pressure-like, shear stress has direction. In fluids at rest, pressure is the only stress.
  • Energy is always scalar: kinetic energy K=21​mv2=21​m(v⋅v) is scalar because it depends on speed squared. Potential energy is scalar. Total mechanical energy is scalar. No form of energy is a vector.
  • Vector fields vs scalar fields: a scalar field assigns a number to each point in space (temperature, pressure, potential). A vector field assigns a vector to each point (velocity field, electric field, gravitational field). The gradient of a scalar field is a vector field.

Confusion

  • Distance is the same as displacement — Distance is the total path length (scalar, always positive). Displacement is the straight-line change in position (vector, can be zero even after traveling far). They are equal only for straight-line motion without reversal.
  • Speed and velocity are the same — Speed is the scalar rate of distance covered (v=d/t). Velocity is the vector rate of displacement (v=Δr/Δt). Speed is the magnitude of velocity: v=∣v∣.
  • All quantities with direction are vectors — A quantity must also obey vector addition (triangle law) to be a vector. Electric current has direction but adds algebraically, so it is a scalar. Stress has direction but is a tensor, not a vector.
  • A vector's position in space matters for equality — Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and direction, regardless of where they are drawn. A free vector can be translated parallel to itself without changing its identity.
  • Zero magnitude means the vector has no direction at all — The zero vector 0 has zero magnitude but its direction is conventionally considered indeterminate. It is the additive identity: A+0=A.
  • Mass is a vector because it has magnitude — Mass is purely a scalar; it has magnitude but no direction. The idea of 'mass in a direction' is physically meaningless.
  • Temperature difference is a vector because it has direction (hot to cold) — Temperature difference is a scalar. While heat flows from hot to cold, that flow direction is described by the heat flux vector, not the temperature difference itself.
  • Any rotating quantity must be a vector — Finite rotations do not commute and therefore are not vectors. Only infinitesimal rotations obey vector addition rules. Angular velocity ω is a vector because it represents an infinitesimal rotation rate.
  • Electric field intensity is a scalar because it has magnitude and direction — Electric field E is a vector. Electric potential V is a scalar. Don't confuse the field (vector) with the potential (scalar). The field is E=−∇V, the gradient of the potential.
  • Pressure exerts force in one direction only — Pressure is a scalar that acts equally in all directions. The force due to pressure on a surface element is dF=−pdA where dA is the area vector perpendicular to the surface.
  • A vector's magnitude can be negative — Magnitude is always non-negative (∣A∣≥0). Negative components do not mean negative magnitude. The components Ax​,Ay​,Az​ can be negative, but ∣A∣=Ax2​+Ay2​+Az2​​ is always positive.
  • Scalars obey vector addition — Scalars obey ordinary algebraic addition, not vector addition. Two scalars add by simple arithmetic: 5kg+3kg=8kg. No angle or direction is involved.

Universal Facts

  • The distinction between scalars and vectors is one of the most fundamental classifications in all of physics. Every equation in mechanics, electromagnetism, and thermodynamics relies on knowing which quantities are vectors and which are scalars.
  • Gibbs and Heaviside independently developed modern vector analysis in the 1880s, stripping it from the more cumbersome quaternion system invented by Hamilton. This made physics education dramatically more accessible.
  • NEB exam tip: scalar/vector classification questions appear almost every year. Memorize the canonical lists: scalars (mass, energy, temperature, speed, distance, volume, density) and vectors (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, weight).
  • The concept of a vector extends far beyond physics — in mathematics, vectors represent elements of any vector space, including polynomials, functions, and complex numbers. The physics definition is a specific application of this broader mathematical idea.
  • Air traffic controllers use vector addition continuously: the aircraft's velocity relative to the ground equals its airspeed vector plus the wind velocity vector. A 500 km/h plane heading north into a 100 km/h west wind has a ground track of roughly 510 km/h at 11.3° west of north.
  • Work is defined as the dot product of force and displacement (W=F⋅d), which is inherently a scalar. This is why energy, despite being associated with directional forces, is a scalar quantity.
  • In NEB numerical problems involving resultants, remember the three special cases: parallel (θ=0°,R=P+Q), perpendicular (θ=90°,R=P2+Q2​), and anti-parallel (θ=180°,R=∣P−Q∣). These cover roughly half of all exam problems.
  • The zero vector 0 is unique: it is the only vector with undefined direction, and it is parallel to every vector (since 0×A=0 for any A).
