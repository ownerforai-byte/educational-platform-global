Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Chain Reaction of Charge

    When a group more or less electronegative than carbon attaches to a chain, it pulls or pushes σ-electrons through the whole chain — the inductive effect (I effect). It is a permanent polarisation, transmitted weakly along σ bonds and fading fast. It explains why chloroacetic acid out-acidifies acetic acid, why tertiary amines are more basic, and why 3° carbocations survive longest.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition and mechanism

    • Inductive effect: the permanent displacement of σ-bond electrons along a saturated chain, caused by an atom or group more (or less) electronegative than carbon, transmitted through successive σ bonds.
    • The effect is permanent (a ground-state polarisation) but fades rapidly — negligible after about the third carbon.

    2.2 −I (electron-withdrawing) groups

    • Groups more electronegative than carbon pull σ-electrons toward themselves: −NO₂, −CN, −COOH, −CHO, −F, −Cl, −Br, −I, −OH, phenyl.
    • Example: in CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–Cl, Cl pulls: C-3 bears δ+, C-2 δδ+, C-1 δδδ+ — shrinking with distance.

    2.3 +I (electron-releasing) groups

    • Groups less electronegative than carbon push σ-electrons toward the chain: alkyl groups (–CH₃, –C₂H₅) and metal atoms (Na, Mg).
    • Alkyl groups are weak +I donors relative to hydrogen — the more alkyl groups on a centre, the more electron density is pushed onto it.

    2.4 Consequences

    • Acidity: −I groups stabilise the carboxylate anion → ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH in acidity; more Cl, stronger still (CCl₃COOH ≈ strong acid).
    • Basicity of amines: +I alkyl groups increase electron density on N → alkylamines more basic than ammonia (in the gas phase; 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃).
    • Carbocation stability: +I alkyl groups disperse the positive charge → 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Reading the arrow convention

    • Textbooks show the σ-electron shift as an arrow along the bond toward the electronegative atom, with partial charges δ+ / δ− marking the chain. The direction is set at the first bond; the rest of the chain feels the consequence.

    4.2 Why the effect dies out

    • Each σ bond transmits only a fraction of the polarisation (roughly a third per bond); after 3–4 bonds the disturbance is below detection. This is why "distance from the group" questions have numerical answers: α-chloroacetic acid is far more acidic than β-chloroacetic acid.

    4.3 Stability logic in three lines

    • Charged species survive by spreading charge. −I groups stabilise negative centres (carboxylate), +I groups stabilise positive centres (carbocation). Acidity and carbocation order are the same physics read in opposite directions.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Inductive is through σ bonds; resonance is through π: the I effect needs no π system and dies with distance; the R effect needs conjugation and does not fade the same way.

  • +I does not mean "positive charge": the plus sign records the direction of electron push (toward the chain), not the sign of any charge.
  • Aqueous amine basicity has extra factors: solvation of the cation complicates the gas-phase order — for NEB purposes, quote +I raising basicity, and note solvation as a refinement.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What is inductive effect? Explain −I and +I effects with examples. — The inductive effect is the permanent displacement of σ-bonding electrons along a carbon chain, caused by an atom or group more (or less) electronegative than carbon; it is transmitted through successive σ bonds and diminishes rapidly with distance. −I effect (electron-withdrawing): groups more electronegative than carbon — −NO₂, −CN, −COOH, −CHO, −F, −Cl, −Br, −I, −OH — pull the σ-electron pair toward themselves, leaving the chain electron-poor (δ+ marks along the chain). Example: in CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl, chlorine withdraws so the adjacent carbons carry diminishing δ+ charges. +I effect (electron-releasing): groups less electronegative than carbon — the alkyl groups — push σ-electrons toward the chain, increasing electron density along it.

  • NEB-style (2m): Explain why chloroacetic acid is stronger than acetic acid. — In chloroacetic acid (ClCH₂COOH) the chlorine exerts a −I effect, pulling σ-electrons away from the carboxyl group. This disperses the negative charge of the carboxylate anion formed on ionisation, stabilising it — and a more stable conjugate base means a stronger acid. Acetic acid's methyl group instead pushes electrons (+I), destabilising its anion, so ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH in acidity (and CCl₃COOH, with three −I chlorines, is stronger still).
  • NEB-style (2m: Why is a tertiary carbocation more stable than a primary one? — A 3° carbocation carries three alkyl groups, each exerting a +I effect, pushing σ-electron density toward the positive carbon and dispersing the positive charge over the alkyl groups. A 1° carbocation has only one alkyl group, so its positive charge is concentrated on one carbon — less stabilised. Hence the stability order 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺, which governs the ease of carbocation-forming reactions.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): What is inductive effect? Explain −I and +I effects with examples. — The inductive effect (I effect) is the permanent displacement of σ-bonding electrons along a saturated carbon chain, produced when an atom or group attached to the chain is more (or less) electronegative than carbon. The displacement is transmitted through successive σ bonds and diminishes rapidly with distance, becoming negligible after about the third carbon. −I effect (electron-withdrawing): groups more electronegative than carbon — −NO₂, −CN, −COOH, −CHO, −F, −Cl, −Br, −I, −OH — pull the σ-electron pair toward themselves, leaving successive chain carbons with decreasing partial positive charges (δ+, δδ+…). Example: in CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–Cl the chlorine withdraws electron density through the chain. +I effect (electron-releasing): groups less electronegative than carbon — the alkyl groups (–CH₃, –C₂H₅) and electropositive metals — push σ-electrons toward the chain, enriching it with electron density.
  • NEB-style (2m): Explain why chloroacetic acid is a stronger acid than acetic acid. — When chloroacetic acid ionises, the conjugate base ClCH₂COO⁻ carries its negative charge on the carboxylate. The chlorine atom exerts a −I effect, withdrawing σ-electron density and thereby dispersing and stabilising that negative charge. A more stable conjugate base corresponds to a stronger acid. Acetic acid's methyl group does the opposite — its +I effect pushes electron density toward the carboxylate, intensifying and destabilising the negative charge. Hence the acidity order ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH — and with three withdrawing chlorines, CCl₃COOH is stronger still.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is a tertiary carbocation more stable than a primary carbocation? — A tertiary (3°) carbocation is bonded to three alkyl groups, each exerting a +I (electron-releasing) effect that pushes σ-electron density toward the electron-deficient carbon and spreads the positive charge over the alkyl substituents — charge dispersal stabilises the ion. A primary (1°) carbocation has only one alkyl group, so the positive charge remains concentrated on a single carbon with minimal dispersal. The stability order is therefore 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺, which is why carbocation-forming reactions occur most readily at tertiary centres.

Key Points

  • Inductive effect (I): permanent displacement of sigma-bond electrons along a chain due to electronegativity difference; transmitted through sigma bonds; fades rapidly (negligible after ~3 carbons). -I (withdrawing): -NO2, -CN, -COOH, -CHO, -F, -Cl, -Br, -I, -OH — pull electrons, leave chain delta+. +I (releasing): alkyl groups -CH3, -C2H5, metals — push electrons toward chain. Consequences: acidity ClCH2COOH > CH3COOH (CCl3COOH strongest — -I stabilises carboxylate); amine basicity rises with +I alkyl groups; carbocation stability 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3+ (+I disperses positive charge).

Summary

The inductive effect is the permanent polarisation of σ bonds along a carbon chain, born of an electronegativity difference and fading to nothing within three or four bonds. −I groups (−NO₂, −CN, −COOH, −CHO, the halogens, −OH) pull σ-electrons toward themselves, leaving the chain δ+; +I groups (alkyl) push electrons into the chain. The consequences are order-of-strength rules: acidity rises as −I groups stabilise the carboxylate anion (CCl₃COOH > CHCl₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH); basicity of amines rises with +I alkyl donation; and carbocation stability follows 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺ as alkyl groups disperse the positive charge. One physics — charge dispersal — read in both directions explains them all.
