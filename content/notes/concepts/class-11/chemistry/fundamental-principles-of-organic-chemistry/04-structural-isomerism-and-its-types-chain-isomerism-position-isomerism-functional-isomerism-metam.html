Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Five Ways to Rewire the Same Atoms

    Structural isomerism is connectivity chemistry: the same atoms, joined differently, give different compounds. Five sub-types cover it — chain, position, functional, metamerism and tautomerism — and each has a signature pair that examiners love. Learn the diagnostic question each type answers and you can classify any pair on sight.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Chain (skeletal) isomerism

    • Same formula, different carbon skeletons — straight vs branched.
    • C₄H₁₀: butane (straight) / 2-methylpropane (branched). C₅H₁₂: n-pentane, isopentane (2-methylbutane), neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane) — three chain isomers.

    2.2 Position isomerism

    • Same skeleton and same functional group; the group sits on different carbons.
    • Propan-1-ol / propan-2-ol; but-1-ene / but-2-ene; o-/m-/p-xylene (methyl positions on benzene).

    2.3 Functional isomerism

    • Same formula, different functional groups — different families entirely.
    • C₂H₆O: ethanol (alcohol) / dimethyl ether (ether). C₃H₆O: propanal (aldehyde) / propanone (ketone).

    2.4 Metamerism

    • Same formula and same functional group, but the group sits between different alkyl groups (unequal distribution around a polyvalent atom/group: O, N, C=O).
    • C₄H₁₀O: ethoxyethane C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅ / methoxypropane CH₃–O–C₃H₇. Also in ketones (C₃H₇COCH₃ vs C₂H₅COC₂H₅) and secondary amines.

    2.5 Tautomerism

    • A dynamic equilibrium between two structural forms differing in the position of one (usually H) atom and a π bond — the classic keto–enol system.
    • Acetaldehyde ⇌ vinyl alcohol; acetone ⇌ its enol. Requires an α-hydrogen next to the C=O; catalysed by acid or base; usually the keto form dominates.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The diagnostic questions

    • Different skeleton? → chain. Same skeleton, group moved? → position. Different group? → functional. Same group, different alkyl flanks? → metamerism. Forms interconverting via an H shift? → tautomerism. Ask them in order and every pair lands in one box.

    4.2 Why tautomerism is special

    • The other four types give stable, isolable compounds. Tautomers interconvert continuously through a proton + π-bond migration; you cannot bottle "the enol of acetone" — the equilibrium mixture is what exists. That dynamic character places tautomerism at the boundary of structural isomerism.

    4.3 Metamerism vs position — the subtle pair

    • Both keep the functional group. Position moves the group along one skeleton; metamerism keeps the group's location type but changes which alkyl groups flank it (ethyl+ethyl vs methyl+propyl). If the group is between two carbons on both sides, think metamerism.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Metamerism is not position isomerism: in metamerism the groups attached to the polyvalent atom change (ethyl/ethyl → methyl/propyl); position isomerism slides one group along an unchanged skeleton.

  • Enols are not isolable curiosities: the keto form usually dominates (>99%), but the tiny enol fraction drives reactions like halogenation of ketones.
  • Functional isomers belong to different series: ethanol is an alkanol, dimethyl ether an ether — different homologous series, different tests.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What is structural isomerism? Explain chain, position and functional isomerism with one example each. — Structural isomerism is the existence of compounds with the same molecular formula but different connectivity of atoms. Chain isomerism: the carbon skeletons differ — butane (straight) and 2-methylpropane (branched), both C₄H₁₀. Position isomerism: the skeleton and functional group are the same but the group occupies different positions — propan-1-ol (OH on C-1) and propan-2-ol (OH on C-2), both C₃H₈O. Functional isomerism: the same formula carries different functional groups — ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH, an alcohol) and dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃, an ether), both C₂H₆O; they belong to different homologous series and show entirely different chemical properties.

  • NEB-style (2m): What is metamerism? Give an example. — Metamerism is a type of structural isomerism in which compounds have the same functional group but differ in the alkyl groups attached to the polyvalent atom or group (O, N or C=O). Example: ethoxyethane (C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅) and methoxypropane (CH₃–O–C₃H₇) — both ethers of formula C₄H₁₀O, but the oxygen flanked by ethyl+ethyl versus methyl+propyl.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is tautomerism? Write the keto–enol equilibrium of acetaldehyde. — Tautomerism is the dynamic equilibrium between two structural forms of a compound that interconvert by migration of a hydrogen atom together with a π bond — most commonly the keto ⇌ enol system, catalysed by acid or base and requiring an α-hydrogen. For acetaldehyde: CH₃CHO ⇌ CH₂=CH–OH (keto form on the left, enol on the right; the keto form predominates).

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): What is structural isomerism? Explain chain, position and functional isomerism with one example each. — Structural isomerism is the phenomenon in which compounds have the same molecular formula but different connectivity — their atoms are joined in different arrangements. Chain (skeletal) isomerism: the carbon skeletons differ — butane (straight chain) and 2-methylpropane (branched chain), both C₄H₁₀; pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane give three chain isomers of C₅H₁₂. Position isomerism: the skeleton and functional group are identical but the group occupies different positions on the chain — propan-1-ol (OH on C-1) and propan-2-ol (OH on C-2), both C₃H₈O; likewise but-1-ene and but-2-ene. Functional isomerism: the same formula carries different functional groups, placing the compounds in different homologous series — ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH, an alcohol) and dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃, an ether), both C₂H₆O; also propanal (aldehyde) and propanone (ketone), both C₃H₆O.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is metamerism? Give an example. — Metamerism is a form of structural isomerism in which compounds share the same functional group but differ in the alkyl groups attached on either side of the polyvalent atom or group (oxygen in ethers, nitrogen in amines, the carbonyl carbon in ketones). Example: ethoxyethane, C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅ (oxygen between two ethyl groups) and methoxypropane, CH₃–O–C₃H₇ (oxygen between methyl and propyl) — both are ethers of formula C₄H₁₀O. The distribution of carbons around the hetero atom, not the skeleton or the group, is what differs.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is tautomerism? Write the keto–enol equilibrium of acetaldehyde. — Tautomerism is the existence of a compound as a dynamic equilibrium between two structural forms that interconvert by the migration of a hydrogen atom together with a π bond — the commonest being the keto ⇌ enol pair. It requires an α-hydrogen adjacent to the carbonyl group and is catalysed by acid or base; the keto form normally predominates. For acetaldehyde: CH₃–CHO ⇌ CH₂=CH–OH — the keto form (left) is in equilibrium with vinyl alcohol, the enol form (right).

  • Structural isomerism five types: chain — different skeletons (butane/2-methylpropane; C5H12 has 3: n-pentane, isopentane, neopentane); position — same group different carbon (propan-1-ol/propan-2-ol; but-1-ene/but-2-ene; o-/m-/p-xylene); functional — different groups (C2H6O ethanol/dimethyl ether; C3H6O propanal/propanone); metamerism — different alkyl groups around polyvalent group (C2H5-O-C2H5 vs CH3-O-C3H7; also ketones, 2-amines); tautomerism — dynamic keto-enol equilibrium via H + pi shift, needs alpha-H, acid/base catalysed, keto dominates (CH3CHO <=> CH2=CH-OH).

Summary

Structural isomerism rewires the same atoms five ways. Chain isomerism changes the skeleton (butane/2-methylpropane; the three C₅H₁₂ isomers). Position isomerism slides one group along an unchanged skeleton (propan-1-ol/propan-2-ol). Functional isomerism swaps the group itself, jumping homologous series (ethanol/dimethyl ether; propanal/propanone). Metamerism keeps the group but redistributes the alkyl groups flanking a polyvalent atom (ethoxyethane/methoxypropane). Tautomerism is the odd one out — a dynamic keto ⇌ enol equilibrium (CH₃CHO ⇌ CH₂=CH–OH) driven by an α-hydrogen and acid/base catalysis, with the keto form dominant. Five diagnostic questions — skeleton? position? group? flanks? H-shift? — classify any pair on sight.
