Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — How Bonds Break and Why It Matters

    Every organic reaction begins with a covalent bond breaking. How it breaks decides everything downstream: homolytic fission splits the pair equally, creating free radicals; heterolytic fission gives both electrons to one atom, creating ions (carbocation + anion). The mechanism — the step-by-step path of bond breaking and making — is written with these two moves and the arrows that show where electrons go.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 What a reaction mechanism is

    • A mechanism is the step-by-step description of a reaction: which bonds break, which form, in what order, through which intermediates, catalysed how.
    • Electron movement is shown with curly arrows: full-head arrow = a pair of electrons moves; half-head (fishhook) = a single electron moves.

    2.2 Homolytic fission (homolysis)

    • The covalent pair splits equally: each fragment takes one electron.
    • Products: free radicals — neutral species with an unpaired electron, written X·.
    • A–B → A· + B· — favoured by symmetrical (non-polar) bonds and by heat or light.
    • Example: Cl₂ → 2Cl· (sunlight) — the initiation step of chlorination of methane.

    2.3 Heterolytic fission (heterolysis)

    • The covalent pair goes entirely to the more electronegative atom.
    • Products: a cation and an anion — A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻ (B keeps both electrons).
    • Favoured by polar bonds (large electronegativity difference) and polar solvents.
    • Example: (CH₃)₃C–Cl → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Cl⁻ in water — the carbocation of substitution reactions; HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻.

    2.4 The intermediates

    • Free radical (from homolysis): neutral, 7-electron carbon or halogen, unpaired electron, paramagnetic.
    • Carbocation (heterolysis, carbon loses the pair): 6-electron carbon, positive, electron-deficient — an electrophile.
    • Carbanion (heterolysis, carbon keeps the pair): negative, electron-rich — a nucleophile.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Choosing between the two fissions

    • The bond's own polarity decides: a nearly non-polar bond (Cl–Cl, C–C) splits evenly — homolysis under heat/light. A polar bond (C–Cl, H–Cl) splits to the electronegative partner — heterolysis. Solvent and conditions modulate: non-polar solvents + light favour radicals; polar solvents favour ions.

    4.2 Why radicals and ions behave differently

    • Radicals are neutral but electron-hungry (7-electron species) — they attack σ/π bonds in chain reactions (initiation → propagation → termination).
    • Carbocations are positively charged and 6-electron — they seek electron pairs (electrophilic behaviour). Carbanions offer pairs (nucleophilic behaviour). The fission mode thus selects the reagent class — the next topic's business.

    4.3 Reading mechanisms

    • Every organic mechanism is a chain of these events: heterolysis creates an electrophile, a nucleophile attacks, a proton shifts, a radical abstracts H. Learn to ask at each step: which bond breaks, and which way do the electrons go?

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Radicals are neutral, not negative: the unpaired electron carries no charge — Cl· is neutral; Cl⁻ is the anion from heterolysis.

  • The arrow shows electron movement, not atom movement: in heterolysis the H⁺ "leaves" because its electrons left with the other atom — track electrons, not protons.
  • Homolysis needs energy input: light or heat is required to split a symmetrical bond — radicals do not appear spontaneously at room temperature.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What is homolytic fission? Explain with an example. — Homolytic fission (homolysis) is the breaking of a covalent bond in which the bonding electron pair is divided equally between the two atoms, each taking one electron. The products are free radicals — neutral species carrying an unpaired electron, written with a dot: A–B → A· + B·. It is favoured by non-polar (symmetrical) bonds and by heat or light. Example: in sunlight, Cl₂ → 2Cl·; each chlorine radical has seven valence electrons with one unpaired, and these radicals initiate the chain chlorination of methane (Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl).

  • NEB-style (3m): What is heterolytic fission? Explain with an example. — Heterolytic fission (heterolysis) is bond breaking in which the entire bonding electron pair passes to the more electronegative atom, producing a cation and an anion: A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻. It is favoured by polar bonds and polar solvents. Example: tert-butyl chloride in water, (CH₃)₃C–Cl → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Cl⁻ — chlorine takes the pair, leaving a carbocation, a 6-electron positively charged carbon that behaves as an electrophile. Similarly HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ in water.
  • NEB-style (2m): Distinguish between a free radical, a carbocation and a carbanion. — A free radical is a neutral species with an unpaired electron (7 electrons at carbon, paramagnetic), formed by homolytic fission (Cl·, CH₃·). A carbocation is a positively charged, 6-electron carbon formed when carbon loses the bonding pair in heterolysis ((CH₃)₃C⁺) — electron-deficient and electrophilic. A carbanion is a negatively charged carbon that has gained the bonding pair (CH₃⁻) — electron-rich and nucleophilic.

Confusion

  • ❌ All cells look the same under a microscope. ✅ Prokaryotic cells lack membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotic cells have them.
  • ❌ DNA and RNA are identical in structure. ✅ DNA is double-stranded with deoxyribose and thymine; RNA is single-stranded with ribose and uracil.
  • ❌ Mitosis produces genetically different cells. ✅ Mitosis produces genetically identical diploid daughter cells.
  • ❌ Meiosis produces identical cells. ✅ Meiosis produces 4 genetically diverse haploid gametes.
  • ❌ Crossing over occurs in mitosis. ✅ Crossing over occurs in Prophase I of meiosis only.
  • ❌ All enzymes are proteins. ✅ Most enzymes are proteins; some RNA molecules (ribozymes) also have catalytic activity.
  • ❌ ATP is the only energy currency. ✅ GTP, UTP, and CTP also serve as energy carriers in specific pathways.
  • ❌ Photosynthesis occurs in all plant cells. ✅ Photosynthesis occurs only in cells containing chloroplasts (mainly mesophyll).
  • ❌ Respiration and photosynthesis are opposite processes with same equation. ✅ They are complementary but occur in different organelles and under different conditions.
  • ❌ Oxygen is produced during respiration. ✅ Oxygen is consumed in aerobic respiration; produced during photosynthesis.
  • ❌ Food chain shows all feeding relationships. ✅ Food chain is linear; food web shows interconnected feeding relationships.
  • ❌ 90% of energy is transferred to next trophic level. ✅ Only ~10% energy transfers; ~90% lost as heat.
  • ❌ Biomagnification decreases toxin concentration up the food chain. ✅ Biomagnification increases toxin concentration at higher trophic levels.
  • ❌ Carbon cycle only involves plants and animals. ✅ Carbon cycle involves atmosphere, oceans, fossils, rocks, and decomposers.
  • ❌ Nitrogen fixation only occurs in bacteria. ✅ Some lightning and industrial processes (Haber) also fix nitrogen.
  • ❌ All fungi are harmful. ✅ Many fungi are beneficial (decomposers, mycorrhizae, food, antibiotics like penicillin).
  • ❌ Viruses are living organisms. ✅ Viruses are acellular — they cannot reproduce without a host and lack metabolism.
  • ❌ Bacteria are always harmful. ✅ Most bacteria are harmless or beneficial (gut flora, nitrogen fixation, decomposition).
  • ❌ Gram-positive and Gram-negative differ only in color. ✅ Gram-positive has thick peptidoglycan layer; Gram-negative has thin layer + outer membrane.
  • ❌ Natural selection creates perfect organisms. ✅ Natural selection favors traits suited to current environment; environments change.
  • ❌ Evolution is goal-oriented. ✅ Evolution has no goal; it acts on random variation filtered by selection.
  • ❌ Homologous and analogous structures mean the same thing. ✅ Homologous = common ancestry (divergent); Analogous = similar function, different origin (convergent).
  • ❌ Fossils are preserved bones only. ✅ Fossils include impressions, trace fossils, amber-preserved organisms, and molecular fossils.
  • ❌ Lamarckism is completely wrong. ✅ Lamarck's inheritance of acquired characteristics is largely rejected, but epigenetics shows some environmental effects can be inherited.
  • ❌ Five-kingdom classification is outdated. ✅ Six-kingdom (adding Archaea) and phylogenetic systems are more current, but five-kingdom remains useful for education.
  • ❌ Binomial nomenclature names change frequently. ✅ Names are stable once formally published; changes require formal revision by taxonomic authorities.
  • ❌ All algae are aquatic. ✅ Some algae live in soil, on tree bark, or in symbiotic relationships (lichens).
  • ❌ Bryophytes have vascular tissue. ✅ Bryophytes (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) lack true vascular tissue (xylem/phloem).
  • ❌ Gymnosperms produce flowers. ✅ Gymnosperms have naked seeds in cones; angiosperms produce flowers and enclosed seeds.
  • ❌ Double fertilization occurs in gymnosperms. ✅ Double fertilization (zygote + endosperm) is unique to angiosperms.
  • ❌ Earthworms have an open circulatory system. ✅ Earthworms have a closed circulatory system with dorsal and ventral vessels.
  • ❌ Frogs breathe only through lungs. ✅ Frogs use cutaneous respiration (skin), buccal cavity, and lungs.
  • ❌ Conservation only means protecting animals. ✅ Conservation includes genetic, species, and ecosystem diversity; in-situ and ex-situ strategies.
  • ❌ In-situ and ex-situ conservation are the same. ✅ In-situ = conservation in natural habitat (national parks); Ex-situ = conservation outside habitat (zoos, seed banks).

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): What is homolytic fission? Explain with an example. — Homolytic fission (homolysis) is the breaking of a covalent bond in which the shared electron pair is divided equally between the two bonded atoms, each fragment receiving one electron. The products are free radicals — neutral species bearing an unpaired electron, denoted by a dot: A–B → A· + B·. Homolysis is favoured by non-polar (symmetrical) bonds and by heat or ultraviolet light. Example: in sunlight, Cl₂ → 2Cl· — each chlorine atom retains seven valence electrons, one of them unpaired. These chlorine radicals initiate the free-radical chlorination of methane (Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl, then CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·), a classic chain reaction of initiation, propagation and termination steps.
  • NEB-style (3m): What is heterolytic fission? Explain with an example. — Heterolytic fission (heterolysis) is bond breaking in which the entire bonding electron pair is transferred to the more electronegative atom, producing a cation and an anion: A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻. It is favoured by polar bonds (large electronegativity difference) and polar ionising solvents. Example: tert-butyl chloride in water undergoes (CH₃)₃C–Cl → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Cl⁻ — chlorine departs with the electron pair, leaving a carbocation: a carbon with only six valence electrons and a positive charge, which is electron-deficient and behaves as an electrophile. Likewise HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ in water. The anion formed (or the partner retaining the pair) is electron-rich and nucleophilic.
  • NEB-style (2m): Distinguish between a free radical, a carbocation and a carbanion. — A free radical is a neutral species with an unpaired electron (seven electrons on carbon; paramagnetic), produced by homolytic fission — e.g. Cl· or CH₃·. A carbocation is a positively charged carbon with only six valence electrons, formed when carbon loses the shared pair during heterolysis — e.g. (CH₃)₃C⁺; it is electron-deficient and attacks as an electrophile. A carbanion is a negatively charged carbon carrying an octet including the borrowed pair, formed when carbon retains the bonding pair in heterolysis — e.g. CH₃⁻; it is electron-rich and behaves as a nucleophile. Charge, electron count and fission origin distinguish all three.

Summary

Every organic reaction starts with a bond breaking, and there are exactly two ways. Homolytic fission divides the shared pair equally — A–B → A· + B· — creating neutral free radicals with unpaired electrons; it favours non-polar bonds and needs heat or light (Cl₂ → 2Cl· in sunlight, starting the chain chlorination of methane). Heterolytic fission hands the whole pair to the more electronegative atom — A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻ — creating ions; it favours polar bonds and polar solvents ((CH₃)₃C–Cl → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Cl⁻; HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻). The fission mode determines the intermediate — neutral 7-electron radical, positive 6-electron carbocation (electrophile), or electron-rich carbanion (nucleophile) — and thus selects the reagent class that drives the rest of the mechanism. Curly arrows (full head for pairs, fishhooks for single electrons) record every move.
