Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Chemistry in Three Dimensions

    Connectivity alone does not fix a molecule: atoms occupy space. Two stereoisomerisms follow. Geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism arises where rotation is locked — the C=C double bond — freezing substituents on the same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans). Optical isomerism arises where a carbon carries four different groups, creating non-superimposable mirror images that rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism

    • Cause: restricted rotation about the C=C double bond (the π bond blocks free spinning).
    • Requirement: each doubly-bonded carbon must carry two different groups (abC=Cab). If either carbon bears two identical groups (e.g. CH₂=), no cis/trans pair exists.
    • cis: identical/similar groups on the same side; trans: on opposite sides.
    • Example: cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene (CH₃ groups same side vs opposite). Also maleic acid (cis) vs fumaric acid (trans).
    • Consequences: different melting/boiling points and dipole moments — cis-but-2-ene is polar (net dipole), trans is nearly non-polar (dipoles cancel).

    2.2 Optical isomerism

    • Cause: chirality — a carbon attached to four different groups (an asymmetric/chiral carbon) creates two non-superimposable mirror-image forms (enantiomers).
    • Examples: lactic acid CH₃CH(OH)COOH; glyceraldehyde; every carbon in sugars except one.
    • Enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light in equal but opposite directions — measured in a polarimeter.
    • d (+) rotates light clockwise (dextrorotatory); l (−) rotates anticlockwise (laevorotatory). Racemic mixture (d+l 1:1) is optically inactive by external compensation.

    2.3 The d/l caution

    • d/l describes the direction of light rotation, not the absolute configuration — the (+) isomer of one compound may have the same spatial arrangement as the (−) isomer of another. The labels are experimental, not structural.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why the double bond locks geometry

    • Rotation about a single σ bond is free; rotation about C=C would break the π bond (side-on overlap destroyed), costing ~250 kJ/mol — so at ordinary temperatures the geometry is frozen. Each carbon's two groups are pinned: same side (cis) or opposite (trans).

    4.2 Why four different groups create handedness

    • With four different groups, a tetrahedral carbon has no symmetry plane: its mirror image cannot be rotated into it — like left and right hands. Two molecules, identical in every pairwise property except their interaction with other chiral things (polarised light, enzymes, receptors).

    4.3 Optical activity in the lab

    • Plane-polarised light passed through a solution of one enantiomer rotates by a characteristic angle; the mirror-image isomer rotates by the same angle the other way. A 1:1 mixture cancels — the racemate. Biology is chiral: one enantiomer of a drug may heal, the other harm.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    d/l is not R/S: d/l records the observed rotation of light; it does not tell you which group sits where. Do not equate d with "right-hand arrangement".

  • cis/trans needs two different groups on EACH carbon: propene (CH₂=CHCH₃) has two H's on one carbon — no geometrical isomerism despite the double bond.
  • Optical activity needs a chiral centre (in this course): the whole molecule must lack a symmetry plane; one asymmetric carbon is the standard route.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What is geometrical isomerism? Explain cis–trans isomerism of but-2-ene. — Geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism arises when molecules of the same structure differ in the spatial arrangement of groups around a bond with restricted rotation — the C=C double bond, whose π bond prevents free rotation. Requirement: each doubly-bonded carbon must carry two different groups. In but-2-ene, CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃, each carbon bears H and CH₃: when the two CH₃ groups lie on the same side of the double bond the isomer is cis-but-2-ene; when they lie on opposite sides it is trans-but-2-ene. The two differ in physical properties — cis is polar with a net dipole moment and the higher boiling point; trans is nearly non-polar because the bond dipoles cancel.

  • NEB-style (3m): What is optical isomerism? Explain with the example of lactic acid. — Optical isomerism is the existence of pairs of stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other and differ only in their effect on plane-polarised light. It arises when a carbon atom is bonded to four different groups (an asymmetric or chiral carbon). Lactic acid, CH₃–CH(OH)–COOH: its central carbon carries –H, –CH₃, –OH and –COOH — four different groups — so two enantiomers exist: the d(+) form rotating plane-polarised light clockwise and the l(−) form rotating it anticlockwise by the same angle. A 1:1 mixture (racemic) shows no rotation because the effects cancel.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why does propene not show geometrical isomerism while but-2-ene does? — Geometrical isomerism requires each doubly-bonded carbon to carry two different groups. In propene (CH₂=CH–CH₃) one sp² carbon bears two identical hydrogen atoms, so interchanging them produces the same molecule — no cis/trans pair. In but-2-ene (CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃) each sp² carbon carries H and CH₃, giving two distinct arrangements — cis (CH₃ groups same side) and trans (opposite sides).

Summary

Space is chemistry's third dimension. Geometrical isomerism comes from the C=C double bond's restricted rotation: with two different groups on each doubly-bonded carbon, the substituents freeze on the same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans) — cis- and trans-but-2-ene differ in dipole moment and boiling point, and propene, with two identical H's on one carbon, shows no such pair. Optical isomerism comes from handedness: a carbon bearing four different groups (the chiral C-2 of lactic acid: –H, –CH₃, –OH, –COOH) generates two non-superimposable mirror images — enantiomers that rotate plane-polarised light equally in opposite directions (d(+) clockwise, l(−) anticlockwise), while a 1:1 racemic mixture cancels to zero. Two locks — the π bond and the asymmetric carbon — and every stereoisomer follows.
