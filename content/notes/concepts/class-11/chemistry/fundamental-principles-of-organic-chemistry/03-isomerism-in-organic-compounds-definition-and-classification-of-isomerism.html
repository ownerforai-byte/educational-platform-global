  • 1. Topic Introduction — Same Formula, Different Molecule

    Isomerism is the phenomenon in which compounds share a molecular formula but differ in properties — the molecules differ in how their atoms are arranged or oriented. The classification splits first into structural isomerism (different connectivity) and stereoisomerism (same connectivity, different spatial arrangement). This topic builds the map; the next three topics tour each branch.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition

    • Isomers: compounds with the same molecular formula but different physical and/or chemical properties.
    • Isomerism: the phenomenon of their existence. Root cause: a molecular formula specifies composition, not arrangement — and arrangement decides properties.

    2.2 The classification map

    • Isomerism
    • Structural (constitutional): atoms connected differently.
    • Chain (skeletal) — different carbon skeletons.
    • Position — same skeleton, group on different carbon.
    • Functional — different functional groups.
    • Metamerism — different alkyl groups on either side of a polyvalent group.
    • Tautomerism — dynamic interconversion (keto ⇌ enol).
    • Stereoisomerism: same connectivity, different 3-D arrangement.
    • Geometrical (cis–trans) — restricted rotation about C=C.
    • Optical (d/l) — non-superimposable mirror images (chirality).

    2.3 The signature examples

    • C₄H₁₀: butane and 2-methylpropane (chain).
    • C₂H₆O: ethanol and dimethyl ether (functional).
    • C₄H₈: cis- and trans-but-2-ene (geometrical).
    • Lactic acid CH₃CH(OH)COOH: two optical isomers.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why isomerism happens at all

    • A molecular formula counts atoms but says nothing about which atom bonds to which (structural level) or how the arrangement sits in space (stereo level). Carbon's tetravalency lets the same atom set be wired and oriented in multiple stable ways — each wiring/orientation is a distinct compound with its own melting point, boiling point, reactivity.

    4.2 How the two great branches differ

    • Structural isomers differ in which atoms are joined — to interconvert them you must break and remake bonds (a chemical change).
    • Stereoisomers have identical connectivity — they differ only in geometry; interconversion needs rotation about a double bond (geometrical) or inversion at a stereocentre (optical), not bond-breaking.

    4.3 Isomerism as evidence

    • Isomerism proved early on that structure — not just composition — is real chemistry. Wöhler's NH₄OCN/urea pair was an isomer pair; every isomer discovered since confirmed that arrangement is destiny.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Isomers are different compounds, not the same compound "in another form": each has its own m.p., b.p., spectra and reactions.

  • Tautomerism sits in the structural branch but is dynamic: the two forms interconvert constantly through a mobile-H shift — unlike other structural isomers, which are stable separate compounds.
  • Same molecular formula is the entry ticket: if the formulas differ, they are homologues or different compounds — not isomers.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): Define isomerism. Classify isomerism with examples. — Isomerism is the phenomenon in which two or more compounds have the same molecular formula but different physical and chemical properties (such compounds are isomers). Confusion

  • ❌ All atoms are identical in an element. ✅ Isotopes have same protons but different neutrons.
  • ❌ Atoms are indivisible. ✅ Atoms consist of protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • ❌ Electrons orbit in fixed paths like planets. ✅ Electrons exist in probability clouds (orbitals).
  • ❌ Heisenberg uncertainty means we cannot measure position and momentum precisely simultaneously — not that we lack good instruments.
  • ❌ de Broglie wavelength applies only to light. ✅ All matter has wave nature; detectable mainly for small masses like electrons.
  • ❌ Quantum number l can be any integer. ✅ l ranges from 0 to n−1.
  • ❌ Magnetic quantum number ml determines energy. ✅ ml determines orbital orientation, not energy (in absence of magnetic field).
  • ❌ Spin quantum number ms can be ±½ or any value. ✅ ms is always +½ or −½.
  • ❌ Aufbau principle means filling from bottom to top of the diagram. ✅ Fill lowest-energy orbitals first: 1s→2s→2p→3s→3p→4s→3d→4p→...
  • ❌ Pauli exclusion allows two electrons per orbital with any spin. ✅ Two electrons in same orbital must have opposite spins.
  • ❌ Hund's rule means pair electrons before spreading. ✅ Electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly first with parallel spins.
  • ❌ The mole is just a number like dozen. ✅ 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ particles (Avogadro constant) — a bridge between atomic and macroscopic scales.
  • ❌ Molar mass and molecular mass are the same thing. ✅ Molecular mass is in amu; molar mass is in g/mol — numerically equal but different units.
  • ❌ Limiting reagent is the reactant with smallest mass. ✅ Limiting reagent is the one that produces the least product (determined by moles, not mass).
  • ❌ % yield can exceed 100%. ✅ % yield >100% indicates impure product or incomplete drying.
  • ❌ Molarity and molality are the same. ✅ Molarity = mol/L solution (changes with T); Molality = mol/kg solvent (temperature-independent).
  • ❌ A strong acid completely ionizes in water. ✅ Strong acids (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄) are ~100% ionized; weak acids partially ionize.
  • ❌ pH can be negative. ✅ pH < 0 is possible for concentrations >1M (e.g., 2M HCl has pH ≈ −0.3).
  • ❌ Kc changes when concentration changes. ✅ Kc is constant at constant temperature; only temperature changes K.
  • ❌ Adding catalyst shifts equilibrium position. ✅ Catalyst speeds both forward and backward reactions equally; no shift in equilibrium.
  • ❌ High pressure always favors products. ✅ High pressure favors side with fewer gas moles (Le Chatelier).
  • ❌ Exothermic reactions are spontaneous. ✅ Spontaneity depends on ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, not just ΔH.
  • ❌ Endothermic reactions cannot be spontaneous. ✅ Endothermic reactions can be spontaneous if TΔS > ΔH (e.g., ice melting above 0°C).
  • ❌ ΔH and ΔU are always the same. ✅ ΔH = ΔU + PΔV; they differ when gas moles change.
  • ❌ Heat (q) and work (w) are state functions. ✅ q and w are path functions; only ΔU, ΔH, ΔS, ΔG are state functions.
  • ❌ Oxidation and reduction happen separately. ✅ Oxidation and reduction always occur together (redox).
  • ❌ Oxidizing agent gets oxidized. ✅ Oxidizing agent accepts electrons and gets reduced.
  • ❌ Reducing agent gets reduced. ✅ Reducing agent donates electrons and gets oxidized.
  • ❌ Faraday's laws apply only to metals. ✅ Faraday's laws apply to any substance deposited/dissolved at electrodes.
  • ❌ Real gases obey PV=nRT at all conditions. ✅ Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature.
  • ❌ van der Waals constants a and b are same for all gases. ✅ a (attraction) and b (volume) are gas-specific.
  • ❌ Surface tension increases with temperature. ✅ Surface tension decreases as temperature increases.
  • ❌ Viscosity decreases with temperature for liquids. ✅ Viscosity of liquids decreases with T; gases increase with T.
  • ❌ Colligative properties depend on nature of solute. ✅ Colligative properties depend only on number of particles, not identity.
  • ❌ Raoult's law applies to all solutions. ✅ Raoult's law is exact for ideal solutions; approximate for dilute real solutions.
  • ❌ Osmotic pressure is a colligative property. ✅ Yes — π = CRT depends on solute particle concentration.
  • ❌ All carbon compounds are organic. ✅ CO, CO₂, carbonates, and carbides are inorganic despite containing carbon.

Key Points

  • Isomerism: same molecular formula, different properties. Two branches: structural (different connectivity) — chain (C4H10: butane/2-methylpropane), position (propan-1-ol/propan-2-ol), functional (C2H6O: ethanol/dimethyl ether), metamerism (C4H10O: ethoxyethane/methoxypropane), tautomerism (keto-enol, dynamic); stereoisomerism (same connectivity, different 3-D) — geometrical cis/trans (but-2-ene), optical d/l (lactic acid, chiral carbon). Summary

Isomers share a molecular formula and nothing else — their atoms are arranged differently, so their properties differ. The classification has two great branches. Structural isomerism changes connectivity: chain (butane/2-methylpropane), position (propan-1-ol/propan-2-ol), functional (ethanol/dimethyl ether), metamerism (ethoxyethane/methoxypropane) and tautomerism (the dynamic keto ⇌ enol shift). Stereoisomerism keeps connectivity and changes geometry: geometrical cis–trans isomerism (but-2-ene, locked by the double bond) and optical d/l isomerism (lactic acid, non-superimposable mirror images around a chiral carbon). Structural isomers interconvert only through bond-breaking; stereoisomers through rotation or inversion — a single molecular formula is an inventory, and arrangement is what makes a molecule itself.
