Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Contact Process: The King of Acids

    Sulphuric acid is made by the Contact process: sulphur (or pyrite) is burned to SO₂, the SO₂ is oxidised to SO₃ over a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst, and the SO₃ is absorbed in concentrated H₂SO₄ to give oleum, diluted to the commercial 98% acid. Its volume of production makes H₂SO₄ the industrial barometer — the process runs the world's largest single chemical product.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Step 1 — SO₂ generation: S + O₂ → SO₂ (burning sulphur), or from pyrite: 4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂.
    • Step 2 — purification (before the catalyst): dust chamber → scrubber/washing tower → drying tower (conc. H₂SO₄) → arsenic remover — the gases must be clean because As and dust poison the V₂O₅ catalyst.
    • Step 3 — the contact step: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, ΔH = −196 kJ/mol, over V₂O₅ at ~450 °C, 1–2 atm, with excess air; 3 mol gas → 2 mol, so modest pressure already gives good conversion and higher pressure is not worth the cost.
    • Step 4 — absorption: SO₃ is absorbed in concentrated H₂SO₄ (98%), not water: H₂SO₄ + SO₃ → H₂S₂O₇ (oleum); the oleum is then diluted with water to 98% H₂SO₄.
    • Le Chatelier: exothermic ⟹ low T favours SO₃, but rate needs heat ⟹ the 450 °C compromise; fewer moles ⟹ pressure favours product, but the equilibrium is already ~99% converted at 1–2 atm ⟹ pressure is not raised.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Why Oleum and Not Water: The Fog Problem

    • Absorbing SO₃ directly into water is thermodynamically fine but engineering-wise a disaster: the reaction is violently exothermic and the product leaves the gas–liquid interface as a mist of tiny H₂SO₄ droplets that is nearly impossible to condense — it escapes with the exit gas as acid fog. Absorbing into 98% acid instead gives a controlled, low-vapour-pressure absorption: the SO₃ dissolves into the acid's own H₂SO₄ molecules to form oleum, which is then diluted downstream at leisure. The lesson: in absorption processes the absorbing medium's vapour pressure is as important as its reactivity — the diluent's job is to keep the product in the liquid phase.
    • This is the classic example of "chemistry right, engineering wrong": the direct hydration would give 100% acid on paper; in practice it gives an acid fog the scrubbers cannot catch. The two-step oleum route converts an uncondensable product into a condensable intermediate.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Why the Pressure Is Low: Reading an Equilibrium Economically

    • 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃ reduces 3 mol to 2 mol, so pressure does favour the product — yet the plant runs at 1–2 atm. The reason: at 450 °C with excess air the equilibrium conversion is already ~99% without added pressure, and pressure equipment for a marginal 1% is uneconomic. The contrast with Haber is the exam's favourite pairing: Haber (4→2 mol, only ~15% per pass) must buy pressure; Contact (3→2 mol, ~99% per pass) need not. The general law: pressure is purchased only when the equilibrium conversion without it is too low to pay — the Le Chatelier arrow is not automatically a plant specification.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (4m): Describe the manufacture of sulphuric acid by the Contact process. — Stage 1 — SO₂ generation: sulphur is burned in dry air: S + O₂ → SO₂ (or pyrite roasted: 4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂). Stage 2 — purification: the gas passes through a dust chamber, a scrubbing tower, a drying tower (concentrated H₂SO₄) and an arsenic remover — essential because dust and arsenic impurities poison the catalyst. Stage 3 — contact step: the purified gas (with excess air) passes over a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst at ~450 °C and 1–2 atm: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ + 196 kJ — about 99% conversion. Stage 4 — absorption: SO₃ is absorbed in concentrated (98%) sulphuric acid, forming oleum: H₂SO₄ + SO₃ → H₂S₂O₇; the oleum is diluted with water to give 98% H₂SO₄: H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄. Deep point: the process's two design decisions — the low pressure (the equilibrium is already ~99% converted, so pressure would buy nothing) and the oleum absorption (direct water gives an uncondensable acid fog) — are the two places where reading the chemistry economically separates a full answer from a list.
  • NEB-style (5m): In the Contact process, SO3 could in principle be dissolved directly in water to give H2SO4. Explain why the plant absorbs it in concentrated acid instead, and write both relevant equations. — The direct route fails in practice: SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ is violently exothermic, and at the gas–liquid interface the heat boils off a mist of tiny sulphuric acid droplets — an aerosol with enormous surface area that passes straight through ordinary condensers and escapes as acid fog, polluting and losing product. The plant route: the SO₃ is absorbed into 98% H₂SO₄, whose very low vapour pressure keeps the product in the liquid phase: H₂SO₄ + SO₃ → H₂S₂O₇ (oleum, fuming liquid). The oleum is then diluted with water at controlled rate downstream: H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄ — the same chemistry, executed where it can be contained. Deep point: the general principle is that in gas absorption the absorbing medium's vapour pressure is as decisive as its reactivity — the diluent's role is to hold the product in the liquid phase, and "chemistry right, engineering wrong" is the phrase that earns the analysis mark.
  • NEB-style (5m): Both the Haber and Contact processes involve an exothermic gas-phase oxidation whose product mole count falls. Explain why one runs at 200-300 atm and the other at 1-2 atm. — Haber: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (4 → 2 mol) has a low equilibrium conversion at plant temperature — only ~15% per pass at 450–500 °C — so pressure is essential on two counts: it raises the per-pass yield (Le Chatelier) and the collision-driven rate; the plant must buy pressure to be viable. Contact: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (3 → 2 mol) at 450 °C with excess air already reaches ~99% conversion at 1–2 atm — the equilibrium sits almost entirely on the product side without help. The economic law: pressure equipment (compressors, thick vessels, pumping power) is capital and operating cost; it is purchased only when the pressureless equilibrium is too low to pay. A marginal improvement from ~99% to ~99.5% cannot justify the plant. Shared compromise: both run at ~450 °C for the same reason — the exothermic equilibrium wants cold, the rate wants heat, and the catalyst lets the compromise sit as low as it does. Deep point: the pairing teaches the meta-lesson of industrial equilibrium — Le Chatelier tells you the direction of the lever, the equilibrium constant tells you whether you need to pull it, and economics tells you how hard.
  • NEB-style (5m): The gases entering the converter in the Contact process are washed, dried and passed through an arsenic remover. Explain what each stage protects, and what would happen if the gas went in dirty. — Dust chamber: removes solid particles that would otherwise physically blanket the catalyst surface and block the beds. Scrubber (washing tower): removes soluble impurities (acid mists, HCl traces) that would corrode and contaminate downstream equipment. Drying tower (conc. H₂SO₄): removes moisture — water in the converter would form acid mist and, with SO₃, cause corrosion and product loss before the absorption stage. Arsenic remover: removes As₂O₃ vapour (from pyrite ores) — arsenic compounds chemically poison the V₂O₅ catalyst, binding to its active sites and destroying its activity permanently. If the gas went in dirty: the catalyst's activity would fall within weeks — conversion would drop from ~99%, the exit SO₂ would rise, and the plant would need a costly catalyst change; the entire purification train is cheaper than one poisoned catalyst bed. Deep point: catalyst poisoning is a capital problem, not a running cost — the purification stages exist because replacing the catalyst costs more than years of washing, drying and filtering, which is why the flow sheet's order (purify, then convert) is not negotiable.

Key Points

  • Step 1: S + O2 -> SO2 (or 4FeS2 + 11O2 -> 2Fe2O3 + 8SO2). Step 2 purification: dust -> scrubber -> drying (conc. H2SO4) -> arsenic remover - protects the catalyst from poisoning. Step 3: 2SO2 + O2 <-> 2SO3, V2O5, ~450 C, 1-2 atm, excess air; 3 mol -> 2 mol but ~99% conversion without pressure - contrast with Haber's 15%. Step 4: SO3 absorbed in 98% H2SO4 -> H2S2O7 (oleum), then diluted to 98% acid; direct water absorption gives an uncondensable H2SO4 fog. Low T favours SO3 (exothermic) but rate needs heat - the 450 C compromise.

Summary

The Contact process makes the king of acids in four stages: burn sulphur to SO₂, purify the gas exhaustively for the V₂O₅ catalyst's sake, oxidise 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ at 450 °C and barely any pressure (the equilibrium is already ~99% converted — the deliberate contrast with Haber's bought pressure), and absorb the SO₃ in 98% acid to oleum because direct water would boil off an uncondensable fog. Purify for the catalyst, dilute through oleum for the condenser, skip the pressure because the chemistry already pays — the process is industrial equilibrium taught by exception.
