Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Sodium Carbonate by the Ammonia–Soda (Solvay) Process

    The Solvay process makes sodium carbonate from three cheap inputs — brine, limestone and ammonia — with the ammonia recycled as a carrier: NH₃ absorbs CO₂ to give ammonium bicarbonate, which metathesises with NaCl to precipitate the sparingly soluble NaHCO₃, calcined to Na₂CO₃. The elegance is that ammonia is not consumed — it is the process's reusable scaffold.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Inputs: brine (NaCl), limestone (CaCO₃), ammonia (recycled), water; fuel for calcination.
    • Step 1 — ammoniation and carbonation: NH₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → NH₄HCO₃ (ammoniated brine absorbs CO₂ from the kiln gas).
    • Step 2 — precipitation: NH₄HCO₃ + NaCl → NaHCO₃↓ + NH₄Cl — sodium bicarbonate's low solubility in the cold ammoniated brine makes it the only solid that crashes out.
    • Step 3 — calcination: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ — the CO₂ is recycled to step 1.
    • Step 4 — ammonia recovery: 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃↑ + 2H₂O — the lime from limestone calcination (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂) regenerates the ammonia.
    • Net reaction (ammonia cancelled): 2NaCl + CaCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂ — the only true consumption.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Ammonia as a Carrier: The Process's Masterstroke

    • Ammonia's role is provisional basicity: it raises the brine's effective carbonate concentration by holding CO₂ as NH₄HCO₃, creating the conditions in which NaHCO₃ precipitates — then it is recovered and returned. In the net equation ammonia never appears; it is a catalyst in the economic sense — a substance that enables the transformation without being consumed. The distinction from a chemical catalyst: ammonia participates stoichiometrically in each step (it is a reagent en route), but the recovery step closes its cycle — "stoichiometric en route, catalytic overall."
    • The recycling is the process's economics: every mole of NH₃ lost is a mole bought again, so the recovery step's efficiency (lime treatment of mother liquor) is watched as carefully as the precipitation itself. Solvay's advantage over the older Leblanc process was precisely this closed loop plus cheap inputs — the Leblanc process died of its waste products; Solvay survives on its recycle.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Why NaHCO3 Precipitates: Solubility as Selectivity

    • The carbonating tower's brine contains Na⁺, NH₄⁺, Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻ — four salts are possible; only NaHCO₃ is sparingly soluble in the cold concentrated solution (NaCl, NH₄Cl, NH₄HCO₃ all stay dissolved). The precipitation is therefore selective by solubility, not by reactivity — the process never needs to remove anything; it needs only to make the wanted salt the least soluble thing present.
    • The by-product question defines the process's fate: the net reaction's other product, CaCl₂, has limited demand — mountains of it accumulated historically. The Solvay process's viability depends on finding outlets (de-icing, dust control, desiccant) or accepting the disposal cost; this is the general lesson that a process's by-product, not its product, often decides its survival.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (3m): Describe the manufacture of sodium carbonate by the ammonia-soda (Solvay) process. — Inputs: brine (NaCl), limestone (CaCO₃), and ammonia (recycled). Step 1: brine is saturated with ammonia and then carbonated with CO₂ (from the lime kiln): NH₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → NH₄HCO₃. Step 2: the ammoniated brine meets NaCl and the sparingly soluble sodium bicarbonate precipitates: NH₄HCO₃ + NaCl → NaHCO₃↓ + NH₄Cl (filtered off). Step 3: the bicarbonate is calcined: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ — the CO₂ returns to step 1. Step 4: the mother liquor's ammonium chloride is treated with lime (from CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ in the kiln): 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃↑ + 2H₂O — ammonia is recovered and recycled. Net: 2NaCl + CaCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂. Deep point: the process's signature is the ammonia loop — consumed in no net amount, it is a carrier whose recovery efficiency decides the plant's economy, and writing the net equation with NH₃ absent is the proof that the loop closes.
  • NEB-style (5m): Ammonia is used in large quantities in the Solvay process yet appears in no net equation. Explain this apparent paradox, distinguishing ammonia's role from that of a true catalyst. — The paradox resolved: ammonia does participate stoichiometrically in steps 1, 2 and 4 — it is absorbed as NH₄HCO₃, exchanged for Na⁺, and liberated again by lime — but the recovery step returns every mole, so the net reaction (2NaCl + CaCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂) contains no ammonia. Carrier vs catalyst: a true catalyst (like V₂O₅ in the Contact process) provides an alternative pathway by surface action and is never part of the product's stoichiometry; ammonia is a reagent en route with a catalyst's overall economics — it forms actual compounds (NH₄HCO₃, NH₄Cl) and must be chemically regenerated, not merely recovered unchanged. Why the distinction matters operationally: a catalyst's losses are trace-metal losses; ammonia's losses are inventory losses — every escaping mole is a purchase order, so the recovery tower's efficiency is a watched operating variable. Deep point: "stoichiometric en route, catalytic overall" is the precise phrase — the Solvay ammonia is neither a reagent (it is not consumed) nor a catalyst (it is not unchanged), but a third category the process invented: the recycled carrier.
  • NEB-style (5m): In the carbonating tower, four salts are possible from the ions Na+, NH4+, Cl- and HCO3-. Explain why only sodium bicarbonate precipitates, and why the tower runs cold. — The four candidates: NaCl, NH₄Cl, NH₄HCO₃, NaHCO₃. The first three are all readily soluble in the concentrated ammoniated brine and remain dissolved; NaHCO₃ alone is sparingly soluble in the cold, concentrated solution — it is the only salt whose solubility product is exceeded, so it alone crystallises. The design logic: the process never removes unwanted ions; it arranges conditions so the wanted ion pair is the least soluble combination present — selectivity by solubility, not by reaction. Why cold: bicarbonate salts of ammonium and sodium are more soluble at higher temperature (and NH₄HCO₃ decomposes) — cooling the tower (counter-current brine at ~30–35 °C) keeps NaHCO₃'s solubility at its minimum and maximises the yield per pass. Deep point: the carbonating tower is a crystalliser pretending to be a reactor — the chemistry merely delivers the four ions to the same solution; the thermodynamics of solubility does the actual selection, which is why temperature control of the tower is a yield control.
  • NEB-style (6m): The Solvay process's net reaction produces calcium chloride as its co-product. Explain why this by-product is a strategic weakness, and what the historical comparison with the Leblanc process teaches about by-products generally. — The weakness: the net reaction is 2NaCl + CaCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂ — for every tonne of soda, roughly a tonne of calcium chloride is formed. Its markets (de-icing, dust suppression, desiccation, concrete set-acceleration) are small relative to the tonnage generated, so plants either sell it at low margin or bear disposal costs — a permanent tax on every tonne of product, and the reason new Solvay capacity is hard to justify where disposal is regulated. The Leblanc lesson: the older Leblanc process made soda via Na₂SO₄ + coal + CaCO₃, emitting HCl gas and calcium sulphide waste that poisoned the neighbourhoods around its plants — it was killed as much by its waste (legislation and nuisance) as by Solvay's economics. The general law: a process's viability is set by its worst output, not its best — product pays the bills, but the by-product decides whether the plant may keep operating. Deep point: modern process design internalises this lesson — the first question asked of a new route is no longer "what does it make?" but "what does everything else become?", which is the Solvay–Leblanc history rewritten as a design principle.

Key Points

  • Inputs: brine, limestone, recycled ammonia. Steps: (1) NH3 + CO2 + H2O -> NH4HCO3 in ammoniated brine; (2) NH4HCO3 + NaCl -> NaHCO3(s) + NH4Cl - selective by low solubility; (3) 2NaHCO3 -> Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2 (CO2 recycled); (4) 2NH4Cl + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCl2 + 2NH3 + 2H2O (ammonia recovered; lime from CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2). Net: 2NaCl + CaCO3 -> Na2CO3 + CaCl2. Ammonia = stoichiometric en route, catalytic overall. CaCl2 by-product's weak market is the process's long-term vulnerability.

Summary

The Solvay process turns brine and limestone into sodium carbonate with ammonia as the reusable scaffold: carbonation gives NH₄HCO₃, solubility selects NaHCO₃ alone to precipitate, calcination yields the soda and returns the CO₂, and lime closes the ammonia loop — leaving the net equation 2NaCl + CaCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂ with ammonia absent. Stoichiometric en route and catalytic overall, the carrier's recovery efficiency is the plant's pulse; and the calcium chloride mountain, like the Leblanc process's poisoned air before it, teaches that a process survives on its product but is judged by its by-products.
