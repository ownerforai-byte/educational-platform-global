Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Sodium Hydroxide by the Diaphragm Cell

    Caustic soda is manufactured by electrolysing concentrated brine in the diaphragm cell: chlorine at the anode, hydrogen at the cathode, and sodium hydroxide accumulating in the cathode compartment — with an asbestos diaphragm keeping the three products apart. The cell is simultaneously an electrochemistry lesson and a separation problem.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O --electrolysis--> 2NaOH + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g).
    • Anode (graphite or coated titanium): 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ — chlorine gas evolved.
    • Cathode (steel mesh): 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ — hydrogen evolved and OH⁻ generated; Na⁺ migrates in, giving NaOH solution (~10–12%).
    • Diaphragm (asbestos): a porous barrier that lets brine percolate from anode to cathode compartment while preventing the Cl₂ from mixing with the NaOH — without it, Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O (and further to NaClO₃) would consume the products.
    • Concentrated brine (25–30%): ensures Cl⁻ discharges at the anode in preference to OH⁻ (high [Cl⁻] wins the competition); the NaOH solution is evaporated to commercial strength.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — The Cell Is Three Products and a Separation Problem

    • The electrolysis of brine produces three sellable products at three electrodes/spaces: Cl₂ (anode space), H₂ (cathode space), NaOH (cathode solution). Their values differ (chlorine often drives the economics — PVC, bleach, disinfectants), and the cell's design is dictated by the fact that each product destroys another if they meet: Cl₂ + NaOH → hypochlorite; Cl₂ + H₂ → explosive HCl mixture (photochemically). The diaphragm is therefore not a detail — it is the product specification.
    • The discharge competitions are the cell's electrochemistry: at the anode, Cl⁻ vs OH⁻ — concentrated brine tips the choice to Cl₂ (dilute NaCl solution would give O₂ instead — the concentration dependence of discharge is the lesson); at the cathode, H₂O (as H⁺) discharges far more readily than Na⁺ — sodium metal is never produced in aqueous cells, which is why the molten Downs cell exists for Na metal and this cell exists for NaOH. Two cells, two products, one ionic difference.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Why the Diaphragm Must Percolate

    • The diaphragm is porous and brine flows through it from anode to cathode side: the flow's purpose is to sweep Na⁺ (and NaOH) to the cathode side while its bulk keeps the hydroxide from back-diffusing to the anode. The design tension: too little flow ⟹ OH⁻ back-diffuses and is oxidised (losing product, contaminating Cl₂); too much flow ⟹ NaOH arrives over-diluted and evaporation costs rise. The percolation rate is a tuned operating variable, not a passive property.
    • The cell's outputs are graded by their separation quality: the NaOH leaves containing residual NaCl (diaphragm cells give ~10–12% NaOH with ~1% NaCl), which membrane and mercury cells improved upon — the evolution of cell technology is a history of improving product separation.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Sodium metal forms at the cathode": never in aqueous solution — water discharges first; Na metal requires molten NaCl (Downs). "The diaphragm is an insulator": it is a selective barrier — ions and brine pass; the gases and products must not mix.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (5m): Describe the diaphragm cell for manufacturing sodium hydroxide, giving the electrode reactions and the role of the diaphragm. — The cell: a steel tank with a graphite (or coated titanium) anode and a steel mesh cathode, separated by a porous asbestos diaphragm; concentrated brine (25–30% NaCl) is fed to the anode compartment and percolates through the diaphragm. Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ — chlorine gas collected above the anode compartment. Cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ — hydrogen gas evolves and OH⁻ accumulates in the catholyte; Na⁺ migrating through the diaphragm gives NaOH solution (~10–12%), evaporated to commercial strength. Diaphragm's role: it permits ion flow and brine percolation but prevents Cl₂ from contacting the NaOH — otherwise Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O (and on to NaClO₃) would destroy both products; it also keeps H₂ and Cl₂ apart (explosive mixture). Deep point: the cell is three products (Cl₂, H₂, NaOH) and one separation problem — the diaphragm's porosity, percolation and placement are the engineering answer to a chemical incompatibility, which is why describing the barrier earns as many marks as the electrodes.
  • NEB-style (5m): In the electrolysis of brine, why is chlorine discharged at the anode rather than oxygen, and hydrogen rather than sodium at the cathode? What does the answer imply about making sodium metal? — Anode competition: both Cl⁻ and OH⁻ could oxidise; in concentrated brine (25–30%) the high chloride concentration and its lower discharge overpotential tip the selection to Cl₂ — in dilute NaCl solution the OH⁻ would win and oxygen would evolve instead. Discharge order at the anode is a competition governed by concentration and overpotential, not a fixed list. Cathode competition: Na⁺ and H₂O (via H⁺) compete for reduction — water is reduced far more readily than Na⁺ (sodium's position in the activity series means its ions hold their electrons), so H₂ evolves and Na⁺ merely spectates, pairing with the generated OH⁻ as NaOH. Implication: sodium metal can never be obtained from aqueous solution — the water always wins the cathode; metallic sodium requires the molten NaCl of the Downs cell, where there is no water to compete. Deep point: the two cells (diaphragm and Downs) are the same ionic competition answered by removing one competitor — the presence or absence of water is the entire difference between making NaOH and making Na.
  • NEB-style (5m): The diaphragm cell's sodium hydroxide product contains about 1% sodium chloride. Explain the origin of this contamination and one quality consequence, and name a cell design that reduces it. — Origin: the diaphragm is porous — Na⁺ and Cl⁻ both migrate/percolate through it with the brine flow; the chloride that reaches the catholyte cannot be discharged (water wins the cathode) and simply accumulates in the NaOH solution. Complete exclusion is impossible with a purely mechanical barrier. Quality consequence: for uses demanding salt-free caustic (rayon, some pharmaceuticals, electrolytic industries), residual NaCl causes problems — it must be removed by crystallisation of the evaporated liquor (NaCl is less soluble in concentrated NaOH and crystallises out) or the product is sold at "technical" grade. Better designs: the membrane cell replaces the porous asbestos with an ion-selective membrane (cation-exchange, passing Na⁺ but not Cl⁻), giving ~50% NaOH with only ppm-level chloride — the modern standard; the mercury cell (historical) produced very pure NaOH but is retired for environmental reasons. Deep point: the product's purity is the separator's selectivity — the history of chlor-alkali technology is the search for a barrier that passes Na⁺ but not Cl⁻, and the ion-exchange membrane is that answer.
  • NEB-style (6m): The diaphragm cell produces three products. Name them, state one major use of each, and explain why the plant's economics are often said to be driven by the chlorine. — Products: (1) chlorine (anode) — PVC (vinyl chloride), bleaching powder, water disinfection, CFC-replacement refrigerants; (2) hydrogen (cathode) — fuel, hydrogenation of oils, methanol synthesis; (3) sodium hydroxide (catholyte) — soap, paper pulping, textiles, alumina refining. Why chlorine drives the economics: the three are produced in fixed stoichiometric ratio (2NaOH : 1Cl₂ : 1H₂) — the plant cannot make one without the others. Chlorine's markets (PVC above all) are typically the largest and most price-elastic, so when PVC demand is high the cell-house runs flat out and caustic floods the market (lowering its price); when chlorine demand sags, caustic becomes scarce and dear. The co-product coupling: this "chlor-alkali balance" is a textbook case of fixed-ratio co-production — the demand curves of unrelated industries (construction via PVC, paper via NaOH) are chained by one electrolysis equation. Deep point: no single-product plant logic applies — the diaphragm cell's business plan is the simultaneous marketing of three commodities whose ratio chemistry fixes and whose prices move independently, and the chlorine leg usually sets the tempo.

Key Points

  • Overall: 2NaCl + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + Cl2 + H2 (electrolysis). Anode (graphite/Ti): 2Cl- -> Cl2 + 2e-. Cathode (steel mesh): 2H2O + 2e- -> H2 + 2OH-; NaOH ~10-12% with ~1% NaCl. Diaphragm (asbestos): porous, brine percolates anode->cathode; prevents Cl2 + NaOH -> NaCl + NaClO (+NaClO3). Concentrated brine (25-30%) ensures Cl- beats OH- at the anode. Water discharges before Na+ at the cathode - Na metal needs the molten Downs cell. Percolation rate is a tuned variable: too slow = OH- back-diffusion, too fast = dilute NaOH.

Summary

The diaphragm cell electrolyses concentrated brine into three sellable products — Cl₂ at the anode, H₂ at the cathode, NaOH in the catholyte — whose mutual incompatibility makes the asbestos diaphragm the product specification itself. Concentration wins the anode for chlorine, water wins the cathode against sodium (hence the molten Downs cell for Na metal), percolation through the barrier is a tuned variable, and the residual NaCl in the product is the separator's imperfection that the modern ion-exchange membrane finally removed. Fixed-ratio co-production means the plant's economics beat to chlorine's drum.
