Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Chemical Fertilizers and the Urea Flow Sheet

    Plants remove nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium from soil with every harvest; fertilizers return them. The three primary nutrients (N, P, K) define the fertilizer types — nitrogenous, phosphatic, potassic — and the NPK grade notation (e.g. 15:15:15) summarises a mixed fertilizer's strengths. Urea, CO(NH₂)₂, is the world's leading nitrogen fertilizer, made from ammonia and carbon dioxide in a two-step synthesis.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Nitrogenous fertilizers: ammonium sulphate (NH₄)₂SO₄ (21% N), ammonium nitrate NH₄NO₃ (35% N), urea CO(NH₂)₂ (46% N — the highest), ammonia itself.
    • Phosphatic fertilizers: superphosphate (rock phosphate Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + H₂SO₄ → soluble Ca(H₂PO₄)₂ + CaSO₄), triple superphosphate (with H₃PO₄); the treatment converts insoluble phosphate into plant-available soluble phosphate.
    • Potassic fertilizers: muriate of potash KCl (from sylvinite deposits), potassium sulphate.
    • NPK notation: the grade numbers are the percentage of N, P₂O₅-equivalent and K₂O-equivalent; a 15:15:15 mixed fertilizer supplies all three.
    • Urea synthesis: 2NH₃ + CO₂ → NH₂COONH₄ (ammonium carbamate) → CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O — the first step at high pressure (~140–200 atm, ~180–200 °C), the second by decomposition; excess NH₃ is recovered and recycled.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Why Urea Wins: The Nutrient-Density Argument

    • Nitrogen content is the currency: per tonne of product, urea delivers 46% N against ammonium nitrate's 35% and ammonium sulphate's 21% — every shipment carries the least ballast. Add non-explosive handling (unlike AN), solid prill form, easy blending, and soil chemistry that releases N gradually via hydrolysis (urease → NH₃), and urea's dominance is a logistics victory as much as a chemical one.
    • The synthesis's elegance: its only inputs are the two products of two processes this unit already studied — NH₃ from the Haber plant and CO₂ recovered from the reformer's shifted gas — so an integrated fertilizer complex makes urea from air, water and natural gas with near-zero carbon waste. Urea is the Haber process's commercial terminus: the ammonia plant's economics are often written in urea.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — What Each Nutrient Actually Does

    • N: builds amino acids, proteins, chlorophyll — drives leafy vegetative growth; deficiency shows as general yellowing (chlorosis), oldest leaves first; excess causes lodging (weak stems) and delayed maturity.
    • P: nucleic acids, ATP, phospholipids — root development, flowering, energy transfer; deficiency shows as purple/reddish tints on older leaves and stunted roots.
    • K: enzyme activation, stomatal regulation, sugar transport — stem strength, disease resistance, fruit quality; deficiency shows as leaf-edge scorching.
    • The diagnostic logic: each nutrient's deficiency signature appears in the tissue that has least of it — mobile nutrients (N, P, K are all mobile in the plant) are scavenged from old leaves for new growth, so deficiency always reads on the oldest leaves first.

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — classify and quantify: fertilizer types with N% comparison.
    • Type B — the urea flow sheet: reactions, conditions, recycle.
    • Type C — deficiency diagnosis: match symptom to nutrient with the mobile-nutrient logic.
    • Type D — NPK grade reading: interpret a grade and compute nutrient supply.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "NPK means equal parts of elements": the numbers are N : P₂O₅ : K₂O equivalents — not elemental P and K. "Urea is ammonium": it is an organic amide; the soil's urease converts it to ammonium before roots absorb it.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (2m): What is NPK fertilizer? How is urea produced? — NPK fertilizer: a mixed fertilizer supplying the three primary nutrients in one product — its grade numbers give the percentage of N, P₂O₅-equivalent and K₂O-equivalent (e.g. 15:15:15 contains 15% N, 15% P₂O₅, 15% K₂O); it is made by blending/compounding nitrogenous, phosphatic and potassic carriers. Urea production: ammonia (from the Haber plant) and carbon dioxide (recovered from the reforming section) are compressed and reacted at high pressure (~140–200 atm) and ~180–200 °C to form ammonium carbamate: 2NH₃ + CO₂ → NH₂COONH₄. The carbamate is then decomposed (by lowering pressure/heat) to urea: NH₂COONH₄ → CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O. The urea solution is concentrated, and the melt is prilled into solid granules; unreacted NH₃ and CO₂ are recycled to the synthesiser. Deep point: the flow sheet's elegance is its inputs — both come from within the same complex (NH₃ synthesis, CO₂ by-product of reforming), making urea the Haber process's commercial terminus and the reason ammonia and urea plants share a fence.
  • NEB-style (5m): Draw the urea flow sheet and write the two reactions with conditions. Why is the excess ammonia recycled rather than vented? — Flow sheet: NH₃ + CO₂ (compressed) → urea synthesiser (carbamate formation + decomposition, ~140–200 atm, 180–200 °C) → decomposer/flash (unreacted carbamate splits) → recycle loop (NH₃ + CO₂ back to the synthesiser) and urea solution forward → evaporator/concentrator → prilling tower → solid urea. Reactions: 2NH₃ + CO₂ → NH₂COONH₄ (ammonium carbamate — exothermic, high pressure drives it); NH₂COONH₄ → CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O (decomposition — heat, moderate pressure). Why recycle: the synthesis reaches only partial conversion per pass (the equilibrium is unfavourable at synthesis temperature); venting the excess NH₃ would waste both the Haber plant's most expensive product and the CO₂ feed — recycling lifts overall conversion to ~95%+ at the cost only of recompression, exactly the logic of the Haber loop itself. Deep point: the recycle-loop pattern appears in three of this unit's processes (Haber, urea, Solvay's ammonia) — the general principle is that a moderate per-pass equilibrium plus a cheap separation equals an excellent overall yield, and the exam answer that names the loop names the economics.
  • NEB-style (6m): A farmer's maize shows yellowing of the older leaves and purple-tinged stunted seedlings in another field. Diagnose the deficiencies, justify using nutrient mobility, and state one suitable fertilizer for each. — Diagnosis 1 — nitrogen: general yellowing (chlorosis) of the older, lower leaves is the classic N deficiency — nitrogen is a constituent of chlorophyll and amino acids, and its shortage shows first where the plant has least of it. Diagnosis 2 — phosphorus: purple/reddish tints with stunted roots indicate P deficiency — phosphorus drives root development and energy transfer (ATP), and its shortage pigments the old leaves anthocyanin-red. The mobility justification: N, P and K are all mobile nutrients — the plant scavenges them from old tissues to feed new growth, so deficiency always appears on the oldest leaves first (immobile nutrients like Fe and Ca, by contrast, show on the youngest). Both symptoms sit on old tissue ⟹ mobile-nutrient deficiencies, consistent with N and P. Fertilizers: N — urea (46% N) or ammonium sulphate; P — superphosphate (rock phosphate treated with H₂SO₄ to give plant-available Ca(H₂PO₄)₂). Deep point: the oldest-leaves-first rule is the topic's one-line diagnostic engine — it converts a symptom chart into a reasoning chain, and quoting the mobility principle (not just the symptom table) is what separates a diagnosis from a guess.
  • NEB-style (6m): Compare urea, ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulphate as nitrogen fertilizers on nitrogen content, handling safety and soil behaviour, and state why urea dominates the world market. — Nitrogen content: urea CO(NH₂)₂ = 28/60 = 46% N (highest); ammonium nitrate NH₄NO₃ = 28/80 = 35% N; ammonium sulphate (NH₄)₂SO₄ = 28/132 = 21% N (lowest — but it also supplies sulphur). Handling safety: urea is non-explosive and thermally stable — safe to store, ship and blend; ammonium nitrate is an oxidiser/explosive (stored under strict regulation after industrial disasters); ammonium sulphate is safe but heavy per unit N. Soil behaviour: urea is an amide — soil urease hydrolyses it to ammonium carbonate over days, releasing N gradually (slower acting, some volatilisation loss if surface-applied unincorporated); both ammonium salts act immediately as NH₄⁺, with nitrate also supplying NO₃⁻ directly; ammonium sulphate acidifies the soil over time. Why urea dominates: highest nutrient density (least ballast per tonne shipped), cheapest manufacture (two commodity inputs from the same complex), safe handling, solid prill form suited to mechanised spreading and blending. Deep point: the market share follows from logistics arithmetic — a fertilizer is a shipped commodity before it is a soil chemistry, and the 46%-vs-21% density gap plus the explosives problem decide the ranking long before agronomy is consulted.

Summary

Fertilizers return the three nutrients every harvest removes: nitrogenous carriers ranked by N density (urea 46%, nitrate 35%, sulphate 21%), phosphatic superphosphate made plant-available by acid treatment, and potassic KCl — combined in NPK grades whose numbers are P₂O₅/K₂O equivalents. Urea rules the market as the Haber process's commercial terminus: carbamate at high pressure, decomposition to CO(NH₂)₂, recycle of excess ammonia, and a soil release governed by urease. Its dominance is logistics arithmetic — and every deficiency diagnosis in the field runs on the same one-line engine: mobile nutrients read on the oldest leaves first.
