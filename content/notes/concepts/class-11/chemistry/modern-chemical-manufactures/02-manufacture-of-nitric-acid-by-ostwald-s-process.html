Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Ostwald Process: Ammonia into Nitric Acid

    The Ostwald process converts ammonia into nitric acid in three steps: catalytic oxidation of NH₃ to NO, oxidation of NO to NO₂, and absorption of NO₂ in water. It is the industrial hinge between the Haber process (NH₃) and the entire nitrogen-chemicals industry — fertilisers, explosives, and nylon all pass through its acid.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Step 1 — catalytic oxidation: 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O, over a platinum–rhodium gauze at 800–900 °C, with roughly 10% NH₃ in air (below the explosive limit), contact time ~10⁻³ s.
    • Step 2 — oxidation of NO: 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ — an exothermic reaction favoured by low temperature, so the converter's gas is quickly cooled (waste-heat boilers) before the oxidation towers.
    • Step 3 — absorption: 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃ in water-filled absorption towers with excess air circulating.
    • Overall: NH₃ → HNO₃ with air and water as the only other inputs; the acid is concentrated by distillation to ~68% (the azeotrope).
    • The quirk: step 1's product NO is thermodynamically unstable at low temperature — the rapid quench is what freezes it in place long enough to be oxidised on demand.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Each Step Has Its Own Temperature Logic

    • Step 1 needs heat: the NH₃ oxidation is run hot (800–900 °C) for rate and selectivity — the Pt–Rh gauze lights the reaction and the mixture's own oxidation sustains it; the short contact time prevents decomposition of NH₃ to N₂ (the undesired competing path).
    • Step 2 needs cold: 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ is exothermic and its equilibrium favours NO₂ only at low temperature — at the converter's 900 °C the oxide would stay as NO. Hence the design's signature move: quench immediately after the gauze, then oxidise in the cool towers.
    • Step 3 needs water and air: the absorption consumes both NO₂ and O₂ together — excess air is circulated so the absorbed NO₂ is continuously replenished from residual NO; the absorption is the step where the acid actually appears.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Why Platinum–Rhodium, and Why a Gauze

    • Catalytic demands: NH₃ oxidation needs a catalyst active at 800–900 °C, selective for NO (not N₂), and resistant to oxidation itself — platinum is nearly unique; rhodium adds mechanical strength and reduces platinum loss (Pt vaporises slowly at operating temperature).
    • Why gauze, not granules: the reaction is mass-transfer limited — a fine woven gauze presents maximum surface with minimum pressure drop, and the heat of oxidation keeps the gauze at operating temperature once lit. Spent gauze is periodically recovered (Pt is worth recovering — the catalyst is the plant's most valuable furniture).

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — describe with equations: the three steps with conditions.
    • Type B — the quench question: why rapid cooling between steps 1 and 2.
    • Type C — catalyst reasoning: Pt–Rh gauze selection and geometry.
    • Type D — chain-of-industry: NH₃ → HNO₃ → ammonium nitrate/ANFO linkage.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "NO₂ is absorbed as such": the net absorption consumes O₂ too — the balanced tower equation includes it. "Cooling is for safety": it is for equilibrium — step 2's product only exists in quantity at low temperature.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (3m): Describe the Ostwald process for nitric acid manufacture. — Step 1 — catalytic oxidation: ammonia (about 10% in air, below the explosive limit) is passed over a platinum–rhodium gauze at 800–900 °C for a fraction of a second: 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O. Step 2 — oxidation of NO: the hot gas is rapidly cooled (waste-heat boilers) and treated with more air in cooling/oxidation towers: 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ (brown gas). Step 3 — absorption: NO₂ with excess air is absorbed in water in packed towers: 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃, giving nitric acid of ~50–60%, concentrated by distillation toward the 68% azeotrope. Deep point: the process's elegance is the temperature choreography — 900 °C to make NO, a quench to freeze it, cool towers to oxidise and absorb it; each step's condition is the previous step's equilibrium consequence, and the three balanced equations are the answer's skeleton.
  • NEB-style (5m): The oxidation of NO to NO2 is exothermic. Explain why the Ostwald plant cools the gas rapidly after the catalytic converter, and what would happen if the gas were held hot. — The equilibrium: 2NO + O₂ ⇌ 2NO₂ releases heat — low temperature favours NO₂; high temperature favours the decomposition back to NO. The quench: cooling the converter's 900 °C gas within seconds traps the NO in a temperature regime where oxidation to NO₂ is both thermodynamically favoured and kinetically achievable in the towers. If held hot: the NO would remain NO (the equilibrium sits on the NO side at high T), and worse — at high temperature NO slowly decomposes toward N₂ and O₂, destroying the oxidation work of step 1; the yield of acid would collapse. Deep point: the quench is the process's most examinable design detail because it is pure Le Chatelier applied in time — the gas is raced from a temperature where NO is the product to one where NO₂ is, and the towers finish the race.
  • NEB-style (5m): Why is the ammonia oxidation catalyst a platinum-rhodium gauze rather than granules of platinum? Give two reasons for the rhodium and one for the gauze geometry. — Catalytic demands: NH₃ → NO requires a catalyst that is active at 800–900 °C, highly selective for the 5-electron oxidation to NO (avoiding the competing decomposition to N₂), and stable against oxidation at temperature — platinum is essentially unique. Rhodium's roles: (1) mechanical strength — pure Pt gauze creeps and tears at 900 °C; (2) reduced platinum loss — Pt vaporises slowly at operating temperature, and Rh alloys suppress it. Gauze geometry: the reaction is mass-transfer limited; a fine woven mesh presents the maximum active surface with the minimum pressure drop, and the exothermic oxidation keeps the lit gauze at its own operating temperature. Deep point: catalyst selection is a materials problem as much as a chemistry one — activity, selectivity, strength and vapour pressure are four independent requirements, and the Pt–Rh gauze is the textbook case of one object answering all four.
  • NEB-style (6m): Trace the industrial chain from natural gas to ammonium nitrate fertiliser, naming the two processes and writing the final neutralisation equation. — Link 1 — Haber: natural gas → (steam reforming CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂; shift CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂) → H₂; N₂ from liquid air ⟹ N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (Fe, 200–300 atm, 450–500 °C). Link 2 — Ostwald: NH₃ + air → (Pt–Rh, 800–900 °C) NO → NO₂ → 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃. Final step — neutralisation: NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃ (ammonium nitrate) — an exothermic salt-forming reaction run in solution, evaporated and prilled. The chain's logic: air's N₂ is inert; the two processes are the two keys that unlock it — Haber reduces it to NH₃, Ostwald oxidises that to the acid, and the acid neutralises the base back into the fertiliser salt. Deep point: the chain explains why ammonia plants and nitric acid plants are built adjacent — the intermediate NH₃ is both the first product and the second process's feedstock; integrated fertiliser complexes are the Haber–Ostwald couple poured in concrete.

Key Points

  • Three steps: (1) 4NH3 + 5O2 -> 4NO + 6H2O, Pt-Rh gauze, 800-900 C, ~10% NH3 in air, millisecond contact; (2) 2NO + O2 -> 2NO2, exothermic, favoured by cold - hence rapid quench after the gauze; (3) 4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4HNO3 in absorption towers with excess air. Pt-Rh gauze: active + selective for NO + strong; gauze geometry = max surface, min pressure drop. Concentration by distillation to the 68% azeotrope. The chain: Haber NH3 -> Ostwald HNO3 -> ammonium nitrate fertiliser/explosive.

Summary

The Ostwald process is a three-temperature choreography: 800–900 °C on a Pt–Rh gauze to oxidise NH₃ to NO, a rapid quench to freeze the thermodynamically fragile NO, and cool absorption towers where 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃ with excess air circulating. The gauze's geometry maximises surface at minimum pressure drop; the quench is Le Chatelier applied in time; and the acid flows onward to ammonium nitrate — the Haber–Ostwald couple being the industrial sentence that turns air into fertiliser.
