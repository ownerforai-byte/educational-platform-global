  • 1. Topic Introduction — Building the Simplest Skeletons

    Alkanes are the saturated hydrocarbons: every C–C bond is a single σ bond, every carbon is sp³, and the formula family is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Since they are chemically quiet, the interesting chemistry is how to make them: from haloalkanes by reduction, by the Wurtz reaction, by decarboxylation of sodium salts, and by catalytic hydrogenation of alkenes and alkynes.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 From haloalkanes — reduction

    • RX + 2[H] → RH + HX — the halogen is stripped and replaced by hydrogen.
    • Reagents: Zn + HCl (nascent hydrogen), LiAlH₄ (dry ether), or H₂/Ni.
    • Example: CH₃Cl + 2[H] → CH₄ + HCl.

    2.2 Wurtz reaction

    • 2RX + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX — dry ether medium; two alkyl radicals couple.
    • Builds symmetrical alkanes with an even number of carbons efficiently: 2 CH₃Br → ethane; 2 C₂H₅Br → butane.
    • Limitation: with two different haloalkanes, a mixture of three alkanes (R–R, R–R′, R′–R′) forms — poor for unsymmetrical targets.

    2.3 Decarboxylation

    • RCOONa + NaOH (CaO) → RH + Na₂CO₃ — soda-lime heat removes one carbon as carbonate.
    • CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃ (CaO, ~300 °C) — the classic methane prep.
    • The alkane has one carbon fewer than the acid.

    2.4 Catalytic hydrogenation

    • Alkene + H₂ (Ni, ~200 °C or Pt/Pd, rt) → alkane; alkyne + 2H₂ → alkane.
    • CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃–CH₃; HC≡CH + 2H₂ → CH₃–CH₃.
    • Syn addition on the metal surface; the cleanest route to any specific alkane.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Choosing the right route

    • Need a specific unsymmetrical alkane? Hydrogenation of the matching alkene/alkyne is cleanest. Need a symmetrical even-carbon alkane? Wurtz. Need methane itself? Decarboxylation of sodium acetate is the textbook answer.

    4.2 Wurtz arithmetic

    • Alkane carbons = sum of the two R groups. Two identical R of n carbons → 2n. This is why odd-carbon alkanes are awkward by Wurtz — mixing R and R′ gives three products.

    4.3 Decarboxylation bookkeeping

    • The carboxyl carbon leaves as Na₂CO₃, so count one carbon less: C₂ acid → C₁ alkane (acetate → methane); C₃ acid → ethane.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Wurtz is in dry ether: water would quench the sodium; "dry ether" is part of the answer.

  • Decarboxylation loses a carbon: acetate (C₂) gives methane (C₁) — students often write ethane.
  • Alkyne needs 2H₂: each π bond takes one H₂; an alkyne has two.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB 2024 (verified PYQ): How is ethane prepared by the Wurtz reaction? Write the chemical equation. (2m) — In the Wurtz reaction, an alkyl halide is treated with sodium metal in dry ether, where two alkyl groups couple to form a symmetrical alkane: 2RX + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX. For ethane, bromomethane (or chloromethane) is used: 2CH₃Br + 2Na → CH₃–CH₃ + 2NaBr (dry ether). The two methyl radicals generated on sodium couple, giving ethane with an even number of carbons; the reaction is best suited to symmetrical alkanes.

  • NEB-style (3m): Explain the preparation of methane by decarboxylation with equation. — Sodium acetate is heated with soda-lime (NaOH + CaO): CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃. The carboxylate loses its carboxyl carbon as carbonate (trapped by CaO), leaving a hydrocarbon with one carbon fewer than the acid. CaO keeps the mixture dry and fused; the reaction runs at ~300 °C. This is the standard laboratory route to methane.
  • NEB-style (2m): Give one limitation of the Wurtz reaction. — With a mixture of two different haloalkanes (RX + R′X), three coupling products form — R–R, R–R′ and R′–R′ — in unpredictable ratios, so the Wurtz reaction gives poor yields of any unsymmetrical alkane; it is reliable only for symmetrical products. (Also: tertiary halides tend to eliminate instead of coupling.)

Examples

  • The Wurtz ledger — two methyls make ethane (2 carbons), two ethyls make butane (4): the reaction is a carbon-doubling machine, which is exactly why odd-carbon alkanes resist it.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (verified PYQ): How is ethane prepared by the Wurtz reaction? Write the chemical equation. (2m) — In the Wurtz reaction, an alkyl halide is heated with sodium metal in dry ether; two alkyl groups couple to give a symmetrical alkane: 2RX + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX. For ethane, take bromomethane: 2CH₃Br + 2Na → CH₃–CH₃ + 2NaBr (dry ether). Sodium first converts the haloalkane into an alkyl-sodium/radical species; two such fragments then couple, so the product contains an even number of carbons. The dry ether medium is essential because water would destroy the sodium.
  • NEB-style (3m): Explain the preparation of methane by decarboxylation with equation. — Decarboxylation drives CO₂ out of a carboxylate. Sodium acetate is heated with soda-lime (a fused mixture of NaOH and CaO): CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃. The acetate's carboxyl carbon leaves as carbonate (Na₂CO₃, held by CaO), so the hydrocarbon product contains one carbon fewer than the starting acid — acetate (C₂) gives methane (C₁). The reaction is run at about 300 °C and is the standard laboratory preparation of methane.
  • NEB-style (2m): Give one limitation of the Wurtz reaction. — When two different haloalkanes are used together (RX and R′X), the coupling is unselective: three products — R–R, R–R′ and R′–R′ — form in an unpredictable mixture, so unsymmetrical alkanes cannot be made cleanly. The Wurtz reaction is therefore useful mainly for symmetrical alkanes with an even number of carbons (and tertiary halides may eliminate instead of coupling).

Key Points

  • Alkanes CnH2n+2, saturated sp3. Preparation: (1) reduction of haloalkane RX + 2[H] -> RH + HX (Zn/HCl, LiAlH4, H2/Ni). (2) Wurtz: 2RX + 2Na -> R-R + 2NaX, dry ether; symmetrical even-carbon alkanes; 2CH3Br + 2Na -> CH3CH3 + 2NaBr; mixed RX/R'X gives three products (limitation). (3) Decarboxylation: RCOONa + NaOH (CaO, soda-lime, ~300C) -> RH + Na2CO3; one carbon fewer; CH3COONa -> CH4. (4) Catalytic hydrogenation: alkene + H2 (Ni 200C or Pt/Pd rt) -> alkane; alkyne + 2H2 -> alkane; CH2=CH2 + H2 -> CH3CH3; HCCH + 2H2 -> CH3CH3.

Summary

Alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) are made four ways. Reduction swaps halogen for hydrogen: RX + 2[H] → RH (Zn/HCl or LiAlH₄). The Wurtz reaction couples two haloalkane molecules with sodium in dry ether — 2RX + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX — a carbon-doubling machine ideal for symmetrical even-carbon alkanes (2CH₃Br + 2Na → CH₃CH₃ + 2NaBr, the NEB 2024 answer) but useless for unsymmetrical targets, where three products mix. Decarboxylation heats a sodium carboxylate with soda-lime — CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃ — always delivering one carbon fewer than the acid, which makes it the route to methane. Catalytic hydrogenation adds H₂ across π bonds over Ni/Pt/Pd: alkenes take one H₂, alkynes two — the cleanest route when a specific alkane is the goal.
