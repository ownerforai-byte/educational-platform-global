Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Electricity Builds Hydrocarbons

    Kolbe's electrolysis passes current through a carboxylate solution: at the anode, carboxylate ions surrender electrons, lose CO₂, and couple into hydrocarbons. Sodium acetate gives ethane; succinic acid gives ethyne; a suitable salt can even deliver ethene — electrolysis as a C–C bond factory.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Kolbe electrolysis of sodium acetate → alkane

    • Electrolyte: concentrated sodium acetate solution; electrodes: platinum; product at anode.
    • CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃· + CO₂ + e⁻ (anodic oxidation); 2CH₃· → CH₃–CH₃ (ethane).
    • Cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ (hydrogen evolves); solution turns alkaline.
    • Overall: 2CH₃COONa + 2H₂O → C₂H₆ + 2CO₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH.

    2.2 Kolbe route to alkynes (potassium succinate)

    • Potassium succinate — KOOC–CH₂–CH₂–COOK — loses both carboxyls at the anode; the two CH₂· radicals couple with two fewer H: product HC≡CH (ethyne).
    • Overall: KOOC–CH₂–CH₂–COOK + 2KOH → HC≡CH + 2K₂CO₃ + 2H₂ (electrolysis).
    • Geometry does the work: the two radicals are born joined — they combine and shed the extra hydrogens.

    2.3 Kolbe route to alkenes

    • Electrolysing salts that leave radicals with a β-H and the right geometry gives alkenes — e.g. electrolysis of potassium succinate under modified conditions/adipate-type salts yields ethene from the coupled intermediate by further loss of H₂; textbook-level: succinate → ethyne; modified/adjacent-dicarboxylate electrolysis → ethene.
    • Simpler framing for the syllabus: Kolbe of sodium propionate gives butane; the alkene/alkyne variants come from dicarboxylates — succinic (alkyne), and electrolysis of potassium maleate/succinate variants for ethene.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The anode is the workshop

    • Every carbon–carbon bond of the product is forged at the anode: RCOO⁻ surrenders its electron, CO₂ departs, the naked R· radicals meet and couple. The cathode merely makes hydrogen and base.

    4.2 Counting carbons

    • Two acetates (C₂ each) − two carboxyl carbons → C₂ ethane. The rule: product carbons = (carbons of R) × 2 where R is the group remaining after –COO⁻.

    4.3 The succinate surprise

    • Succinate's two carboxylates sit at the two ends of –CH₂–CH₂–; decarboxylating both leaves two joined CH₂· units that shed H₂ to become C≡C — the only syllabus route where a triple bond appears from electrolysis.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    The product appears at the anode: oxidation happens there; students who put ethane at the cathode lose the logic (and the marks).

  • Carboxyl carbons vanish as CO₂: acetate (C₂) gives ethane (C₂) because both carboxyl carbons leave — count R groups, not the whole salt.
  • Cathode chemistry still happens: H₂ evolves and NaOH forms — the full equation includes them.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe the Kolbe electrolysis of sodium acetate with the overall equation. — Concentrated sodium acetate solution is electrolysed with platinum electrodes. At the anode: acetate ions are oxidised — CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃· + CO₂ + e⁻ — and the methyl radicals dimerise: 2CH₃· → CH₃–CH₃ (ethane). At the cathode: water is reduced — 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂↑ + 2OH⁻ — so hydrogen evolves and the solution becomes alkaline (NaOH). Overall: 2CH₃COONa + 2H₂O → C₂H₆ + 2CO₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH. The two carboxyl carbons leave as CO₂; the two methyl groups couple into the alkane.

  • NEB-style (2m): How does the electrolysis of potassium succinate give ethyne? — Potassium succinate, KOOC–CH₂–CH₂–COOK, is electrolysed: both carboxylate groups are oxidised at the anode, each losing CO₂ and an electron, leaving the two central carbons as joined radicals — ·CH₂–CH₂· — which shed two hydrogens to form the triple bond: HC≡CH (ethyne). Overall: KOOC–CH₂–CH₂–COOK + 2KOH(electrolysis) → HC≡CH + 2K₂CO₃ + 2H₂. The dicarboxylate's own skeleton becomes the alkyne.
  • NEB-style (2m): Predict the Kolbe product of sodium propionate. — Sodium propionate, CH₃CH₂COONa: anodic oxidation gives ethyl radicals (C₂H₅·) which couple: 2C₂H₅· → C₄H₁₀ (n-butane). Overall 2CH₃CH₂COONa + 2H₂O → C₄H₁₀ + 2CO₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH. The rule: product carbons = 2 × (carbons in R) — propionate's R has 2 carbons, so the alkane has 4.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Describe the Kolbe electrolysis of sodium acetate with the overall equation. — A concentrated solution of sodium acetate is electrolysed using platinum electrodes. At the anode (oxidation): acetate ions lose an electron and decarboxylate: CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃· + CO₂ + e⁻; the methyl radicals so formed dimerise on the anode surface: 2CH₃· → CH₃–CH₃ — ethane is the anodic product. At the cathode (reduction): water is reduced instead: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂↑ + 2OH⁻ — hydrogen gas evolves and hydroxide accumulates, so the solution around the cathode turns alkaline (NaOH forms). Overall equation: 2CH₃COONa + 2H₂O → C₂H₆ + 2CO₂↑ + H₂↑ + 2NaOH. Note the carbon accounting: the two carboxyl carbons leave as CO₂; the two methyl carbons (one from each acetate) couple into ethane.
  • NEB-style (2m): How does the electrolysis of potassium succinate give ethyne? — Potassium succinate carries two carboxylate groups on a –CH₂–CH₂– skeleton: KOOC–CH₂–CH₂–COOK. During electrolysis both ends are oxidised at the anode, each losing CO₂ and an electron, which leaves the two central carbons as a joined diradical pair, ·CH₂–CH₂·. This pair eliminates two hydrogen atoms to form the triple bond: ·CH₂–CH₂· → HC≡CH (ethyne). Overall: KOOC–CH₂–CH₂–COOK + 2KOH →(electrolysis) HC≡CH + 2K₂CO₃ + 2H₂. The alkyne is built entirely from the succinate's own two central carbons — intramolecular coupling.
  • NEB-style (2m): Predict the Kolbe product of sodium propionate. — Sodium propionate is CH₃CH₂COONa. At the anode each propionate ion decarboxylates: CH₃CH₂COO⁻ → C₂H₅· + CO₂ + e⁻, and two ethyl radicals couple: 2C₂H₅· → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ — n-butane. Overall: 2CH₃CH₂COONa + 2H₂O → C₄H₁₀ + 2CO₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH. The counting rule: the alkane contains twice the carbons of the R group (R = C₂H₅, 2 carbons → 4-carbon product), because the carboxyl carbon is always lost as CO₂.

Summary

Kolbe's electrolysis forges C–C bonds with electricity. In sodium acetate solution (Pt electrodes) the anode oxidises acetate — CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃· + CO₂ + e⁻ — and methyl radicals couple to ethane, while the cathode makes H₂ and NaOH: 2CH₃COONa + 2H₂O → C₂H₆ + 2CO₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH. The carbon rule: carboxyl carbons exit as CO₂, so the product carries 2 × (R carbons) — propionate gives n-butane. Dicarboxylates close the loop: potassium succinate loses both carboxyls and its own –CH₂–CH₂– skeleton sheds hydrogen to become ethyne; modified dicarboxylate electrolyses deliver ethene. One apparatus, three families: alkane from the acetate, alkyne from the succinate, alkene from the engineered middle ground — all welded at the anode.
