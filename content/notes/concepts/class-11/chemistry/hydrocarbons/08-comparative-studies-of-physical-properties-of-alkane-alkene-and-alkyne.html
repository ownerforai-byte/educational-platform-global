Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Three Families, One Table

    Alkanes, alkenes and alkynes differ by π-bond count — and every physical property follows: boiling points, melting points, densities, solubilities rise with molar mass and with unsaturation's extra intermolecular contact, while all three stay non-polar and water-insoluble.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 State and odour

    • C₁–C₄ gases (all three families), C₅–C₁₇ liquids, C₁₈+ waxy solids.
    • Pure alkanes/alkenes/alkynes are odourless; commercial fuels smell from additives/impurities (ethyne's garlic smell is phosphine impurity).

    2.2 Boiling and melting points

    • Rise with carbon number (more surface → stronger van der Waals).
    • At equal carbons: alkyne ≥ alkene > alkane — the π electrons are more polarisable, tightening intermolecular attraction. (B.p.: ethane −89 °C < ethene −104 °C... small-member anomalies exist; the general ladder holds from C₃ up.)
    • Branching lowers b.p. (less contact surface): n-butane −0.5 °C > isobutane −11.7 °C.

    2.3 Density and solubility

    • All less dense than water (float); density rises slowly with carbon number (≈0.6–0.8 g/cm³).
    • Insoluble in water (non-polar vs polar); soluble in non-polar organic solvents (benzene, ether, CCl₄) — like dissolves like.

    2.4 Combustion and stability

    • All burn to CO₂ + H₂O; alkynes burn with a smoky, luminous flame (higher carbon percentage).
    • Stability: more substituted π systems are more stable; alkynes are less reactive toward electrophilic addition than alkenes (vinyl cation instability) despite "two π bonds".

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Reading the periodic trend across the table

    • Within a family, properties change gradually with n — gases to liquids to solids. Across families at fixed n, unsaturation nudges b.p./density up because π clouds are more polarisable than σ frameworks.

    4.2 Why water says no

    • Water's hydrogen-bonded network gains nothing by hosting a non-polar hydrocarbon; dissolving would cost order without repaying energy. Organic solvents offer van der Waals company instead.

    4.3 The smoky flame

    • Alkynes carry the highest carbon-to-hydrogen ratio; combustion is carbon-rich and oxygen-starved at the flame front — free carbon glows (luminous, smoky). A diagnostic flame test in itself.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    The C₂ anomaly: ethene boils below ethane (−104 vs −89 °C) despite more unsaturation — small-molecule packing quirks; the alkyne ≥ alkene > alkane ladder is reliable from C₃ upward.

  • Branching beats unsaturation: a branched alkane can boil lower than a straight alkene of the same formula — surface area matters as much as π.
  • Insoluble ≠ unreactive with water: calcium carbide reacts with water; alkanes/alkenes/alkynes themselves do not dissolve in it.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Compare the boiling points of alkanes, alkenes and alkynes of the same carbon number with reasons. — For the same carbon number (from C₃ upward), alkynes boil highest, alkenes next, alkanes lowest. Boiling depends on van der Waals (London) forces, which strengthen with polarisability: the π-electron clouds of C=C and especially C≡C are more easily distorted than σ frameworks, so alkynes/alkenes attract their neighbours more strongly than alkanes of equal molar mass. Within any family, b.p. rises with chain length (more contact surface) and falls with branching (n-butane −0.5 °C vs isobutane −11.7 °C). All three families boil far below water because only weak dispersion forces must be overcome.

  • NEB-style (2m): Why are hydrocarbons insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents? — Hydrocarbons are non-polar molecules held to each other only by weak dispersion forces. Water is strongly hydrogen-bonded and polar: inserting a non-polar molecule would break water's H-bond network without any compensating attraction — energetically uphill, so insoluble. Organic solvents (benzene, ether, CCl₄) are themselves non-polar: solute–solvent dispersion interactions replace solute–solute ones at no energetic penalty — "like dissolves like".
  • NEB-style (2m): Why does ethyne burn with a smoky flame? — Ethyne has the highest carbon percentage of the three families (C₂H₂ ≈ 92% carbon). In a luminous flame the oxygen supply at the flame front cannot fully oxidise so much carbon, so free carbon particles form and glow incandescently — the smoky, luminous flame used in the oxy-acetylene torch. A more oxygen-rich mixture burns it cleanly with high temperature.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Compare the boiling points of alkanes, alkenes and alkynes of the same carbon number with reasons. — For equal carbon numbers (reliably from C₃ upward) the order is alkyne > alkene > alkane. Boiling requires overcoming only van der Waals (London dispersion) forces, whose strength grows with molecular surface area and polarisability. The π-electron clouds of double and triple bonds are more polarisable than σ frameworks, so unsaturated molecules attract their neighbours more strongly: at the same carbon count the alkyne, with its two dense π clouds, boils highest and the σ-only alkane lowest. Two modifiers: within any family b.p. rises with chain length (C₁–C₄ gases → C₅–C₁₇ liquids → C₁₈+ solids), and branching lowers b.p. by shrinking the contact surface — n-butane (−0.5 °C) vs isobutane (−11.7 °C). Note the small-member anomaly: ethene (−104 °C) boils below ethane (−89 °C); the general ladder is trustworthy only from C₃ up.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why are hydrocarbons insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents? — Hydrocarbons are non-polar: their only intermolecular glue is weak dispersion force. Water is polar and extensively hydrogen-bonded; dissolving a hydrocarbon would force water molecules to break H-bonds to make room, with no compensating attraction offered by the non-polar guest — the process costs energy, so hydrocarbons are insoluble (they float, being less dense than water). Organic solvents such as benzene, ether or CCl₄ are themselves non-polar: solute–solvent dispersion interactions simply replace solute–solute ones, so mixing is energetically neutral or favourable — "like dissolves like". This is why hexane mixes with benzene but forms a separate layer over water.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why does ethyne burn with a smoky flame? — Ethyne's carbon percentage is the highest of the three families (C₂H₂ is ≈92% carbon by mass). In a normal luminous flame the local oxygen supply cannot fully oxidise so much carbon at once, so free carbon particles are generated in the flame; these particles are heated to incandescence and glow — giving the luminous, smoky flame characteristic of alkynes (and used deliberately in the oxy-acetylene torch, where excess oxygen raises the flame to ~3300 °C for cutting steel). Alkanes, being hydrogen-richer, burn with a cleaner, less luminous flame.

  • C1-C4 gases, C5-C17 liquids, C18+ solids; all odourless when pure. B.p./m.p. rise with carbon number; at same n (C3+): alkyne > alkene > alkane (pi polarisability); branching lowers b.p. (n-butane -0.5C > isobutane -11.7C); C2 anomaly: ethene -104C < ethane -89C. All < 1 g/cm3 (float on water), density rises slowly with n. Insoluble in water (non-polar), soluble in organic solvents (like dissolves like). Combustion: all to CO2 + H2O; alkynes smoky luminous flame (highest C%). Summary

One table settles the three families. Formulas CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ / CₙH₂ₙ / CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; states gas (C₁–C₄), liquid (C₅–C₁₇), solid (C₁₈+). Boiling/melting climb with chain length as dispersion forces accumulate; at equal carbon (C₃+) the polarisable π clouds lift the order to alkyne > alkene > alkane, while branching drops it (n-butane −0.5 °C vs isobutane −11.7 °C; C₂ shows the famous anomaly). All three are less dense than water, water-insoluble (non-polar vs hydrogen-bonded water) and organic-solvent-soluble — like dissolves like. In flame, the carbon-rich alkynes smoke and glow; in reactivity, despite two π bonds, alkynes add more slowly than alkenes because the vinyl cation intermediate is unstable. Physical behaviour is contact accounting — surface, polarisability, polarity — and nothing else.
