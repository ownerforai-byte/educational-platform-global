Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Washing Soda and Its Reactions

    Sodium carbonate exists anhydrous (soda ash) and as the decahydrate Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda). It is a mild base — too weak to liberate NH₃ from ammonium salts but strong enough to neutralise acids and precipitate metal carbonates. Its reactions with CO₂, SO₂, water and metal salts are the topic's checklist.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Properties

    • White crystalline decahydrate (washing soda); effloresces on exposure — loses 9 H₂O to become the monohydrate powder. Anhydrous Na₂CO₃ mp 851 °C.
    • Hydrolysis makes its solutions mildly basic: CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ (pH ~11 for 0.1 M).
    • Solubility anomaly: Na₂CO₃ is less soluble in cold water than NaHCO₃'s family trend suggests, and its solubility decreases on heating near the decahydrate's decomposition — the classic exception quoted in solubility questions.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why Na₂CO₃ is basic but NaHCO₃ is weaker

    • The carbonate ion is the conjugate base of a very weak acid (HCO₃⁻, pKa₂ ≈ 10.3), so it grabs a proton from water: CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻. The bicarbonate ion is the conjugate base of a stronger acid (H₂CO₃, pKa₁ ≈ 6.4), so its hydrolysis is weaker — pH ~8.3. Two ions, two pH values — the essence of "mild base".

    4.2 Why washing soda softens hard water

    • Permanent hardness (CaSO₄) is removed by exchange of anions: Na₂CO₃ + CaSO₄ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄ — the calcium leaves the solution as insoluble carbonate, so soap no longer scums. Magnesium behaves the same (MgCO₃↓).

    4.3 Why copper gives a basic carbonate

    • Cu²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ in water precipitates not pure CuCO₃ but a basic carbonate (CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂-ish, green-blue) because the carbonate solution is basic enough to co-precipitate OH⁻. The colour — green-blue — is the exam observation.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Na₂CO₃ + SO₂ gives Na₂SO₃, not sulphate: sulphur dioxide is the +4 anhydride — no oxygen is added; oxidation to sulphate would need O₂/oxidant.

  • Efflorescence is not deliquescence: the decahydrate loses water to air (efflorescent); NaOH gains water (deliquescent). Opposite directions, both about vapour pressure.
  • No ammonia from ammonium salts with carbonate: NaOH does, Na₂CO₃ does not — the standard distinguishing test between the two bases.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe the action of sodium carbonate on (i) CO₂, (ii) SO₂, (iii) calcium chloride solution, with equations. — (i) CO₂: in water, sodium carbonate absorbs carbon dioxide to form the bicarbonate, Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2NaHCO₃ — the reverse of the baking-soda decomposition. (ii) SO₂: the acidic oxide displaces the weaker anhydride: Na₂CO₃ + SO₂ → Na₂SO₃ + CO₂↑ (sodium sulphite, the basis of SO₂ absorption scrubbers). (iii) CaCl₂: double decomposition precipitates calcium carbonate, Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂ → CaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl — a white precipitate; the same reaction removes permanent hardness from water (with CaSO₄: Na₂CO₃ + CaSO₄ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄).

  • NEB-style (2m): Why is an aqueous solution of sodium carbonate alkaline, although the salt contains no OH⁻? — Sodium carbonate is a salt of a strong base and a very weak acid, so its anion hydrolyses: CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻. The equilibrium releases hydroxide ions into solution, making it alkaline (pH ≈ 11 for 0.1 M) — the alkalinity belongs to the hydrolysis of CO₃²⁻, not to any OH⁻ in the formula. Bicarbonate hydrolyses less strongly (HCO₃⁻ is the conjugate base of the stronger acid H₂CO₃), so NaHCO₃ solutions are only mildly alkaline (pH ≈ 8.3).
  • NEB-style (2m): How does washing soda remove permanent hardness of water? — Permanent hardness is due to dissolved calcium (and magnesium) salts such as CaSO₄ that soap cannot precipitate. Washing soda supplies carbonate ions which precipitate them: Na₂CO₃ + CaSO₄ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄ (and MgSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → MgCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄). The calcium and magnesium leave the solution as insoluble carbonates, the sodium salts left behind do not scum with soap, and the water lathers freely.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Describe the action of sodium carbonate on (i) carbon dioxide, (ii) sulphur dioxide, (iii) calcium chloride solution, with equations. — (i) CO₂: in aqueous solution the carbonate absorbs carbon dioxide and water to form the bicarbonate: Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2NaHCO₃ (the reverse of baking soda's decomposition). (ii) SO₂: the more acidic oxide displaces carbon dioxide from its salt: Na₂CO₃ + SO₂ → Na₂SO₃ + CO₂↑ — the product is sodium sulphite (sulphur at +4, no oxidation involved), and this reaction is the basis of SO₂ scrubbing. (iii) CaCl₂: double decomposition gives a white precipitate of calcium carbonate: Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂ → CaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl — the same precipitation by which washing soda removes permanent hardness (Na₂CO₃ + CaSO₄ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄).
  • NEB-style (2m): Explain why an aqueous solution of sodium carbonate is alkaline although it contains no hydroxide ions in its formula. — Sodium carbonate is the salt of a strong base (NaOH) and a very weak acid (carbonic acid), so the carbonate ion hydrolyses water: CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻. Every carbonate ion that grabs a proton from water leaves an OH⁻ behind, and the equilibrium lies well to the right because HCO₃⁻ is an extremely weak acid (pKa ≈ 10.3). The solution therefore becomes alkaline (pH ≈ 11 for 0.1 M) from hydrolysis, not from any OH⁻ written in the formula; bicarbonate, whose anion is the conjugate base of the stronger acid H₂CO₃, hydrolyses less and gives only pH ≈ 8.3.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is efflorescence? Why does washing soda effloresce, and what is the product? — Efflorescence is the loss of water of crystallisation by a hydrated salt to the atmosphere, forming a lower hydrate or the anhydrous salt, when the vapour pressure of the salt's water exceeds that of the ambient air. Washing soda, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, has ten loosely held water molecules; on standing in air it loses nine of them and crumbles to a white powder of the monohydrate, Na₂CO₃·H₂O — which is why old washing-soda crystals are coated with powder and have lost their translucent clarity.

Key Points

  • Na2CO3: anhydrous soda ash mp 851 C; decahydrate Na2CO3.10H2O washing soda, effloresces -> monohydrate. Mild base by hydrolysis CO3^2- + H2O <-> HCO3- + OH- (pH ~11); NaHCO3 weaker (pH ~8.3). Acids: Na2CO3 + 2HCl -> 2NaCl + H2O + CO2 (brisk effervescence). CO2 + H2O -> 2NaHCO3. SO2 -> Na2SO3 + CO2 (sulphite, not sulphate). Precipitation: CaCl2 -> CaCO3 white; CuSO4 -> green-blue basic carbonate; ZnSO4 -> white. Water softening: Na2CO3 + CaSO4 -> CaCO3 + Na2SO4. No NH3 from NH4+ salts (vs NaOH). Solvay manufacture; uses: glass, cleaning, water softening, baking soda, primary standard.

Summary

Sodium carbonate — anhydrous soda ash (mp 851 °C) or efflorescent decahydrate washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·H₂O in air) — is a mild base by hydrolysis: CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ gives pH ≈ 11, while bicarbonate sits at 8.3. It neutralises acids with brisk effervescence (Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂), absorbs CO₂ to bicarbonate and SO₂ to sulphite (Na₂SO₃ + CO₂ — never sulphate), and precipitates Ca²⁺/Ba²⁺ as white carbonate (Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂ → CaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl) — the reaction that softens permanent hardness (CaSO₄ → CaCO₃↓) and gives copper its green-blue basic carbonate. Unlike NaOH it cannot liberate ammonia from ammonium salts — the standard distinguishing test. Manufactured by Solvay, consumed by the glass industry, and kept in every lab as the primary standard for acidimetry.
