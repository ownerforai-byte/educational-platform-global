Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Small Cations Burn Their Carbonates

    Thermal stability of the Group IIA carbonates and nitrates is the Fajans polarisation story: the smaller the M²⁺ ion, the harder it distorts the big anion's electron cloud, weakening a C–O or N–O bond until CO₂ or NO₂+O₂ escapes. The result is a clean ladder — BeCO₃ decomposes on a warm bench, BaCO₃ needs a blast furnace — and a clean set of facts NEB asks every year.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The decomposition reactions

    • Carbonates: MCO₃ → MO + CO₂↑ (all except BeCO₃ need heating; BeCO₃ decomposes below 100 °C, MgCO₃ ~350 °C, CaCO₃ ~900 °C, SrCO₃ ~1290 °C, BaCO₃ ~1360 °C).
    • Nitrates: all decompose on heating, but in two stages of product:
    • Be and Mg: 2M(NO₃)₂ → 2MO + 4NO₂↑ + O₂↑ (nitrate → oxide).
    • Ca, Sr, Ba: M(NO₃)₂ → M(NO₂)₂ (nitrite) + O₂↑ (nitrate → nitrite + oxygen).
    • The nitrate split is the exam's favourite contrast.

    2.2 The polarisation (Fajans) explanation

    • CO₃²⁻ (and NO₃⁻) is a large, polarisable anion. A small, doubly-charged M²⁺ (Be²⁺, Mg²⁺) has high charge density and polarises the anion: it drags electron density toward one O, weakening that C–O bond, so the anion breaks into oxide + CO₂ with little heat. A large M²⁺ (Ba²⁺) polarises weakly — the anion stays intact until very high temperature.
    • The same polarisation explains BeO/Be(OH)₂ amphoterism and Be's covalency — one idea, many consequences.

    2.3 Hydrates and stability in context

    • MgCO₃ and CaCO₃ occur as magnesite and limestone/chalk/marble; the thermal lability of MgCO₃ is why magnesite calcines easily to magnesia, while limestone needs a proper kiln (~1000 °C) for quick lime.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why the carbonate ladder runs Be → Ba

    • Decomposition temperature climbs steadily: BeCO₃ <100, MgCO₃ 350, CaCO₃ 900, SrCO₃ 1290, BaCO₃ 1360 °C. The cation's polarising power falls as its radius grows (charge constant), so the anion distortion weakens and the C–O bond survives longer. The intermediate "basic carbonate" stage (MO·CO₂ character) shrinks as stability grows.

    4.2 Why nitrates split into two product families

    • For Be and Mg, even the nitrite stage is too polarised to survive: heating drives the decomposition all the way to the oxide + NO₂ + O₂. For Ca, Sr, Ba, the nitrate anion survives the first step and only loses oxygen: nitrite + O₂. The dividing line sits between Mg and Ca — exactly where polarising power drops below the threshold that destroys NO₂⁻.

    4.3 Connecting to the alkali metals

    • Group IA nitrates (except Li) also give nitrites + O₂ on heating — NaNO₃ → NaNO₂ + ½O₂; LiNO₃, like Mg(NO₃)₂, gives the oxide + NO₂ + O₂ (the diagonal Li–Mg similarity). Carbonates of IA (except Li₂CO₃) never decompose on heating — the +1 cation is too weak a polariser; only Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    **"More stable down the group" applies to carbonates AND to the nitrate decomposition temperature, but the nitrate products change family:** Be/Mg give oxide, Ca/Sr/Ba give nitrite — stability and product identity are different statements.

  • NO₂ is brown, O₂ is not: the decomposition of Mg(NO₃)₂ gives brown fumes (NO₂) plus a colourless gas that relights a glowing splint (O₂) — quote both observations.
  • BaCO₃ does not decompose in a Bunsen flame: ~1360 °C is beyond ordinary lab heat; writing "carbonates decompose on heating" without the temperature ladder loses the discrimination mark.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe the trend in the thermal stability of the carbonates of the alkaline earth metals and explain it using the polarisation concept. — The carbonates become more thermally stable down the group: BeCO₃ decomposes below 100 °C, MgCO₃ at about 350 °C, CaCO₃ at about 900 °C (lime kiln), SrCO₃ at about 1290 °C, and BaCO₃ only at about 1360 °C — all giving MO + CO₂. Explanation (Fajans polarisation): the M²⁺ cation distorts the electron cloud of the large CO₃²⁻ anion; the distortion pulls electron density onto one oxygen and weakens its C–O bond, so the anion splits into oxide + CO₂. Polarising power = charge/size; down the group the charge stays +2 while the radius grows, so polarising power falls from Be²⁺ (extreme) to Ba²⁺ (weak). The smaller the cation, the more the carbonate is destabilised and the lower its decomposition temperature — hence the ladder Be → Ba.

  • NEB-style (3m): What happens when the nitrates of beryllium, magnesium and barium are heated? Write equations and explain the difference. — Beryllium and magnesium nitrates decompose all the way to the oxide, with brown nitrogen dioxide and oxygen: 2Be(NO₃)₂ → 2BeO + 4NO₂↑ + O₂↑ and 2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂↑ + O₂↑. Barium nitrate decomposes only to the nitrite with oxygen: Ba(NO₃)₂ → Ba(NO₂)₂ + O₂↑ (similarly Ca and Sr). Explanation: Be²⁺ and Mg²⁺ are small, doubly charged ions with high polarising power; they distort NO₃⁻ so strongly that even the nitrite product cannot survive — the decomposition proceeds to the oxide. The larger Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺ polarise weakly, the anion framework survives the first stage, and only oxygen is lost. The same split appears in Group IA: LiNO₃ gives the oxide, NaNO₃ and KNO₃ give nitrite + O₂.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is Li₂CO₃, alone among the alkali metal carbonates, decomposed on heating while Na₂CO₃ and K₂CO₃ are not? — Li⁺ is the smallest alkali cation and, although singly charged, its high charge density polarises CO₃²⁻ enough to weaken a C–O bond, so lithium carbonate decomposes on heating: Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂↑. Na⁺ and K⁺ are much larger with far lower polarising power — the carbonate anion remains intact and the salts are stable to strong heating (Na₂CO₃ melts at 851 °C without decomposing). This is one face of the Li–Mg diagonal similarity: Li₂CO₃ behaves like MgCO₃, not like Na₂CO₃.

Examples

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Describe the trend in thermal stability of the carbonates of the alkaline earth metals and explain it with the polarisation concept. — The carbonates grow steadily more stable down the group: BeCO₃ decomposes below 100 °C (it is stable only under a CO₂ atmosphere), MgCO₃ at about 350 °C, CaCO₃ at about 900 °C, SrCO₃ at about 1290 °C, and BaCO₃ only at about 1360 °C — each giving the oxide and carbon dioxide, MCO₃ → MO + CO₂↑. Explanation: the carbonate ion is large and easily polarised. The doubly charged M²⁺ cation attracts and distorts the anion's electron cloud; the distortion concentrates electron density toward one oxygen and weakens that C–O bond, so the anion breaks into oxide plus CO₂. Polarising power is proportional to charge/size — down the group the charge stays +2 while the radius grows from Be²⁺ to Ba²⁺, so the distortion, and hence the destabilisation, falls continuously: Be²⁺ wrecks its carbonate on a warm bench, Ba²⁺ needs a blast furnace.
  • NEB-style (3m): What products are obtained on heating the nitrates of beryllium, magnesium and barium? Write the equations and explain the difference in behaviour. — Beryllium nitrate and magnesium nitrate decompose completely to the oxide, evolving brown nitrogen dioxide and oxygen: 2Be(NO₃)₂ → 2BeO + 4NO₂↑ + O₂↑; 2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂↑ + O₂↑. Barium nitrate (like calcium and strontium nitrates) decomposes only to the nitrite, losing oxygen: Ba(NO₃)₂ → Ba(NO₂)₂ + O₂↑. Explanation: Be²⁺ and Mg²⁺ are small, doubly charged cations of high polarising power; they distort the nitrate ion so strongly that even the intermediate nitrite (NO₂⁻) cannot survive the heating — the decomposition goes all the way to the oxide. The larger Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺ and Ba²⁺ polarise weakly, the anion framework survives the first step, and only the extra oxygen is expelled. The same dividing behaviour appears in Group IA: LiNO₃ gives the oxide (like Mg), while NaNO₃ and KNO₃ give the nitrite.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why does lithium carbonate, unlike the other alkali metal carbonates, decompose on heating? — Li⁺ is the smallest alkali cation, so despite its single charge it has an unusually high charge density and polarises the carbonate ion appreciably; the distortion weakens a C–O bond and the salt decomposes on heating: Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂↑. The larger Na⁺ and K⁺ polarise CO₃²⁻ far too weakly to break it, so sodium and potassium carbonates are thermally stable (Na₂CO₃ melts at 851 °C without decomposition). This is the lithium–magnesium diagonal similarity at work: in thermal behaviour Li₂CO₃ resembles MgCO₃ (which decomposes at ~350 °C), not Na₂CO₃.

Key Points

  • Carbonates MCO3 -> MO + CO2; stability increases down: BeCO3 <100 C, MgCO3 ~350 C, CaCO3 ~900 C, SrCO3 ~1290 C, BaCO3 ~1360 C. Explanation: Fajans polarisation - small M2+ (high charge density) distorts CO3^2-, weakens C-O, anion splits; polarising power = charge/size falls down the group. Nitrates split: Be/Mg -> MO + NO2 + O2 (2Mg(NO3)2 -> 2MgO + 4NO2 + O2); Ca/Sr/Ba -> nitrite + O2 (Ba(NO3)2 -> Ba(NO2)2 + O2); divide at Mg/Ca by polarising threshold destroying NO2-. Parallel: LiNO3 -> Li2O + NO2 + O2 but NaNO3/KNO3 -> nitrite + O2; Li2CO3 decomposes, Na2CO3/K2CO3 stable (Li-Mg diagonal similarity).

Summary

Thermal stability in Group IIA is the polarisation ladder. The M²⁺ cation bends the big CO₃²⁻/NO₃⁻ electron cloud; polarising power = charge/size, so it falls steadily from Be²⁺ to Ba²⁺. Carbonates respond with a clean decomposition-temperature ladder — BeCO₃ <100 °C, MgCO₃ 350 °C, CaCO₃ 900 °C, SrCO₃ 1290 °C, BaCO₃ 1360 °C, all yielding MO + CO₂. Nitrates split at the Mg/Ca boundary: the small, fiercely polarising Be²⁺ and Mg²⁺ drive the decomposition past nitrite to the oxide with brown NO₂ and O₂ (2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂), while the placid Ca²⁺/Sr²⁺/Ba²⁺ stop at the nitrite, releasing only O₂ (Ba(NO₃)₂ → Ba(NO₂)₂ + O₂). The same dial explains the alkali side: only Li₂CO₃ decomposes (Li–Mg diagonal similarity) and only LiNO₃ gives the oxide, while Na and K salts hold their anions intact.

  • "The thermal stability of the carbonates of the alkaline earth metals increases with increasing atomic number, because the polarising power of the doubly charged cation decreases as its size increases."
