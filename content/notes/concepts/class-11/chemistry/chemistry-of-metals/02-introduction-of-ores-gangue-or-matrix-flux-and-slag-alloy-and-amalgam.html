  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Vocabulary of Extraction

    Before any metal flows, the ore must be described: what mineral it is, what worthless rock travels with it (gangue), what is added to make the impurities flow (flux), what the flux turns them into (slag), and what metals are blended into alloys. These five words — ore, gangue, flux, slag, alloy — are the working vocabulary of every extraction plant, and NEB asks them as short questions almost every year.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Ore, mineral, gangue

    • A mineral is any natural compound of a metal; a mineral becomes an ore when the metal can be extracted profitably.
    • Gangue (matrix) is the worthless sandy/rocky material mixed with the ore — silica, clay, limestone. Concentration removes most of it; the flux deals with the rest.

    2.2 Flux and slag

    • A flux is a substance added to the charge to combine with the gangue and form a fusible slag.
    • Acidic flux removes basic gangue: silica (SiO₂) for iron oxides/lime. Basic flux removes acidic gangue: limestone (CaCO₃) for silica in the iron blast furnace — CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, then CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag).
    • A good slag: low melting, immiscible with the metal, lighter — it floats and is drawn off, protecting the metal from oxidation.

    2.3 Alloys and amalgam

    • An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of a metal with other metals (or non-metals) made to improve hardness, resistance to corrosion, or casting quality: brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni + C).
    • An amalgam is an alloy in which one component is mercury: sodium amalgam (a safe handling form of reactive sodium, used as a reducing agent), dental amalgam (Ag–Sn–Hg). Iron does not form an amalgam — which is why iron vessels can hold mercury.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 How flux chooses its gangue

    • Chemistry of pairing: an acidic oxide neutralises a basic one. Iron ore carries silica (acidic) → basic flux CaO (from limestone) snares it into calcium silicate slag. If the gangue were basic (lime), the flux would be acidic sand. Picking the wrong flux thickens the slag, slows the furnace and loses metal into it.

    4.2 Why alloys beat pure metals

    • Pure gold is too soft for rings — copper (and silver) harden it. Pure iron is soft and rusts — chromium and nickel make stainless steel. Bronze (Cu–Sn ~88/12) is harder than either component and cast the ancient world's tools. Alloying lowers grain-boundary slip and can passivate the surface (Cr₂O₃ film on stainless).

    4.3 Sodium amalgam in practice

    • Sodium metal reacts violently with water; sodium amalgam reacts calmly — the mercury dilutes the reactivity — so it is used as a portable reducing agent and was the cathode product of older chlor-alkali cells, water later decomposing it to NaOH + H₂ + Hg (mercury returned).

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Flux is not fuel: the flux does not burn or reduce anything — it only chemically binds the gangue into a fusible slag. The coke both heats and reduces; the limestone only cleans.

  • Slag is not dross: dross is oxide scum floating on a melt; slag is the deliberate flux+gangue product with a controlled composition (CaSiO₃).
  • Amalgam ≠ alloy with any liquid: an amalgam is specifically with mercury; brass in mercury is not an amalgam.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ NEB 2024 (2m): Write the composition of brass, bronze, and stainless steel, with one use of each. — Brass: copper + zinc (roughly 60–90 % Cu, the rest Zn); use — musical instruments and fittings (machinable, gold-like lustre). Bronze: copper + tin (about 88 % Cu, 12 % Sn); use — statues, bearings and bells (hard, corrosion-resistant). Stainless steel: iron + at least ~10.5 % chromium (often with nickel and some carbon); use — cutlery and chemical-plant equipment, because the chromium forms a passive Cr₂O₃ film that stops rust.

Universal Facts

  • Slag is not waste: ground blast-furnace slag is a cement ingredient, and slag wool is insulation — the by-product outsells some metals by tonnage.

Examples

  • The blast furnace's limestone — every tonne of iron needs ~0.25 t of limestone: not for the iron, but to carry the sand away as CaSiO₃ slag; without it the furnace chokes on pasty silica.

Practice Questions

  • Verified PYQ NEB 2024 (2m): Write the composition of brass, bronze, and stainless steel, with one use of each. — Brass is copper alloyed with zinc (about 60–90 % Cu, remainder Zn); it machines well and has a gold-like appearance, so it is used for musical instruments, fittings and cartridge cases. Bronze is copper with tin (about 88 % Cu, 12 % Sn); it is hard and resists corrosion, so it is used for statues, bells and bearings. Stainless steel is iron with at least about 10.5 % chromium (commonly also nickel and ~0.1 % carbon); the chromium forms a self-repairing passive Cr₂O₃ film, so it is used for cutlery, sinks and chemical-plant equipment.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is a flux? Give one acidic and one basic flux with the gangue each removes. — A flux is a substance added to the concentrated ore during smelting to combine with the gangue and form a low-melting, immiscible slag that floats on the metal and can be drawn off. Acidic flux: silica (SiO₂) — it removes basic gangue such as lime or iron(II) oxide, forming silicate slag. Basic flux: limestone (CaCO₃, which decomposes to CaO) — it removes acidic gangue such as silica in the iron blast furnace: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ and CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag).
  • NEB-style (2m): What is an amalgam? Why is sodium kept as amalgam, and why can mercury be stored in an iron vessel? — An amalgam is an alloy in which one of the metals is mercury (sodium amalgam, dental Ag–Sn–Hg amalgam). Sodium is kept as sodium amalgam because dispersion in mercury moderates its violent reactivity: the amalgam can be handled and even decomposed with water in a controlled way (Na–Hg + H₂O → NaOH + Hg + ½H₂), the mercury being recovered. Iron does not form an amalgam — mercury neither dissolves nor wets iron — so mercury is stored and shipped in iron or steel flasks.

Formulas

    • CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (basic flux from limestone)
    • CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag)
    • brass = Cu + Zn; bronze = Cu + Sn; stainless steel = Fe + Cr + Ni + C
    • Na–Hg + H₂O → NaOH + Hg + ½H₂ (amalgam decomposed by water)

Key Points

  • Ore = mineral extracted profitably (bauxite yes, clay no). Gangue/matrix = worthless rock (silica, clay). Flux binds gangue into slag: acidic flux SiO2 removes basic gangue; basic flux CaCO3 -> CaO removes acidic gangue, iron furnace: CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2, CaO + SiO2 -> CaSiO3 slag. Slag: fusible, immiscible, lighter, floats, protects metal. Alloy = homogeneous metal mixture: brass Cu+Zn (instruments), bronze Cu+Sn (statues/bells), stainless steel Fe+Cr(>=10.5%)+Ni+C (passive Cr2O3 film, cutlery), solder Pb+Sn. Amalgam = alloy with mercury: Na-Hg (moderated reducer), dental Ag-Sn-Hg; iron forms no amalgam -> mercury stored in iron flasks.

Summary

The extraction vocabulary in one pass: an ore is a mineral worth mining (bauxite, not clay); gangue is the worthless rock riding with it; a flux is added to bind that gangue — acidic silica for basic impurities, basic limestone (→ CaO) for acidic silica — into a fusible, floating slag (CaSiO₃ in the iron furnace). Alloys blend metals to beat pure-metal weaknesses: brass (Cu–Zn) for fittings, bronze (Cu–Sn) for statues and bells, stainless steel (Fe–Cr–Ni) whose passive Cr₂O₃ film stops rust. An amalgam is the mercury alloy — sodium amalgam tames sodium's reactivity, dental amalgam fills teeth, and iron alone refuses to form one, so iron flasks hold mercury.
