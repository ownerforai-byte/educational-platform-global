Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Base of the Chemical World

    Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) is produced on a scale matched only by sulphuric acid. Its two faces — a strong base that neutralises and precipitates, and a strong alkali that absorbs acidic gases like CO₂ and SO₂ — plus its manufacture route make this topic a guaranteed short-question source.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Properties

    • White deliquescent solid, mp 318 °C; extremely soluble in water with strong heat evolution; fully dissociated: NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻ — a strong monobasic base.
    • Soapy touch, corrosive to skin (saponifies fats) — hence "caustic".
    • Absorbs CO₂ and moisture from air (must be stoppered): 2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O.

    2.2 Precipitation reactions (the exam list)

    CationObservationEquation
    Cu²⁺pale blue ppt, insoluble in excessCuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄
    Fe²⁺dirty green pptFeSO₄ + 2NaOH → Fe(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄
    Fe³⁺reddish-brown pptFeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaCl
    Zn²⁺, Al³⁺, Pb²⁺white ppt, soluble in excess (amphoteric)ZnSO₄ + 2NaOH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄; Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O
    NH₄⁺no ppt — pungent NH₃ on warmingNH₄Cl + NaOH → NaCl + NH₃↑ + H₂O

    2.3 Action with CO₂ and SO₂ (acidic oxides)

    • Limited CO₂: 2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O; excess CO₂: Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2NaHCO₃.
    • SO₂: 2NaOH + SO₂ → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O (then NaHSO₃ with excess) — the chemistry of scrubbing acidic gases, and the reason NaOH pellets must be kept sealed.

    2.4 Action with carbon monoxide (the topic's namesake)

    • Under pressure (~6–8 atm, ~200 °C) sodium hydroxide absorbs CO to give sodium formate (sodium methanoate): CO + NaOH → HCOONa — the historical basis of formic acid manufacture. No reaction at ordinary pressure/temperature; the pressure forces the weakly acidic CO into addition.

    2.5 Manufacture and uses

    • Chlor-alkali electrolysis of brine: cathode 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻; anode 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂; products NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂ (diaphragm or membrane cell keeps Cl₂ away from the OH⁻).
    • Uses: soap and detergents (saponification), paper pulping, rayon, petroleum refining, bauxite purification (Bayer), laboratory reagent, drain cleaner.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why Zn(OH)₂ dissolves in excess NaOH but Cu(OH)₂ does not

    • Zinc and aluminium hydroxides are amphoteric: their oxides/hydroxides react with strong alkali to form zincate/aluminate, Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O. Copper(II) hydroxide is purely basic — no acid character — so excess NaOH leaves the pale blue precipitate untouched. This single contrast is the standard qualitative-analysis question.

    4.2 Why NaOH grabs CO₂ from the air

    • OH⁻ is a stronger base than CO₃²⁻'s conjugate position requires: the acid anhydride CO₂ + OH⁻ → HCO₃⁻ → CO₃²⁻ + H₂O is fast and irreversible on drying. A pellet left on the bench becomes a crust of soda ash overnight — the reason "standard" NaOH solutions are standardised against a primary standard, not weighed as pellets.

    4.3 Saponification — the oldest use

    • Fat (glyceryl ester) + 3NaOH → 3RCOONa (soap) + glycerol. The OH⁻ attacks the ester bonds; the sodium salts of long-chain acids are the soap molecules. This is why NaOH is "caustic" to skin — skin fat is being saponified.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Soluble in excess" means the hydroxide is amphoteric: Zn(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, Pb(OH)₂ dissolve in excess NaOH; Cu(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₂/₃ do not — memorise which side of the line each cation sits on.

  • NaOH does not precipitate NH₃: ammonium salts give no precipitate with NaOH — only ammonia gas on warming; writing "NH₄OH ppt" is wrong.
  • Caustic soda vs washing soda: NaOH is caustic soda; Na₂CO₃·10H₂O is washing soda — different compounds, different strengths.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe the precipitation reactions of sodium hydroxide with solutions of copper sulphate, ferric chloride and zinc sulphate. — Copper sulphate: NaOH gives a pale blue precipitate of copper(II) hydroxide, CuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄; the precipitate is insoluble in excess NaOH (Cu(OH)₂ is not amphoteric). Ferric chloride: a reddish-brown precipitate of ferric hydroxide, FeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaCl, also insoluble in excess. Zinc sulphate: a white precipitate of zinc hydroxide, ZnSO₄ + 2NaOH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄, which dissolves in excess NaOH because zinc hydroxide is amphoteric: Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ (sodium zincate) + 2H₂O.

  • NEB-style (2m): What happens when excess CO₂ is passed into sodium hydroxide solution? Write both stages. — Stage 1 (limited CO₂): the hydroxide takes up the acid anhydride to form the carbonate, 2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O. Stage 2 (excess CO₂): the carbonate is converted to the soluble bicarbonate, Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2NaHCO₃. The same chemistry explains why NaOH pellets left open to air gain mass and turn into a crust of sodium carbonate.
  • NEB-style (2m): How is sodium hydroxide manufactured? Give the electrode reactions of the brine electrolysis. — Sodium hydroxide is made by the chlor-alkali electrolysis of brine (concentrated NaCl solution) in a diaphragm or membrane cell that keeps the chlorine away from the hydroxide. Cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂↑ + 2OH⁻ (Na⁺ stays spectator; NaOH accumulates in the cathode compartment). Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂↑ + 2e⁻. Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ + Cl₂ — three products at once.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Describe the action of sodium hydroxide on solutions of copper sulphate, ferric chloride and zinc sulphate, noting the behaviour in excess NaOH. — Copper sulphate: addition of NaOH gives a pale blue precipitate of copper(II) hydroxide, CuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄; the precipitate does not dissolve in excess NaOH, since Cu(OH)₂ is purely basic. Ferric chloride: a reddish-brown precipitate of ferric hydroxide forms, FeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaCl; again insoluble in excess. Zinc sulphate: a white gelatinous precipitate of zinc hydroxide, ZnSO₄ + 2NaOH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄, which dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide because zinc hydroxide is amphoteric, forming sodium zincate: Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is observed when carbon dioxide is passed through sodium hydroxide solution (i) in limited amount, (ii) in excess? — (i) Limited CO₂: the hydroxide is converted to the carbonate — 2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O — no visible change beyond possible slight cloudiness. (ii) Excess CO₂: the carbonate takes up more CO₂ and water to form the soluble bicarbonate — Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2NaHCO₃. The reverse of stage (i) explains why an open bottle of NaOH solution slowly loses its strength: the OH⁻ has been converted to CO₃²⁻ by atmospheric CO₂.
  • NEB-style (2m): What happens when carbon monoxide is heated with sodium hydroxide under pressure? — Sodium hydroxide absorbs the weakly acidic CO under pressure (~6–8 atm, ~200 °C) to form sodium formate (sodium methanoate): CO + NaOH → HCOONa. At ordinary pressure and temperature there is no reaction — the pressure is what drives the addition; acidification of the formate regenerates formic acid, which was the historical route to it.
  • NEB-style (2m): How is sodium hydroxide manufactured by the electrolysis of brine? Give the electrode reactions and the overall equation. — Concentrated brine is electrolysed in a cell with a diaphragm/membrane that prevents the chlorine liberated at the anode from mixing with the hydroxide formed at the cathode. Cathode (steel): 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂↑ + 2OH⁻ — hydrogen gas evolves and OH⁻ accumulates with the spectator Na⁺, giving NaOH solution. Anode (graphite/Ti): 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂↑ + 2e⁻. Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑ + Cl₂↑ — one process, three saleable products.

Key Points

  • NaOH: white deliquescent solid, mp 318 C, very soluble (exothermic), strong monobasic base, soapy touch, absorbs CO2+H2O from air -> Na2CO3 crust. Precipitations: Cu2+ pale blue Cu(OH)2 (insoluble in excess), Fe2+ dirty green, Fe3+ reddish-brown Fe(OH)3, Zn2+/Al3+/Pb2+ white ppt soluble in excess (amphoteric -> Na2ZnO2/NaAlO2), NH4+ no ppt but NH3 on warming. Acidic oxides: 2NaOH + CO2 -> Na2CO3 + H2O, excess CO2 -> NaHCO3; 2NaOH + SO2 -> Na2SO3 + H2O. Manufacture: chlor-alkali brine electrolysis, cathode 2H2O + 2e -> H2 + 2OH-, anode 2Cl- -> Cl2; overall 2NaCl + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2 + Cl2. Uses: soap/saponification, paper, rayon, Bayer alumina, drain cleaner.

Summary

Sodium hydroxide — white, deliquescent, mp 318 °C, strongly exothermic in water — is the strongest cheap base in the lab: NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻ completely. It precipitates metal hydroxides with exam-signature colours: pale blue Cu(OH)₂ (insoluble in excess), dirty green Fe(OH)₂, reddish-brown Fe(OH)₃, and white Zn(OH)₂/Al(OH)₃ that dissolve in excess as zincate/aluminate because they are amphoteric; ammonium salts yield no precipitate, only NH₃ on warming. As a base it drinks acidic oxides: 2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ (then NaHCO₃ with excess CO₂) and 2NaOH + SO₂ → Na₂SO₃ — the chemistry of scrubbing and of the crust on an unstoppered pellet. Industrially it comes from the chlor-alkali electrolysis of brine (2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ + Cl₂) and is consumed saponifying fats into soap, pulping wood, and digesting bauxite.
