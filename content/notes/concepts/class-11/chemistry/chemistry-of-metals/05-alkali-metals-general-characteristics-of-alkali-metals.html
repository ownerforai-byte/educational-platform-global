Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Softest Family

    The alkali metals — Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr — are Group IA: one valence electron, desperate to lose it. Everything characteristic follows from that single fact: extreme reactivity, low densities, low melting points, flame colours, ionic chemistry only. This topic collects the group trends NEB asks every year.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Electronic structure and the losing electron

    • General configuration [NG]ns¹. The single s-electron is shielded by the noble-gas core and easily lost → +1 oxidation state, no other common state (unlike neighbouring groups).
    • Ionisation energy decreases down the group (Li 520 → Cs 376 kJ/mol): the valence shell grows farther from the nucleus with more shielding — so reactivity increases Li → Cs.

    2.2 Physical trends

    PropertyTrend down the groupReason
    Atomic/ionic radiusincreasesnew shell each period
    Densityincreases (but Na, K < 1 g/mL)mass grows faster than volume
    Melting pointdecreasesmetallic bond weakens as radius grows
    Ionisation energydecreasesshielding + distance
    Electropositivity/reactivityincreaseseasier electron loss
    Flame colourLi crimson, Na golden-yellow, K lilac, Rb red-violet, Cs blueemission lines

    2.3 Chemical behaviour

    • Soft, low-melting metals — cut with a knife; mp Li 181 °C → Cs 28 °C (Cs melts in the hand, nearly).
    • Vigorous reaction with water: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂↑; Li fizzes, Na skates and burns, K ignites instantly, Rb/Cs explode — the hydroxides are the strongest bases known (KOH, NaOH).
    • Reaction with oxygen: Li → normal oxide Li₂O; Na → peroxide Na₂O₂; K → superoxide KO₂. (The famous exception to "form normal oxides".)
    • With hydrogen: 2M + H₂ → 2MH — ionic hydrides with H⁻.
    • All compounds are ionic, colourless (unless anion coloured), water-soluble; ions are hydrated heavily — Li⁺ most hydrated (smallest), which makes Li salts often the least soluble despite the smallest lattice.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why sodium glows yellow in every flame test

    • The 3s electron, excited in the flame, falls back through the intense sodium D-lines (589.0/589.6 nm) — a transition with enormous oscillator strength. A pinprick of Na salt colours a whole Bunsen flame, which is why the sodium test must be looked through cobalt glass when testing potassium (the yellow masks the lilac).

    4.2 Why Li is the odd one out

    • Li⁺ is tiny and heavily hydrated; its salts (Li₂CO₃, LiF, Li₃PO₄) are less soluble than the Na/K analogues (opposite of the group trend). Li burns to the normal oxide Li₂O (not peroxide), forms the nitride Li₃N directly in air (no other alkali metal does), and its compounds show covalent character (Fajans: high charge density polarises anions).

    4.3 Why K is more reactive than Na despite similar size

    • Each step down adds a shell: the electron is farther out and better shielded, so the ionisation energy drops by ~70–80 kJ/mol per step. The water reaction's activation energy is paid by easier electron transfer — hence the escalation fizz → fire → explosion.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Low density" does not mean light across the group: Li, Na, K float on water (ρ < 1); Rb and Cs sink (1.53, 1.93). The trend is "increases", with the famous dip at Na/K.

  • Flame colour vs radioactivity: the colours are emission lines of the neutral atoms, not heat colour; the test identifies the element, not the temperature.
  • Li's exceptions are not errors: Li₂O (normal oxide), Li₃N (direct nitride), low solubility of Li salts — all follow from the tiny, heavily hydrated Li⁺ and its polarising power.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe the general characteristics of alkali metals: electronic configuration, trends in ionisation energy and reactivity, and their reaction with water. — Alkali metals (Group IA: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) have the general outer configuration ns¹ — one valence electron over a noble-gas core. The ionisation energy decreases down the group (Li 520 → Na 496 → K 419 → Cs 376 kJ/mol) because each element adds a shell, so the valence electron is farther from the nucleus and better shielded. Consequently reactivity increases down the group: with water, Li reacts steadily with effervescence, sodium melts and races about burning with a yellow flame, potassium ignites instantly with a lilac flame, and rubidium and caesium explode — all according to 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂, forming the strongest known bases, the hydroxides MOH.

  • NEB-style (2m): Give the flame colours of Li, Na, K and Cs, and explain the origin of the colour. — Lithium: crimson; sodium: golden-yellow; potassium: lilac; caesium: blue. In the flame the s-electron of the vapourised metal atoms is excited to higher levels and, falling back, emits photons of characteristic wavelength — for sodium the intense 589 nm D-lines, which is why even traces of sodium colour the flame golden-yellow and why the potassium (lilac) test is viewed through cobalt glass to filter the sodium yellow.
  • NEB-style (2m): What oxide does each of Li, Na and K form on burning in air, and what ion is present in the potassium product? — Lithium burns to the normal oxide Li₂O (4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O), sodium to the peroxide Na₂O₂ (2Na + O₂ → Na₂O₂), and potassium to the superoxide KO₂ (K + O₂ → KO₂). The potassium product contains the superoxide ion O₂⁻, with one unpaired electron — paramagnetic — and it is the basis of oxygen masks (KO₂ releases O₂ on absorbing CO₂/H₂O).

Universal Facts

  • Caesium's ionisation energy (376 kJ/mol) is the lowest of any stable element — in photoelectric cells caesium surfaces eject electrons under visible light, which is exactly how "photocells" got their name.

Examples

  • Potassium through cobalt glass — the lilac of potassium is invisible whenever sodium is nearby, because the 589 nm sodium D-lines overwhelm everything; cobalt glass absorbs the yellow and lets the lilac through — the flame test teaching both emission and absorption.

The anion in KO₂ is the superoxide ion O₂⁻ — a paramagnetic species with one unpaired electron, which is why KO₂ is used in breathing apparatus: it releases oxygen while absorbing CO₂ and moisture from exhaled air.

Summary

The alkali metals — Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr — carry a single ns¹ electron that defines everything: it is easily lost (+1 only), so the metals are soft, low-melting (181 °C → 28 °C down the group), light (Li 0.53 g/mL; Na and K float), and violently reactive. Ionisation energy falls from 520 to 376 kJ/mol as shells are added, so reactivity climbs — water gives 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂ from a fizz (Li) to an explosion (Cs), and the MOH products are the strongest bases known. Burning in air steps through the oxide ladder Li₂O → Na₂O₂ → KO₂ (the superoxide O₂⁻ anion), flames step crimson → golden-yellow (Na 589 nm) → lilac → blue, and lithium plays its exceptions — normal oxide, direct Li₃N, poorly soluble salts — because the tiny, heavily hydrated Li⁺ polarises anions and hogs the water.
