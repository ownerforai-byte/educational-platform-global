Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Designing and Running a Chemical Plant

    A chemical plant is a network of unit operations — reactors, absorbers, distillation columns, heat exchangers, filters, pumps — arranged by a process flowsheet. Designing one means choosing the operations, sizing them by mass and energy balances, selecting materials, and engineering safety in; running one means keeping that network at its designed operating point, safely, for years.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Design sequence: (1) process flowsheet — the reaction route drawn as connected unit operations; (2) mass balance — atoms in = atoms out for every unit; (3) energy balance — heat duties for every exchanger, reactor, column; (4) equipment selection and sizing; (5) materials of construction (corrosion, temperature, pressure); (6) safety engineering (relief, containment, control interlocks); (7) plant layout and siting.
    • Unit operations: physical steps (distillation, filtration, absorption, drying, crystallisation, heat exchange) — distinguished from unit processes (chemical transformations: nitration, sulphonation, hydrogenation).
    • Running the plant: steady-state monitoring (flow, T, P, composition), quality control, scheduled maintenance, safety drills, log-keeping, and continuous optimisation.
    • Siting factors: raw material and energy supply, water, transport links, labour, waste-disposal capacity, distance from population, climate.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — The Plant as a Balanced Network, Not a Machine

    • The flowsheet's discipline is the balance: every unit operation obeys "in = out" for mass and energy, and the design arithmetic is these balances applied unit by unit. A plant is therefore verifiable on paper before it exists — the mass balance of the whole plant is the sum of the units', and any discrepancy in operation (unaccounted loss) is a leak, a side reaction, or an instrument error — diagnosable by the balance itself.
    • Unit operation vs unit process is the design's grammar: chemical transformations set what must happen; unit operations set how it is arranged — the reactor is surrounded by the physical machinery that feeds, separates, heats, and recycles. Most of a plant's volume and cost is unit operations, not reaction — the chemistry is done in a few cubic metres; the separations occupy the rest.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Safety Is Designed In, Not Bolted On

    • The design sequence places safety after materials but before layout deliberately: relief valves, containment, interlocks, and inherently safer choices (less hazardous solvent, milder conditions, smaller inventory) are cheapest at the flowsheet stage and impossible to retrofit cheaply. Inherent safety — minimise inventory of hazardous material, substitute, moderate conditions, simplify — outranks protective equipment in the hierarchy.
    • Running is the design's continuation: a plant at steady state is a balance being satisfied every second; operators watch the variables whose drift signals balance failure (temperature creep, pressure rise, composition shift). The log-book is the plant's memory and the accident investigation's first document.

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — flowsheet reading: identify unit operations and unit processes in a given scheme.
    • Type B — mass balance arithmetic: simple in–out with recycle.
    • Type C — siting argument: justify a plant location against the factor list.
    • Type D — safety hierarchy: order protective measures by the inherent-safety principle.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Unit operation and unit process are synonyms": operations are physical, processes are chemical — distillation vs nitration. "Safety means alarms": alarms are the last layer; inherent safety (less hazardous inventory) is the first.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (6m): Distinguish unit operations from unit processes, giving three examples of each, and state which occupies most of a plant's volume. — Unit operations (physical): steps that change physical state or separation without chemical change — distillation (separation by volatility), filtration (solid–liquid separation), absorption (gas into liquid, e.g. SO₂ scrubbing); also drying, crystallisation, heat exchange. Unit processes (chemical): steps that transform substances by reaction — nitration (HNO₃/H₂SO₄ onto the ring), hydrogenation (H₂/Ni addition), sulphonation (SO₃ onto the ring); also oxidation, halogenation. Volume: unit operations dominate — a reactor may occupy a few cubic metres while the distillation columns, heat exchangers, absorbers, filters, pumps and tanks that feed, separate and recycle around it occupy the great majority of the plant's volume, cost and control loops. Deep point: the distinction is the design's grammar — the route's chemistry (unit processes) fixes what equipment is needed; the operations fix how it is arranged, and since separations usually outnumber reactions, the plant engineer's work is mostly physical, not chemical.
  • NEB-style (6m): A mixer receives 100 kg/h of solution containing 20% salt and evaporates water to produce a 50% salt solution. Find the product rate and the water evaporated per hour. — Salt balance (salt is conserved): in: 100 × 0.20 = 20 kg/h salt. Product P at 50% salt: 0.50 P = 20 ⟹ P = 40 kg/h. Water evaporated: total mass balance: 100 = P + W ⟹ W = 100 − 40 = 60 kg/h. Check by water: in: 80 kg; out in product: 40 × 0.50 = 20 kg; evaporated 80 − 20 = 60 ✓. Deep point: the two-balances method (conserved component + total) solves every simple evaporation/mixing problem — pick the component that passes through unchanged, fix its flow, and the total balance hands over the removed stream; the check is free and confirms both numbers.
  • NEB-style (5m): List the factors governing the siting of a chemical plant and explain why "distance from population" conflicts with "transport links". — Factors: (1) raw material and energy supply; (2) water availability (cooling and process); (3) transport links — road, rail, port for feed-in and product-out; (4) labour availability; (5) waste-disposal capacity (effluent, emissions); (6) distance from dense population (safety buffer); (7) climate and natural-hazard exposure; (8) land cost and regulations. The conflict: transport links and labour concentrate near cities, while safety and environmental buffers demand distance from them — every siting decision is a compromise along this axis, typically resolved by industrial zones: close enough for logistics, far enough for the hazard footprint, with the buffer zoned for non-residential use. Deep point: siting is the design stage where economics and safety meet in one map — ranking the factors honestly (raw materials and water usually first for a chemical plant) matters more than listing them all.
  • NEB-style (6m): State the hierarchy of safety measures for a plant handling a toxic gas, from most to least preferred, and justify the ordering. — 1. Inherent safety (most preferred): eliminate or minimise the hazard itself — use a less toxic solvent, reduce the inventory of gas held on site, moderate the temperature/pressure so less energy is available for release. 2. Engineered (passive/active) protection: contain what cannot be eliminated — sealed systems, relief valves to scrubbers, double-walled tanks, automatic interlocks that shut down on drift. 3. Procedural controls: operating procedures, permits to work, training, maintenance schedules. 4. Alarms and emergency response (least preferred): gas detectors, alarms, evacuation plans — they act after the release has begun. Justification: the hierarchy is ordered by reliability — inherent safety cannot fail (the hazard is absent), engineered barriers fail rarely and passively, procedures depend on human compliance, alarms depend on detection plus human response under stress. Deep point: each layer is cheaper to install and more certain to work than the next; the exam question "why is inherent safety first?" is answered by that single sentence — you cannot have a release of what you never stored.

Summary

A chemical plant is a network of unit operations arranged around a few unit processes, sized by mass and energy balances that make it verifiable on paper and diagnosable in operation. Design runs flowsheet → balances → equipment → materials → safety → siting, with inherent safety placed before engineered protection and alarms — ordered by reliability, not gadgetry. Running the plant is keeping the balance at steady state; most of its volume is separations, not reaction, and every siting decision negotiates the transport-versus-population compromise.
