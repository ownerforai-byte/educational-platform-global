Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Chemical Industry's Environmental Impact

    The chemical industry transforms matter at scale, and its wastes are transformations too: gaseous emissions (SO₂, NOₓ, CO₂, VOCs), liquid effluents (acids, alkalis, heavy metals, organics), and solid wastes (slag, spent catalysts, plastics). The modern response has two halves — treatment (scrubbers, effluent plants) and prevention (green chemistry: atom economy, catalysis, recycling) — with prevention ranked higher.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Air emissions: SO₂ (from sulphur burning/metallurgy) → acid rain; NOₓ (high-temperature combustion) → acid rain + photochemical smog; CO₂ (combustion, calcination) → greenhouse forcing; VOCs (solvent evaporation) → smog precursors.
    • Water effluents: acids/alkalis (pH damage), heavy metals (Pb, Hg, Cd — bioaccumulation), organic loads (BOD/COD depletion of dissolved oxygen), nutrients (eutrophication).
    • Solid wastes: slag, phosphogypsum, spent catalysts, plastics (persistence).
    • Treatment methods: gas — scrubbing (alkaline wash for SO₂: SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O), catalytic converters, bag filters/ESP for particulates; liquid — neutralisation, precipitation, biological treatment (activated sludge); solid — landfill, encapsulation, recycling.
    • Green chemistry principles (the syllabus core): prevent waste rather than treat it; atom economy = (mass of desired product / total mass of reactants) × 100; catalysis over stoichiometric reagents; safer solvents; recycling and energy recovery.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Prevention Ranks Above Treatment: Why

    • Treatment is a cost — scrubbers, effluent plants and landfills consume capital and energy while converting one waste into another (a scrubber turns an air problem into a sludge problem). Prevention eliminates the waste stream at its source: a route with higher atom economy generates less waste per tonne of product by design, saving raw material cost and treatment cost simultaneously — the only environmental measure that pays twice.
    • Atom economy vs yield — the distinction that organises the whole topic: yield measures how much of the limiting reactant ended up in product; atom economy measures how much of the total mass fed ends up in product. A reaction can run at 100% yield with 30% atom economy (most of the mass leaves as by-product) — the classic case is substitution routes whose leaving groups become waste. Yield is a laboratory virtue; atom economy is a planetary one.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — The Impact Is a Flow, Not a List

    • Pollutants act through flows and fates: SO₂ emitted high travels and returns as acid rain hundreds of kilometres away; mercury discharged to water bioaccumulates up the food chain (Minamata); CO₂ accumulates over decades. The impact of a plant is therefore not its stack's composition but its emissions × time × dispersion × food-chain transport — which is why regulation sets load limits (mass per day) and concentration limits (mass per volume), and why the same kilo of mercury is a local accident and a global problem depending on its fate.
    • The life-cycle view closes the argument: a product's environmental cost includes its feedstock extraction, manufacture, use and disposal — a "green" product made by a dirty route is not green. Life-cycle assessment is the accounting method that prevents burden-shifting (solving an air problem by creating a water one).

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — classify the wastes: gas/liquid/solid with one example and one harm each.
    • Type B — treatment matching: pair each waste with its standard treatment and equation.
    • Type C — atom economy calculation: compare two routes to the same product.
    • Type D — fate reasoning: explain bioaccumulation or acid-rain transport for a named pollutant.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "High yield means clean process": yield and atom economy are independent — a 100%-yield substitution can dump 70% of its mass as by-product. "Scrubbing solves pollution": it transfers the pollutant to another phase — the sludge still needs disposal; only prevention reduces the total.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (6m): Describe the gaseous, liquid and solid wastes of the chemical industry, giving one example and one environmental harm for each class. — Gaseous: SO₂ from sulphur-burning and metallurgical plants — returns as acid rain (damages forests, lakes, buildings); also NOₓ (acid rain + smog), CO₂ (greenhouse), VOCs (smog precursors). Liquid: acidic/alkaline effluents and heavy-metal-bearing streams (Pb, Hg, Cd from electroplating, battery manufacture) — bioaccumulation in the food chain and pH destruction of aquatic life; organic effluents raise BOD, depleting dissolved oxygen and killing rivers. Solid: slag from metallurgy, phosphogypsum from phosphoric acid plants, spent catalysts, plastic wastes — land persistence and, for plastics, centuries-long fragmentation. Deep point: the three classes differ in mobility — gases disperse regionally, liquids enter the water web locally but move biologically, solids stay put but persist; the mobility gradient is why each class needs a different regulatory tool (stack limits, effluent standards, landfill containment).
  • NEB-style (6m): Define atom economy and calculate it for the preparation of ethene by (a) dehydration of ethanol (C2H5OH -> C2H4 + H2O) and (b) cracking of dodecane (C12H26 -> C10H22 + C2H4). Comment on the comparison with yield. — Definition: atom economy = (mass of desired product ÷ total mass of all reactants) × 100% — the fraction of the fed mass that ends up in the wanted molecule. (a) Dehydration: reactant C₂H₅OH = 46 g; desired C₂H₄ = 28 g ⟹ atom economy = 28/46 = 60.9% (the other 39.1% leaves as water). (b) Cracking: reactant C₁₂H₂₆ = 170 g; desired C₂H₄ = 28 g ⟹ atom economy = 28/170 = 16.5% — but the co-product C₁₀H₂₂ is itself a useful fuel, so the practical waste is small. Yield contrast: both reactions can run at high yield (little of the limiting reactant is lost to side reactions) while their atom economies differ by a factor of four — yield and atom economy are independent measures: yield asks how well the reaction ran; atom economy asks whether the route was worth running. Deep point: the cracking case adds the mature nuance — atom economy assumes co-products are waste; when they are saleable (fuels), the mass-balance verdict softens, and the full tool is life-cycle thinking, not the single fraction.
  • NEB-style (5m): A plant emits SO2. Describe its treatment by alkaline scrubbing with the equation, and explain why scrubbing alone does not end the environmental problem. — Scrubbing: the flue gas passes through a scrubber where an alkaline solution (NaOH or slaked lime slurry) contacts it: SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O (with lime: SO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaSO₃ + H₂O); the gas-phase SO₂ is absorbed into the liquid as sulphite/sulphate. Why the problem persists: the sulphur has been transferred, not destroyed — the scrubber liquor is now a sulphite/sulphate sludge requiring oxidation, neutralisation and disposal; an air-pollution problem has become a solid/liquid-waste problem. Completing the solution: the sludge should be converted to a useful product (gypsum for plasterboard, in the lime route) or the process redesigned (Claus process recovering elemental sulphur) — prevention or valorisation, not transfer. Deep point: the mass balance never forgets — sulphur in the feed must appear somewhere in the outputs; every treatment choice is a decision about where, and only route redesign changes how much.
  • NEB-style (6m): Explain how mercury discharged into a river can poison people far downstream and years later. Name the classic case. — The mechanism — bioaccumulation and biomagnification: mercury (especially as methylmercury, formed by anaerobic bacteria in sediments) is absorbed by plankton and algae; small fish eat many plankton, large fish eat many small fish — at each trophic step the mercury concentrates (it is excreted far more slowly than it is absorbed), so predators carry concentrations thousands to millions of times the water's. Time and distance: the metal persists in sediment for decades and moves with the food web, not the water current — people eating fish from a contaminated river are poisoned long after and far from the discharge point. The classic case: Minamata, Japan (1950s) — a acetaldehyde plant discharged mercury compounds into Minamata Bay; the community's fish diet delivered methylmercury, causing the neurological disease named for the bay. Deep point: the impact chain (discharge → sediment → methylation → plankton → fish → human) explains both the delay and the distance — the pollutant's fate, not its discharge point, defines its harm; this is why fate-based regulation (bioaccumulation limits) exists alongside concentration limits.

Summary

The chemical industry's footprint splits into gases (SO₂, NOₓ, CO₂, VOCs), liquids (acids, heavy metals, oxygen-demanding organics) and solids (slag, catalysts, plastics) — each with a mobility and a fate that define its harm. Treatment transfers waste between phases (scrubbing turns air into sludge); green chemistry prevents it — atom economy measuring the mass that becomes product, catalysis replacing stoichiometric reagents, recycling closing loops. Yield and atom economy are independent dials, impacts are flows with fates (Minamata's methylmercury the permanent warning), and the life-cycle view is what stops one problem being renamed as another.
