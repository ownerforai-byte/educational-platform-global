Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Benzene's Physical Profile

    Benzene is a colourless, mobile liquid with a characteristic odour: bp 80.5 °C (80.1 °C in some texts), mp 5.5 °C, density 0.879 g/cm³ (lighter than water), immiscible with water, and an excellent solvent for non-polar and moderately polar solutes. Every one of these numbers has a structural reason.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • State and appearance: colourless liquid; characteristic (aromatic) odour; volatile.
    • Boiling point 80.5 °C: higher than cyclohexane (81 °C is close) but the comparison that matters — vs expected for its molar mass, benzene's planar disk shape gives strong instantaneous dipole dispersion contact.
    • Melting point 5.5 °C: remarkably high for a small molecule — the flat hexagonal disks pack efficiently into a crystal lattice (symmetry aids packing); the high mp/symmetry link is the physical proof of planarity.
    • Density 0.879 g/cm³: floats on water; immiscible — the non-polar ring cannot hydrogen-bond into the water network.
    • Solvent behaviour: dissolves iodine, sulphur, rubber, fats, many organics; the "like dissolves like" non-polar champion.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Symmetry Packs, Shape Boils

    • Melting point is a packing story: 5.5 °C is high for a 78 g/mol molecule (cyclohexane, 84 g/mol, melts at 6.5 °C — similar, but n-hexane, 86 g/mol, melts at −95 °C!). The difference is shape: flat, rigid, symmetric disks stack into efficient crystals; floppy chains waste contact area in the solid. Benzene's mp is the macroscopic fingerprint of its planar hexagonal geometry.
    • Boiling point is a surface story: liquids boil when molecules escape the collective dispersion contact; benzene's disk presents large flat faces for π–π and dispersion contact, giving bp 80.5 °C — comparable to cyclohexane's despite lower molar mass, because contact quality compensates for mass.
    • Water immiscibility is a network story: inserting benzene into water would break hydrogen-bond bridges without replacing them — the ring offers no donors or acceptors. The two-layer test tube picture is the hydrophobic effect in one glance.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Reading a Property Table as Evidence

    • Each physical constant cross-checks a structural claim: mp 5.5 °C (planarity/packing), bp 80.5 °C (contact area), density < 1 (no strong interaction with water), immiscibility (no H-bonding), sooty combustion (92.3% C). The table is not trivia — it is the same delocalisation story told in five different instruments.
    • Solvent power follows polarity: benzene dissolves non-polar solutes (I₂, S₈, oils) via dispersion; it will not dissolve ionic or strongly H-bonding solutes — the solvent list is the polarity list read backwards.

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — recite with reasons: state each property with its structural cause.
    • Type B — compare: benzene vs cyclohexane vs hexane on mp/bp — the shape argument.
    • Type C — miscibility prediction: which of two solvents mixes with benzene, why.
    • Type D — density arithmetic: mass of benzene delivered by a measured volume.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Benzene is denser than water because it is aromatic": 0.879 < 1 — it floats; aromaticity does not imply density. "High mp means strong hydrogen bonding": benzene has none — its mp is pure packing efficiency.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (5m): List the important physical properties of benzene, giving a structural reason for each. — Appearance: colourless, mobile, volatile liquid with a characteristic odour. Boiling point 80.5 °C: the flat disk presents large faces for dispersion (and π–π) contact — strong intermolecular attraction for its mass. Melting point 5.5 °C: flat, symmetric hexagons pack efficiently into the crystal — packing quality, not hydrogen bonding, sets the high mp. Density 0.879 g/cm³: less than water — it floats; the non-polar ring forms no strong interactions to compact it. Water immiscibility: the ring offers no H-bond donors or acceptors, so it cannot plug into water's network. Solvent power: dissolves non-polar solutes (iodine, sulphur, fats) by dispersion. Combustion: burns with a smoky, luminous flame — 92.3% carbon. Deep point: each property is a different instrument measuring the same two facts — planarity and non-polarity; answering "property + reason" pairs is the full-mark pattern.
  • NEB-style (6m): Benzene (78 g/mol) melts at 5.5 C while n-hexane (86 g/mol) melts at -95 C. Explain the contrast and state what it proves. — Mass is not the decider: hexane is heavier yet melts 100 °C lower. The decider is crystal packing: n-hexane is a floppy chain — in the solid it must adopt extended conformations and still leaves poor contact, weak van der Waals registration between chains ⟹ low mp. Benzene is a rigid, flat, sixfold-symmetric disk — disks stack face-to-face with maximal contact per molecule and minimal wasted volume ⟹ efficient lattice, high mp for its size. What it proves: the planar, symmetric geometry of the benzene ring — a macroscopic property (melting point) acting as evidence for a molecular-structure claim. Deep point: physical-property comparisons are structure determinations in disguise — when two molecules of similar mass differ by 100 °C in mp, the cause is shape and rigidity; the argument generalises to why spherical/planar molecules (neopentane, benzene) melt high and boil relatively low.
  • NEB-style (5m): A mixture of benzene and water is shaken and allowed to stand. Describe what is observed and explain at the molecular level. — Observation: two layers form — benzene on top (density 0.879 < 1.00), water below; a sharp meniscus, no mixing even on standing. Molecular explanation: water molecules hold each other by an extensive hydrogen-bond network; benzene's ring has no O–H, N–H or lone-pair-exposed sites to donate or accept hydrogen bonds — inserting benzene into water would break water–water H-bonds and replace them with only weak dispersion contact ⟹ energetically unfavourable (the hydrophobic effect). The system minimises free energy by demixing. Deep point: immiscibility is usually stated as "non-polar vs polar" — the deeper statement is about networks: each liquid keeps its own interaction network intact rather than paying to merge two incompatible ones; that is why even vigorous shaking only emulsifies temporarily.
  • NEB-style (6m): Iodine is shaken with (a) water and (b) benzene. Describe and explain the distribution of iodine between the two solvents. — Observation: iodine is barely soluble in water (pale yellow-brown, ~0.3 g/L) but dissolves readily in benzene, giving the characteristic violet solution. Explanation: I₂ is non-polar and polarisable — it interacts with benzene's polarisable π cloud by strong dispersion (and weak charge-transfer) forces; water offers only weak dipole-induced contacts and I₂ cannot H-bond ⟹ the non-polar solvent wins decisively. Distribution: on shaking with both, iodine partitions overwhelmingly into the benzene layer (the basis of the classic distribution-coefficient demonstration). Deep point: "like dissolves like" is the slogan; the mechanism is contact quality — a polarisable solute seeks the most polarisable solvent, and the π cloud of benzene is the best contact available in the pair; the violet colour itself signals I₂ pairs in a non-polar medium (vs the brown complexation seen in polar/donating solvents like KI solution).

Key Points

  • Colourless liquid, characteristic odour; bp 80.5 C, mp 5.5 C, density 0.879 g/cm3 (floats on water), immiscible with water, excellent non-polar solvent, burns with sooty flame. mp argument: flat symmetric disks pack efficiently (vs n-hexane mp -95 C at similar mass) - packing is the proof of planarity. bp argument: disk faces give strong dispersion contact. Immiscibility: no H-bond donors/acceptors for the water network.

Summary

Benzene's physical profile — colourless liquid, bp 80.5 °C, mp 5.5 °C, density 0.879, water-immiscible, non-polar solvent, sooty flame — is one structure read by five instruments: flat symmetric disks pack (high mp), present broad faces (bp), refuse the water network (immiscibility), dissolve the non-polar (solvent list), and burn carbon-rich (flame). The hexane comparison turns the melting point into proof of planarity, and every property question wants the number with its structural reason.
