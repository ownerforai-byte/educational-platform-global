Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Burning Benzene and Using It

    Benzene's combustion is the sooty-flame exemplar: 2C₆H₆ + 15O₂ → 12CO₂ + 6H₂O, highly exothermic but incomplete in ordinary flames because the ring is 92.3% carbon. Its uses follow its properties: solvent, precursor to styrene/phenol/cyclohexane, and — increasingly restricted — fuel additive.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Combustion equation: 2C₆H₆ + 15O₂ → 12CO₂ + 6H₂O + heat (ΔH ≈ −3268 kJ per 2 mol).
    • Flame character: luminous, smoky (sooty) — the C : H ratio (1 : 1) leaves carbon unoxidised in the flame's fuel-rich interior; the soot is elemental carbon escaping before full combustion.
    • Generalisation: aromatic and high-carbon fuels burn smoky; the flame is a composition readout.
    • Principal uses: (1) solvent for resins, fats, iodine, sulphur; (2) raw material — ethylbenzene → styrene (polystyrene), cumene → phenol + acetone, cyclohexane → nylon precursors (adipic acid), nitrobenzene → aniline (dyes); (3) motor-fuel component (anti-knock value high) — now restricted by benzene's carcinogenicity; (4) laboratory reagent and standard.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — The Sooty Flame as Stoichiometry Made Visible

    • The balanced equation demands 15 O₂ per 2 benzene — an oxygen-hungry ratio. In a real flame the fuel-rich zones cannot supply that oxygen locally, so carbon is released as fine particles before reaching CO₂; these particles glow incandescently (luminous yellow) and deposit as soot. The flame's colour is a local stoichiometry deficit.
    • **Why benzene is more sooty than cyclohexane:** C₆H₆ needs 7.5 O₂ per molecule; C₆H₁₂ needs 9 O₂ per molecule but delivers 6 carbons with 12 hydrogens carrying their own combustion oxygen budget — the H : C ratio (2 : 1 vs 1 : 1) is the cleaner predictor: more H per C ⟹ more complete combustion ⟹ cleaner flame.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Uses Follow Structure, and Safety Follows Toxicity

    • Solvent use is the non-polarity chapter (dispersion contact with non-polar solutes); precursor use is the substitution chemistry chapter (nitration → aniline chain; alkylation → styrene chain; hydrogenation → cyclohexane/nylon chain) — each industrial route is a syllabus reaction scaled up.
    • The safety reversal: benzene's historical ubiquity (solvent, fuel) met its toxicology — benzene is a proven human carcinogen (bone-marrow suppression, leukaemia via its metabolites). Modern practice substitutes toluene (less volatile, metabolised differently) wherever the solvent property, not the ring reactivity, is needed. The uses list is now partly historical by law.

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — write and balance: the combustion equation with coefficients.
    • Type B — explain the soot: C% and H : C ratio reasoning.
    • Type C — list uses by category: solvent / precursor / fuel with one example each.
    • Type D — energy calculation: heat released from mass of benzene burnt.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Sooty flame means incomplete equation": the equation is complete; the flame is locally fuel-rich — the soot is kinetics and mixing, not stoichiometry error. "Benzene is still a common solvent": carcinogenicity has largely retired it from open laboratory use — toluene is the modern stand-in.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (5m): Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of benzene and explain why it burns with a smoky flame. — Equation: 2C₆H₆ + 15O₂ → 12CO₂ + 6H₂O + heat (check: C 12 = 12; H 12 = 12; O 30 = 24 + 6 ✓). Sooty flame explanation: benzene's C : H ratio is 1 : 1 — 92.3% carbon by mass; in the fuel-rich interior of a normal flame the local oxygen supply cannot match the 7.5 O₂-per-molecule demand, so elemental carbon particles form, glow incandescently (the luminous yellow), and escape as soot before full oxidation. Deep point: the balanced equation and the smoky flame are not in conflict — the equation describes complete combustion with sufficient oxygen; the flame is a mixing and kinetics problem where sufficiency fails locally. Stating both halves is the full answer.
  • NEB-style (6m): Compare the flames of burning benzene (C6H6) and cyclohexane (C6H12), and justify the difference using composition. — Benzene: H : C = 6 : 6 = 1 ⟹ oxygen-hungry, fuel-rich zones common ⟹ luminous, heavily sooty flame. Cyclohexane: H : C = 12 : 6 = 2 ⟹ each carbon arrives with twice the hydrogen, whose combustion supplies water and drives the carbon out as CO₂ ⟹ cleaner, less sooty flame. Generalisation: down the saturation ladder (benzene → cyclohexene → cyclohexane) the H : C ratio rises 1 → 1.67 → 2 and the flame cleans progressively. Deep point: the H : C ratio is a better flame predictor than either C% or molar mass alone — hydrogens are the fuel's internal oxygen-delivery system, and the ratio encodes how much self-help each carbon gets; the soot ladder is a composition ladder.
  • NEB-style (6m): List the important uses of benzene, grouped by the property or reaction each use exploits. — Solvent uses (exploit non-polarity): dissolves resins, fats, waxes, iodine, sulphur — the dispersion-contact champion of the early chemical industry. Precursor uses (exploit substitution and addition chemistry): nitration → nitrobenzene → aniline (dyes industry); alkylation with ethene → ethylbenzene → styrene (polystyrene); Friedel–Crafts with propene → cumene → phenol + acetone (the cumene process); hydrogenation → cyclohexane → adipic acid (nylon-6,6 chain). Fuel use (exploits high anti-knock value): petrol additive — now heavily restricted. Laboratory standard: reference solvent and calorimetric standard. Deep point: each use is a syllabus reaction scaled to industry — mapping the uses back to the reactions (nitration, alkylation, hydrogenation) converts a memorisation task into a structure-uses argument.
  • NEB-style (5m): Calculate the heat released when 78 g of benzene is completely burnt, given that the enthalpy of combustion of benzene is -3268 kJ per mole of reaction as written (2 mol C6H6). — Moles: 78 g / 78 g/mol = 1 mol C₆H₆. Scaling: the quoted ΔH corresponds to 2 mol benzene ⟹ per mole: −3268/2 = −1634 kJ/mol. Answer: burning 78 g releases 1634 kJ. Cross-check via the equation: 2C₆H₆ + 15O₂ → 12CO₂ + 6H₂O — one mole of benzene is half the reaction entity, so half the quoted enthalpy. Deep point: "per mole of reaction as written" is the trap — the coefficient sum (2 + 15) defines the reaction entity; dividing by the benzene coefficient before multiplying by moles is the one algebraic step that separates the correct 1634 kJ from a doubled or halved error.

Key Points

  • 2C6H6 + 15O2 -> 12CO2 + 6H2O, highly exothermic; luminous sooty flame from C:H = 1:1 - fuel-rich zones release glowing carbon particles. H:C ratio predicts flame cleanliness (cyclohexane cleaner, benzene smokier). Uses: non-polar solvent; precursor (ethylbenzene->styrene, cumene->phenol+acetone, cyclohexane->nylon chain, nitrobenzene->aniline dyes); fuel anti-knock value - now restricted (carcinogen, bone-marrow/leukaemia); toluene is the modern solvent substitute.

Summary

Benzene burns as 2C₆H₆ + 15O₂ → 12CO₂ + 6H₂O — exothermic and oxygen-hungry, with a luminous sooty flame that is the 1 : 1 H : C ratio made visible in glowing carbon particles. Its uses split by the property exploited: non-polarity (solvent), substitution and addition chemistry (styrene, phenol, aniline, nylon precursors), anti-knock value (restricted fuel). The carcinogenicity verdict has retired the solvent uses to history — toluene now stands where benzene stood — but the precursor web remains the ring's industrial biography.
