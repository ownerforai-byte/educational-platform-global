Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Body's Electricity Bill

    Ion pumps are the molecular machines that move ions against their concentration gradients, spending ATP to do it. The syllabus names two: the sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase), which keeps nerves liveable, and the sodium–glucose pump, which uses the sodium gradient as a battery to absorb glucose. Understanding them means understanding active versus passive transport — one of the cleanest contrasts in the unit.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Passive versus active transport

    • Passive transport (diffusion): particles move down their concentration gradient; no energy spent. Ions need a channel/protein, but the gradient does the work.
    • Active transport: particles are pushed against their gradient; this costs energy, supplied directly by ATP (primary active transport) or indirectly by another ion's gradient (secondary active transport).

    2.2 The sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase)

    • A membrane enzyme (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase) that, per ATP hydrolysed, expels 3 Na⁺ and imports 2 K⁺ — both against their gradients.
    • Consequences it maintains:
    • high K⁺ inside, high Na⁺ outside → the resting membrane potential (inside ≈ −70 mV) that nerves and muscles fire on;
    • cell volume control (osmotic balance);
    • the Na⁺ gradient itself, which powers secondary transport.
    • The ratio 3:2 is the exam's favourite number.

    2.3 The sodium–glucose pump (Na⁺–glucose symport)

    • In the intestine and kidney tubules, glucose must be absorbed against its concentration gradient.
    • A carrier protein (SGLT) binds Na⁺ and glucose on the same side and moves them together in the same direction (symport): Na⁺ rushes in down its gradient (maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) and drags glucose up its gradient with it.
    • This is secondary active transport: the direct energy is the Na⁺ electrochemical gradient; the ultimate energy is the ATP the pump spent building that gradient.
    • The same principle underlies oral rehydration therapy (ORS): sugar plus salt in the drink lets the gut co-absorb water and glucose even in diarrhoea.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why the pump must run constantly

    • Na⁺ and K⁺ leak slowly through membrane channels down their gradients all the time; without continuous pumping the gradients would collapse within minutes and every nerve would fall silent. The pump is estimated to consume a large fraction of the body's resting ATP (up to ~⅓ in some tissues, more in brain) — the body's single biggest energy customer.

    4.2 How the sodium–glucose pump chains two gradients

    • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump builds a steep Na⁺ gradient (high outside). The symporter opens a door for Na⁺ to flow back in down that gradient — but the door only opens when glucose is aboard. Na⁺'s downhill trip pays for glucose's uphill one. One gradient spends, one gradient earns; the ATP bill was paid in advance by the pump.

    4.3 Clinical hooks

    • ORS (oral rehydration solution): glucose actively co-transports Na⁺ (and water follows osmotically) — the reason ORS contains both sugar and salt and saves lives in cholera/diarrhoea.
    • Digitalis/digoxin (heart drug) works by inhibiting the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, indirectly raising Ca²⁺ inside heart cells and strengthening contraction.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    The sodium–glucose pump does not split ATP: its direct energy is the Na⁺ gradient; the ATP was spent upstream by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Calling it "ATP-driven" without the intermediate loses the mechanism mark.

  • 3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in — not the reverse: the pump exports more than it imports (which is why it is electrogenic), but the counts are 3 out, 2 in.
  • Diffusion never needs ATP; active transport always needs an energy source — but the source may be indirect (a gradient), which is why "no ATP here" does not mean "passive".

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe the sodium–potassium pump. What is its stoichiometry and why is it important? — The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase) is a membrane protein that hydrolyses ATP and uses the energy to transport ions against their gradients: for every ATP hydrolysed, 3 Na⁺ are pumped out of the cell and 2 K⁺ are pumped in. Importance: (i) it maintains high K⁺ inside and high Na⁺ outside, establishing the resting membrane potential (≈ −70 mV) on which nerve impulses and muscle contraction depend; (ii) it controls cell volume (osmotic balance); (iii) it maintains the Na⁺ gradient that powers secondary active transport, including the sodium–glucose pump. Because ions constantly leak back down their gradients, the pump must run continuously — it is one of the body's largest consumers of ATP.

  • NEB-style (3m): How is glucose absorbed in the intestine against its concentration gradient? Explain the sodium–glucose pump. — Glucose is absorbed by secondary active transport. A carrier protein in the intestinal lining (SGLT, the sodium–glucose symporter) binds Na⁺ and glucose together on the luminal side and changes shape to release both inside the cell. The Na⁺ moves down the steep Na⁺ electrochemical gradient maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump; glucose hitches a ride and is carried up — against — its own gradient. The energy for glucose transport is therefore the Na⁺ gradient, which the Na⁺/K⁺ pump built with ATP — hence "secondary" active transport. The same mechanism in the kidney reclaims glucose from the filtrate, and it is the basis of oral rehydration solution (ORS): supplying glucose and sodium together lets the gut absorb water and salts even during diarrhoea.
  • NEB-style (2m): Distinguish active transport from diffusion with one example of each. — Diffusion (passive transport) moves particles down their concentration gradient with no energy input (e.g. O₂ crossing the alveolar membrane into blood). Active transport moves particles against their gradient and requires energy (e.g. the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, which expels 3 Na⁺ and imports 2 K⁺ per ATP hydrolysed). The direction of movement relative to the gradient, and the need for energy, are the two discriminating features.

Summary

Ion pumps move ions uphill, and the syllabus names two machines. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase) is primary active transport: one ATP buys 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, maintaining the ≈ −70 mV resting potential, cell volume, and the Na⁺ gradient everything else feeds on. The sodium–glucose pump is secondary active transport: in the intestine and kidney tubules a symporter lets Na⁺ rush in down its gradient and drags glucose up its gradient in the same step — one gradient spends, one earns. Because the Na⁺/K⁺ pump built the sodium gradient with ATP, every glucose molecule absorbed carries a hidden ATP price — and oral rehydration solution exploits exactly this co-transport to keep patients absorbing water in cholera.
