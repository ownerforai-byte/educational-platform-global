Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — When the Metal Is the Poison

    The same d-block chemistry that makes Fe, Cu and Zn essential makes Pb, Hg, Cd and As dangerous: they bind sulphur and other biological ligands where they do not belong, or imitate essential ions and hijack their machinery. The syllabus names five toxic metals — Fe (in excess), As, Hg, Pb, Cd — each with a classic disease story and a chemistry explanation.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Iron — essential metal, toxic in excess

    • Overdose (especially iron tonic tablets in children) causes gastrointestinal corrosion, liver damage and death; free Fe²⁺ catalyses formation of damaging radicals (Fenton chemistry) and overwhelms transport proteins.
    • Haemochromatosis — chronic iron overload: iron deposits in liver, pancreas and heart; treated by repeated blood removal or chelation (desferrioxamine).
    • The lesson: even the most essential metal poisons above its window.

    2.2 Arsenic — the enzyme poison

    • Arsenic (especially As(III), arsenite) binds –SH (thiol) groups of enzymes, blocking them; it also interferes with ATP production.
    • Chronic exposure → arsenicosis: skin lesions (keratosis), skin/lung cancer, peripheral nerve damage.
    • Groundwater contamination — the classic NEB-relevant case: arsenic in tube-well water (WHO limit 10 ppb); Nepal's Terai and neighbouring Gangetic plains are affected regions.

    2.3 Mercury — the nerve poison

    • Elemental Hg vapour and organic methylmercury are the dangerous forms; methylmercury bioaccumulates up the aquatic food chain.
    • Attacks the central nervous system: tremors, memory loss, numbness, mood changes.
    • Minamata disease (Japan, 1950s): a factory discharged mercury compounds into a bay; methylmercury concentrated in fish; thousands poisoned — the textbook case study.

    2.4 Lead — the childhood poison

    • Pb²⁺ imitates Ca²⁺ (deposits in bone) and inhibits the enzymes of haem synthesis (ALA dehydratase, ferrochelatase) → anaemia.
    • In children: neurological damage — lowered IQ, learning and behaviour problems, even at low exposure; also abdominal colic ("lead colic").
    • Sources: old lead paints, leaded petrol (now phased out), lead pipes, battery recycling.

    2.5 Cadmium — the kidney poison

    • Cd²⁺ imitates Zn²⁺ (same group) and slips into zinc enzymes, but cannot do their chemistry; accumulates in kidneys (protein in urine) and bones.
    • Itai-itai disease (Japan): cadmium-polluted irrigation water → kidney damage plus painful bone decalcification ("ouch-ouch").
    • Sources: Ni–Cd batteries, galvanised/plated metal, tobacco smoke (smokers carry significantly more cadmium).

    2.6 Chelation therapy — the chemistry antidote

    • Toxic metals are removed by chelating agents — ligands that grip the metal in soluble complexes excreted in urine:
    • EDTA (lead); dimercaprol/BAL (arsenic, mercury); penicillamine (copper, Wilson's disease); desferrioxamine (iron).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why soft metals attack sulphur

    • Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺ and As(III) are soft/borderline Lewis acids with high affinity for the soft –SH sulphur of cysteine residues. Binding an enzyme's essential cysteine disables it. This one preference explains why these metals hit so many different enzymes at once — and why the antidotes (BAL, penicillamine) are dithiols/thiols: they out-compete the enzyme for the metal.

    4.2 Mimicry as a poisoning strategy

    • Pb²⁺ ≈ Ca²⁺ (same charge, not too different radius) → enters bone, disrupts signalling and haem synthesis enzymes.
    • Cd²⁺ ≈ Zn²⁺ (same group) → occupies zinc's enzyme seats but is catalytically useless.
    • This is why the toxic metals are dangerous in trace amounts: they do not need to be abundant, only similar.

    4.3 Bioaccumulation and the food chain

    • Mercury enters water as inorganic Hg, is converted by bacteria to lipid-soluble methylmercury, and concentrates at each step of the food chain — top predators (tuna, swordfish) carry the highest loads. Cadmium and lead behave similarly in soils. Human exposure is therefore often via food and water, not direct contact.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Mercury's danger is the organic form and vapour, not liquid contact: liquid metallic mercury swallowed in small amount passes through the gut largely unabsorbed; the vapour and methylmercury are the killers.

  • Iron toxicity is about dose, not identity: iron is essential; its toxicity (overdose, haemochromatosis) is the dose rule of row 91 seen from the other side.
  • Minamata = mercury, itai-itai = cadmium: the two Japanese disease names are the most-swapped pair in the exam — mercury ate the nerves, cadmium ate the kidneys and bones.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB 2024 (verified PYQ): Write the harmful effects of lead and mercury on human health. — Lead: Pb²⁺ enters via contaminated water, paint dust, batteries and formerly leaded petrol. It inhibits the enzymes of haem synthesis, causing anaemia; it deposits in bone like calcium; and it is a potent neurotoxin in children, causing reduced IQ, learning and behavioural problems even at low exposure, plus abdominal pain (lead colic) and kidney damage in chronic cases. Mercury: the dangerous forms are mercury vapour and methylmercury; methylmercury bioaccumulates in fish and attacks the central nervous system, causing tremors, numbness of the limbs, memory loss, mood disturbance and, in severe cases (Minamata disease, Japan), paralysis and death; it also damages the kidneys and harms the developing fetus.

  • NEB-style (2m): Why does arsenic in groundwater pose a health hazard? Name its chronic disease. — Arsenic (mainly As(III), arsenite) leached into tube-well groundwater binds the –SH groups of enzymes, disabling them, and impairs energy metabolism. Chronic drinking of arsenic-contaminated water (above the WHO limit of 10 ppb) causes arsenicosis: skin keratosis and pigmentation, skin and other cancers, and peripheral nerve damage. The Terai belt of Nepal and the Gangetic plains are affected regions; the remedy is switching to tested deep wells or arsenic-removal filters.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is chelation therapy? Give one chelating agent and the metal it removes. — Chelation therapy treats metal poisoning by administering a chelating ligand that binds the toxic metal ion into a stable, water-soluble complex which the body excretes in urine. Examples: EDTA for lead; dimercaprol (BAL) for arsenic and mercury; penicillamine for copper (Wilson's disease); desferrioxamine for iron overload. The ligand must grip the metal more tightly than the biological target does — antidote chemistry is coordination chemistry.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (verified PYQ): Write the harmful effects of lead and mercury on human health. — Lead: Pb²⁺ enters the body through lead-painted dust, contaminated water and pipes, battery recycling and (formerly) leaded petrol. It inhibits the enzymes that synthesise haem (ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase), producing anaemia; being Ca²⁺-like it deposits in bone; it is a powerful neurotoxin in children, lowering IQ and causing learning and behavioural disorders even at low exposure; chronic adult exposure gives abdominal "lead colic", kidney damage and raised blood pressure. Mercury: the harmful forms are mercury vapour (inhaled) and methylmercury (from contaminated fish, where it bioaccumulates up the food chain). Both attack the central nervous system: tremors, numbness of limbs, memory loss, mood changes, and in severe poisoning (the classic Minamata disease, Japan) paralysis and death; mercury also damages the kidneys and the developing fetus.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is arsenic-contaminated groundwater dangerous, and what disease does chronic exposure cause? — Arsenic in groundwater occurs mainly as arsenite, As(III), a soft Lewis acid that binds the –SH (thiol) groups of enzymes and blocks them, besides disrupting energy metabolism. Drinking water above the WHO limit of 10 ppb over years causes arsenicosis: thickened skin patches (keratosis), pigmentation changes, skin and internal cancers, and peripheral nerve damage. Tube-well water in the Terai of Nepal and the Gangetic plains is the classic affected source; the remedies are tested alternative wells and arsenic-removal filters.
  • NEB-style (2m): Name the disease caused by cadmium poisoning and explain why cadmium is toxic. — Chronic cadmium poisoning causes itai-itai disease ("ouch-ouch", Japan) — severe kidney damage together with painful bone decalcification and fractures. Why toxic: Cd²⁺ sits directly below Zn²⁺ in Group IIB and mimics zinc, occupying the zinc sites of enzymes; but cadmium cannot perform zinc's catalytic chemistry, so the enzymes are silenced. Cadmium also accumulates in the kidneys over decades (biological half-life ~10–30 years) and reaches humans through Ni–Cd batteries, contaminated rice/irrigation water and tobacco smoke.

Key Points

  • Iron excess: overdose (children's tonics) -> gut corrosion, liver damage; chronic overload = haemochromatosis (liver/pancreas/heart); chelator desferrioxamine. Arsenic: As(III) binds -SH groups of enzymes; arsenicosis = skin lesions + cancer + neuropathy; groundwater hazard, WHO limit 10 ppb (Nepal Terai affected). Mercury: vapour + methylmercury (bioaccumulates in fish) attack CNS; Minamata disease (Japan 1950s). Cadmium: Cd2+ mimics Zn2+, kidney + bone damage; itai-itai disease (Japan); Ni-Cd batteries, tobacco smoke. Chelation therapy: EDTA (Pb), BAL/dimercaprol (As, Hg), penicillamine (Cu), desferrioxamine (Fe).

Summary

Toxicity has two mechanisms and five names. Mechanism one — soft-metal sulphur binding: As(III), Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺ and Cd²⁺ all latch onto the –SH groups of enzymes (arsenicosis from >10 ppb groundwater; Minamata disease from methylmercury that bioaccumulates in fish and destroys the CNS). Mechanism two — mimicry: Pb²⁺ poses as Ca²⁺ (bone deposits) while blocking haem-synthesis enzymes → anaemia and permanent IQ loss in children; Cd²⁺ poses as Zn²⁺, wrecking kidneys and bones (itai-itai disease; batteries and tobacco smoke are the sources). Even essential iron poisons in excess — overdose and haemochromatosis. The chemical antidote is chelation therapy: EDTA for lead, BAL for arsenic and mercury, penicillamine for copper, desferrioxamine for iron — ligands that grip the poison more tightly than the body does.
