Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Grams versus Micrograms

    Nutrition divides the inorganic diet into two weight classes. Macro-nutrients (macro-minerals) are needed in amounts of more than about 100 mg per day — Ca, P, K, S, Na, Cl, Mg. Micro-nutrients (trace elements) are needed in less than 100 mg per day — Fe, Zn, Cu, I, Co, Mn, Mo, Cr, Se, F — yet a shortage of any one of them produces a named deficiency disease. The exam loves the pairs: Fe–anaemia, I–goitre, Co–B₁₂.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The macro-minerals (7 of them)

    • Ca — bones and teeth, muscle contraction, blood clotting, nerve signalling.
    • P — bones (with Ca), ATP and nucleic acids, energy currency.
    • K — principal intracellular cation; nerve impulses, enzyme activation.
    • Na — principal extracellular cation; fluid balance, nerve impulses.
    • Cl — gastric HCl, electrolyte balance.
    • Mg — chlorophyll in plants; enzyme activator in animals.
    • S — amino acids (cysteine, methionine), proteins, vitamins.

    2.2 The trace elements

    • Fe — haemoglobin and myoglobin (O₂ transport), cytochromes; deficiency → anaemia.
    • I — thyroxine (thyroid hormone); deficiency → goitre; ~150 µg/day.
    • Co — vitamin B₁₂ (cyanocobalamin); deficiency → pernicious anaemia.
    • Zn — carbonic anhydrase, carboxypeptidase, insulin storage; wound healing.
    • Cu — haemocyanin (oxygen carrier in molluscs), cytochrome oxidase, enzyme cofactor.
    • Mn, Mo, Cr, Se, F — Mn enzymes; Mo in nitrogenase; Cr in glucose tolerance factor (with insulin); Se antioxidant enzymes; F tooth enamel.

    2.3 Deficiency diseases — the exam table

    • Iodine deficiency → goitre: the thyroid swells as it struggles to make thyroxine; prevented by iodised salt.
    • Iron deficiency → anaemia: too little haemoglobin, pale weak blood.
    • Cobalt (B₁₂) deficiency → pernicious anaemia: defective red-cell maturation.
    • Magnesium deficiency in soil → chlorosis: yellowing leaves, failing photosynthesis.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why the 100 mg line matters

    • The macro/trace division is about daily requirement, not importance. Na⁺ and K⁺ must be replaced in grams because the body leaks them constantly; Fe must be supplied in milligrams because it is recycled almost perfectly. Losing a trace element hurts just as much — it simply takes longer to run out.

    4.2 Iodine — the smallest dose with a visible disease

    • Iodine is concentrated by the thyroid and built into thyroxine (T₄) and tri-iodothyronine (T₃), the hormones that set metabolic rate. Without iodine the thyroid enlarges (goitre) and metabolism slows; severe childhood deficiency causes cretinism. The public-health fix — iodised common salt — is one of chemistry's cheapest victories.

    4.3 Iron and cobalt — the two anaemias

    • Iron is the O₂-binding atom of haem; deficiency lowers haemoglobin → anaemia (pallor, fatigue). Cobalt is the metal of vitamin B₁₂, which red-blood-cell precursors need to divide properly; its deficiency gives pernicious anaemia — a different anaemia with a different metal.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Macro-nutrient ≠ protein/carb/fat here: in this chemistry unit "macro-nutrients" means the mineral elements Ca–Mg, not the energy foods; the food-group sense belongs to biology.

  • Cobalt deficiency causes anaemia indirectly: Co is not in haemoglobin — it is in vitamin B₁₂, which is needed to make red blood cells. Iron anaemia and B₁₂ (pernicious) anaemia are different diseases.
  • Iodine is a trace element, not a macro-mineral: at ~150 µg/day it is one of the smallest requirements in the table — easy to misplace because goitre is such a large disease.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB 2024 (verified PYQ): What is the role of iodine in the human body? What disorder does its deficiency cause? — Iodine is a trace element essential for the synthesis of the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T₄) and tri-iodothyronine (T₃), which regulate the body's metabolic rate, growth and nervous development. The thyroid gland concentrates dietary iodine and incorporates it into these hormones. Deficiency disorder: when iodine is lacking the thyroid cannot make enough thyroxine; the gland enlarges in compensation, producing goitre — a visible swelling at the base of the neck. Prolonged deficiency also causes hypothyroidism, and in children severe deficiency causes cretinism (stunted growth and mental retardation). Preventive measure: iodised common salt (KIO₃/KI added to NaCl).

  • NEB 2022 (verified PYQ, MCQ): Which of the following trace elements is essential for the formation of haemoglobin in the human body? (a) zinc (b) copper (c) iron (d) iodine — (c) iron. Fe²⁺ is the oxygen-binding metal at the centre of each haem group of haemoglobin; without adequate dietary iron the blood cannot make enough haemoglobin and iron-deficiency anaemia results. Zinc and copper are essential trace elements with other roles (metalloenzymes; haemocyanin/cytochrome oxidase), and iodine is needed for thyroxine — none of them sits in the haem group.
  • NEB-style (2m): Name the vitamin that contains cobalt and the disease caused by its deficiency. — Vitamin B₁₂ (cyanocobalamin) contains cobalt at the centre of a corrin ring. Its deficiency causes pernicious anaemia — defective maturation of red blood cells — because B₁₂ is required for red-cell formation; the vitamin must ultimately come from the diet (or from microorganisms), since the body cannot build the cobalt complex.

Universal Facts

  • Iron is the most abundant trace metal in the human body (~4 g), almost all of it in haemoglobin and myoglobin; the body recycles it so efficiently that daily dietary need is only about 1-2 mg.

Examples

  • Iodised salt, chemistry's cheapest public-health win — adding a pinch of KIO3/KI to NaCl supplies the ~150 ug of iodine the thyroid needs and removes goitre from whole countries.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (verified PYQ): What is the role of iodine in the human body? What disorder does its deficiency cause? — Iodine is a trace element required for the synthesis of the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T₄) and tri-iodothyronine (T₃), which control the metabolic rate, growth and nervous development. The thyroid concentrates dietary iodine and builds it into these hormones. Deficiency: without iodine the thyroid cannot synthesise enough thyroxine and enlarges in compensation, giving goitre — a swelling at the base of the neck; long-term deficiency also causes hypothyroidism and, in children, cretinism. Prevention: iodised common salt (KI/KIO₃ mixed with NaCl) supplies the ~150 µg/day the body needs.
  • NEB 2022 (verified PYQ, MCQ): Which of the following trace elements is essential for the formation of haemoglobin in the human body? (a) zinc (b) copper (c) iron (d) iodine — (c) iron. Each of the four haem groups of haemoglobin carries an Fe²⁺ ion that reversibly binds one O₂ molecule; iron deficiency therefore lowers haemoglobin production and causes iron-deficiency anaemia (pallor, fatigue, breathlessness). Zinc (metalloenzymes), copper (haemocyanin, cytochrome oxidase) and iodine (thyroxine) are essential for other jobs and do not sit in the haem group.

Phosphorus — bones (with Ca), ATP and nucleic acids. Potassium — principal intracellular cation, nerve impulses, enzyme activation. Sodium — principal extracellular cation, fluid balance, nerve impulses. Chlorine — gastric HCl, electrolyte balance. Magnesium — chlorophyll in plants; enzyme activator in animals. Sulphur — sulphur amino acids (cysteine, methionine) in proteins.

Key Points

  • Macro-nutrients: needed >100 mg/day — Ca, P, K, S, Na, Cl, Mg (Ca bones/clotting, P ATP/DNA, K-Na nerve impulses, Cl HCl, Mg chlorophyll, S proteins). Micro-nutrients: <100 mg/day — Fe, Zn, Cu, I, Co, Mn, Mo, Cr, Se, F. Deficiency diseases: Fe->anaemia, I->goitre (thyroxine, ~150 ug/day, iodised salt), Co (B12 cyanocobalamin)->pernicious anaemia, Mg in soil->chlorosis, F->dental caries, Cr->glucose tolerance factor. Zn: carbonic anhydrase, insulin storage. Cu: haemocyanin, cytochrome oxidase. Mo: nitrogenase.

Summary

The mineral diet splits by daily requirement. Macro-nutrients (>100 mg/day): Ca, P, K, S, Na, Cl, Mg — structure (Ca/P bones), electrolytes (Na/K/Cl nerves and fluids), chlorophyll (Mg) and proteins (S). Micro-nutrients (<100 mg/day): Fe, Zn, Cu, I, Co, Mn, Mo, Cr, Se, F — the catalytic and hormonal tier. The deficiency diseases are the exam's favourite table: iron → anaemia, iodine → goitre (thyroxine needs ~150 µg/day; iodised salt prevents it), cobalt → pernicious anaemia (vitamin B₁₂, cyanocobalamin), magnesium in soil → chlorosis, fluorine → dental caries, chromium → impaired glucose tolerance. Quantity differs a million-fold between the tiers; importance does not.
