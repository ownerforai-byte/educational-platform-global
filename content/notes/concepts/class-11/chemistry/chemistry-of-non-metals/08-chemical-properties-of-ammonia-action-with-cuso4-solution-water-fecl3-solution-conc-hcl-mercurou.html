Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Gas That Feeds and Chokes

    Ammonia is nitrogen's most useful compound — fertiliser feedstock, refrigerant, laboratory base — and its chemistry with CuSO₄, water, FeCl₃, concentrated HCl, mercurous nitrate paper and oxygen is a standard NEB checklist. This topic runs the reaction list, then weighs its applications against its hazards.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Preparation and structure

    • Laboratory: heating an ammonium salt with an alkali: 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃ (dried over quicklime CaO — never conc. H₂SO₄, which absorbs it).
    • Industrial (Haber): N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, 450 °C, 200 atm, Fe catalyst.
    • Structure: pyramidal, sp³ nitrogen with one lone pair, bond angle 107°; the lone pair drives every reaction — basicity, complexation, hydrogen bonding (bp −33 °C, unusually high for its mass).

    2.3 Applications

    • Fertilisers: urea, ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate, ammonium phosphate — the largest use (~80 % of production).
    • Refrigerant: large latent heat of vaporisation (1370 kJ/kg at bp), non-CFC — cold-storage plants and ice plants.
    • Chemical feedstock: nitric acid (Ostwald), ammonium nitrate (explosives), sodium carbonate (Solvay), nylon via caprolactam.
    • Laboratory reagent: NH₄OH in qualitative analysis (precipitating and complexing hydroxides).

    2.4 Harmful effects

    • Toxicity: irritates eyes and respiratory tract; 2500+ ppm life-threatening; suffocating in enclosed spaces.
    • Environmental: ammonia run-off from fertilisers causes eutrophication of water bodies; volatilised NH₃ contributes to acid deposition (NH₄⁺ → NOₓ chemistry) and PM2.5.
    • Explosion risk: NH₃–air mixtures (15–28 % NH₃) are flammable; refrigeration leaks dangerous.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The CuSO₄ two-step

    • NH₄OH first precipitates pale-blue Cu(OH)₂; excess ammonia then dissolves it as the deep-blue tetraammine complex [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ — the solubility-in-excess is the distinguishing observation (contrast Fe(OH)₃, insoluble in excess).

    4.2 Why the HCl white ring works

    • NH₃ and HCl gases meet and form fine NH₄Cl particles — a solid aerosol, seen as white smoke. Glass rod dipped in concentrated NH₄OH near open HCl bottle: instant white fumes — the classic ammonia test.

    4.3 The two oxygen reactions

    • Without catalyst NH₃ burns in O₂ with a yellowish-green flame to N₂: 4NH₃ + 3O₂ → 2N₂ + 6H₂O.
    • Over heated platinum (Ostwald step), oxidation goes to NO: 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O — the gateway to nitric acid (topic 65).

    4.4 The mercurous nitrate test

    • Ammonia blackens mercurous nitrate paper: Hg₂²⁺ is reduced/aminated to finely divided black nitrogen-containing mercury compounds (Millon's base chemistry, Hg₂N•H₂O related species) — a sensitive confirmatory test.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Excess reagent matters: with CuSO₄, "blue precipitate" is only half the story — the deep-blue solution in excess NH₄OH is the observation that earns the complex-ion mark.

  • Drying agent choice: drying NH₃ over conc. H₂SO₄ or CaCl₂ destroys it (both absorb it); only quicklime CaO works — a permanent NEB trap.
  • The two O₂ products: uncatalysed burning gives N₂; catalytic (Pt) oxidation gives NO — quoting the wrong one for the wrong condition costs the equation.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What happens when ammonia is passed into copper sulphate solution — first in limited and then in excess amount? Give equations. — Limited ammonia (as NH₄OH): the hydroxide it generates precipitates pale-blue copper(II) hydroxide: CuSO₄ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + (NH₄)₂SO₄. Excess ammonia: the precipitate dissolves because ammonia ligands replace water around Cu²⁺, forming the deep-blue soluble complex tetraamminecopper(II) sulphate: Cu(OH)₂ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄. The final deep-blue clear solution is the diagnostic observation distinguishing copper from iron(III), whose brown hydroxide stays insoluble in excess.

  • NEB-style (2m): A glass rod dipped in concentrated ammonium hydroxide brought near the mouth of a bottle of concentrated hydrochloric acid produces white fumes. Explain with equation. — Ammonia vapour from the rod meets hydrogen chloride vapour from the bottle; they combine directly to form minute solid particles of ammonium chloride, seen as dense white fumes (and as the "white ring" in the gas-jar demonstration): NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s). This is a standard confirmatory test for ammonia gas.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is ammonia used as a refrigerant? State two of its harmful effects. — Refrigerant: ammonia has a very high latent heat of vaporisation (about 1370 kJ/kg at its boiling point, −33 °C), so it absorbs large amounts of heat when it evaporates — ideal for the compression–expansion refrigeration cycle; it is also cheap and free of ozone-depleting chlorine, which is why cold-storage and ice plants still use it. Harmful effects: (1) it is toxic and irritating — even moderate concentrations attack the eyes and respiratory tract, and 15–28 % mixtures with air are flammable; (2) environmentally, ammonia run-off and volatilisation from fertilisers cause eutrophication of lakes and contribute to acidification and secondary particulate (PM2.5) formation.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): What happens when ammonia is passed into copper sulphate solution, first in limited amount and then in excess? Give equations and observations. — Limited ammonia (added as ammonium hydroxide): the OH⁻ ions generated by NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ precipitate pale-blue copper(II) hydroxide: CuSO₄ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + (NH₄)₂SO₄ — a pale blue precipitate forms. Excess ammonia: ammonia molecules act as ligands, coordinating to Cu²⁺ and dissolving the hydroxide as the deep-blue complex tetraamminecopper(II) sulphate: Cu(OH)₂ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ — the precipitate disappears and the solution turns an intense royal blue. This "dissolves in excess to deep blue" behaviour is diagnostic for copper(II) ions; by contrast, the reddish-brown Fe(OH)₃ obtained from FeCl₃ + 3NH₄OH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NH₄Cl remains insoluble in excess ammonia.
  • NEB-style (2m): A glass rod dipped in concentrated ammonium hydroxide and brought near the mouth of a bottle of concentrated hydrochloric acid produces dense white fumes. Explain with an equation. — The rod releases ammonia vapour and the bottle releases hydrogen chloride vapour; where the two gases meet they combine directly to form extremely fine solid particles of ammonium chloride, which scatter light and appear as dense white fumes (in a gas jar the same chemistry draws the famous "white ring"): NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s). Because no other common gas gives this instant white smoke with HCl vapour, it serves as a confirmatory test for ammonia.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is ammonia used as a refrigerant in cold-storage plants? Mention two harmful effects of ammonia. — Refrigerant use: ammonia has an exceptionally high latent heat of vaporisation — about 1370 kJ/kg at its normal boiling point of −33 °C — so each kilogram evaporating absorbs a large quantity of heat, making the compression–expansion cycle highly effective; it is also cheap, chemically stable in the cycle, and contains no chlorine, so unlike CFCs it does not deplete the ozone layer — reasons it remains the working fluid of industrial ice plants and cold storages. Harmful effects: (1) Toxicity and flammability: ammonia strongly irritates the eyes, skin and respiratory tract (concentrations above ~2500 ppm are life-threatening), and mixtures with air containing 15–28 % ammonia are flammable, so leaks are dangerous. (2) Environmental damage: ammonia lost from over-applied fertilisers runs into water bodies causing eutrophication (algal blooms, oxygen depletion), and volatilised NH₃ contributes to soil acidification and to secondary fine particulate (PM2.5) formation in the atmosphere.

Summary

Ammonia is the lone pair with a résumé. Pyramidal, sp³, 107°, with bp −33 °C and a refrigerant-grade latent heat of 1370 kJ/kg, it donates that pair to H⁺ (alkaline solution, fountain experiment, red-litmus-blue), to HCl vapour (the white NH₄Cl fumes of the rod-and-bottle test), and to Cu²⁺ — where the NEB checklist unfolds in two acts: CuSO₄ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ (pale blue) then Cu(OH)₂ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ (deep blue, dissolves in excess) — while FeCl₃ gives its brown Fe(OH)₃ that stubbornly stays. It blackens mercurous nitrate paper (Millon's base chemistry) and meets oxygen twice: uncatalysed as the yellow-green flame to N₂ (4NH₃ + 3O₂ → 2N₂ + 6H₂O), catalysed over platinum at 800 °C to NO (4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O) — the Ostwald gateway to nitric acid. Made in the lab from NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ (dried over quicklime only — H₂SO₄ and CaCl₂ eat it) and industrially by Haber, its uses span fertilisers (~80 %), refrigeration, Solvay soda and nylon; its harms are toxicity and flammability (15–28 % in air) plus eutrophication and PM2.5 — the gas that feeds and chokes in the same breath.

Give equations and observations. — Limited ammonia (added as ammonium hydroxide): the OH⁻ ions generated by NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ precipitate pale-blue copper(II) hydroxide: CuSO₄ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + (NH₄)₂SO₄ — a pale blue precipitate forms. Excess ammonia: ammonia molecules act as ligands, coordinating to Cu²⁺ and dissolving the hydroxide as the deep-blue complex tetraamminecopper(II) sulphate: Cu(OH)₂ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ — the precipitate disappears and the solution turns an intense royal blue. This "dissolves in excess to deep blue" behaviour is diagnostic for copper(II) ions; by contrast, the reddish-brown Fe(OH)₃ obtained from FeCl₃ + 3NH₄OH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NH₄Cl remains insoluble in excess ammonia.
  • NEB-style (2m): A glass rod dipped in concentrated ammonium hydroxide and brought near the mouth of a bottle of concentrated hydrochloric acid produces dense white fumes. Explain with an equation. — The rod releases ammonia vapour and the bottle releases hydrogen chloride vapour; where the two gases meet they combine directly to form extremely fine solid particles of ammonium chloride, which scatter light and appear as dense white fumes (in a gas jar the same chemistry draws the famous "white ring"): NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s). Because no other common gas gives this instant white smoke with HCl vapour, it serves as a confirmatory test for ammonia.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is ammonia used as a refrigerant in cold-storage plants? Mention two harmful effects of ammonia. — Refrigerant use: ammonia has an exceptionally high latent heat of vaporisation — about 1370 kJ/kg at its normal boiling point of −33 °C — so each kilogram evaporating absorbs a large quantity of heat, making the compression–expansion cycle highly effective; it is also cheap, chemically stable in the cycle, and contains no chlorine, so unlike CFCs it does not deplete the ozone layer — reasons it remains the working fluid of industrial ice plants and cold storages. Harmful effects: (1) Toxicity and flammability: ammonia strongly irritates the eyes, skin and respiratory tract (concentrations above ~2500 ppm are life-threatening), and mixtures with air containing 15–28 % ammonia are flammable, so leaks are dangerous. (2) Environmental damage: ammonia lost from over-applied fertilisers runs into water bodies causing eutrophication (algal blooms, oxygen depletion), and volatilised NH₃ contributes to soil acidification and to secondary fine particulate (PM2.5) formation in the atmosphere.

Important Concepts

  • One lone pair runs ammonia's chemistry: it grabs H⁺ (basicity), grabs Cu²⁺ (the deep-blue complex), and hydrogen-bonds to itself (the anomalously high boiling point and the refrigerant-grade latent heat).

Important Statements

  • "Ammonia turns moist red litmus blue and forms dense white fumes with hydrogen chloride gas."
