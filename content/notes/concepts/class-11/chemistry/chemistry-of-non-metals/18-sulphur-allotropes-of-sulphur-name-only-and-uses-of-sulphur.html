  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Yellow Element of Matches and Medicine

    Sulphur's allotropes (names only per NEB) and its uses: rhombic, monoclinic, plastic (amorphous), plus milk of sulphur and colloidal sulphur.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The allotropes (names only, with essentials)

    AllotropeAppearanceStructureKey facts
    Rhombic (α) sulphurpale yellow crystals, octahedral ("dandelion" shape)discrete S₈ puckered rings (crown)stable below 96 °C; insoluble in water, soluble in CS₂; density 2.07
    Monoclinic (β) sulphuramber needle crystalsS₈ rings, different packingstable 96–119 °C; mp 119 °C; both revert to rhombic below 96 °C
    Plastic (γ, amorphous) sulphurrubbery dark brown masszig-zag supercooled chainsmade by pouring molten sulphur (boiling, ~250 °C+) into cold water; reverts to rhombic on standing
    Milk of sulphurwhite powderfine precipitated Sfrom boiling S with Ca(OH)₂ then acidifying; medical (skin)
    Colloidal sulphurcolloidal solnanodispersionfrom H₂S + oxidants (e.g. Br₂ water) or Na₂S₂O₃ + acid; used as fungicide/medicine

    2.2 Transition temperature and ring–chain story

    • 96 °C: rhombic ⇌ monoclinic transition temperature — below it rhombic is stable, above it monoclinic.
    • Molten sulphur's viscosity drama: S₈ rings → ring opening at ~160 °C → long chains entangle (viscosity maximum ~200 °C, dark) → chains shorten near boiling (444 °C bp). Pouring the viscous melt into water traps the chains: plastic sulphur.

    2.3 Uses of sulphur

    • Vulcanisation of rubber (cross-linking polyisoprene); H₂SO₄ manufacture (contact process feed); sulphur dioxide for bleaching/fumigation/refrigeration; gunpowder (S + KNO₃ + charcoal); matches, fireworks; medicines (skin ointments, milk of sulphur); CS₂ solvent manufacture; insecticides/fungicides (sulphur dust, lime sulphur); carbon disulphide and sulphur dyes.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why two crystalline forms with one ring

    • Both rhombic and monoclinic contain S₈ rings; they differ in how the rings pack. Packing energy differences are tiny, so the stability flips at 96 °C — a textbook case of enthalpy vs entropy balance at a transition temperature.

    4.2 The viscosity mountain

    • Ring-opening polymerisation at ~160 °C builds long chains: viscosity climbs to a maximum near 200 °C (dark, treacle). Beyond that, thermal scission shortens chains and viscosity falls again toward boiling. Quenching freezes the chains — plastic sulphur — which slowly re-crystallises to rhombic (the stable low-T form).

    4.3 Why vulcanisation transformed transport

    • Raw rubber is sticky and soft; sulphur cross-links (C–S–S–C bridges) lock the polymer chains together: elasticity with strength, temperature-stable. 3–5% S for flexible rubber, ~30% for hard ebonite. The tyre industry is built on this reaction.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "Rhombic and monoclinic have different molecules." Both are S₈ rings — only the crystal packing differs.
    • "Plastic sulphur is a new compound." It is supercooled polymeric sulphur, reverting to rhombic in days — metastable, not permanent.
    • "The transition temperature is the melting point." 96 °C is the α⇌β equilibrium temperature; melting is at 119 °C (monoclinic).

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Name the allotropes of sulphur and distinguish rhombic from monoclinic sulphur. — Allotropes: rhombic (α), monoclinic (β), plastic (amorphous) sulphur; additionally milk of sulphur and colloidal sulphur as fine-particle forms. Rhombic (α): pale yellow octahedral crystals of S₈ crown-shaped rings; the stable form below 96 °C; density 2.07 g/cm³; soluble in CS₂. Monoclinic (β): amber needle crystals, also S₈ rings but packed differently; stable between 96 °C and its melting point 119 °C; density 1.98 g/cm³. The two interconvert at the transition temperature 96 °C — below it rhombic is thermodynamically stable, above it monoclinic. Both revert to rhombic on long standing at room temperature. Plastic sulphur: rubbery brown supercooled chains, made by pouring molten sulphur into cold water; metastable, slowly reverts to rhombic.
  • NEB-style (2m): Describe what happens to the viscosity of molten sulphur as it is heated from its melting point to boiling. — Just above the melting point the melt is a mobile liquid of S₈ rings. Around 160 °C the rings begin to open and polymerise into long chains, which entangle: the viscosity rises steeply, reaching a maximum near 200 °C where the melt is dark and treacle-like. Above that, thermal energy breaks the chains into shorter fragments, and the viscosity falls again until the liquid boils at 444 °C. If the viscous melt is poured into cold water, the chains are frozen in as plastic sulphur.
  • NEB-style (2m): State four important uses of sulphur. — (1) Vulcanisation of rubber: sulphur cross-links the polymer chains of raw rubber (3–5 % for flexible rubber, ~30 % for hard ebonite), converting sticky gum into durable, elastic material — the basis of the tyre industry. (2) Manufacture of sulphuric acid: sulphur is burned to SO₂, the feedstock of the contact process. (3) SO₂ production for bleaching silk/wool, fumigation and refrigeration. (4) Gunpowder and fireworks: the classic S + KNO₃ + charcoal mixture. (Other acceptable uses: skin ointments/milk of sulphur, carbon disulphide solvent, sulphur dyes, fungicides.)

Key Points

  • Sulphur allotropes (names only): RHOMBIC (alpha) - pale yellow octahedral crystals, S8 crown rings, stable below 96 C, CS2-soluble, density 2.07. MONOCLINIC (beta) - amber needles, S8 rings packed differently, stable 96-119 C, mp 119 C. PLASTIC (amorphous) - rubbery brown supercooled chains, made by pouring molten S (~250 C) into cold water, reverts to rhombic. MILK OF SULPHUR - fine white precipitated S (boil S with Ca(OH)2, acidify); medical. COLLOIDAL SULPHUR - sol from H2S + oxidant or Na2S2O3 + acid; fungicide/medicine. Transition temperature 96 C (alpha <=> beta). Viscosity: rings -> chains ~160 C, max ~200 C, falls to bp 444 C. Uses: vulcanisation (3-5% S flexible rubber, ~30% ebonite), H2SO4 feedstock, SO2 bleaching/fumigation, gunpowder (S + KNO3 + C), matches/fireworks, skin medicines, CS2, sulphur dyes, fungicides.

Summary

Sulphur's allotropy is a story of one ring and three fates. Rhombic (α) sulphur — yellow octahedra of S₈ crown rings — rules below 96 °C; monoclinic (β) sulphur — amber needles of the same rings, differently packed — rules from 96 °C to its 119 °C melting point; the transition temperature 96 °C is their handshake. Melt either and the rings survive until ~160 °C, then open into entangled chains that treacle the liquid at 200 °C before scission frees it again toward the 444 °C boil; quench that melt in cold water and plastic sulphur — rubbery, metastable chains — is born, slowly crumbling back to rhombic. Fine-particle cousins: milk of sulphur (medical) and colloidal sulphur (fungicide). Uses: vulcanisation (3–5 % S for tyres, ~30 % for ebonite), H₂SO₄ manufacture, SO₂ for bleaching and fumigation, gunpowder, matches, medicines, CS₂ and sulphur dyes.
