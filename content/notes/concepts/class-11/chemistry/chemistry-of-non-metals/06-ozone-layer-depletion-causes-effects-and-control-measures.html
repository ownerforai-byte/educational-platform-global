  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Hole We Made and Are Closing

    The ozone layer's depletion is chemistry's best-known environmental lesson: CFCs — once celebrated as miracle refrigerants — drift to the stratosphere, surrender chlorine atoms to UV, and each chlorine atom destroys tens of thousands of ozone molecules. This topic covers the causes, the Antarctic hole's mechanism, the effects, and the Montreal Protocol's control measures.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The natural ozone balance

    • Formation: O₂ + UV (< 242 nm) → 2O; O + O₂ → O₃.
    • Destruction: O₃ + UV → O₂ + O; O₃ + O → 2O₂.
    • In the unpolluted stratosphere formation and destruction balance — a steady ~300 DU (Dobson units) column.

    2.2 Causes of depletion

    • CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons, freons: CFCl₃, CF₂Cl₂): inert in the troposphere (that was their selling point), but UV in the stratosphere breaks the C–Cl bond: CFCl₃ + UV → CFCl₂• + Cl•.
    • The catalytic cycle: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂; ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂. Net: O₃ + O → 2O₂ — the chlorine atom is regenerated, so one Cl destroys ~100,000 O₃ molecules before it is sequestered (as ClONO₂ reservoirs).
    • Other culprits: halons (bromine — even more efficient per atom), methyl bromide (pesticides), N₂O (nitrous oxide from fertilisers → NO catalytic cycle), carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform.

    2.3 The Antarctic ozone hole

    • Polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) form in the Antarctic winter's −80 °C vortex; they convert inactive reservoirs (ClONO₂, HCl) into active Cl₂; spring sunlight (September–October) photolyses Cl₂ → 2Cl• — the hole opens.
    • Discovered by Farman et al. (1985, British Antarctic Survey); column ozone fell below 100 DU at the minimum.

    2.4 Effects

    • Human health: more UV-B → skin cancers (melanoma), cataracts, immune suppression.
    • Ecosystems: phytoplankton damage (marine food web base), crop yield losses, polymer degradation.
    • Climate interaction: CFCs are also potent greenhouse gases (thousands of times CO₂'s warming power per kg).

    2.5 Control measures

    • Montreal Protocol (1987): phase-out schedule for CFCs and halons — universally ratified; amended (London, Copenhagen, Kigali) to cover HCFCs and HFCs.
    • Replacements: HCFCs (transitional, lower ozone depletion potential), HFCs (zero ODP but greenhouse — Kigali 2016 phases them down), and newer options (HFO-1234yf, hydrocarbons, CO₂ systems, ammonia in industry).
    • Individual/national measures: recover and recycle CFC refrigerants, ban CFC production, monitor with satellites (TOMS/OMI), halon banking.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why the catalytic cycle is so lethal

    • The Cl• + O₃ and ClO• + O steps are both fast and both regenerate the chlorine radical — a true catalyst of ozone destruction. Compare: natural cycles destroy ozone slowly; each chlorine atom multiplies the destruction by ~100,000× over its stratospheric lifetime (1–2 years before conversion to HCl reservoirs).

    4.2 Why the hole is Antarctic

    • Only the Antarctic gets the cold isolated vortex + PSCs + spring sunlight sequence. The Arctic's vortex is weaker and warmer, so depletion there is milder (though 2011 and 2020 saw Arctic "mini-holes").

    4.3 The recovery scoreboard

    • CFC atmospheric concentrations peaked ~1994 and are declining; the hole's September minimum shows slow healing (projected full recovery ~2066 for Antarctica); the 2023-published confirmation of declining CFC-11 attributed a bump to illegal East Asian production (now curbed).

  • 6. Common Confusions

    The chlorine atom is recycled, not consumed: the classic error is writing Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂ as the end — the second step regenerating Cl• is what makes the cycle catalytic.

  • The hole is thinning, not a literal hole: "hole" means column ozone below ~220 DU — the layer thins dramatically; it does not vanish to zero.
  • Ozone depletion ≠ global warming: different mechanisms, though CFCs contribute to both; HFCs warm without depleting — conflating them loses analytical marks.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What are CFCs? Explain with equations how they deplete the ozone layer. — CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons, freons) such as CFCl₃ and CF₂Cl₂ are fully halogenated organic compounds once used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants and foam-blowing agents; they are chemically inert in the lower atmosphere and therefore survive to reach the stratosphere. Depletion: intense UV there breaks the C–Cl bond: CFCl₃ + UV → CFCl₂• + Cl•. The chlorine radical then destroys ozone catalytically: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, and ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂. Summing: O₃ + O → 2O₂ — ozone is destroyed while the chlorine atom is regenerated, so a single Cl atom can destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules before being locked away in reservoirs such as ClONO₂.

  • NEB-style (2m): State three harmful effects of ozone depletion and two control measures. — Effects: (1) increased UV-B reaching the ground raises skin cancers (including melanoma) and eye cataracts; (2) damage to phytoplankton disturbs marine food chains and lowers crop yields; (3) faster degradation of plastics, paints and polymers outdoors. Control measures: (1) the Montreal Protocol (1987) and its amendments — international phase-out of CFC and halon production; (2) substitution by safe alternatives — HCFCs/HFCs transitionally and now HFOs, hydrocarbons, CO₂ or ammonia systems — plus recovery and recycling of old refrigerants rather than venting.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is the ozone hole found over Antarctica? — The Antarctic winter creates an isolated, extremely cold (−80 °C) circumpolar vortex in which polar stratospheric clouds form; their ice surfaces convert the inactive chlorine reservoirs (ClONO₂, HCl) into molecular chlorine (Cl₂). When spring sunlight returns (September–October), Cl₂ is rapidly photolysed into chlorine radicals, which destroy ozone in the catalytic cycle — hence the deep, seasonal hole appears over Antarctica and not elsewhere.

Confusion

  • ❌ All cells look the same under a microscope. ✅ Prokaryotic cells lack membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotic cells have them.
  • ❌ DNA and RNA are identical in structure. ✅ DNA is double-stranded with deoxyribose and thymine; RNA is single-stranded with ribose and uracil.
  • ❌ Mitosis produces genetically different cells. ✅ Mitosis produces genetically identical diploid daughter cells.
  • ❌ Meiosis produces identical cells. ✅ Meiosis produces 4 genetically diverse haploid gametes.
  • ❌ Crossing over occurs in mitosis. ✅ Crossing over occurs in Prophase I of meiosis only.
  • ❌ All enzymes are proteins. ✅ Most enzymes are proteins; some RNA molecules (ribozymes) also have catalytic activity.
  • ❌ ATP is the only energy currency. ✅ GTP, UTP, and CTP also serve as energy carriers in specific pathways.
  • ❌ Photosynthesis occurs in all plant cells. ✅ Photosynthesis occurs only in cells containing chloroplasts (mainly mesophyll).
  • ❌ Respiration and photosynthesis are opposite processes with same equation. ✅ They are complementary but occur in different organelles and under different conditions.
  • ❌ Oxygen is produced during respiration. ✅ Oxygen is consumed in aerobic respiration; produced during photosynthesis.
  • ❌ Food chain shows all feeding relationships. ✅ Food chain is linear; food web shows interconnected feeding relationships.
  • ❌ 90% of energy is transferred to next trophic level. ✅ Only ~10% energy transfers; ~90% lost as heat.
  • ❌ Biomagnification decreases toxin concentration up the food chain. ✅ Biomagnification increases toxin concentration at higher trophic levels.
  • ❌ Carbon cycle only involves plants and animals. ✅ Carbon cycle involves atmosphere, oceans, fossils, rocks, and decomposers.
  • ❌ Nitrogen fixation only occurs in bacteria. ✅ Some lightning and industrial processes (Haber) also fix nitrogen.
  • ❌ All fungi are harmful. ✅ Many fungi are beneficial (decomposers, mycorrhizae, food, antibiotics like penicillin).
  • ❌ Viruses are living organisms. ✅ Viruses are acellular — they cannot reproduce without a host and lack metabolism.
  • ❌ Bacteria are always harmful. ✅ Most bacteria are harmless or beneficial (gut flora, nitrogen fixation, decomposition).
  • ❌ Gram-positive and Gram-negative differ only in color. ✅ Gram-positive has thick peptidoglycan layer; Gram-negative has thin layer + outer membrane.
  • ❌ Natural selection creates perfect organisms. ✅ Natural selection favors traits suited to current environment; environments change.
  • ❌ Evolution is goal-oriented. ✅ Evolution has no goal; it acts on random variation filtered by selection.
  • ❌ Homologous and analogous structures mean the same thing. ✅ Homologous = common ancestry (divergent); Analogous = similar function, different origin (convergent).
  • ❌ Fossils are preserved bones only. ✅ Fossils include impressions, trace fossils, amber-preserved organisms, and molecular fossils.
  • ❌ Lamarckism is completely wrong. ✅ Lamarck's inheritance of acquired characteristics is largely rejected, but epigenetics shows some environmental effects can be inherited.
  • ❌ Five-kingdom classification is outdated. ✅ Six-kingdom (adding Archaea) and phylogenetic systems are more current, but five-kingdom remains useful for education.
  • ❌ Binomial nomenclature names change frequently. ✅ Names are stable once formally published; changes require formal revision by taxonomic authorities.
  • ❌ All algae are aquatic. ✅ Some algae live in soil, on tree bark, or in symbiotic relationships (lichens).
  • ❌ Bryophytes have vascular tissue. ✅ Bryophytes (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) lack true vascular tissue (xylem/phloem).
  • ❌ Gymnosperms produce flowers. ✅ Gymnosperms have naked seeds in cones; angiosperms produce flowers and enclosed seeds.
  • ❌ Double fertilization occurs in gymnosperms. ✅ Double fertilization (zygote + endosperm) is unique to angiosperms.
  • ❌ Earthworms have an open circulatory system. ✅ Earthworms have a closed circulatory system with dorsal and ventral vessels.
  • ❌ Frogs breathe only through lungs. ✅ Frogs use cutaneous respiration (skin), buccal cavity, and lungs.
  • ❌ Conservation only means protecting animals. ✅ Conservation includes genetic, species, and ecosystem diversity; in-situ and ex-situ strategies.
  • ❌ In-situ and ex-situ conservation are the same. ✅ In-situ = conservation in natural habitat (national parks); Ex-situ = conservation outside habitat (zoos, seed banks).

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): What are CFCs? Explain, with equations, how they deplete the ozone layer. — CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons or freons) — compounds such as CFCl₃ (freon-11) and CF₂Cl₂ (freon-12) in which hydrogen of the alkane is fully replaced by chlorine and fluorine — were widely used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants, foam-blowing agents and solvents. Their very chemical inertness in the lower atmosphere lets them survive unreacted for years and slowly diffuse up to the stratosphere. Depletion mechanism: in the stratosphere, intense ultraviolet radiation breaks the weakest bond, C–Cl: CFCl₃ + UV → CFCl₂• + Cl•. The chlorine radical then destroys ozone in a two-step catalytic cycle: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, followed by ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂. Adding the two steps gives the net destruction O₃ + O → 2O₂. The chlorine atom is regenerated in the second step, so it acts as a catalyst: a single Cl atom destroys on the order of 100,000 ozone molecules before it is finally locked into inactive reservoirs such as ClONO₂ or HCl. Bromine radicals from halons and methyl bromide act through an analogous, even more efficient cycle.
  • NEB-style (2m): State three harmful effects of ozone depletion and two measures to control it. — Effects: (1) Human health: increased UV-B at the surface raises the incidence of skin cancers (including malignant melanoma), eye cataracts and suppression of the immune system. (2) Ecosystems: UV-B damages phytoplankton — the base of marine food chains — and reduces yields of sensitive crops such as rice and soybean. (3) Materials: outdoor plastics, paints, rubber and polymers degrade faster, shortening their service life. Control measures: (1) International regulation: the Montreal Protocol (1987) and its amendments, which mandated the phase-out of CFC and halon production — the first treaty ratified by every country. (2) Substitution and recovery: replacing CFCs first with HCFCs (lower ozone-depletion potential) and then with zero-ODP alternatives (HFCs, and now low-GWP HFOs, hydrocarbons, CO₂ or ammonia systems), while recovering and recycling refrigerant from old equipment instead of venting it.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is severe ozone depletion observed over Antarctica rather than over the tropics, where most CFCs were released? — Depletion requires not just chlorine but a mechanism to activate it. During the Antarctic winter, a circumpolar wind vortex isolates the stratosphere and cools it below −80 °C, allowing polar stratospheric clouds to form. Reactions on their ice surfaces convert the inactive chlorine reservoirs (ClONO₂ and HCl) into molecular chlorine: ClONO₂ + HCl → Cl₂ + HNO₃. When the spring sun returns (September–October), Cl₂ is rapidly split into chlorine radicals, which destroy ozone in the catalytic cycle — producing the dramatic seasonal "hole". The tropics lack these cold, isolated clouds, so chlorine there stays mostly locked in reservoirs; the CFCs' origin is irrelevant — stratospheric circulation delivers them everywhere.

Practice Questions
  • NEB-style (3m): What are CFCs? Explain, with equations, how they deplete the ozone layer. — CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons or freons) — compounds such as CFCl₃ (freon-11) and CF₂Cl₂ (freon-12) in which hydrogen of the alkane is fully replaced by chlorine and fluorine — were widely used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants, foam-blowing agents and solvents. Their very chemical inertness in the lower atmosphere lets them survive unreacted for years and slowly diffuse up to the stratosphere. Depletion mechanism: in the stratosphere, intense ultraviolet radiation breaks the weakest bond, C–Cl: CFCl₃ + UV → CFCl₂• + Cl•. The chlorine radical then destroys ozone in a two-step catalytic cycle: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, followed by ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂. Adding the two steps gives the net destruction O₃ + O → 2O₂. The chlorine atom is regenerated in the second step, so it acts as a catalyst: a single Cl atom destroys on the order of 100,000 ozone molecules before it is finally locked into inactive reservoirs such as ClONO₂ or HCl. Bromine radicals from halons and methyl bromide act through an analogous, even more efficient cycle.
  • NEB-style (2m): State three harmful effects of ozone depletion and two measures to control it. — Effects: (1) Human health: increased UV-B at the surface raises the incidence of skin cancers (including malignant melanoma), eye cataracts and suppression of the immune system. (2) Ecosystems: UV-B damages phytoplankton — the base of marine food chains — and reduces yields of sensitive crops such as rice and soybean. (3) Materials: outdoor plastics, paints, rubber and polymers degrade faster, shortening their service life. Control measures: (1) International regulation: the Montreal Protocol (1987) and its amendments, which mandated the phase-out of CFC and halon production — the first treaty ratified by every country. (2) Substitution and recovery: replacing CFCs first with HCFCs (lower ozone-depletion potential) and then with zero-ODP alternatives (HFCs, and now low-GWP HFOs, hydrocarbons, CO₂ or ammonia systems), while recovering and recycling refrigerant from old equipment instead of venting it.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is severe ozone depletion observed over Antarctica rather than over the tropics, where most CFCs were released? — Depletion requires not just chlorine but a mechanism to activate it. During the Antarctic winter, a circumpolar wind vortex isolates the stratosphere and cools it below −80 °C, allowing polar stratospheric clouds to form. Reactions on their ice surfaces convert the inactive chlorine reservoirs (ClONO₂ and HCl) into molecular chlorine: ClONO₂ + HCl → Cl₂ + HNO₃. When the spring sun returns (September–October), Cl₂ is rapidly split into chlorine radicals, which destroy ozone in the catalytic cycle — producing the dramatic seasonal "hole". The tropics lack these cold, isolated clouds, so chlorine there stays mostly locked in reservoirs; the CFCs' origin is irrelevant — stratospheric circulation delivers them everywhere.

Summary

The ozone story is a catalytic horror story with a happy regulatory ending. CFCs — prized precisely for their inertness — ride to the stratosphere where UV cracks their C–Cl bonds; the freed Cl• strips ozone (Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂) and is regenerated by ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂, so each atom destroys ~100,000 ozone molecules — net O₃ + O → 2O₂. Halons' bromine is ~45× worse per atom; N₂O runs an NO• cousin cycle. The Antarctic hole is geography's amplifier: winter polar stratospheric clouds (−80 °C vortex) convert reservoirs ClONO₂/HCl into Cl₂, and spring sunlight detonates it, collapsing the column below 100 DU (discovered 1985, Farman). The costs: melanoma and cataracts, phytoplankton and crop losses, crumbling polymers — with CFCs doubling as potent greenhouse gases. The Montreal Protocol (1987) — universally ratified, amended through Kigali 2016 — phased out CFCs and halons, marched through HCFCs (ODP ~0.05) to HFCs (ODP 0, but high GWP) and onward to HFOs, hydrocarbons, CO₂ and ammonia; refrigerant recovery, satellite monitoring and even the catching of illegal CFC-11 production have the layer healing toward full recovery near 2066. Thin, not gone — and mending.
