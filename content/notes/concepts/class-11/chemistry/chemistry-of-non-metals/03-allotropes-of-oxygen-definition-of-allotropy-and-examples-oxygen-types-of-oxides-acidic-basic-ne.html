  • 1. Topic Introduction — One Element, Two Masks, Four Oxides

    Oxygen exists as two allotropes — the life-giving dioxygen O₂ and the pungent, reactive ozone O₃. And its compounds with other elements sort into four oxide families — acidic, basic, neutral, amphoteric (plus peroxides and mixed oxides) — a classification NEB loves because it tests both bonding and acid-base reasoning at once.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Allotropy and the two allotropes of oxygen

    • Allotropy: the existence of an element in two or more physical forms in the same physical state, differing in properties. Oxygen's allotropes: dioxygen (O₂) and ozone (O₃, trioxygen).
    • O₂: colourless, odourless, paramagnetic (two unpaired electrons), bp −183 °C; the common, stable form.
    • O₃: pale blue, pungent ("smell of a thunderstorm"), diamagnetic, bp −112 °C, unstable and strongly oxidising; prepared silently by electric discharge: 3O₂ ⇌ 2O₃ (ΔH = +142 kJ).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why ozone is the sharper oxidant

    • O₃'s third oxygen is held by a weak, delocalised bond; the molecule readily releases it: O₃ → O₂ + [O]. Nascent oxygen attacks organic double bonds, bleaches dyes, kills microbes — ozone's uses all flow from this lability (developed fully in topic 61).

    4.2 Amphoteric behaviour dissected

    • ZnO with acid: ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O (base-like, accepting O²⁻'s proton partner).
    • ZnO with alkali: ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O (acid-like, donating oxide to form zincate).
    • The same dual behaviour for Al₂O₃: AlCl₃ with acid, NaAlO₂ (aluminate) with base.

    4.3 Peroxides vs dioxides — the oxygen oxidation state test

    • In peroxides oxygen is −1 (O₂²⁻ ion, O–O single bond); in normal oxides −2; in OF₂ +2; in superoxides (KO₂) −½.
    • Test: Na₂O₂ + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂O₂ — a peroxide releases hydrogen peroxide with water/acid; a dioxide like MnO₂ does not.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    CO is not acidic: despite carbon being a non-metal, CO is a neutral oxide — it neither dissolves to an acid nor reacts with base. Classification follows behaviour, not element type.

  • Amphoteric ≠ neutral: amphoteric oxides react with both acids and bases; neutral oxides react with neither — the distinction is tested every year.
  • Peroxides are not dioxides: Na₂O₂ (peroxide, O at −1) vs NO₂ or MnO₂ (normal oxides, O at −2) — count the oxygen oxidation state before labelling.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Define allotropy. Name the allotropes of oxygen and state three points of difference between them. — Allotropy is the phenomenon in which an element exists in two or more different physical forms in the same physical state, the forms (allotropes) differing in physical and chemical properties. Allotropes of oxygen: dioxygen (O₂) and ozone (O₃, trioxygen). Differences: (1) Odour and colour: O₂ is colourless and odourless, while O₃ is pale blue with a pungent fishy smell. (2) Stability: O₂ is the stable form at ordinary conditions, whereas O₃ is unstable and readily decomposes to O₂ (2O₃ → 3O₂). (3) Oxidising power and magnetism: O₂ is only moderately oxidising and paramagnetic (two unpaired electrons), while O₃ is a very powerful oxidant and diamagnetic, with a bond order of 1.5 due to resonance.

  • NEB-style (2m): Classify the following oxides as acidic, basic, amphoteric or neutral: CO₂, Na₂O, ZnO, CO. — CO₂ — acidic (dissolves in water to give carbonic acid, reacts with bases: CO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O). Na₂O — basic (gives NaOH with water: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH). ZnO — amphoteric (ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O with acid, and ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O with base). CO — neutral (reacts with neither acids nor bases).
  • NEB-style (2m): What are mixed oxides? Give two examples with their component oxides. — Mixed oxides are oxides that behave as if two simpler oxides of the same metal in different oxidation states are combined in one crystal lattice. Examples: (1) Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) = FeO·Fe₂O₃ — iron(II) oxide combined with iron(III) oxide; (2) Pb₃O₄ (red lead) = 2PbO·PbO₂ — two parts lead(II) oxide with one part lead(IV) oxide.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Define allotropy. Name the allotropes of oxygen and give three points of difference between them. — Allotropy is the existence of an element in two or more different physical forms in the same physical state; the forms, called allotropes, differ in physical properties (and often in chemical reactivity) because their atoms are arranged or bonded differently. Allotropes of oxygen: ordinary dioxygen (O₂) and ozone (O₃, trioxygen). Three differences: (1) Appearance and odour: O₂ is a colourless, odourless gas; O₃ is a pale blue gas with a characteristic pungent, fishy smell (noticeable after thunderstorms and around electric machines). (2) Stability: O₂ is the thermodynamically stable form; O₃ is unstable and slowly decomposes to O₂ (2O₃ → 3O₂), the decomposition being catalysed by MnO₂ and many metal oxides. (3) Magnetism and oxidising power: O₂ is paramagnetic (its MO configuration leaves two unpaired electrons) and a moderate oxidising agent, while O₃ is diamagnetic with a resonance bond order of 1.5 and is a far stronger oxidant, readily liberating nascent oxygen (O₃ → O₂ + [O]) which bleaches, disinfects and oxidises iodide to iodine.
  • NEB-style (2m): Classify the following oxides as acidic, basic, amphoteric or neutral, giving one reaction for each: CO₂, Na₂O, ZnO, CO. — CO₂ — acidic oxide: it dissolves in water to form carbonic acid and neutralises bases: CO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O. Na₂O — basic oxide: it reacts with water to give the alkali sodium hydroxide: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH. ZnO — amphoteric oxide: it reacts with both an acid and a base to give salt and water: ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O and ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ (sodium zincate) + H₂O. CO — neutral oxide: it shows neither acidic nor basic character — it does not react with water, acids or alkalis under ordinary conditions.
  • NEB-style (2m): What are mixed oxides? Give two examples with their component oxides, and distinguish a peroxide from a normal oxide by oxidation state. — Mixed oxides are oxides that behave as though two simple oxides of the same metal, in different oxidation states, are combined in a single compound; they are formulated as the combination of the component oxides. Examples: (1) Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) = FeO·Fe₂O₃, iron(II) oxide combined with iron(III) oxide; (2) Pb₃O₄ (red lead) = 2PbO·PbO₂, lead(II) oxide combined with lead(IV) oxide. Peroxide vs normal oxide: in a peroxide oxygen has the oxidation state −1, present as the O₂²⁻ ion with an O–O single bond (e.g. Na₂O₂, BaO₂, H₂O₂), whereas in a normal oxide oxygen is −2 (e.g. Na₂O, CuO). A practical test: peroxides give hydrogen peroxide with dilute acid or water (Na₂O₂ + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂O₂), normal oxides do not.

Key Points

  • Allotropy: same element, different forms, same state. O2: colourless, odourless, paramagnetic, bp -183 C, stable, bond order 2. O3: pale blue, pungent, diamagnetic, bp -112 C, unstable (3O2 = 2O3, dH = +142 kJ), bond order 1.5, strong oxidant (O3 -> O2 + [O]). Oxide types: acidic (CO2, SO2, SO3, P4O10, N2O5, Cl2O7 - non-metal oxides), basic (Na2O, CaO, MgO, CuO), neutral (CO, NO, N2O), amphoteric (ZnO, Al2O3, PbO, SnO - react with both acid and base: ZnO + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2O; ZnO + 2NaOH -> Na2ZnO2 + H2O), peroxides (O2^2-, O at -1: Na2O2, BaO2, H2O2), mixed oxides (Fe3O4 = FeO.Fe2O3, Pb3O4 = 2PbO.PbO2). Period trend: basic -> amphoteric -> acidic across a period.

Summary

Oxygen's chemistry splits along two axes. Allotropy: the same element appears as stable, colourless, paramagnetic, odourless dioxygen O₂ (bond order 2, bp −183 °C) and as pale-blue, pungent, diamagnetic, unstable ozone O₃ (bond order 1.5, bp −112 °C, forged from 3O₂ by +142 kJ of discharge energy and forever leaking its third oxygen as [O]). Oxide classification then sorts every oxygen compound by behaviour: acidic oxides of non-metals (CO₂, SO₃, P₄O₁₀) dissolving to acids; basic oxides of metals (Na₂O, CaO) dissolving to alkalis; neutral oxides (CO, NO, N₂O) that do neither; amphoteric oxides (ZnO, Al₂O₃, PbO) that answer both acid and alkali with salt and water — ZnCl₂ one way, sodium zincate the other; peroxides carrying oxygen at −1 in the O₂²⁻ ion (Na₂O₂, H₂O₂) and surrendering H₂O₂ to water; and mixed oxides (Fe₃O₄ = FeO·Fe₂O₃, Pb₃O₄ = 2PbO·PbO₂) housing two oxidation states in one lattice. The periodic trend — basic to amphoteric to acidic across a period — is the metallic character gradient written in oxides.

  • NEB-style (2m): Classify the following oxides as acidic, basic, amphoteric or neutral, giving one reaction for each: CO₂, Na₂O, ZnO, CO. — CO₂ — acidic oxide: it dissolves in water to form carbonic acid and neutralises bases: CO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O. Na₂O — basic oxide: it reacts with water to give the alkali sodium hydroxide: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH. ZnO — amphoteric oxide: it reacts with both an acid and a base to give salt and water: ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O and ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ (sodium zincate) + H₂O. CO — neutral oxide: it shows neither acidic nor basic character — it does not react with water, acids or alkalis under ordinary conditions.
  • NEB-style (2m): What are mixed oxides? Give two examples with their component oxides, and distinguish a peroxide from a normal oxide by oxidation state. — Mixed oxides are oxides that behave as though two simple oxides of the same metal, in different oxidation states, are combined in a single compound; they are formulated as the combination of the component oxides. Examples: (1) Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) = FeO·Fe₂O₃, iron(II) oxide combined with iron(III) oxide; (2) Pb₃O₄ (red lead) = 2PbO·PbO₂, lead(II) oxide combined with lead(IV) oxide. Peroxide vs normal oxide: in a peroxide oxygen has the oxidation state −1, present as the O₂²⁻ ion with an O–O single bond (e.g. Na₂O₂, BaO₂, H₂O₂), whereas in a normal oxide oxygen is −2 (e.g. Na₂O, CuO). A practical test: peroxides give hydrogen peroxide with dilute acid or water (Na₂O₂ + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂O₂), normal oxides do not.

Important Concepts

  • Oxide classification follows behaviour, not element type: CO is a non-metal oxide yet neutral; ZnO is a metal oxide yet reacts with base — test with acid and alkali, then label.

Important Statements

  • "Amphoteric oxides react with both acids and bases to form salts and water."
