Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Phosphorus's Answer to Ammonia

    PH₃ (phosphine): prepared from phosphides, weakly basic, strongly reducing, and famous for spontaneous flammability from trace P₂H₄. Preparation, properties, action with halogens and oxygen, uses.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Preparation

    • Lab: white P + concentrated NaOH solution (in inert atmosphere, H₂ evolved): P₄ + 3NaOH + 3H₂O → PH₃ + 3NaH₂PO₂ (sodium hypophosphite).
    • From metal phosphides: Ca₃P₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2PH₃ (carbide-style hydrolysis).
    • Pure PH₃: heat phosphonium iodide with NaOH: PH₄I + NaOH → PH₃ + NaI + H₂O.
    • Impure lab gas contains P₂H₄ (diphosphine) — which makes it spontaneously flammable (the "vortex rings" of burning phosphine demo).

    2.2 Properties

    • Colourless, garlic/rotten-fish odour, highly poisonous; much less soluble in water than NH₃.
    • Pyramidal (like NH₃) but bond angle 93.6° (nearly pure p-bonds — P's 3s barely hybridises); dipole 0.58 D vs NH₃ 1.47 D.
    • Weak base: forms phosphonium salts only with strong acids (PH₄I, PH₄Cl) — PH₃ + HBr → PH₄Br; does NOT form PH₄OH-type stable hydroxide; no hydrogen bonding (hence low bp −87.7 °C, below NH₃'s −33 °C).
    • Reducing nature: precipitates metals from their salt solutions: 3CuSO₄ + 2PH₃ → Cu₃P + 2H₂SO₄ (copper phosphide, black) — note it is NOT simple displacement to metal; with AgNO₃: 8AgNO₃ + PH₃ + 4H₂O → 8Ag + H₃PO₄ + 8HNO₃ (silver mirror-type).
    • Combustion: PH₃ + 2O₂ → H₃PO₄ (burns to phosphoric acid); with limited O₂: P₄O₆/H₃PO₃.

    2.3 Action with halogens and oxygen

    • Halogens: PH₃ + HX addition → phosphonium halides (PH₄Cl, PH₄Br, PH₄I — HI strongest since HI is strongest acid of the three). Direct halogenation: PH₃ + Cl₂ → PCl₃ + HCl (excess Cl₂ → PCl₅).
    • Oxygen: burns to H₃PO₄ (above); the P₂H₄ impurity ignites spontaneously, igniting the PH₃ — vortex rings of smoke in the classic demonstration.

    2.4 Uses

    • Holme's signal (marine distress): containers of CaC₂ and Ca₃P₂ dumped in sea — acetylene + phosphine ignite spontaneously, red flame + smoke.
    • Smoke screens; doping/semiconductor (PH₃ as dopant gas in silicon processing); match industry historically.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why PH₃ is a weak base compared to NH₃

    • Phosphorus is larger, less electronegative; its lone pair is diffuse and poorly held. Protonation is unfavourable; PH₄⁺ exists only with strong acids. Also no hydrogen bonding: PH₃ boils at −87.7 °C, far below NH₃'s −33 °C despite being heavier — the H-bonding absence is visible in the boiling point.

    4.2 Why the 93.6° angle

    • P's 3s–3p gap is large; hybridisation is poor, so PH₃ uses nearly pure p orbitals (which are 90° apart) with a slight squeeze from the lone pair: 93.6° vs NH₃'s 107°. A textbook hybridisation-exception.

    4.3 Why impure phosphine self-ignites

    • The P₂H₄ impurity oxidises at room temperature; its flame then ignites the PH₃, and the burning phosphorus produces the beautiful smoke vortex rings. Pure PH₃ is not self-igniting — the demo depends on the impurity.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "PH₃ + CuSO₄ gives copper metal." With CuSO₄ the product is Cu₃P (copper phosphide), not Cu — the phosphorus stays bonded. Silver nitrate is the case that gives metal (Ag).
    • "PH₃ is as basic as NH₃." No — phosphonium hydroxide is not isolable; PH₃ needs strong, dry acids to form PH₄⁺.
    • "Pure phosphine self-ignites." The P₂H₄ impurity is the self-igniter; pure PH₃ needs a flame.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): How is phosphine prepared in the laboratory? Why is the gas spontaneously flammable? — Preparation: white phosphorus is heated with concentrated sodium hydroxide solution in an inert atmosphere (hydrogen or CO₂, to exclude air): P₄ + 3NaOH + 3H₂O → PH₃ + 3NaH₂PO₂. The gas comes off with the hydrogen already flowing, is dried, and collected over warm water (it is only slightly soluble). It may also be made by hydrolysing calcium phosphide: Ca₃P₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2PH₃. Pure PH₃ is not self-igniting — it needs a flame to burn (PH₃ + 2O₂ → H₃PO₄).
  • NEB-style (2m): Compare the basic character of NH3 and PH3 with reasons. — NH₃ is strongly basic: the nitrogen lone pair is compact and tightly held, protonation to NH₄⁺ is easy, and ammonium hydroxide exists in water. PH₃ is very weakly basic: phosphorus is larger and less electronegative, its lone pair is diffuse, so PH₄⁺ forms only with strong, dry acids (PH₄I, PH₄Br, PH₄Cl) and no phosphonium hydroxide is isolable. Supporting evidence: the bond angle collapses from 107° (sp³-ish in NH₃) to 93.6° (nearly pure p-bonds in PH₃), and the dipole moment falls from 1.47 D to 0.58 D — the lone pair is simply less available for donation. Basicity falls down group 15.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is Holme's signal? Explain the chemistry. — Holme's signal is a marine distress signal: a perforated container holding calcium carbide (CaC₂) and calcium phosphide (Ca₃P₂) is thrown into the sea. Seawater enters and hydrolyses both: CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂ (acetylene) and Ca₃P₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2PH₃. The phosphine, contaminated with self-igniting P₂H₄, catches fire spontaneously and ignites the acetylene — a bright flame with dense smoke rises from the water surface, marking the ship's position for rescue.

Key Points

  • PH3 prep: P4 + 3NaOH + 3H2O -> PH3 + 3NaH2PO2 (conc NaOH, inert atmosphere); Ca3P2 + 6H2O -> 3Ca(OH)2 + 2PH3; pure from PH4I + NaOH. Impure gas has P2H4 -> spontaneously flammable (vortex rings). Properties: colourless, garlic/rotten-fish odour, very poisonous, slight water solubility, pyramidal 93.6 deg (near-pure p bonds), dipole 0.58 D. WEAK base: PH4+ only with strong acids (PH4I/Br/Cl); no H-bonding, bp -87.7 C. Reducing: 3CuSO4 + 2PH3 -> Cu3P + 2H2SO4 (copper PHOSPHIDE, not Cu); 8AgNO3 + PH3 + 4H2O -> 8Ag + H3PO4 + 8HNO3. Combustion: PH3 + 2O2 -> H3PO4. Halogens: PH3 + HX -> PH4X; with Cl2 -> PCl3/PCl5. Uses: Holme's signal (CaC2 + Ca3P2 in sea), smoke screens, Si doping gas.

Summary

Phosphine is ammonia's sickly, dangerous cousin. Prepared by heating white phosphorus with concentrated NaOH (P₄ + 3NaOH + 3H₂O → PH₃ + 3NaH₂PO₂) or hydrolysing Ca₃P₂, it is a colourless garlic-smelling poison, pyramidal at 93.6° (nearly pure p-bonding), with a tiny dipole (0.58 D) and a low boiling point (−87.7 °C — no hydrogen bonding). Its lone pair is too diffuse for strong basicity: PH₄⁺ salts form only with strong acids (PH₄I, PH₄Br). As a reducer it attacks metal salts: CuSO₄ gives black Cu₃P (copper phosphide — the exam trap), AgNO₃ gives metallic silver. It burns to H₃PO₄, adds HX to give phosphonium halides, and its P₂H₄ impurity self-ignites — the smoke vortex rings of the lecture demo and the working principle of Holme's signal (CaC₂ + Ca₃P₂ overboard: acetylene plus self-igniting phosphine, a flame from the sea). Uses: smoke screens, silicon doping, distress signals.
