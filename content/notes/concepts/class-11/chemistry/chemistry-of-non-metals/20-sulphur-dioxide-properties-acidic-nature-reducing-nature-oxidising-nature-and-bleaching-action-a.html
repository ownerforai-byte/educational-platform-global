Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Two-Faced Gas

    SO₂: reducing and oxidising, acidic, bleaching (temporarily), preservative, refrigerant. Its dual redox personality and bleaching contrast with Cl₂ are the exam heart.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Preparation

    • Lab: Na₂SO₃ + dil. H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + SO₂ + H₂O (or NaHSO₃ + HCl).
    • From sulphur: S + O₂ → SO₂ (burning); from sulphides: 4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂ (roasting, industrial); Cu + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O; Cu + dil. HNO₃ route also gives NO — avoid confusion.
    • Collected by upward delivery (heavier than air, 2.27 g/L).

    2.2 Properties — acidic nature

    • Colourless, pungent, suffocating; angular molecule (sp², one lone pair, bond angle ~119°).
    • Acidic oxide: SO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂SO₃ (sulphurous acid, dibasic); with alkalis: 2NaOH + SO₂ → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O; Na₂SO₃ + SO₂ + H₂O → 2NaHSO₃.
    • Liberates CO₂ from carbonates? No — rather: Na₂CO₃ + SO₂ → Na₂SO₃ + CO₂ (SO₂ the stronger acid former).

    2.3 Reducing nature (S +4 → +6)

    • Decolourises acidified KMnO₄: 5SO₂ + 2KMnO₄ + 2H₂O → K₂SO₄ + 2MnSO₄ + 2H₂SO₄
    • Reduces Fe³⁺: 2FeCl₃ + SO₂ + 2H₂O → 2FeCl₂ + 2HCl + H₂SO₄
    • Reduces I₂: SO₂ + I₂ + 2H₂O → H₂SO₄ + 2HI (iodometric estimation of SO₂)
    • Reduces H₂O₂: SO₂ + H₂O₂ → H₂SO₄; bleaches paper pulp (sulphite pulping context).

    2.4 Oxidising nature (S +4 → 0/−2)

    • 2H₂S + SO₂ → 3S + 2H₂O (sulphur fog; room temperature — the classic counter-demonstration)
    • SO₂ + 2Mg → 2MgO + MgS (burning magnesium continues in SO₂)
    • SO₂ + 2CO → S + 2CO₂ (hot)

    2.5 Bleaching action — temporary, by reduction

    • SO₂ + 2H₂O → H₂SO₄ + 2[H] (nascent hydrogen); the [H] reduces the dye to a colourless form.
    • Temporary: air re-oxidises the reduced dye — the colour returns (silk, wool, straw hats). Contrast Cl₂ bleaching: oxidation, permanent.
    • Uses as bleach: delicate fabrics where Cl₂ would damage fibres.

    2.6 Uses

    • H₂SO₄ manufacture (contact process intermediate); bleaching silk/wool/straw; food preservative (E220, dried fruits, wine — antimicrobial + antioxidant); refrigerant (bp −10 °C, historical); fumigation/disinfection; SO₂ in sugar refining; sulphites in paper pulping.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why SO₂ is both reducer and oxidiser

    • S sits at +4, the middle of its range (−2 to +6): it can climb to +6 (reducing behaviour with KMnO₄, I₂, Fe³⁺) or sink to 0/−2 (oxidising behaviour with H₂S, Mg, CO). The H₂S reaction shows both faces in one flask — SO₂ oxidises H₂S while any excess SO₂ in water would reduce permanganate.

    4.2 Why its bleaching is temporary

    • Bleaching by reduction leaves the dye molecule intact but reduced (colourless leuco-form). Atmospheric oxygen slowly re-oxidises it: the straw hat yellows again by summer. Chlorine's HOCl destroys the chromophore by oxidation — irreversible. Mechanism, not intensity, is the difference.

    4.3 Why SO₂ preserves food

    • Dual action: antimicrobial (kills yeasts/bacteria) and antioxidant (scavenges O₂, prevents browning of cut fruit). Sulphite residues on dried apricots are the visible trade-off (label: contains sulphur dioxide).

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "SO₂ bleaches like Cl₂." Opposite mechanisms and permanence — quote both equations.
    • "SO₂ is a strong acid anhydride." It gives the weak H₂SO₃; SO₃ gives strong H₂SO₄.
    • "SO₂ is always a reducer." With H₂S and Mg it is the oxidiser — the +4 middle state cuts both ways.

  • 7. Exam Application

    • Two reduction equations (KMnO₄, I₂) + one oxidation equation (H₂S) = the dual-personality answer.

  • NEB-style (2m): Compare the bleaching action of SO2 with that of Cl2. — SO₂ bleaches by reduction: in water, SO₂ + 2H₂O → H₂SO₄ + 2[H]; the nascent hydrogen reduces the dye to a colourless leuco-form. The change is temporary — air slowly re-oxidises the reduced dye and the colour returns (straw hats, silk flowers yellow with age). It is gentle, suiting delicate fibres like silk and wool. Same result (white), opposite chemistry, opposite permanence — mechanism decides.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is SO2 used as a food preservative? — Sulphur dioxide (E220, as sulphites) preserves food by two simultaneous actions: (1) antimicrobial — it kills or inhibits yeasts, moulds and bacteria that spoil fruit, wine and juices; (2) antioxidant — it scavenges oxygen and blocks enzymatic browning, keeping dried apricots bright and cut fruit fresh. It is cheap, water-soluble and volatile (it dissipates on boiling). The trade-off is sulphite residue, which must be declared on labels because a small fraction of people are sensitive — hence "contains sulphur dioxide" on the packet.

Summary

Sulphur dioxide is the two-faced gas. Prepared from Na₂SO₃ + dil. H₂SO₄ (or burning S, or roasting FeS₂), it is an angular, pungent, water-soluble oxide that gives the weak dibasic H₂SO₃. Sitting at S(+4), it reduces upward — decolourising KMnO₄, converting I₂ to HI, lifting Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ — and oxidises downward — the sulphur fog of 2H₂S + SO₂ → 3S + 2H₂O, and continued Mg burning in the gas. Its bleaching is reduction by nascent hydrogen (SO₂ + 2H₂O → H₂SO₄ + 2[H]): gentle, suited to silk and wool, and temporary — air re-oxidises the dye and the colour returns, the exact opposite of chlorine's permanent oxidative bleach. Uses: contact-process feedstock for H₂SO₄, food preservative (E220 — antimicrobial plus antioxidant in dried fruit and wine), historical refrigerant (bp −10 °C), fumigation and sugar refining.
