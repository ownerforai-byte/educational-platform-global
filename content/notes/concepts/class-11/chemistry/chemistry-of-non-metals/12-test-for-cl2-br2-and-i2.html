  • 1. Topic Introduction — Three Gases, Three Signatures

    Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂ announce themselves in tests: colours, bleaching, displacement, starch, and the classic chlorine + ammonia white fumes. This topic collects the identification chemistry in one place.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Physical identification

    Gas/VapourAppearanceGive-away
    Cl₂greenish-yellow gaspungent, bleaches moist litmus
    Br₂red-brown vapour (from liquid)stains skin/litmus brown
    I₂violet vapour from black solidsublimes to violet crystals

    2.2 Chemical tests

    • Cl₂:
    1. Moist blue litmus → bleached (first red, then white).
    2. Excess NH₃ → dense white fumes of NH₄Cl: 8NH₃ + 3Cl₂ → 6NH₄Cl + N₂.
    3. Passed into KI solution → I₂ liberated (blue-black with starch).
    • Br₂:
    1. Vapour turns moist blue litmus red then bleaches slowly (weaker than Cl₂).
    2. Starch test negative (no blue) — distinguishes from I₂.
    3. In water: orange colour; in KI solution, I₂ displaced (Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂).
    4. With NaOH (colourless) then acidified — orange returns.
    • I₂:
    1. Starch test: deep blue-black adsorption complex — the definitive test; disappears on heating, returns on cooling.
    2. Violet vapour; sublimation.
    3. With AgNO₃: yellow AgI precipitate insoluble in NH₃.

    2.3 Halide ion tests (context)

    I⁻yellow AgIinsoluble

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why starch turns blue-black with iodine

    • I₂ (actually I₃⁻ in solution) slips inside the amylose helix of starch; the guest–host charge-transfer complex absorbs visible light, giving the intense blue-black. Heating shakes the complex apart (colour fades); cooling rebuilds it — a reversible, diagnostic colour.

    4.2 Choosing the right test

    • Bleaching power separates Cl₂ (strong) from Br₂ (weak) and I₂ (none).
    • Starch separates I₂ (blue-black) from Br₂ (nothing).
    • Ammonia fumes confirm Cl₂.
    • For solutions, the AgNO₃ colour + ammonia solubility ladder finishes the identification.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "Bromine water is a starch test." No — bromine never gives the blue starch colour; that belongs to iodine alone.
    • "AgBr is white." AgCl is white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow — the colour ladder plus ammonia solubility distinguishes the three.
    • "The starch colour is a chemical reaction." It is physical adsorption/complexation inside the helix — which is exactly why heat reverses it.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): How would you distinguish between chlorine, bromine and iodine using chemical tests? — Chlorine: a greenish-yellow gas that bleaches moist litmus rapidly; passed into excess ammonia it gives dense white fumes of NH₄Cl (8NH₃ + 3Cl₂ → 6NH₄Cl + N₂); passed into KI solution it liberates iodine, which turns starch blue-black. Bromine: a red-brown vapour that bleaches moist litmus only slowly; it gives no colour with starch; shaken with KI solution it displaces iodine (Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂, blue-black with starch appears only after the displacement). Iodine: a violet vapour subliming from a black crystalline solid; its defining test is the deep blue-black colour with starch — an adsorption complex that fades on heating and returns on cooling. Iodine bleaches nothing. The combination — bleaching speed, starch colour and ammonia fumes — separates all three unambiguously.
  • NEB-style (2m): A colourless halide solution gives a cream precipitate with AgNO3 that dissolves only in concentrated ammonia. Identify the halide and explain. — The precipitate is AgBr (cream): Ag⁺ + Br⁻ → AgBr↓. The ammonia-solubility ladder runs AgCl (white, dissolves in dilute NH₃ via [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺), AgBr (cream, needs concentrated NH₃), AgI (yellow, insoluble). Cream colour plus conc.-ammonia-only solubility identifies the bromide ion. The solubility trend follows the falling stability of the AgX lattice against ammonia complexation as X grows from Cl to I.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why does the blue colour of the starch-iodine complex disappear on heating and reappear on cooling? — The colour comes from iodine molecules (I₂/I₃⁻) occupying the helical cavity of amylose, forming a guest–host charge-transfer complex that absorbs visible light. Heating supplies thermal energy that disrupts the weak van der Waals-hosted complex, expelling the iodine — the blue fades. On cooling, the helix re-adopts its cavity geometry and re-entraps iodine, restoring the blue. The process is physical (adsorption/complexation), not a chemical reaction, hence fully reversible.

Key Points

  • Cl2: greenish-yellow gas, bleaches moist litmus fast, excess NH3 -> white NH4Cl fumes (8NH3 + 3Cl2 -> 6NH4Cl + N2), liberates I2 from KI. Br2: red-brown vapour, bleaches moist litmus slowly, no starch colour, orange in water, displaces I2 from KI. I2: violet vapour from black solid, sublimes, definitive STARCH TEST deep blue-black (adsorption complex in amylose helix; fades on heating, returns on cooling), AgI yellow insoluble in NH3. Halide ladder: AgCl white soluble in dil NH3; AgBr cream soluble only in conc NH3; AgI yellow insoluble.

Summary

Three halogens, three signatures. Chlorine is the greenish-yellow gas that bleaches moist litmus quickly and betrays itself to excess ammonia as dense white NH₄Cl fumes. Bromine is the red-brown vapour that bleaches slowly and never colours starch. Iodine is the violet-vapoured black solid whose starch test — a deep blue-black guest–host complex inside the amylose helix, fading on heating and returning on cooling — is the single most famous colour test in chemistry. For halide solutions, the AgNO₃ ladder finishes the job: white AgCl (soluble in dilute NH₃), cream AgBr (conc. NH₃ only), yellow AgI (insoluble). Bleaching is oxidation by HOCl and needs water; the starch colour is physical adsorption and needs the helix; the fumes test is simple acid–base chemistry — three mechanisms, three fingerprints.
