Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Silent Killer With a Useful Streak

    Carbon monoxide: colourless, odourless, and — because it binds haemoglobin 200× better than O₂ — lethal at 1000 ppm. Yet the same reducing power strips oxygen from ores and feeds organic synthesis.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Preparation

    • Lab: HCOOH + conc. H₂SO₄ → CO + H₂SO₄·H₂O (dehydration of formic acid at ~140 °C); or K₂[Cu(CN)₃]... simpler: oxalic acid + conc. H₂SO₄ gives CO + CO₂ (CO₂ absorbed in NaOH).
    • Incomplete combustion: 2C + O₂ (limited) → 2CO; C + CO₂ → 2CO (red-hot coke).
    • Industrial: water gas (C + H₂O → CO + H₂, 1000 °C) and producer gas (CO + N₂) mixtures.

    2.2 Properties

    • Colourless, odourless, almost water-insoluble; lighter than air; burns with a blue flame: 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂.
    • Structure: :C≡O: with a formal negative on C — linear, bond order 3, strong π-acceptor ligand (hence metal carbonyls Ni(CO)₄, Fe(CO)₅).
    • Reducing action:
    • Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (blast furnace)
    • CuO + CO → Cu + CO₂; I₂O₅ + 5CO → I₂ + 5CO₂ (basis of CO detection in mines)
    • Reaction with Cl₂ (light, charcoal catalyst): CO + Cl₂ → COCl₂ (phosgene, the WWI poison gas; also an industrial reagent).
    • With NaOH (pressure): CO + NaOH → HCOONa (sodium formate) — proof CO is the anhydride of formic acid.
    • With Ni: Ni + 4CO → Ni(CO)₄ (Mond process, 50 °C; decomposes at 230 °C — nickel refining).

    2.3 Toxicity and haemoglobin

    • CO binds Fe²⁺ of haemoglobin as carboxyhaemoglobin with ~200–250× the affinity of O₂ — 0.1% CO in air ties up half the blood's capacity. Symptoms: headache → unconsciousness → death; treatment: fresh air/pure O₂ (or hyperbaric O₂) to displace CO.
    • Sources: incomplete combustion (stoves, engines, geysers), smoking.

    2.4 Uses

    • Reducing agent in metallurgy (Fe, Zn, Ni oxides); water gas/producer gas as fuel; Mond process Ni refining; synthesis of methanol, phosgene, carbonyls; CO₂ lasers' cousin — CO lasers.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why CO is poisonous yet stable

    • The lone pair on carbon makes CO a strong σ-donor and π-acceptor to transition metals — haemoglobin's Fe²⁺ is simply the wrong metal to bind first. The same ligand strength that kills also drives Ni(CO)₄ refining and catalysis.

    4.2 Why CO reduces metal oxides

    • CO₂ is thermodynamically more stable (C=O bonds); CO grabs oxygen from oxides below carbon in the Ellingham series at furnace temperatures — the blast furnace's chemistry in one line.

    4.3 Why the blue flame matters

    • CO's blue flame in stoves signals complete secondary combustion; a yellow, lazy flame with fumes warns of CO leakage — the practical safety lesson embedded in the chemistry.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "CO₂ is poisonous." CO₂ is not toxic — it displaces O₂ (asphyxiation). CO is the true poison, by chemical hijacking of haemoglobin.
    • "CO is an oxide of carbon, so it is acidic." CO is a neutral oxide — it does not react with water to give an acid; it is the (formal) anhydride of formic acid only under pressure with NaOH.
    • "Water gas is pure CO." Water gas is CO + H₂; producer gas is CO + N₂ — quote both compositions.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): How is carbon monoxide prepared in the laboratory? Give two of its reducing reactions. — Preparation: formic acid is dehydrated by concentrated sulphuric acid at about 140 °C: HCOOH —(conc. H₂SO₄)→ CO + H₂O. The acid acts as a dehydrating agent, removing the elements of water from formic acid; the gas is collected over water (CO is practically insoluble). Alternatively, oxalic acid with conc. H₂SO₄ gives a CO + CO₂ mixture, the CO₂ being absorbed in NaOH. Reducing reactions: (1) Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ — the blast-furnace reduction of iron(III) oxide, where CO strips the oxide of its oxygen and is itself oxidised to CO₂; (2) I₂O₅ + 5CO → I₂ + 5CO₂ — iodine pentoxide is reduced to iodine, the basis of detecting CO in mine air (the liberated I₂ is measured). CO also reduces CuO to copper at moderate heat.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is carbon monoxide poisonous? What is the treatment? — CO binds to the Fe²⁺ of haemoglobin to form carboxyhaemoglobin, with an affinity 200–250 times greater than oxygen. It therefore occupies the oxygen sites preferentially: air containing only 0.1 % CO disables about half the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity, causing headache, unconsciousness and death by internal suffocation. The gas is odourless, so the victim gets no warning. Treatment: remove the victim to fresh air and administer pure oxygen (in severe cases hyperbaric O₂) — the high O₂ partial pressure displaces CO from haemoglobin and restores oxygen transport.
  • NEB-style (2m): Describe the Mond process for refining nickel. — Impure nickel is treated with carbon monoxide at about 50 °C: Ni + 4CO → Ni(CO)₄ (nickel tetracarbonyl), a volatile liquid that distils away from the impurities (Fe, Co, etc. do not form volatile carbonyls under these conditions). The carbonyl is then heated to about 230 °C, where it decomposes: Ni(CO)₄ → Ni + 4CO — depositing pure nickel powder (on pellets for the Mond process) and regenerating the CO for recycle. The temperatures — 50 °C to form, 230 °C to decompose — are the marks of this answer.

Summary

Carbon monoxide is chemistry's double agent. Prepared by dehydrating formic acid with conc. H₂SO₄, it is a colourless, odourless, water-insoluble gas with a linear triple-bonded structure and a lone pair on carbon — which makes it burn with a blue flame (2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂), reduce metal oxides (Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ in the blast furnace; I₂O₅ + 5CO → I₂ for mine-air detection), form phosgene COCl₂ with chlorine, give sodium formate with NaOH (its formic-acid anhydride secret), and refine nickel through the Mond process (Ni + 4CO at 50 °C → Ni(CO)₄; decomposed at 230 °C). The same ligand power, aimed at haemoglobin's Fe²⁺, makes it a silent killer: carboxyhaemoglobin forms with ~200× oxygen's affinity, 0.1 % CO in air halves the blood's capacity, and only pure-O₂ therapy can win the site back. Water gas (CO + H₂) and producer gas (CO + N₂) turn this poison into fuel.
