Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The King of Chemicals

    H₂SO₄ — "king of chemicals" — as acid, oxidant and dehydrating agent, plus its uses. Its three personalities and the safety they demand.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 As an acid

    • Strong dibasic acid: ionises in two steps (H₂SO₄ → H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ fully; HSO₄⁻ → H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ fairly); dense oily liquid ("oil of vitriol"), non-volatile (bp 338 °C).
    • Normal acid reactions: metals below H (Zn + H₂SO₄(dil) → ZnSO₄ + H₂), bases/oxides (CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O), carbonates (Na₂CO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + CO₂ + H₂O).
    • Non-volatile acid displacer: NaCl + H₂SO₄(conc) → NaHSO₄ + HCl; CaF₂ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + 2HF; KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HNO₃ — it makes the volatile acids.

    2.2 As an oxidising agent (hot, concentrated)

    • Cu + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O (blue solution + pungent gas)
    • C + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O (sugar-charcoal oxidation)
    • S + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → 3SO₂ + 2H₂O
    • 2HBr + H₂SO₄(conc) → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O (why HBr/HI can't use it — row 69)
    • S reduced from +6 to +4; dilute H₂SO₄ is NOT an oxidant (H⁺ is the only oxidant there).

    2.3 As a dehydrating agent

    • Sugar charring: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ —(conc. H₂SO₄)→ 12C + 11H₂O (the black snake demonstration)
    • Formic acid → CO: HCOOH —(conc. H₂SO₄)→ CO + H₂O (row 71's prep)
    • Oxalic acid → CO + CO₂
    • Ethanol → ethene: C₂H₅OH —(conc. H₂SO₄, 170 °C)→ C₂H₄ + H₂O
    • Dehydrates blue CuSO₄·5H₂O → white CuSO₄; kills skin cells by extracting water — the burn mechanism.
    • Hygroscopic: dries gases (except those it reacts with); used in desiccators.

    2.4 Uses

    • Fertilisers (ammonium sulphate, superphosphate); petroleum refining; storage batteries (lead-acid, ~30% H₂SO₄); paints/pigments (TiO₂ route); detergents; steel pickling; laboratory non-volatile acid; dehydration in organic chemistry; DDT-era insecticide manufacture.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why concentrated H₂SO₄ oxidises but dilute does not

    • The oxidising species is the molecule itself (S at +6 with strongly bound water): hot concentrated H₂SO₄ accepts electrons, being reduced to SO₂. In dilute solution the molecule is fully ionised — only H⁺ remains as a (weak) oxidant, giving H₂ with active metals. Concentration is the switch.

    4.2 Why it dehydrates sugar to pure carbon

    • H₂SO₄ has an enormous affinity for water (hydration enthalpy huge). It strips H and O from sucrose in stoichiometric proportion (11 H₂O per 12 C), leaving a porous black carbon column that grows out of the beaker — dehydration so exothermic it steams.

    4.3 Why it is the "king of chemicals"

    • Production tonnage of any chemical ever measured; it sits upstream of fertilisers, paints, batteries, detergents, steel — a nation's H₂SO₄ consumption was once a proxy for its industrial development.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "H₂SO₄ always oxidises." Only hot concentrated acid; dilute acid behaves as a normal strong acid (H₂ with Zn).
    • "It dehydrates by absorbing water molecules only." It removes the elements of water (H and O) from compounds like sugar and formic acid that contain no water at all.
    • "Adding water to acid is safe if slow." Never — always add acid to water; the hydration heat boils droplets of acid out of the vessel.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Describe sulphuric acid as (a) an acid, (b) an oxidising agent, and (c) a dehydrating agent, with one equation each. — (a) Acid: dilute H₂SO₄ is a strong dibasic acid: Zn + H₂SO₄(dil) → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑ (also neutralises bases: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O; liberates CO₂ from carbonates). (b) Oxidising agent: only hot, concentrated acid oxidises, being itself reduced to SO₂: Cu + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O (blue solution, pungent fumes); it also oxidises carbon: C + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O. Dilute acid is not an oxidant — with zinc it merely gives hydrogen. (c) Dehydrating agent: it removes the elements of water: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ —(conc. H₂SO₄)→ 12C + 11H₂O (sugar charring), HCOOH → CO + H₂O (CO preparation), C₂H₅OH → C₂H₄ + H₂O at 170 °C, and it turns blue CuSO₄·5H₂O white. Three roles, one molecule.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is concentrated H2SO4 used to prepare HCl from NaCl but not HBr from KBr? — With NaCl the reaction is simple non-volatile-acid displacement: NaCl + H₂SO₄(conc) → NaHSO₄ + HCl↑ — chloride is too weak a reducer to be oxidised, so clean HCl distils off. With KBr the concentrated acid acts as an oxidising agent: 2KBr + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → K₂SO₄ + Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O — bromide reduces the sulphate to SO₂ while becoming brown bromine, contaminating the product. Hence KBr + conc. H₃PO₄ (non-oxidising) is used for HBr. The acid's oxidising power, dormant against Cl⁻, wakes against Br⁻.
  • NEB-style (2m): State the rule for diluting concentrated sulphuric acid and explain why. — The rule: always add acid to water, never water to acid ("do as you oughta, add acid to water"). Concentrated H₂SO₄ has an enormous heat of hydration; when water is poured into the dense acid, the heat released at the interface boils the water layer instantly and spits droplets of hot acid out of the vessel. When acid is added slowly to a large volume of water, the heat spreads through the water and no violent boiling occurs. Stirring while adding keeps local hot spots down.

Key Points

  • H2SO4 "king of chemicals": strong dibasic acid (sulphate + hydrogensulphate salts), dense oily non-volatile liquid (bp 338 C). ACID: Zn + H2SO4(dil) -> ZnSO4 + H2; CuO + H2SO4 -> CuSO4 + H2O; Na2CO3 -> CO2. NON-VOLATILE DISPLACER: NaCl + H2SO4(conc) -> NaHSO4 + HCl; CaF2 -> HF; KNO3 -> HNO3. Summary

Sulphuric acid earns "king of chemicals" by doing three jobs. As a strong dibasic acid (dilute) it etches metals below hydrogen (Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂), dissolves oxides and carbonates, and — being non-volatile (bp 338 °C) — displaces the volatile acids HCl, HF and HNO₃ from their salts. As an oxidising agent it works only hot and concentrated, being reduced to SO₂: Cu + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O, carbon to CO₂, and bromide to Br₂ (the reason HBr needs H₃PO₄ instead). As a dehydrating agent it strips the elements of water from anything: sugar to a black carbon snake (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O), formic acid to CO, ethanol to ethene at 170 °C, and blue vitriol to white powder. Its uses span fertilisers, lead-acid batteries (~30 % acid), petroleum refining, paints, detergents and steel pickling — and its dilution rule is the first safety lesson of every lab: add acid to water, never the reverse.
