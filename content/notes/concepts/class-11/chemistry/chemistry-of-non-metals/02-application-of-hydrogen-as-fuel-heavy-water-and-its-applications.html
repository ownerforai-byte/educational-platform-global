Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Hydrogen as Fuel and the Water That Is Not

    Two applied faces of hydrogen: D₂O (heavy water) with its nuclear-reactor career, and hydrogen as the fuel of the future — the only combustion product of which is water. This topic covers hydrogen fuel's chemistry, its storage problem, and heavy water's preparation and applications.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Hydrogen as a fuel

    • Combustion: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, ΔH = −286 kJ/mol — the highest energy per unit mass of any chemical fuel (~142 kJ/g vs petrol ~48 kJ/g).
    • Zero-carbon: the only product is water — no CO₂, CO, SO₂ or particulates.
    • Uses: oxy-hydrogen flame (~2800 °C) for welding; fuel cells converting H₂ + O₂ directly to electricity (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O; efficiency ~60–70 %, twice internal combustion); rocket propellant (liquid H₂ + liquid O₂).

    2.2 The storage problem

    • Hydrogen's low density means huge volumes: storage as compressed gas (high-pressure cylinders, ~350–700 bar), liquid (cryogenic, −253 °C, liquefaction costs ~30 % of the energy content), or metal hydrides (LaNi₅H₆, MgH₂ — solid-state absorption, safest but heavy).

    2.3 Heavy water (D₂O)

    • Preparation: by prolonged electrolysis of ordinary water — H₂ (light) evolves faster than D₂ (isotope effect), so the residual electrolyte enriches in D₂O; also by fractional distillation exploiting the 1.4 °C bp difference.
    • Applications: (1) neutron moderator in nuclear reactors — slows fast neutrons to thermal speeds while absorbing very few (light water absorbs too many); (2) coolant in the reactor core; (3) tracer in mechanism studies (deuterium labelling); (4) deuterated drugs and NMR solvents.

    2.4 Why hydrogen fuel is attractive — and hard

    • Attractive: highest specific energy, clean product, producible from water by electrolysis using renewable electricity.
    • Hard: storage volume, production currently mostly from fossil reforming (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂), embrittlement of metals, and the safety perception (Hindenburg legacy).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The fuel cell in one picture

    • At the anode: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻; at the cathode: ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O. Electrons flow through the external circuit — electricity directly, no combustion, ~2× the efficiency of a heat engine.

    4.2 The isotope effect in heavy-water preparation

    • H–O bonds break faster than D–O bonds (lower zero-point energy for D), so during electrolysis H₂ leaves preferentially; after many stages the leftover water approaches pure D₂O (modern plants reach 99.75 %).

    4.3 Why D₂O and not H₂O moderates best

    • Fast neutrons must be slowed by elastic collisions; light nuclei (H, D) take the most energy per collision. But ¹H also captures neutrons (¹H + n → ²H), degrading the chain reaction; deuterium's capture cross-section is ~600× smaller — D₂O slows neutrons while barely eating them, so natural (non-enriched) uranium works.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Heavy water is not "more purified water": D₂O is chemically H₂O with deuterium in place of protium — a different isotope, not a different purity.

  • Fuel cells do not burn hydrogen: they electrochemically combine it — no flame, higher efficiency; calling a fuel cell "hydrogen combustion" loses the point.
  • The moderator's job is slowing, not stopping: it reduces neutron speed to thermal energies so ²³⁵U fission becomes probable; control rods (cadmium/boron) do the absorbing.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What is heavy water? How is it prepared, and why is it used in nuclear reactors? — Heavy water is deuterium oxide, D₂O, in which both hydrogen atoms of water are the isotope deuterium (²H). Preparation: by the prolonged electrolysis of ordinary water — because H–O bonds break more readily than D–O bonds, light hydrogen (H₂) evolves at the cathode faster than deuterium, so the residual electrolyte becomes progressively enriched in D₂O; repeated electrolysis of many stages yields water containing up to ~99.75 % D₂O. (It may also be concentrated by fractional distillation, using the small boiling-point difference, 101.4 °C vs 100 °C.) Use in reactors: D₂O serves as the neutron moderator — it slows the fast neutrons released in fission to thermal speeds at which they can fission further ²³⁵U nuclei — and, because deuterium's neutron-capture cross-section is hundreds of times smaller than that of ordinary hydrogen, very few neutrons are lost, so the chain reaction can even run on natural uranium; it doubles as the reactor coolant.

  • NEB-style (2m): Give two advantages of hydrogen as a fuel and one major difficulty in its use. — Advantages: (1) the highest energy per unit mass of any chemical fuel (about 142 kJ/g, roughly three times petrol), and (2) its combustion or fuel-cell reaction produces only water — no CO₂, CO, SO₂ or particulate pollution. Difficulty: storage — hydrogen's very low density means it must be kept either as a highly compressed gas (~700 bar), as a cryogenic liquid (−253 °C, liquefaction itself consuming ~30 % of the energy content), or absorbed in heavy metal hydrides; each option is costly, bulky or heavy.
  • NEB-style (2m): Write the electrode reactions of the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell and state one advantage over internal combustion. — Anode: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. Cathode: ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O. Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Advantage: the fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electricity without a combustion step, achieving roughly 60–70 % efficiency — about twice that of an internal-combustion engine — with water as the only emission.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): What is heavy water? How is it prepared, and why is it used in nuclear reactors? — Heavy water is deuterium oxide, D₂O — water in which both hydrogen atoms are the isotope deuterium (²H) instead of ordinary protium. Its physical constants differ measurably from ordinary water: melting point 3.8 °C, boiling point 101.4 °C, density 1.108 g/mL. Preparation: by the prolonged electrolysis of ordinary water (NaOH solution). Because the O–H bond breaks more readily than the O–D bond (the isotope effect), light hydrogen gas evolves at the cathode faster than deuterium, so as electrolysis proceeds the residual water becomes progressively richer in D₂O; after many stages of electrolysis and concentration, water containing up to about 99.75 % D₂O is obtained. Fractional distillation (exploiting the 1.4 °C boiling-point difference) can assist the concentration. Use in reactors: D₂O acts as the neutron moderator — fast neutrons from fission collide with deuterium nuclei and are slowed to thermal speeds at which they can efficiently induce further fission of ²³⁵U — and, crucially, deuterium's neutron-capture cross-section is several hundred times smaller than that of protium, so very few neutrons are wasted; this allows the chain reaction to run even with natural uranium fuel. D₂O also serves as the reactor coolant, circulating heat from the core.
  • NEB-style (2m): Give two advantages of hydrogen as a fuel and one major difficulty in its use. — Advantages: (1) Highest energy density per unit mass of any chemical fuel — about 142 kJ per gram, roughly three times that of petrol — so a small mass of hydrogen delivers a large amount of energy. (2) Clean combustion: whether burned or used in a fuel cell, hydrogen combines with oxygen to give only water (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O) — no carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide or particulates, so it does not contribute to global warming or urban air pollution. Difficulty: storage and transport. Hydrogen's extremely low density means a given mass occupies a huge volume; it must be stored as a highly compressed gas (up to ~700 bar in heavy cylinders), as a cryogenic liquid at −253 °C (with liquefaction consuming about 30 % of the fuel's own energy), or absorbed in metal hydrides such as LaNi₅H₆ (safe but very heavy) — each option is expensive, bulky or weighty.
  • NEB-style (2m): Write the electrode reactions of the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell and state one advantage it has over an internal-combustion engine. — Anode (negative electrode): H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ — hydrogen is oxidised, releasing electrons to the external circuit. Cathode (positive electrode): ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O — oxygen is reduced, consuming the returning electrons. Overall cell reaction: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Advantage: the fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy without any combustion step, achieving efficiencies of roughly 60–70 % — about double the ~30–40 % of an internal-combustion engine — while emitting only water vapour.

Key Points

  • Hydrogen fuel: 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, dH = -286 kJ/mol, ~142 kJ/g (3x petrol), only product water. Uses: oxy-hydrogen flame ~2800 C, fuel cells (anode H2 -> 2H+ + 2e-, cathode 1/2O2 + 2H+ + 2e- -> H2O, ~60-70% efficient), rocket propellant. Storage: compressed ~700 bar, liquid -253 C (30% energy cost), metal hydrides LaNi5H6. Heavy water D2O: mp 3.8 C, bp 101.4 C, d 1.108; prepared by prolonged electrolysis (H evolves faster - isotope effect) to ~99.75%; used as neutron moderator (capture cross-section ~600x smaller than H) + coolant; also deuterium tracer.

Summary

Hydrogen's applied chapter has two stars. As a fuel, H₂ outranks every chemical energy carrier per gram (~142 kJ/g, three times petrol) and burns to nothing but water — in the oxy-hydrogen flame at ~2800 °C, in rocket tanks as cryogenic liquid, and most elegantly in the fuel cell, where anode H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ and cathode ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O deliver electricity at 60–70 % efficiency with steam the only exhaust. The catch is storage: 700-bar cylinders, −253 °C liquefaction (costing ~30 % of the energy), or heavy metal hydrides like LaNi₅H₆. As heavy water, D₂O — mp 3.8 °C, bp 101.4 °C, density 1.108 g/mL — is won from ordinary water by prolonged electrolysis, where the isotope effect lets light hydrogen escape faster and the residue enriches to ~99.75 % D₂O. In the reactor it is the moderator of choice: deuterium slows fast neutrons to fission-friendly thermal speeds while capturing several hundred times fewer of them than protium, letting natural uranium sustain the chain, and doubles as the coolant. One isotope effect makes it; one cross-section difference justifies it.
