Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Triple Bond That Refuses to Break

    Nitrogen is 78 % of every breath yet almost inert: its N≡N triple bond (941 kJ/mol) is among chemistry's strongest. This topic explains that inertness quantitatively, then shows the two ways chemistry defeats it — the violent "active nitrogen" of the electric discharge and the enzymatic miracle of nitrogen fixation.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Occurrence and preparation

    • Air: 78.1 % by volume (N₂); combined: nitrates (Chile saltpetre NaNO₃), ammonium salts, proteins.
    • Laboratory: heating ammonium dichromate (green-route volcano): (NH₄)₂Cr₂O₇ → N₂ + Cr₂O₃ + 4H₂O; or NH₄Cl + NaNO₂ solution warmed: NH₄Cl + NaNO₂ → N₂ + NaCl + 2H₂O.
    • Industrial: fractional distillation of liquid air (N₂ bp −196 °C vs O₂ −183 °C).

    2.2 Reason for inertness — the NEB core answer

    1. Triple bond energy: N≡N bond dissociation energy = 941 kJ/mol — one of the strongest bonds known; breaking it costs enormous energy.
    2. Short bond length: 110 pm; the small atoms overlap strongly.
    3. Non-polarity: the symmetrical N₂ molecule has no dipole, so it is barely solvated or polarised — weak interactions with reagents.
    4. High ionisation energy and stable electronic configuration (each N has a lone pair + 3 shared pairs — full octet per atom within the molecule).
    • Consequence: N₂ reacts only with the most aggressive partners — Li (6 Li + N₂ → 2Li₃N at room temperature, the only alkali metal to do so), Mg burning in air (Mg₃N₂), O₂ under lightning/Haber conditions, H₂ at high T/P with catalyst.

    2.3 Active nitrogen

    • Passing N₂ through an electric discharge at low pressure excites the molecule into energetic atomic/molecular states; the glowing yellow "active nitrogen" recombines, emitting light, and reacts with metals, H₂S, and even water vapour — chemistry the ground-state molecule refuses.
    • Active nitrogen's reactivity comes from stored electronic excitation energy (metastable states) that substitutes for the bond-breaking cost.

    2.4 Nitrogen fixation — beating the bond biologically

    • Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium in legume root nodules, free-living Azotobacter, cyanobacteria) use the enzyme nitrogenase to convert N₂ → NH₃ at ambient temperature and pressure — chemistry the Haber process needs 450 °C/200 atm to force.
    • Lightning's discharge also fixes nitrogen naturally (N₂ + O₂ → 2NO, then to nitrates delivered by rain).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The bond-energy ledger

    • To react N₂ with H₂ (Haber), the 941 kJ/mol bond must be broken before N–H bonds (386 kJ/mol each, ×3) can form — the balance only tips with high temperature, high pressure and an iron catalyst; and even then the equilibrium is unfavourable, which is why the process is a masterpiece of compromise.

    4.2 Why lithium is special

    • Li⁺ is tiny and Li₃N is extremely stable (lattice energy compensates the bond-breaking cost); the reaction runs even at room temperature — a favourite exam contrast with Na, K, Rb, Cs which need heating.

    4.3 Active nitrogen in the discharge tube

    • Low-pressure N₂ + discharge → pink-yellow afterglow persisting seconds after the discharge stops — metastable N atoms recombining. This "active nitrogen" attacks H₂S (giving NH₃ + S), metals (nitrides), and hydrocarbons — demonstrating that inertness is kinetic, not absolute.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Inertness is kinetic, not thermodynamic: N₂ → NH₃ is thermodynamically favourable (ΔG negative) but kinetically blocked by the triple bond — that distinction is the analytical heart of the answer.

  • Active nitrogen is not ionised nitrogen: it is electronically excited N atoms/molecules from the discharge — no net charge involved.
  • Nitrogen's non-polarity matters: quoting only the bond energy misses the second reason (no dipole → weak intermolecular attraction with reagents); full answers cite both.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Why is molecular nitrogen inert at ordinary conditions? — (1) The triple bond: N₂ contains an N≡N triple bond with a very high dissociation energy of about 941 kJ/mol — among the strongest bonds in chemistry — so breaking it requires enormous energy that ordinary conditions cannot supply. (2) Short bond length: the bond is only 110 pm long, the small nitrogen atoms overlapping very strongly. (3) Non-polar symmetry: the molecule has zero dipole moment, so it interacts only weakly with other reactants (poor solvation, poor polarisation). (4) Complete octets: each nitrogen atom already has an octet (one lone pair plus three shared pairs) within the molecule, so there is no driving force to react. Hence N₂ reacts only under forcing conditions (Haber process: 450 °C, 200 atm, Fe catalyst) or with very electropositive partners (Li at room temperature: 6Li + N₂ → 2Li₃N).

  • NEB-style (2m): What is active nitrogen? How is it prepared and how does it differ from ordinary nitrogen? — Active nitrogen is nitrogen in an electronically excited, highly reactive state, produced by passing nitrogen gas at low pressure through an electric discharge; it glows with a characteristic yellow afterglow that persists briefly after the discharge stops. Differences: ordinary N₂ is inert — it does not react with H₂S, metals or water vapour at ordinary conditions — whereas active nitrogen reacts readily: it converts H₂S to NH₃ and sulphur, forms nitrides with metals, and attacks many substances at room temperature, because the excitation energy it carries substitutes for the enormous energy otherwise needed to break the N≡N triple bond.
  • NEB-style (2m): How do nitrogen-fixing bacteria manage to fix nitrogen at ordinary conditions while industry needs 450 °C and 200 atm? — Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (e.g. Rhizobium in legume root nodules) possess the enzyme complex nitrogenase, which binds N₂ at an iron–molybdenum cofactor and reduces it stepwise to ammonia using electrons and ATP from the plant's respiration — all at ambient temperature and pressure. The enzyme's active site weakens and progressively hydrogenates the triple bond without ever requiring its full 941 kJ/mol to be supplied at once. Industry, lacking such catalysis, must force the same overall reaction (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃) with high temperature, high pressure and an iron catalyst — and even then the equilibrium is only moderately favourable.

Summary

Nitrogen dominates the atmosphere and refuses to join it. Its inertness is a four-reason argument: the N≡N triple bond at 941 kJ/mol (bond length 110 pm), the molecule's zero dipole moment (weak grip on reagents), the complete octets inside N₂, and hence a purely kinetic block — N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ is thermodynamically downhill yet needs the Haber process's 450 °C, 200 atm and iron to run at all. The exceptions prove the accounting: lithium nitridises cold (6Li + N₂ → 2Li₃N) because its nitride's lattice energy repays the bond cost, and burning magnesium sneaks in Mg₃N₂. Active nitrogen shows the block is surmountable by energy storage: a low-pressure electric discharge excites N₂ into a yellow-glowing state that attacks H₂S, metals and hydrocarbons the ground-state molecule ignores. And biology runs the ultimate workaround: nitrogenase (Fe–Mo cofactor) in Rhizobium and Azotobacter reduces N₂ to NH₃ at ambient conditions using ATP — fixing 100+ kg/ha/year in legume fields where industry must burn fuel to match. Preparation routes — ammonium dichromate's volcano, NH₄Cl/NaNO₂, and liquid-air distillation (N₂ bp −196 °C) — supply the gas for all of it.
