Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — One Family, Three Personalities

    HCl, HBr and HI: same formula shape, steeply different behaviour. This topic compares their preparation, properties, reducing strength, acidic nature and solubility — and explains every trend from the bond.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Preparation

    • HCl (lab): NaCl + conc. H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl (below 200 °C; dry gas, upward delivery — heavier than air). Industrial: burning H₂ in Cl₂ (synthesis), or by-product of chlorination.
    • HBr: cannot use conc. H₂SO₄ (it oxidises Br⁻ to Br₂); use KBr + conc. H₃PO₄ instead: KBr + H₃PO₄ → KH₂PO₄ + HBr.
    • HI: same problem, worse; use KI + conc. H₃PO₄ → KH₂PO₄ + HI. Or hydrolysis of PBr₃/PI₃: PBr₃ + 3H₂O → H₃PO₃ + 3HBr.

    2.2 Comparative properties

    2.3 The two opposing trends

    • Acidity and reducing power increase down the group (bond weakens, H–X breaks easier, X⁻ bigger and more polarisable).
    • Thermal stability decreases down the group for the same reason.
    • HI reduces even conc. H₂SO₄: 2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O — which is exactly why H₂SO₄ cannot be used to make it.

    2.4 Uses

    • HCl: gastric juice (digestion, pH 1.5–3.5), metal pickling (descaling), chlorination, lab reagent, making Cl₂/MnCl₂.
    • HBr: ethylene dibromide (historical petrol additive), AgBr emulsions, alkyl bromides in organic synthesis.
    • HI: reducing agent in organic chemistry, iodide salts, hydriodic acid for iodometry.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why H₂SO₄ is disqualified for HBr and HI

    • Concentrated H₂SO₄ is an oxidising acid. With NaCl it merely forms HCl (Cl⁻ too weak a reducer to be oxidised further). With KBr it oxidises half the bromide to Br₂ (2KBr + 2H₂SO₄(conc) → K₂SO₄ + Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O) — the gas comes out contaminated and purple. With KI it goes further, to SO₂ or even H₂S, and liberates I₂. Hence the switch to non-oxidising H₃PO₄.

    4.2 Why acidity rises as stability falls

    • Both trace to the same bond: longer, weaker H–X means easier proton release (acidity up) and easier homolysis (stability down, reducing power up). One parameter, three symptoms — the exam's favourite "explain the trend" question.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "HCl is the strongest of the three because Cl is most electronegative." Backwards — acidity increases down the group; HI is the strongest acid of the three (bond weakness beats electronegativity).
    • "HF belongs in this comparison with the same trend." HF breaks the pattern: its H-bonding gives it a surprisingly high boiling point and, despite F's electronegativity, it is a weak acid in water.
    • "Boiling point rises smoothly." For HCl–HBr–HI yes; including HF it does not — HF's hydrogen bonding is the anomaly.

Examples

  • The self-defeating preparation — try to make HI with concentrated H2SO4 and the acid oxidises its own product: the iodide reduces the sulphate to SO2 while becoming brown I2 vapour. H3PO4, too weak to oxidise anything, is the only honest choice.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): How is hydrogen chloride prepared in the laboratory? Why is the same method not used for HI? — HCl: dry sodium chloride is heated with concentrated sulphuric acid below 200 °C: NaCl + H₂SO₄(conc) → NaHSO₄ + HCl. The gas is dried with concentrated H₂SO₄ and collected by upward delivery (heavier than air). Why not for HI: concentrated H₂SO₄ is an oxidising acid. Chloride is too weak a reducer to be oxidised, so HCl comes through clean; but iodide is a strong reducer — 2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O — the acid destroys its own product, giving violet I₂ vapour and SO₂ contamination (with bromide the same happens to a lesser degree). Hence KI + concentrated H₃PO₄, a non-oxidising acid, is used instead: KI + H₃PO₄ → KH₂PO₄ + HI.
  • NEB-style (2m): Arrange HCl, HBr and HI in order of (a) thermal stability and (b) reducing strength, with reasons. — (a) Stability: HCl > HBr > HI. Bond energies fall 431 → 366 → 299 kJ/mol as the bond lengthens (127 → 141 → 161 pm); the weaker the bond, the more easily heat snaps it, so HI decomposes visibly (2HI → H₂ + I₂) while HCl is stable to red heat. (b) Reducing strength: HI > HBr > HCl. The same weak bond releases the halide's electrons readily: iodide is oxidised to I₂ even by conc. H₂SO₄, bromide less readily, chloride hardly at all. Both trends are one cause wearing two faces — the falling H–X bond energy down the group.
  • NEB-style (2m): State two uses each of HCl and HI. — HCl: (1) in gastric juice (pH 1.5–3.5) it activates pepsin and kills microbes in the stomach; (2) industrially it pickles steel — dissolving oxide scale before galvanising or rolling — and serves as the feedstock for chlorination and Cl₂ manufacture. HI: (1) as a reducing agent in organic chemistry (converting, e.g., alkyl halides back to alkanes); (2) in iodometry, where hydriodic acid supplies iodide for iodine-liberating titrations, and for preparing iodide salts.

Summary

One family, three personalities, one cause. HCl (bond 431 kJ/mol) is stable, the weakest reducer, and the only one clean enough to make with oxidising H₂SO₄; HBr (366) needs H₃PO₄ because H₂SO₄ turns half its bromide into brown Br₂; HI (299) is so reducing it reduces the sulphate to SO₂ while turning to violet I₂ vapour. Down the group the H–X bond lengthens (127 → 141 → 161 pm), so thermal stability falls (HCl > HBr > HI) while acidity and reducing power rise (HI > HBr > HCl) — the same weakened bond releases its proton easily and its electrons even more easily. All three fume in moist air and all three are caught by the AgX colour ladder (white, cream, yellow). Uses: HCl pickles steel and digests food at pH 1.5; HBr feeds photographic emulsions; HI reduces and titrates.
