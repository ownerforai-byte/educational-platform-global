Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — White, Red and Black

    Phosphorus exists as allotropes named only (NEB scope): white (yellow), red, black (and violet/scarlett in older texts). Structure differences explain everything: reactivity, toxicity, storage, uses.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The allotropes (names only, with essentials)

    AllotropeAppearanceStructureKey properties
    White (yellow) Pwaxy, white/yellow soliddiscrete P₄ tetrahedra, 60° strained anglesvery reactive; ignites in air at ~35 °C (stored under water); soluble in CS₂; poisonous (~0.1 g lethal); garlic odour; glows in dark (slow oxidation — chemiluminescence)
    Red Pdark red powderpolymeric chains (P₄ tetrahedra opened and linked)much less reactive; does not ignite spontaneously; insoluble in CS₂; non-poisonous; made by heating white P at 250–300 °C in inert atmosphere
    Black Pblack, flakylayered (like graphite, corrugated sheets)most stable; least reactive; conducts (semiconductor); made under high pressure

    2.2 Conversion chemistry

    • White → red: heat 250–300 °C (inert atmosphere / with a trace of I₂ catalyst).
    • Red → white: distil red P above 400 °C; vapour condenses as white P (P₄ re-forms).
    • Allotropy is reversible through the vapour: the P₄ unit is the thermodynamic root.

    2.3 Uses

    • White P: matches (old "strike-anywhere"), rat poison, smoke screens (P burns to P₂O₅ fog), phosphor bronze.
    • Red P: safety matches (on the box side), fireworks, semiconductors (InP), protection of metal surfaces.
    • Black P: research/semiconductor (phosphorene layers).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why white phosphorus is so reactive

    • The P₄ tetrahedron forces 60° angles on atoms whose bonding orbitals prefer ~90–109° — the bent bonds are strained, energy-rich, and desperate to react. Ignition at 35 °C follows; hence storage under water (cutting it under water too — it ignites on the knife).
    • Red phosphorus polymerises those tetrahedra: strain released, reactivity tamed.

    4.2 Why white P glows

    • Slow oxidation at the surface gives excited P₄O₆/P₂O₃ fragments that emit light — chemiluminescence, the old "phosphorescence" that named the element (light-bearer). The glow is cold light: no flame.

    4.3 Safety vs strike-anywhere matches

    • Safety match: red P on the box, KClO₃ + antimony sulphide + glue on the stick head — friction ignites the red P/KClO₃ pair only at the box.
    • Strike-anywhere: the oxidant and reducer both live in the head (P₄S₃ + KClO₃) — ignites on any rough surface. Red P's low reactivity makes the safety match possible.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "Red phosphorus is red because of impurities." The colour is structural — polymerised chains with different electronic structure, not dirt.
    • "White P is stored under kerosene." Under water — it must be excluded from air; it is also insoluble in water, which kerosene is not guaranteed against.
    • "Black phosphorus is just dirty red." Black P is the high-pressure, layered, semiconducting allotrope — the most stable form.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Name the allotropes of phosphorus and compare white and red phosphorus in structure, reactivity and uses. — Allotropes: white (yellow), red and black phosphorus. White phosphorus: discrete P₄ tetrahedra with bond angles strained to 60°; consequently extremely reactive — it ignites spontaneously in air at about 35 °C, is soluble in CS₂, poisonous (about 0.1 g is lethal), glows in the dark (chemiluminescence of slow oxidation) and must be stored under water. Uses: old strike-anywhere matches, rat poison, smoke screens. Red phosphorus: a polymeric form made by heating white P at 250–300 °C in an inert atmosphere; the strain is relieved, so it is far less reactive, non-poisonous, insoluble in CS₂ and safe to handle dry. Uses: the striking surface of safety matches, fireworks, InP semiconductors. Black phosphorus: layered like graphite, the most stable allotrope, a semiconductor (made under high pressure). The pattern: same element, less strain, more safety.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is white phosphorus kept under water, and what happens when red phosphorus is distilled above 400 °C? — White phosphorus ignites in air at about 35 °C because its P₄ tetrahedra carry severe bond-angle strain; it is stored (and cut) under water to exclude air — water also does not dissolve it. Distilling red phosphorus above 400 °C: the polymeric chains break up into P₄ vapour; on cooling, the vapour condenses back to white phosphorus. Allotropy here is reversible through the vapour phase — the P₄ unit is the thermodynamic root from which both forms grow.
  • NEB-style (2m): Explain why red phosphorus is used on the side of a safety-match box while white phosphorus was used in old strike-anywhere matches. — Safety match: the box side carries red phosphorus; the stick head carries the oxidant (KClO₃) with antimony sulphide and glue. Rubbing brings red P and chlorate into frictional contact: the red P ignites locally (it needs the friction heat — it does not ignite spontaneously), and the chlorate supplies oxygen to sustain the flame. Because the fuel and oxidant live apart, the match is safe in the pocket. Strike-anywhere matches: the head contains both reducer (P₄S₃) and oxidant, so any rough surface can light it — possible only because the mixture is stable until struck, but inherently more hazardous. Red P's low reactivity is what makes the safety design work.

Key Points

  • Phosphorus allotropes (names only per NEB): WHITE (yellow) P - waxy, discrete P4 tetrahedra with strained 60 deg angles, ignites ~35 C, stored under water, soluble in CS2, very poisonous (~0.1 g), garlic odour, glows in dark (chemiluminescence from slow oxidation); uses old matches, rat poison, smoke screens. RED P - polymeric chains, made by heating white P 250-300 C (inert/I2 trace), much less reactive, non-poisonous, insoluble in CS2; uses safety matches (box side), fireworks, InP semiconductors. BLACK P - layered like graphite, most stable, semiconductor, high pressure. Reversal: red -> white by distilling > 400 C (P4 re-forms in vapour). Safety match: red P on box + KClO3 on stick; strike-anywhere: P4S3 + KClO3 in head.

Summary

Phosphorus wears three structural masks. White P — waxy, discrete P₄ tetrahedra whose 60° angles strain every bond — ignites at 35 °C, glows in the dark, dissolves in CS₂, poisons at 0.1 g, and lives under water; it fed the old strike-anywhere matches, rat poisons and smoke screens. Red P — the same atoms polymerised into strain-free chains (250–300 °C conversion) — is safe, non-poisonous, CS₂-insoluble, and powers the safety match's box side plus fireworks and InP chips. Black P — layered like graphite, made under pressure — is the most stable and semiconducting. Distil red P above 400 °C and the P₄ vapour re-condenses as white: allotropy reversible through the vapour. The lesson of the pair: strain is reactivity, and polymerising the strain buys safety.
