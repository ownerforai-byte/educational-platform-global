Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Element That Builds Two Worlds

    Carbon: 4 electrons, 4 bonds, catenation without rival. Crystalline (diamond, graphite, fullerene) and amorphous (charcoal, coke, carbon black) forms; structure, general properties and uses of each.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Why carbon is special

    • Catenation: C–C bond 355 kJ/mol — strong enough for chains, rings, sheets and cages of thousands of atoms. Silicon's Si–Si (222 kJ/mol) barely chains.
    • Tetravalency + hybridisation: sp³, sp², sp give diamond, graphite and fullerene from the same atom.
    • Isotopes ¹²C (98.9%), ¹³C (1.1%, NMR), ¹⁴C (trace, dating, t½ 5730 y).

    2.2 Crystalline allotropes

    AllotropeHybridisationStructurePropertiesUses

    2.3 Amorphous forms

    • Charcoal (wood), animal charcoal (bone char), sugar charcoal, lamp black, coke, carbon black, gas carbon.
    • Amorphous = microcrystalline graphite; enormous surface area → adsorption.
    • Uses: charcoal — fuel, decolourising, gas masks (adsorbs gases), water filters; coke — reducing agent in metallurgy (blast furnace); carbon black — tyre filler, ink; gas carbon — electrodes.

    2.4 General properties of carbon

    • Inert at room temperature (high atomisation energy 715 kJ/mol); burns above 700 °C: C + O₂ → CO₂; reducing agent at high T (ZnO + C → Zn + CO).
    • Forms CO and CO₂; with S → CS₂; with Ca → CaC₂ (carbide).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Diamond vs graphite: hybridisation decides everything

    • sp³ gives 4 σ bonds in 3-D → rigid, hard, insulating (all electrons locked in bonds), best thermal conductor (vibrational transport).
    • sp² gives 3 σ bonds + one delocalised electron per atom in π clouds between layers → soft (layers slide), electrically conducting along layers, lubricating.
    • Same element, two engineering choices: lock the electrons (diamond) or free them (graphite).

    4.2 Fullerene: the third way

    • Discovered 1985 (Kroto, Curl, Smalley; Nobel 1996). C₆₀ = truncated icosahedron; the pentagons force curvature, closing the sheet into a ball. All carbons sp² but strained — hence reactivity at the 6,6 junctions, ability to add atoms inside (endohedral metallofullerenes).

    4.3 Amorphous carbon's superpower

    • "Amorphous" carbons are graphite micro-crystals in disorder — the disorder creates porosity, and porosity creates surface area (good charcoal ≈ 1000 m²/g). Adsorption, not reaction, is their job: gas masks, decolourising sugar, water filters.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    • "Graphite is amorphous." No — it is crystalline (X-ray proves sharp layers); charcoal and soot are the amorphous ones.
    • "Diamond conducts electricity because it is pure carbon." Diamond is an insulator; graphite conducts. Purity is irrelevant — hybridisation decides.
    • "C₆₀ has only hexagons." A closed ball of hexagons is impossible; exactly 12 pentagons are required (Euler's rule) — 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons.

  • 7. Exam Application

    • The diamond-graphite contrast (hybridisation → hardness/conductivity) is the classic 3-mark question.
    • Quote numbers: C–C 355 kJ/mol, densities 3.51 vs 2.25, hardness 10.
    • Amorphous uses: tie each to adsorption or reduction.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Compare diamond and graphite with respect to structure, electrical conductivity and hardness, explaining the differences. — Structure: diamond is sp³-hybridised carbon in a 3-D tetrahedral network, every atom bonded to four neighbours by strong σ bonds (bond length 154 pm); graphite is sp²-hybridised carbon in planar hexagonal layers, each atom bonded to three neighbours with the fourth electron delocalised over the layer, layers held together only by weak van der Waals forces (separation 340 pm). Conductivity: in diamond all valence electrons are locked in σ bonds — it is an insulator; in graphite one electron per atom roams the layer — it conducts electricity along the planes. Hardness: diamond's 3-D network must be broken in every direction to deform it — hardest natural solid (Mohs 10); graphite's layers slide over each other — soft and greasy (a lubricant and pencil "lead"). Same element, different hybridisation, opposite materials.
  • NEB-style (2m): What is buckminsterfullerene? Describe its structure and give one use. — C₆₀, discovered in 1985 (Kroto, Curl, Smalley — Nobel Prize 1996), is a football-shaped molecule about 0.7 nm across: a truncated icosahedron of 20 hexagons and 12 pentagons, all carbons sp²-hybridised but strained at the pentagon junctions, which force the sheet to close into a sphere. Atoms or small molecules can be trapped inside the cage (endohedral fullerenes), and alkali-metal salts of C₆₀ show superconductivity. Use: research in nanotechnology — drug delivery cages, nanoscale electronics, and lubricant additives.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is charcoal used in gas masks and sugar refining? — Charcoal is amorphous carbon: disordered micro-crystals of graphite riddled with pores, giving an enormous internal surface area (good activated charcoal ≈ 1000 m²/g). Gas molecules and coloured impurities are held on these surfaces by adsorption (van der Waals + partial chemical attraction). In a gas mask the charcoal adsorbs toxic gases while air passes through; in sugar refining it adsorbs the coloured impurities from brown sugar syrup, which is then filtered off to leave white sugar. The working principle is surface capture, not chemical reaction.

Key Points

  • Carbon: tetravalent, catenation champion (C-C 355 kJ/mol), isotopes C-12/C-13/C-14 (t1/2 5730 y). Crystalline allotropes: DIAMOND sp3 3-D tetrahedral network - hardest natural solid (10), thermal conductor, insulator, RI 2.42, density 3.51; uses cutting/jewellery/heat sinks. GRAPHITE sp2 hexagonal layers with weak van der Waals between - soft/slippery, conducts along layers (1 delocalised e- per C), density 2.25; uses pencils/electrodes/lubricant/nuclear moderator. FULLERENE C60 (1985, Nobel 1996) sp2 strained ball of 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons, ~0.7 nm, semiconductor, endohedral doping. Amorphous: charcoal, coke, carbon black, lamp black, gas carbon - microcrystalline graphite, huge surface area (~1000 m2/g) -> adsorption; uses gas masks, decolourising, water filters (charcoal), metallurgical reducer (coke), tyre filler (carbon black), electrodes (gas carbon). Inert at RT (atomisation 715 kJ/mol), burns > 700 C, reduces ZnO.

Summary

Carbon is the element of two worlds. Crystalline: diamond (sp³, 3-D tetrahedral network — hardest natural solid, thermal-conduction champion, electrical insulator, RI 2.42, for cutting tools and jewellery), graphite (sp², hexagonal layers with one delocalised electron per atom — soft, slippery, conducting along planes, for electrodes, pencils, lubricants and nuclear moderators), and C₆₀ buckminsterfullerene (1985; a 0.7 nm ball of 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons, semiconductor, endohedral cages for nanotech). Amorphous: charcoal, coke, carbon black, lamp black, gas carbon — disordered microcrystalline graphite whose porosity gives ~1000 m²/g of adsorbing surface (gas masks, decolourising, water filters) or whose cheapness makes it the blast furnace's reducing agent (coke) and the tyre's filler (carbon black). Behind it all: catenation (C–C 355 kJ/mol) and tetravalency — the strongest self-bonding in chemistry, giving carbon more compounds than everything else combined.
