Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Reactant That Runs Out First

    Real reactions mix measured amounts of two reactants, and they almost never match the stoichiometric ratio. The reactant consumed completely first — the limiting reactant — decides how far the reaction goes and how much product forms. Everything left over is the excess reactant.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Identification algorithm

    1. Balance the equation.
    2. Convert each reactant's amount to moles.
    3. Divide each by its coefficient (mol ÷ coefficient).
    4. The smallest quotient marks the limiting reactant; all product yields follow from it.

    2.2 Worked numericals

    Q (NEB 2023, 3m): 4.8 g of magnesium is burnt in 6.4 g of oxygen. Identify the limiting reactant and calculate the mass of MgO formed. (Mg = 24, O = 16)

    • 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
    • n(Mg) = 4.8/24 = 0.20 mol → 0.20/2 = 0.10
    • n(O₂) = 6.4/32 = 0.20 mol → 0.20/1 = 0.20
    • Mg has the smaller quotient → Mg is limiting
    • n(MgO) = n(Mg) = 0.20 mol → m = 0.20 × 40 = 8.0 g MgO (O₂ left: 0.20 − 0.10 = 0.10 mol = 3.2 g)

    Q (NEB 2024, 3m): 10 g of H₂ reacts with 64 g of O₂ to form water. Which is the limiting reagent, and what is the maximum mass of water formed?

    • 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
    • n(H₂) = 10/2 = 5 mol → 5/2 = 2.5
    • n(O₂) = 64/32 = 2 mol → 2/1 = 2
    • O₂ has the smaller quotient → O₂ is limiting
    • n(H₂O) = 2 × 2 = 4 mol → m = 4 × 18 = 72 g water (H₂ left: 5 − 4 = 1 mol = 2 g)

    Q (NEB-style): 28 g of N₂ reacts with 6 g of H₂ (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃). Find the limiting reactant and mass of NH₃ formed. (N = 14, H = 1)

    • n(N₂) = 28/28 = 1 mol → 1/1 = 1; n(H₂) = 6/2 = 3 mol → 3/3 = 1 — equal quotients: neither is in excess; both fully consumed
    • n(NH₃) = 2 mol → m = 2 × 17 = 34 g NH₃

    2.3 Limiting vs excess reactant — similarities and differences

    Similarities: both are reactants consumed by the same reaction; both are measured in moles before comparing. Differences: the limiting reactant is completely consumed and sets the product ceiling; the excess reactant is partly left over and its leftover mass is recoverable. Product yield is calculated from the limiting reactant only — never from the excess.

    2.4 Why the concept matters

    • Industrial economics: you feed the cheaper reactant in excess to burn up the expensive one.
    • Air is free: combustion problems always have O₂ in excess, fuel limiting.
    • Pollution: incomplete consumption of the excess (e.g. unburnt fuel) causes emissions.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To mole calculations (Topic 5): the identification algorithm is two mole hops plus a comparison.
    • To yield (Topic 7): theoretical yield is computed from the limiting reactant.
    • To equilibrium (Unit 8): limiting vs excess sets initial concentrations.

Confusion

  • Limiting vs excess: limiting = runs out first, controls yield; excess = left over, irrelevant to yield.
  • "Smallest quotient" vs "smallest moles": 0.2 mol with coefficient 1 beats 0.2 mol with coefficient 2 — always divide by the coefficient first.

Practice

  • In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 3 mol H₂ and 1 mol O₂, which is limiting and how much H₂ remains?

Formulas

    • Quotient = n(reactant) ÷ coefficient
    • Product moles = limiting moles × (product coefficient ÷ limiting coefficient)
    • Excess left = n(initial) − n(consumed)

Universal Facts

  • In industrial ammonia synthesis, H₂ is usually fed slightly in excess because N₂ from air is cheaper.

Examples

  • 10 g H₂ + 64 g O₂ — O₂ limiting (quotient 2 vs 2.5): 72 g water maximum, 2 g H₂ unreacted (NEB 2024).

Summary

The limiting reactant — identified by dividing each reactant's moles by its equation coefficient and taking the smallest quotient — is completely consumed and caps the product yield; the excess reactant is partly left over. 4.8 g Mg + 6.4 g O₂: Mg limiting → 8.0 g MgO (NEB 2023). 10 g H₂ + 64 g O₂: O₂ limiting → 72 g H₂O with 2 g H₂ left (NEB 2024). Product calculations must always start from the limiting reactant, never the excess.
