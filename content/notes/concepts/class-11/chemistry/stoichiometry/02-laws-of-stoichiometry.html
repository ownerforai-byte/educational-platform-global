Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Rules the Mole Obey

    Before any calculation, chemistry agrees on five quantitative laws: mass is conserved, composition is definite, ratios are multiple, equivalents are reciprocal, and reacting gas volumes are simple. Together with Avogadro's law they are the laws of stoichiometry — the grammar of every balanced equation.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The five laws at a glance

    LawScientistStatement
    Conservation of massLavoisier (1789)Total mass of reactants = total mass of products in a chemical reaction
    Definite proportionsProust (1799)A pure compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed proportion by mass
    Multiple proportionsDalton (1803)When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one combining with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple whole-number ratio
    Reciprocal proportionsRichter (1792)Two elements combining separately with a fixed mass of a third combine with each other in the same ratio or a simple multiple of it
    Gay-Lussac's gaseous volumesGay-Lussac (1808)Gases react in simple whole-number volume ratios at the same temperature and pressure

    2.2 Worked illustrations

    • Definite proportions: every sample of CuO is 79.9% Cu / 20.1% O — from any source, any method.
    • Multiple proportions: C and O form CO (C:O = 12:16) and CO₂ (12:32); oxygen masses per 12 g C are 16:32 = 1:2.
    • Multiple proportions: N forms NO (14:16), NO₂ (14:32), N₂O (28:16 = 14:8); oxygen per 14 g N = 8, 16, 32 → 1:2:4.
    • Reciprocal proportions: H:O = 1:8 in H₂O; H:N = 3:14 in NH₃; then N:O in NO = 14:8 — (3/14 ÷ 1/8) consistent with a simple multiple.
    • Gay-Lussac: 2 volumes H₂ + 1 volume O₂ → 2 volumes steam (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O(g)); 1:2:2.

    2.3 Similarities and differences — definite vs multiple proportions

    Similarities: both are mass laws; both hold for pure compounds; both were explained by Dalton's atomic theory. Differences: definite proportions concerns one compound (fixed composition of H₂O everywhere); multiple proportions concerns two or more compounds of the same pair of elements (CO vs CO₂) and compares ratios between compounds.

    2.4 Similarities and differences — conservation of mass vs Gay-Lussac's law

    Similarities: both describe reacting amounts; both are experimentally discovered regularities. Differences: conservation of mass applies to mass in all reactions (nuclear reactions excepted); Gay-Lussac applies to gas volumes only, at the same T and P, and is explained not by mass but by Avogadro's molecule counting.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Dalton (Topic 1): the atomic theory was built to explain the first three laws.
    • To Avogadro (Topic 3): Avogadro's hypothesis reconciled Gay-Lussac's volume ratios with Dalton's atomism.
    • To the mole (Topic 4): the laws justify why balanced-equation coefficients are mole ratios.

Summary

The laws of stoichiometry — conservation of mass (Lavoisier), definite proportions (Proust), multiple proportions (Dalton), reciprocal proportions (Richter) and Gay-Lussac's gaseous volumes — fix how much of each element reacts. Definite proportions fixes one compound's composition; multiple proportions compares ratios across compounds of the same element pair (CO 1:2 vs CO₂ with oxygen per 12 g C). Mass is conserved in every reaction, while gas volumes react in simple ratios only at the same T and P — the fact Avogadro's hypothesis explains.

Confusion
  • Definite vs multiple proportions: definite = one compound, one fixed ratio; multiple = several compounds of the same two elements, ratio of ratios.
  • Gay-Lussac's law vs Avogadro's law: Gay-Lussac compares reacting volumes; Avogadro explains it — equal volumes contain equal molecules.
Key Points

Important Concepts
  • Fixed-mass reference — in multiple and reciprocal proportions, always divide by the mass of the fixed element before comparing.
