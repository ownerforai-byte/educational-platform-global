Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Reading a Formula Backwards

    Percentage composition is chemistry's fingerprint: measure what percentage of a compound's mass each element occupies, and the empirical formula (simplest whole-number atom ratio) follows by arithmetic. Multiply by n = M/empirical-mass and the molecular formula — the true atom count — appears. NEB has asked this three years running (2020, 2022, 2024).

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The two formulas

    FormulaDefinitionExample (glucose)
    EmpiricalSimplest whole-number ratio of atomsCH₂O
    MolecularActual number of atoms per moleculeC₆H₁₂O₆

    Molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ, where n = molecular mass ÷ empirical-formula mass.

    2.2 The four-step ritual (percentage → empirical formula)

    1. Assume 100 g of compound — percentages become grams.
    2. Convert to moles: divide each mass by the element's atomic mass.
    3. Divide by the smallest mole value.
    4. Clear fractions — multiply through by the smallest integer that makes all values whole (0.5 → ×2; 0.33 → ×3; 0.25 → ×4; 0.67 → ×3).

    2.3 Worked numericals

    Q (NEB 2022 & 2020, 3m): A compound contains 40% C, 6.67% H and 53.33% O by mass. Calculate its empirical formula.

    • Per 100 g: C = 40/12 = 3.33 mol; H = 6.67/1 = 6.67 mol; O = 53.33/16 = 3.33 mol
    • Divide by 3.33: C : H : O = 1 : 2 : 1
    • Empirical formula = CH₂O

    Q (NEB 2024, 2m): A compound has empirical formula CH₂O and molar mass 180 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

    • Empirical mass = 12 + 2(1) + 16 = 30
    • n = 180/30 = 6
    • Molecular formula = (CH₂O)₆ = C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose)

    Q (NEB-style): An oxide of iron contains 69.9% Fe and 30.1% O. Find its empirical formula. (Fe = 56, O = 16)

    • n(Fe) = 69.9/56 = 1.248; n(O) = 30.1/16 = 1.881
    • Divide by 1.248: Fe : O = 1 : 1.5 → ×2 → Fe₂O₃ (haematite)

    2.4 Combustion analysis (organic compounds)

    Burn a known mass of the compound; weigh the CO₂ and H₂O produced.

    • moles of C = moles of CO₂ absorbed
    • moles of H = 2 × moles of H₂O
    • oxygen in the compound = total mass − (mass C + mass H)

    Then apply the same four-step ritual.

    2.5 Similarities and differences — empirical vs molecular formula

    Similarities: both express atom ratios; both are derived from the same percentage data; both identify the elements present. Differences: the empirical formula is the simplest ratio (CH₂O) and is unique to the ratio, not the substance; the molecular formula is the actual count per molecule (C₆H₁₂O₆) and identifies the real compound. Different substances can share an empirical formula (glucose C₆H₁₂O₆, formaldehyde CH₂O, acetic acid C₂H₄O₂ — all CH₂O) but never a molecular formula. The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical one; they coincide when n = 1.

    • To the mole (Topic 4): the entire ritual is mole arithmetic.

    2. Using the molar mass as the empirical mass when computing n.
    3. Rounding 2.01 to 3 instead of 2 — small deviations are experimental noise.

Confusion

  • Empirical mass vs molecular mass: empirical mass = mass of the simplest unit (CH₂O = 30); molecular mass = mass of the true molecule (C₆H₁₂O₆ = 180). n = 180/30 = 6.
  • Ratio vs substance: CH₂O is the empirical formula of glucose, formaldehyde AND acetic acid — the empirical formula alone cannot identify a compound.

Summary

The empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number atom ratio; the molecular formula gives the true atom count and equals the empirical formula multiplied by n = molecular mass ÷ empirical mass. The four-step ritual — assume 100 g, convert to moles, divide by the smallest, clear fractions (0.5→×2, 0.33→×3, 0.25→×4) — turns percentage composition into the empirical formula: 40% C, 6.67% H, 53.33% O → CH₂O (NEB 2020, 2022); with M = 180, n = 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆ (NEB 2024). Combustion analysis extends the method to organics via CO₂ and H₂O masses. Many substances share one empirical formula; only the molecular formula identifies the compound.
