Notes

  • Focus of this tab

  • The first law of chemical combination in depth — statement, historical verification, microscopic explanation and its one important limitation.

  • Statement and history

  • 'In a chemical reaction, matter can neither be created nor destroyed; the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.' Established by Antoine Lavoisier (1774) from careful weighings — e.g. mercury calx (HgO) heated in a sealed retort gave mercury and oxygen with no loss of mass — the work that made chemistry quantitative.

  • Experimental verification (Landolt, 1893)

  • Landolt placed two reacting solutions (AgNO₃ and NaCl) in separate limbs of an H-shaped sealed tube, weighed it, inverted to mix, waited for complete precipitation (AgCl), and reweighed — mass unchanged within 1 part in 10⁷. Modern verification uses any precipitation, neutralisation or redox reaction carried out in a sealed system.

  • Microscopic explanation

  • A reaction only rearranges atoms: bonds break and form, but no atom disappears or appears (Dalton's third postulate). The equation must therefore be balanced — atom counts, and hence masses, are equal on both sides. This is why balancing is the first step of every stoichiometric calculation.

  • What must be included in the weighing

  • All gaseous reactants/products (CO₂ escaping an open flask breaks the balance), the solvent, the precipitate, and the container. The classic school error: burning magnesium on an open balance shows a mass gain — oxygen has entered; in a sealed flask the mass is constant.

  • Limitation (important)

  • The law holds for chemical reactions only. In nuclear reactions (fission, fusion, radioactivity) a measurable mass defect converts to energy: E = Δmc² — mass and energy are jointly conserved. Even in chemistry the energy change has a mass equivalent, but it is ≈10⁻¹⁰ of the reacting mass, far below weighing precision.

  • Applications

  • Balancing equations; material balances in industry (input = output accounting for ammonia, cement, steel plants); gravimetric analysis; detecting loss of product (leaks, volatilisation); and every 'how much product' calculation in stoichiometry.

Confusion

  • ❌ Magnesium burning in air contradicts the law because the ash is heavier. ✅ The extra mass is the oxygen that combined; in a closed system the total is constant.
  • ❌ The law applies to nuclear reactions. ✅ It is limited to chemical reactions; nuclear processes show mass–energy equivalence.
  • ❌ A balanced equation conserves molecules. ✅ It conserves atoms and mass; molecular counts may change (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: 4 molecules → 2).

Formulas

  • ∑mreactants​=∑mproducts​;E=Δmc2 (nuclear limit)

Universal Facts

  • Landolt's sealed-tube experiments confirmed conservation to 1 part in 10⁷.
  • Lavoisier's mercury(II) oxide decomposition was the founding quantitative experiment of chemistry.
  • In the burning of 12 g of carbon, exactly 44 g of CO₂ is formed — conservation in one line.

Examples

  • Problem: 2.16 g of Ag₂O is completely decomposed; 2.00 g of silver remains. Find the mass of oxygen evolved. Solution: m(O₂) = 2.16 − 2.00 = 0.16 g — the mass balance of the law applied directly.

Important Notes

  • Use 'total mass of reactants = total mass of products' verbatim in the statement.
  • Quote Landolt's sealed tube as the verification and E = Δmc² as the limitation.
