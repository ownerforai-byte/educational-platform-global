  • Theoretical yield, experimental yield and % yield

    1. Topic Introduction

    Theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product that can be formed from the limiting reactant assuming 100% conversion — the stoichiometric ceiling. Experimental (actual) yield is what you actually isolate in the lab or plant. The gap between them is quantified as percentage yield — the single most important efficiency metric in chemical industry and lab practice.

    Why it matters. Every industrial process (Haber ammonia, Contact sulphuric acid, cement, pharmaceuticals) is judged by its yield. A 1% yield improvement in a million-tonne plant saves millions of dollars. In the lab, % yield tells you how clean your technique was; in exams, it's a guaranteed numerical problem combining limiting reactant and purity concepts.

    What you will learn: (i) definitions of theoretical, experimental, and % yield; (ii) calculation from limiting reactant; (iii) effect of impurities (% purity) on yield; (iv) why actual < theoretical (six standard reasons); (v) % yield > 100% diagnosis; (vi) atom economy vs % yield distinction.

    Learning outcomes. You can: calculate theoretical yield from limiting reactant; compute % yield from experimental data; correct for % purity before yield calculation; explain why actual yield < theoretical; diagnose % yield > 100%; distinguish atom economy from % yield.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding

    What theoretical yield is

    The maximum amount of product that can be obtained from a given amount of limiting reactant, assuming the reaction goes to completion (100% conversion) with no side reactions or losses. It is a calculated ideal — the stoichiometric maximum.

    Why it exists

    Reactions are never perfect. Side reactions, equilibrium limitations, mechanical losses, and purification steps all reduce the amount of product you can actually hold in your hand. Theoretical yield gives the benchmark against which real performance is measured.

    How it works

    From the balanced equation and the limiting reactant's moles, calculate the maximum moles of product possible using the mole ratio. Convert to mass using the product's molar mass. This is the theoretical yield.

    Where it occurs

    Every chemical process: lab synthesis, industrial reactor (Haber, Contact, Solvay), pharmaceutical manufacturing, food processing, environmental remediation.

    What its components are

    Balanced equation, limiting reactant moles, mole ratio (product:limiting), product molar mass, theoretical yield (mass), actual yield (mass), % yield.

    How components interact

    Limiting reactant moles × (product coeff / limiting coeff) = theoretical product moles → × M_product = theoretical yield (g). Actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100 = % yield. Impure reactant: correct mass first (pure mass = impure mass × %purity/100).

    What makes it biologically important

    Enzyme yield: theoretical = 1 product per substrate; actual < theoretical due to side reactions, inhibition. Metabolic yield: ATP per glucose theoretical = 38; actual ~30-32 due to proton leak, shuttle costs. Photosynthetic yield: theoretical 12% PAR; actual 3-6% due to photoinhibition, respiration.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Theoretical yield calculation (step-by-step)

    1. Balance the chemical equation.
    2. Identify the limiting reactant (mole/coefficient method).
    3. Calculate moles of limiting reactant (apply purity correction if needed: pure mass = impure mass × %purity/100).
    4. Use mole ratio from balanced equation: n_product = n_limiting × (coeff_product / coeff_limiting).
    5. Convert to mass: theoretical yield = n_product × M_product.

    4.2 Percentage yield formula

    % yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100

    • Normal range: 0–100%
    • 0% = no product formed
    • 100% = perfect conversion (ideal, rarely achieved)
    • >100% = product impure, wet, or contains by-product

    4.3 Worked example 1: Thermal decomposition of limestone

    Reaction: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) Given: 100 g CaCO₃ (M = 100 g/mol) → 47.6 g CaO (M = 56 g/mol)

    • n_CaCO₃ = 100/100 = 1 mol (limiting, only reactant)
    • n_CaO theoretical = 1 mol × (1/1) = 1 mol
    • Theoretical yield = 1 mol × 56 g/mol = 56 g
    • Actual yield = 47.6 g
    • % yield = (47.6 / 56) × 100 = 85%

    4.3 Worked example 2: Ethene hydration

    Reaction: C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) → C₂H₅OH(l) Given: 0.25 mol C₂H₄, excess steam → 9.2 g ethanol obtained (M = 46 g/mol)

    • n_C₂H₄ = 0.25 mol (limiting)
    • n_ethanol theoretical = 0.25 mol × (1/1) = 0.25 mol
    • Theoretical yield = 0.25 × 46 = 11.5 g
    • Actual yield = 9.2 g
    • % yield = (9.2 / 11.5) × 100 = 80%

    4.4 Worked example 3: With impure reactant

    Reaction: 2NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl Given: 10 g impure NaCl (80% pure), excess H₂SO₄ → 8.5 g Na₂SO₄ obtained (M_Na₂SO₄ = 142, M_NaCl = 58.5)

    • Pure NaCl mass = 10 × 0.80 = 8 g
    • n_NaCl = 8 / 58.5 = 0.1368 mol (limiting)
    • n_Na₂SO₄ theoretical = 0.1368 × (1/2) = 0.0684 mol
    • Theoretical yield = 0.0684 × 142 = 9.71 g
    • Actual yield = 8.5 g
    • % yield = (8.5 / 9.71) × 100 = 87.5%

    4.5 Six standard reasons actual yield < theoretical yield

    ReasonExplanationExample
    1. Side reactionsReactants form undesired by-productsCombustion: CO forms instead of CO₂ if O₂ limited
    2. Equilibrium limitationReversible reaction doesn't go to completionHaber process: NH₃ ⇌ N₂ + 3H₂ at high T
    3. Mechanical lossesTransfer, filtration, distillation lossesCrystals left on filter paper, liquid in flask
    4. Purification lossesProduct discarded with impuritiesRecrystallisation mother liquor contains product
    5. Incomplete reactionReaction stops before limiting reactant consumedSlow kinetics, insufficient time, low T
    6. Volatilisation/decompositionProduct evaporates or decomposes on heatingNH₄Cl sublimes; H₂O₂ decomposes on heating

    4.6 % yield > 100% — diagnosis

    If a student reports % yield > 100%, the product is impure or wet. Common causes:

    • Residual solvent (water, ethanol, ether) not fully dried
    • By-product co-precipitated with product
    • Reactant impurity not removed (e.g., unreacted starting material)
    • Balance error (zero error, wrong tare)
    • Hygroscopic product absorbing moisture from air

    Correct answer in exam: "% yield > 100% indicates the product is impure (contains solvent/by-product) or wet (not fully dried); true yield cannot exceed 100%."

  • 6. Chemical Processes and Mechanisms

    Process: Yield determination (lab)

    1. Definition: Determining the experimental yield of a reaction product.
    2. Location: Laboratory synthesis, quality control lab.
    3. Requirements: Balanced equation, limiting reactant mass, product isolation procedure.
    4. Components: Reactants, product, purification steps (filtration, recrystallisation, distillation), analytical balance.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Weigh limiting reactant (apply purity); (ii) Run reaction to completion; (iii) Isolate product (filter, distil, extract); (iv) Purify (recrystallise, dry); (v) Weigh pure product; (vi) Calculate theoretical yield; (vii) Compute % yield.
    6. Result: Experimental yield (g), % yield (%), purity assessment.
    7. Significance: Core lab skill; directly tests stoichiometry understanding.

    Process: Yield optimisation (industrial)

    1. Definition: Maximising % yield while minimising cost, energy, and waste.
    2. Location: Chemical plant (reactor, separator, recycle loop).
    3. Requirements: Reaction kinetics, equilibrium data, catalyst, separation technology, economics.
    4. Components: Reactor, separator, recycle loop, purge stream, feed pre-treatment.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Optimise T, P, catalyst for kinetics/equilibrium; (ii) Use excess cheap reactant to drive limiting reactant conversion; (iii) Separate product; (iv) Recycle unreacted limiting reactant; (v) Purge inerts; (vi) Monitor yield and adjust.
    6. Result: High per-pass conversion + high overall yield via recycle.
    7. Significance: 1% yield gain in million-tonne plant = millions $/year; also reduces waste (E-factor).

  • 10. Topic Mastery Check

    Important definitions

    1. Theoretical yield — maximum product mass from limiting reactant at 100% conversion.
    2. Experimental (actual) yield — product mass actually isolated and weighed.
    3. Percentage yield — (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100.
    4. Atom economy — MW(desired product) / ΣMW(all reactants) × 100.
    5. Side reactions — competing reactions forming undesired by-products.
    6. Equilibrium yield — maximum yield at equilibrium for reversible reactions.
    7. Mechanical losses — product lost during handling (filtration, transfer, distillation).
    8. Purification losses — product discarded with impurities during recrystallisation/chromatography.

    Key concepts to remember

    • Theoretical yield from limiting reactant only: n_limiting × mole ratio × M_product.
    • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100; ≤ 100% for pure dry product.
    • >100% yield = impure product (solvent, by-product) or wet (not fully dried).
    • Six reasons actual < theoretical: side reactions, equilibrium, mechanical losses, purification losses, incomplete reaction, volatilisation/decomposition.
    • Purity correction: pure mass = impure mass × (%/100) BEFORE mole calculation.
    • Atom economy ≠ % yield — economy is theoretical (stoichiometry), yield is experimental (practice).
    • Multi-step overall yield = product of step yields (e.g., 5 steps × 90% = 59%).

    Important classifications

    • Yield types: Theoretical (ideal), Actual (isolated), % (efficiency).
    • Loss categories: Side reactions, equilibrium, mechanical, purification, incomplete, volatilisation.
    • Efficiency metrics: % yield (practical), atom economy (theoretical), E-factor (waste), PMI (total mass).

    Common conceptual confusions

    • % yield > 100% is valid for efficient reactions — WRONG (means impure/wet).
    • Atom economy and % yield are the same — WRONG (economy theoretical, yield experimental).
    • Forgetting purity correction before yield calc — WRONG (impurities don't react).
    • Assuming 100% yield is achievable — WRONG (equilibrium, side reactions, losses prevent it).
    • % yield applies to reversible reactions without modification — WRONG (equilibrium limits max).

    2. % yield = (Actual ÷ Theoretical) × 100.

Confusion

  • ❌ % yield > 100% is valid for efficient reactions. ✅ Means product impure (solvent/by-product) or wet (not fully dried).

  • ❌ 100% yield is achievable in practice. ✅ Equilibrium, side reactions, losses prevent it.
  • ❌ % yield applies to reversible reactions without modification. ✅ Equilibrium limits max yield; use equilibrium yield instead.
  • ❌ Multi-step yield = average of step yields. ✅ Overall = PRODUCT of step yields (multiplicative).

  • Theoretical yield = n_limiting × (coeff_P / coeff_L) × M_P
  • % yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100
  • n_limiting = pure mass / M_limiting
  • pure mass = impure mass × (%purity / 100)
  • Atom economy = MW(product) / Σ MW(reactants) × 100
  • Overall yield (multi-step) = ∏ step yields
  • E-factor = total waste (kg) / product (kg)
  • Conversion = reactant consumed / reactant fed

Numericals

  • CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂: 100 g → 56 g theoretical; 47.6 g actual → 85% yield.
  • C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH: 0.25 mol → 11.5 g theoretical; 9.2 g actual → 80% yield.
  • Impure CaCO₃ (80%): 5 g impure → 4 g pure → 0.04 mol → 2.24 g CaO theoretical; 90% yield → 2.016 g actual.
  • 5-step pharma at 90% each: 0.9^5 = 59% overall.
  • Haber: per-pass 15% → recycle → overall 98%.

Key Points

  • Theoretical yield from limiting reactant: n_lim × ratio × M_P.
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100; ≤ 100% for pure dry product.
  • Six reasons actual < theoretical: side reactions, equilibrium, mechanical, purification, incomplete, volatilisation.

  • 100% yield = impure/wet product; true yield ≤ 100%.

  • Purity correction: pure mass = impure mass × (%/100) BEFORE mole calc.
  • Atom economy (theoretical) ≠ % yield (experimental).
  • Multi-step overall yield = product of step yields.

Summary

Theoretical yield = n_limiting × mole ratio × M_product; % yield = (actual/theoretical)×100. Actual < theoretical due to: side reactions, equilibrium, mechanical losses, purification losses, incomplete reaction, volatilisation. % yield > 100% = impure/wet product. Purity correction (impure mass × %/100) before mole calc. Atom economy ≠ % yield — economy theoretical (stoichiometry), yield experimental (practice). Multi-step overall yield = product of step yields. Figures: yield flow, yield gap, Haber recycle, pharma cascade.
