  • Calculation of empirical and molecular formula from % composition (solving related numerical problems)

    1. Topic Introduction

    A compound's identity is locked in its molecular formula — the actual number of each atom in one molecule. But elemental analysis only gives percentage composition — the mass % of each element. The bridge from % to formula is the empirical formula (simplest whole-number ratio) followed by the molecular formula (actual atom count) using the molar mass. This topic is the reverse of percentage composition: % → formula instead of formula → %.

    Why it matters. Every unknown compound — a new drug metabolite, an environmental pollutant, a natural product, a forensic trace — is identified by this exact workflow: combustion analysis → %C, %H, %N, %S → empirical formula → M_r (from vapour density or mass spec) → molecular formula. It is the detective work of chemistry.

    What you will learn: (i) empirical formula from % composition (forward algorithm); (ii) molecular formula from empirical + M_r; (iii) combustion analysis route (%C from CO₂, %H from H₂O); (iv) vapour density method for M_r; (v) hydrated salt water of crystallisation; (v) common traps and how to avoid them.

    Learning outcomes. You can: derive empirical formula from % data; obtain molecular formula from empirical + M_r; solve combustion analysis problems; use vapour density (M_r = 2×VD) to find molecular formula; determine water of crystallisation in hydrates.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding

    What empirical formula is

    The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. It is the formula reduced to lowest terms (like a fraction). CH₂O is the empirical formula of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), formaldehyde (CH₂O), and lactic acid (C₃H₆O₃) — same ratio, different molecules.

    Why it exists

    Elemental analysis gives mass percentages. Converting % → grams → moles → simplest ratio gives the empirical formula. It is the first step in identifying an unknown compound from analytical data.

    How it works

    % composition → assume 100 g sample → grams of each element = % → convert to moles (÷ A_r) → divide all by smallest mole value → simplest whole-number ratio → empirical formula. If M_r known: n = M_r/E_r → molecular formula = n × empirical.

    Where it occurs

    Organic analysis lab (combustion analyser), pharmaceutical structure elucidation, forensic toxicology, environmental pollutant ID, natural product isolation, quality control (assay of hydrated salts).

    What its components are

    % composition data (C, H, O, N, S, halogens), atomic masses (A_r), mole values, simplest ratio, empirical formula, empirical formula mass (E_r), molar mass (M_r), multiplier n = M_r/E_r, molecular formula.

    How components interact

    % → grams (per 100 g) → moles (÷ A_r) → ratio (÷ smallest, clear decimals ×2/×3) → empirical formula → E_r → M_r (given or measured) → n = M_r/E_r → molecular = n × empirical.

    What makes it biologically important

    Drug metabolite ID: unknown metabolite → LC-MS → % composition → empirical → M_r (HRMS) → molecular formula → structure. Metabolomics: thousands of unknowns → formula first, structure later. Forensic: trace evidence → elemental analysis → formula → database match.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Empirical formula from % composition (the standard algorithm)

    Step 1: % → grams. Assume 100 g sample → % = grams of each element. Step 2: Grams → moles. Divide each gram value by its atomic mass (A_r). Step 3: Moles → simplest ratio. Divide all mole values by the smallest. If decimals remain, multiply ALL by 2, 3, 4... to clear to whole numbers. Step 4: Write empirical formula from the whole-number ratio.

    Worked example 1 — standard % data:

    • Data: 40% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O
    • Per 100 g: C 40 g, H 6.7 g, O 53.3 g
    • Moles: C = 40/12 = 3.33; H = 6.7/1 = 6.7; O = 53.3/16 = 3.33
    • Ratio ÷ 3.33: C 1, H 2, O 1 → CH₂O (E_r = 30)

    Worked example 2 — with decimals needing multiplication:

    • Data: 26.6% K, 35.4% Cr, 38.0% O
    • Per 100 g: K 26.6 g, Cr 35.4 g, O 38.0 g
    • Moles: K = 26.6/39 = 0.682; Cr = 35.4/52 = 0.681; O = 38.0/16 = 2.375
    • Ratio ÷ 0.681: K 1.00, Cr 1.00, O 3.49 ≈ 3.5
    • Multiply by 2: K 2, Cr 2, O 7 → K₂Cr₂O₇ (potassium dichromate)

    Worked example 3 — near-half decimals:

    • Data: 32.4% Na, 22.5% S, 45.1% O
    • Per 100 g: Na 32.4, S 22.5, O 45.1
    • Moles: Na = 32.4/23 = 1.409; S = 22.5/32 = 0.703; O = 45.1/16 = 2.819
    • Ratio ÷ 0.703: Na 2.00, S 1.00, O 4.01 → Na₂SO₄

    4.2 Molecular formula from empirical + M_r

    Formula: n = M_r / E_r (must be integer); Molecular formula = n × empirical formula.

    Worked example 4 — glucose:

    • Empirical: CH₂O (E_r = 30)
    • M_r = 180 (given or from vapour density)
    • n = 180 / 30 = 6
    • Molecular = 6 × CH₂O = C₆H₁₂O₆

    Worked example 5 — same empirical, different M_r:

    • Empirical: CH₂O (E_r = 30)
    • If M_r = 30 → n = 1 → CH₂O (formaldehyde)
    • If M_r = 60 → n = 2 → C₂H₄O₂ (acetic acid)
    • If M_r = 90 → n = 3 → C₃H₆O₃ (lactic acid)
    • If M_r = 120 → n = 4 → C₄H₈O₄ (erythrose)
    • If M_r = 150 → n = 5 → C₅H₁₀O₅ (ribose)
    • If M_r = 180 → n = 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose)

    Key insight: Same empirical formula (CH₂O) can represent completely different compounds — M_r is essential to distinguish them.

    4.3 Combustion analysis (the experimental source of %C and %H)

    Principle: C_xH_yO_z + (x + y/4 − z/2)O₂ → xCO₂ + (y/2)H₂O

    • All carbon in sample → CO₂; all hydrogen → H₂O
    • Mass CO₂ → %C = mass CO₂ × (12/44) × 100 / mass sample
    • Mass H₂O → %H = mass H₂O × (2/18) × 100 / mass sample
    • %O = 100 − %C − %H − %N − %S − ... (by difference)

    Worked example 6 — combustion analysis:

    • 4.4 g organic compound → 8.8 g CO₂ + 3.6 g H₂O
    • %C = 8.8 × 12/44 × 100 / 4.4 = 54.55%
    • %H = 3.6 × 2/18 × 100 / 4.4 = 9.09%
    • %O = 100 − 54.55 − 9.09 = 36.36%
    • Moles: C = 54.55/12 = 4.546; H = 9.09/1 = 9.09; O = 36.36/16 = 2.273
    • Ratio ÷ 2.273: C 2.00, H 4.00, O 1.00 → C₂H₄O (E_r = 44)
    • If M_r = 88 → n = 2 → C₄H₈O₂ (ethyl acetate, etc.)

    4.4 Vapour density method for M_r

    Definition: Vapour density (VD) = mass of a volume of gas ÷ mass of same volume of H₂ under identical T, P. Formula: M_r = 2 × VD (since H₂ = 2 g/mol on this scale). Air reference: M_r = 28.94 × VD(air).

    Worked example 7:

    • VD of a gas = 44 → M_r = 2 × 44 = 88
    • If empirical = C₂H₄O (E_r = 44) → n = 88/44 = 2 → molecular = C₄H₈O₂

    4.5 Water of crystallisation in hydrated salts

    Definition: Moles of H₂O per formula unit of anhydrous salt in a hydrate. Method: Heat hydrate → mass loss = water → % water = mass loss / initial mass × 100. Moles H₂O = mass loss / 18; moles anhydrous = residue mass / M_anhydrous; ratio = n.

    Worked example 8 — CuSO₄·xH₂O:

    • 2.5 g hydrate heated → 1.6 g anhydrous CuSO₄ (M = 160)
    • Mass loss = 0.9 g H₂O = 0.05 mol
    • Moles CuSO₄ = 1.6 / 160 = 0.01 mol
    • Ratio H₂O : CuSO₄ = 0.05 : 0.01 = 5 → CuSO₄·5H₂O

    Worked example 9 — % water given:

    • Hydrated salt: 36.1% water; anhydrous M_r = 120
    • % anhydrous = 63.9% → in 100 g: 36.1 g H₂O (2.006 mol), 63.9 g anhydrous (0.5325 mol)
    • Ratio = 2.006 / 0.5325 = 3.77 ≈ 4 → Salt·4H₂O

  • 6. Chemical Processes and Mechanisms

    Process: Combustion analysis (organic elemental analysis)

    1. Definition: Burning a known mass of organic compound in excess oxygen; absorbing and weighing the CO₂ and H₂O produced to determine %C and %H.
    2. Location: Organic analysis laboratory; also automated CHNS/O analysers.
    3. Requirements: Combustion furnace (CuO, 800°C), O₂ carrier gas, H₂O absorber (Mg(ClO₄)₂ or anhydrous CaCl₂), CO₂ absorber (KOH/Ascarite), analytical balance (±0.1 mg).
    4. Components: Sample boat, combustion tube, absorption train, balance, data logger.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Weigh empty absorbers; (ii) Weigh ~0.1 g sample accurately; (iii) Introduce sample to furnace in O₂ stream; (iv) Combustion: C_xH_yO_z + (x + y/4 − z/2)O₂ → xCO₂ + (y/2)H₂O; (v) Gases pass through H₂O absorber (mass gain = H₂O mass); (vi) Then through CO₂ absorber (mass gain = CO₂ mass); (vii) Re-weigh absorbers; (viii) Calculate: %C = Δm_CO₂ × (12/44) × 100 / m_sample; %H = Δm_H₂O × (2/18) × 100 / m_sample; %O = 100 − %C − %H − %N − %S − ...
    6. Result: %C, %H, (%O by difference) — the data for empirical formula determination.
    7. Significance: The classic experimental source of composition data for unknown organic compounds; automated analysers do this in minutes for QC.

    Process: Empirical → molecular formula determination (structure elucidation)

    1. Definition: Identifying a compound's molecular formula from composition data and molar mass.
    2. Location: Natural products lab, pharmaceutical R&D, forensic lab, environmental analysis.
    3. Requirements: % composition (CHNS/O analyser), M_r (high-resolution mass spectrometry, or vapour density for volatiles), NMR/IR for structure confirmation.
    4. Components: Elemental analyser, HRMS (ESI, EI, MALDI), NMR, IR, software (formula calculator).
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Obtain %C, %H, %N, %S, %O (by difference); (ii) Compute empirical formula (CH₂O, C₂H₄O, etc.); (iv) Obtain exact M_r from HRMS (e.g., 180.0634 for C₆H₁₂O₆); (v) n = M_r/E_r; (vi) Molecular formula = n × empirical; (vii) Confirm with isotope pattern (M+1, M+2) and NMR.
    6. Result: Unique molecular formula identifying the compound; first step in structure elucidation.
    7. Significance: Turns anonymous % data into specific molecular identity — essential for new drug/natural product characterization, metabolite ID, pollutant tracking.

    Process: Hydrate water determination (qualitative analysis)

    1. Definition: Determining the number of water molecules per formula unit in a hydrated salt.
    2. Location: Qualitative analysis lab, cement testing, fertiliser QC.
    3. Requirements: Crucible, tongs, balance (±0.1 mg), Bunsen burner, desiccator.
    4. Components: Hydrated salt sample, crucible, heat source, desiccator for cooling.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Weigh empty crucible; (ii) Weigh ~2 g hydrated salt; (iii) Heat gently then strongly (avoid decomposition); (iv) Cool in desiccator; (v) Weigh residue (anhydrous); (vi) Mass loss = water; (vii) Moles H₂O = mass loss / 18; moles anhydrous = residue / M_anhydrous; (viii) Ratio = n → Salt·nH₂O.
    6. Result: Formula of hydrate (e.g., CuSO₄·5H₂O, FeSO₄·7H₂O, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O).
    7. Significance: Standard qualitative analysis test; cement CaSO₄·2H₂O (gypsum) controls setting time; fertiliser MgSO₄·7H₂O (epsom salt).

  • 10. Topic Mastery Check

    Important definitions

    1. Empirical formula — simplest whole-number atom ratio in a compound.
    2. Molecular formula — actual atom numbers in one molecule.
    3. Empirical formula mass (E_r) — sum of A_r in empirical formula.
    4. Molar mass (M_r) — mass of one mole in g·mol⁻¹.
    5. Vapour density (VD) — mass of gas volume ÷ mass of same volume H₂; M_r = 2×VD.
    6. Combustion analysis — burn sample, weigh CO₂ and H₂O → %C (×12/44), %H (×2/18), %O by difference.
    7. Oxygen by difference — %O = 100 − Σ(%other elements); assumes only C,H,O,N,S,halogens.
    8. Empirical formula mass (E_r) — sum of A_r in empirical formula.
    9. n = M_r/E_r — multiplier from empirical to molecular; must be integer.
    10. Water of crystallisation — moles H₂O per formula unit of anhydrous salt in hydrate.
    11. Hydrated salt — salt with water of crystallisation (e.g., CuSO₄·5H₂O).

    Key concepts to remember

    • %E = (mass E per mole ÷ M_r) × 100; Σ% = 100.
    • Forward: formula → %; Backward: % → empirical → (with M_r) → molecular.
    • Combustion factors: C from CO₂ (×12/44), H from H₂O (×2/18), O by difference.
    • n = M_r/E_r — needs measured M_r (VD×2, mass spec); never assumed.
    • Empirical gives ratio, never identity — CH₂O ×n family hides different molecules.
    • Ionic salts (NaCl) stop at empirical — no molecular formula for giant lattice.
    • Quality audit: forward route verifies label; backward route identifies unknowns.
    • Vapour density: M_r = 2×VD (H₂=2); balance precision (±0.1 mg) decides analysis quality.

    Important classifications

    • Forward vs Backward: Formula → % (audit) vs % → Formula (identification).
    • Organic vs Inorganic: Combustion analysis for organics; gravimetric/titration for inorganics.
    • Volatile vs Non-volatile: VD for volatiles; colligative/MS for non-volatiles.
    • Covalent vs Ionic: Molecular + empirical vs empirical only.
    • Hydrated vs Anhydrous: Salt·nH₂O vs anhydrous salt; water of crystallisation = n.

    Common conceptual confusions

    • Mass % ≠ mole % — convert with A_r before comparing.
    • O by difference assumes only C, H, O present; N/S need separate estimation.
    • Empirical gives ratio, never identity — CH₂O ×n family hides different molecules.
    • Vapour density needs H₂ = 2 scale stated; M_r = 2×VD.
    • Balance precision (±0.1 mg) decides analysis quality; ±0.001 g on 0.1 g sample = ±1%.
    • Ionic salts (NaCl) stop at empirical — no molecular formula for giant lattice.
    • % yield >100% indicates impure product or incomplete drying, not excess product.
    • Same empirical (CH₂O) ≠ same compound — glucose, formaldehyde, lactic acid all CH₂O.

    5. Compare urea (46-0-0) and ammonium nitrate (35-0-0) for a farmer buying 100 kg at equal price per kg.

    Conceptual questions

    1. Why must percentages sum to 100? What if they don't?
    2. Can two different compounds have the same % composition? Explain.
    3. Why is vapour density defined against H₂, not air?
    4. How does combustion analysis work for compounds containing N or S?
    5. What happens if n = M_r/E_r is not an integer?

    Application-based questions

    1. 100 kg urea at ₹25/kg vs 100 kg NH₄NO₃ at ₹25/kg — which gives more N? By how much?
    2. 4.4 g organic → 8.8 g CO₂ + 3.6 g H₂O. M_r = 88. Find molecular formula.
    3. A limestone sample claims 45% Ca. Audit: CaCO₃ has 40% Ca. What does the claim imply?
    4. Protein contains 16% N. A food has 3.2% N by Kjeldahl. What is its % protein?
    5. Steel spec: 0.2% C max. A 5 tonne batch has 12 kg C. Pass or fail?

    Answers

    1. %E = (mass of E per mole ÷ M_r) × 100; Σ%E = 100.
    2. Urea CO(NH₂)₂, M_r = 60: %N = (2×14)/60 × 100 = 28/60 × 100 = 46.67%.
    3. 40/12 = 3.33; 6.7/1 = 6.7; 53.3/16 = 3.33 → ÷3.33 → 1:2:1 → CH₂O.
    4. VD = mass of gas volume / mass of same volume H₂; M_r = 2 × VD (H₂ = 2 scale).
    5. %C = 8.8×12/44 × 100/4.4 = 54.55%; %H = 3.6×2/18 × 100/4.4 = 9.09%.
    6. O by difference = 100 − Σ(other %); assumes only C,H,O,N,S,halogens — no unmeasured elements.
    7. Urea: 46.67 kg N per 100 kg; NH₄NO₃: 35 kg N per 100 kg. Urea gives 11.67 kg more N per 100 kg at same price.
    8. %C = 44×12/44 = 12% (if CO₂ is 44% of product mass — unclear question). Better: 4.4 g sample → 8.8 g CO₂ → %C = 54.55%; 3.6 g H₂O → %H = 9.09%; %O = 36.36% → C₂H₄O empirical; VD 44 → M_r 88 → n=2 → C₄H₈O₂.
    9. %N = 3.2% → % protein = 3.2 × 6.25 = 20%.
    10. 5 tonne = 5000 kg; 0.2% C = 10 kg max; 12 kg C = 0.24% → FAIL (exceeds spec).
    11. Sum ≠ 100 means missing element or calculation error — re-check.
    12. Yes — isomers (C₂H₆O: ethanol vs dimethyl ether) have same % composition. Also different compounds with same empirical formula (CH₂O family).
    13. H₂ is the lightest gas, historically available pure, M_r = 2 exactly → simple integer scale (VD = M_r/2). Air is mixture, variable.
    14. N/S measured separately (e.g., Dumas for N, Schöniger for S) and added to % sum; O by difference still used.
    15. Non-integer n means: wrong M_r, wrong empirical, or impure sample — re-check all data.
    16. Pure CaCO₃ = 5×0.8 = 4 g = 0.04 mol → theoretical CaO = 0.04×56 = 2.24 g; 90% yield = 2.016 g actual.
    17. Pure A = 10×0.9 = 9 g = 0.18 mol; B excess; theoretical C = 0.18/2 × 190 = 17.1 g; % yield = 6/17.1 × 100 = 35.1%.
    18. Empirical gives ratio, never identity — CH₂O ×n family: glucose, formaldehyde, lactic acid.
    19. Ionic lattice: no discrete molecules; formula = simplest ratio (NaCl).
    20. %N = 3.2% → % protein = 3.2 × 6.25 = 20%.
    21. 5 tonne = 5000 kg; 0.2% C = 10 kg max; 12 kg C = 0.24% → FAIL (exceeds spec).

Confusion

  • Mass % and mole % differ — convert with A_r before comparing.
  • O-by-difference assumes only C, H, O present; N/S need separate estimation.
  • Empirical gives ratio, never identity (CH₂O ×n family: glucose, formaldehyde, lactic acid).
  • Vapour density needs the H₂ = 2 scale stated; M_r = 2×VD.
  • Balance precision (±0.1 mg) decides analysis quality; ±0.001 g on 0.1 g sample = ±1%.
  • Ionic salts (NaCl) stop at empirical — no molecular formula for giant lattice.
  • % yield >100% indicates impure product or incomplete drying, not excess product.

Formulas

  • %E = (mass of E per mole ÷ M_r) × 100
  • M_r = Σ A_r
  • M_r = 2 × vapour density (H₂ = 2)
  • n = M_r / E_r (empirical → molecular)
  • %C = mass CO₂ × 12/44 × 100 / mass sample
  • %H = mass H₂O × 2/18 × 100 / mass sample
  • %O = 100 − (%C + %H + %N + %S + ...)
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100

  • %E = (mass of E per mole ÷ M_r) × 100; bars sum to 100.
