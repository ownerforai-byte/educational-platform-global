  • Limiting reactant and excess reactant

    1. Topic Introduction

    In a chemical reaction, reactants rarely appear in the exact mole ratio dictated by the balanced equation. One reactant runs out first — the limiting reactant (or limiting reagent) — and stops the reaction. The other reactant(s) are present in excess and remain partly unreacted. Identifying the limiting reactant is the gatekeeper of every stoichiometric calculation: product amounts, theoretical yield, and excess remaining all depend on it.

    Why it matters. In industry, the limiting reactant is often the expensive one (e.g., catalyst, rare metal) while the excess reactant is cheap (air, water, excess acid). Optimising the excess saves money and reduces waste. In the lab, running with a slight excess of one reagent ensures the expensive one is fully consumed.

    What you will learn: (i) concept of limiting and excess reactant; (ii) how to identify the limiting reactant by the mole/coefficient method; (iii) calculating product mass from the limiting reactant; (iv) calculating excess reactant remaining; (iv) real-world examples (combustion, ammonia synthesis, titration).

    Learning outcomes. You can: identify the limiting reactant from given masses/volumes; calculate theoretical yield based on the limiting reactant; determine the mass/amount of excess reactant remaining; explain why excess air is used in combustion and why it has limits.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding

    What limiting reactant is

    The reactant that is completely consumed first in a chemical reaction, determining the maximum amount of product that can be formed. Once it is used up, the reaction stops regardless of how much other reactants remain.

    Why it exists

    Reactants are almost never mixed in the exact stoichiometric ratio. One will run out first — this is the limiting reactant. The reaction cannot proceed beyond the point where this reactant is exhausted because there are no more particles of it to collide and react.

    How it works

    The balanced equation gives the ideal mole ratio. You compare the actual mole ratio of the reactants provided to the ideal ratio. The reactant whose mole/coefficient ratio is smaller is the limiting one — it has fewer "reaction turns" available.

    Where it occurs

    Every reaction vessel: industrial reactors (Haber process, Contact process), car engines (fuel + air), lab titrations, baking (flour vs baking soda), biological cells (substrate vs enzyme).

    What its components are

    Balanced equation, mole ratios (coefficients), actual amounts of reactants (mass, volume, concentration), product formed, excess remaining.

    How components interact

    Moles of each reactant ÷ its stoichiometric coefficient → the smaller quotient wins (limiting). Product moles = limiting reactant moles × (product coefficient / limiting coefficient). Excess remaining = initial excess moles − (limiting moles × mole ratio of excess/limiting).

    What makes it biologically important

    Enzyme kinetics: substrate is often limiting; enzyme is in excess. Metabolic pathways: rate-limiting step determined by limiting substrate. Drug dosage: active ingredient limiting, excipients in excess.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The mole/coefficient method (the universal algorithm)

    Step 1: Write the balanced chemical equation. Step 2: Convert given amounts of ALL reactants to moles (mass → n = m/M; gas volume → n = V/22.4; solution → n = M × V). Step 3: For each reactant, divide its moles by its stoichiometric coefficient from the balanced equation. Step 4: The reactant with the SMALLEST quotient is the limiting reactant. Step 5: All product calculations use the limiting reactant's moles and the mole ratio. Step 6: Excess reactant remaining = initial moles − (limiting moles × mole ratio of excess/limiting).

    4.2 Worked example 1: Ammonia synthesis

    Reaction: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) Given: 3 mol N₂ and 6 mol H₂ mixed.

    • N₂: 3 mol ÷ 1 = 3
    • H₂: 6 mol ÷ 3 = 2
    • Limiting: H₂ (smaller quotient = 2)
    • NH₃ produced: 2 mol H₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 3 mol H₂) = 1.33 mol NH₃ = 22.6 g
    • N₂ consumed: 2 mol H₂ × (1 mol N₂ / 3 mol H₂) = 0.67 mol N₂
    • N₂ excess remaining: 3 − 0.67 = 2.33 mol = 65.2 g

    4.3 Worked example 2: Water formation from elements

    Reaction: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) Given: 4 g H₂ (2 mol) and 32 g O₂ (1 mol)

    • H₂: 2 mol ÷ 2 = 1
    • O₂: 1 mol ÷ 1 = 1
    • Both equal → stoichiometric mixture (no limiting, no excess)
    • H₂O produced: 2 mol H₂ → 2 mol H₂O = 36 g

    4.4 Worked example 3: Combustion with excess air

    Reaction: 2C₈H₁₈(l) + 25O₂(g) → 16CO₂(g) + 18H₂O(g) Given: 114 g octane (1 mol) burned in 500 g air (≈17.2 mol O₂)

    • C₈H₁₈: 1 mol ÷ 2 = 0.5
    • O₂: 17.2 mol ÷ 25 = 0.69
    • Limiting: C₈H₁₈ (0.5 < 0.69)
    • CO₂ produced: 1 mol C₈H₁₈ × (16/2) = 8 mol CO₂ = 352 g
    • O₂ consumed: 1 mol C₈H₁₈ × (25/2) = 12.5 mol O₂ = 400 g
    • O₂ excess: 17.2 − 12.5 = 4.7 mol = 150 g

    4.6 Percentage purity correction

    If a sample is impure, correct mass before mole calculation:

    • Pure mass = impure mass × (purity% / 100)
    • Then n = pure mass / M
    • All subsequent calculations use this corrected mole value.

    4.7 Industrial context

    • Haber process: H₂ often limiting (from natural gas); N₂ from air (excess). Optimal H₂:N₂ = 3:1.
    • Contact process: SO₂ limiting; O₂ from air (excess ~2× stoichiometric).
    • Cement kiln: CaCO₃ limiting; fuel in excess to ensure complete calcination.
    • Combustion engines: Fuel limiting; air in excess (λ = 1.1–1.3) to minimise CO/HC emissions.
    • Titration: Analyte limiting; titrant added until equivalence (stoichiometric point).

  • 6. Chemical Processes and Mechanisms

    Process: Identifying limiting reactant (step-by-step)

    1. Definition: Determining which reactant will be consumed first in a chemical reaction.
    2. Location: Reaction vessel (lab, industrial reactor, engine cylinder).
    3. Requirements: Balanced equation, amounts of all reactants (mass, volume, concentration).
    4. Components: Balanced equation, stoichiometric coefficients, actual moles of each reactant.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Balance equation; (ii) Convert all reactants to moles; (iii) Divide each by its coefficient; (iv) Smallest quotient = limiting; (vi) Product from limiting; (vii) Excess remaining = initial − consumed.
    6. Result: Limiting reactant identified, theoretical yield calculated, excess quantified.
    7. Significance: Every quantitative chemistry problem starts here; errors here propagate.

    Process: Excess reactant management (industrial)

    1. Definition: Optimising the amount of excess reactant to maximise yield, minimise cost, and ensure safety.
    2. Location: Industrial reactor design (Haber, Contact, cement, combustion).
    3. Requirements: Cost of reactants, safety constraints, equilibrium limitations, separation costs.
    4. Components: Fresh feed, recycle stream, purge stream, reactor, separator.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Set target conversion; (ii) Calculate stoichiometric requirement; (iii) Add excess (typically 10–50% for gases, more for liquids); (iv) Separate product; (vi) Recycle unreacted excess; (vii) Purge inerts to prevent buildup.
    6. Result: Optimised feed ratio, high yield, minimal waste, safe operation.
    7. Significance: Excess is not waste — it's a process variable that is optimised economically.

  • 10. Topic Mastery Check

    Important definitions

    1. Limiting reactant — reactant completely consumed first; determines maximum product.
    2. Excess reactant — reactant present in amount greater than stoichiometric requirement; remains partly unreacted.
    3. Theoretical yield — maximum product possible from limiting reactant (100% conversion).
    4. Actual yield — product actually isolated experimentally.
    5. % yield — (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100.
    6. Mole/coefficient method — divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient; smallest = limiting.
    7. Excess reactant remaining — initial moles − (limiting moles × mole ratio of excess/limiting).

    Key concepts to remember

    • Limiting reactant has the smallest (moles ÷ coefficient).
    • Product always calculated from limiting reactant.
    • Excess remaining = initial − consumed; consumed = limiting moles × (coeff_excess/coeff_limiting).
    • Purity correction: pure mass = impure mass × (%purity/100) — do this FIRST.
    • % yield > 100% = impure product or wet solid.
    • Excess air in combustion: prevents CO, but too much wastes heat.

    Important classifications

    • Reactants: Limiting (controls yield) vs Excess (remains).
    • Yields: Theoretical (max possible) vs Actual (isolated) vs % yield (efficiency).
    • Purity: Pure sample (direct moles) vs Impure sample (correct first).

    Common conceptual confusions

    • Comparing masses directly instead of moles/coefficient — WRONG (masses don't reflect mole ratios).
    • Calculating product from excess reactant — WRONG (excess doesn't determine yield).
    • Forgetting purity correction — impure sample gives wrong moles.
    • Reporting % yield > 100% without explanation — means impure/wet product.
    • Thinking excess reactant is "waste" — it's often optimised (safety, equilibrium shift).
    • Not accounting for volume change in gas reactions — mole ratios = volume ratios only at same T,P.

    5. Impurities don't react; mass includes non-reacting material → wrong moles.

    9. Mass doesn't reflect mole ratios; 32 g O₂ = 1 mol, 4 g H₂ = 2 mol — same mass ratio but different mole ratio.
    10. Purity reduces effective mass; e.g., 80% pure 10 g = 8 g reactant; skipping gives 10 g → wrong limiting ID.
    11. No — impure product (residual solvent, by-product) or incomplete drying adds mass.
    12. No — excess air prevents CO (safety), shifts equilibrium (Le Chatelier), but too much loses heat.
    13. Yes — initial amounts determine limiting; equilibrium constant determines final extent.
    14. H₂: 8/2 = 4 mol; O₂: 32/32 = 1 mol → O₂ limiting. H₂O = 2 mol = 36 g. H₂ consumed = 2 mol = 4 g; excess H₂ = 2 mol = 4 g.

    PYQ Solutions

    Q: Explain how the limiting reactant is identified from mole ratios. (2 marks) Answer: Convert masses of all reactants to moles. Divide each by its stoichiometric coefficient. The reactant with the smallest quotient is the limiting reactant. Keywords: moles, coefficient, smallest quotient.

    Q: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: 3 mol N₂ and 6 mol H₂. Find limiting, NH₃ mass, excess left. (3 marks) Answer: N₂: 3/1 = 3; H₂: 6/3 = 2 → H₂ limiting. NH₃ = 6 × 2/3 = 4 mol = 68 g. N₂ consumed = 6 × 1/3 = 2 mol; excess N₂ = 3 − 2 = 1 mol = 28 g. Keywords: quotient comparison, H₂ limiting, 68 g NH₃, 28 g N₂ excess.

    Q: Calculate mass of water when 4 g H₂ reacts with 32 g O₂; identify excess. (3 marks) Answer: H₂ = 2 mol; O₂ = 1 mol. Quotients: H₂: 2/2 = 1; O₂: 1/1 = 1 → stoichiometric mixture (no limiting/excess). H₂O = 2 mol = 36 g. No excess. Keywords: stoichiometric mixture, 36 g H₂O, no excess.

    Q: Why is excess air supplied in combustion? Disadvantages of too much? (3 marks) Answer: Excess air ensures complete combustion (prevents CO, unburnt hydrocarbons). Disadvantages: carries away heat (lowers flame temperature, reduces efficiency), increases NOx formation, larger flue gas handling equipment. Keywords: complete combustion, CO prevention, heat loss, NOx, equipment cost.

  • 12. Summary and Self-check

    The limiting reactant (smallest mole/coefficient) controls theoretical yield; all products calculated from it. Excess reactant remains: initial − consumed (consumed = limiting moles × excess/limiting ratio). Purity correction (impure mass × %/100) must precede mole calculation. % yield = (actual/theoretical)×100; >100% = impure/wet product. Industrial excess optimisation: air in combustion (λ=1.1–1.3), recycle in Haber/Contact processes.

Universal Facts

  • Limiting reactant = smallest (moles ÷ stoichiometric coefficient).
  • Theoretical yield from limiting reactant only.
  • Excess remaining = initial − (limiting moles × coeff_excess/coeff_limiting).
  • % yield = (actual ÷ theoretical) × 100; >100% = impure/wet product.
  • Purity correction: pure mass = impure mass × (%/100) BEFORE mole calc.

Examples

  • N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: 3 mol N₂, 6 mol H₂ → H₂ limiting (quotient 2 vs 3); NH₃ = 4 mol; N₂ excess 1 mol.

  • Combustion: fuel limiting, air excess (λ = 1.1–1.3) prevents CO.

Formulas

  • n = m / M
  • n = V / 22.4 (gas at STP)
  • n = M_conc × V_L (solution)
  • limiting: min( n_i / coeff_i )
  • n_product = n_limiting × (coeff_product / coeff_limiting)
  • excess remaining = n_initial − n_limiting × (coeff_excess / coeff_limiting)
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100
  • pure mass = impure mass × (%purity / 100)
  • atom economy = MW(product) / Σ MW(reactants) × 100

Numericals

  • N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: 3 mol N₂, 6 mol H₂ → H₂ limiting (2 vs 3); NH₃ = 4 mol = 68 g; N₂ excess = 1 mol = 28 g.
  • 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: 4 g H₂ (2 mol), 32 g O₂ (1 mol) → stoichiometric; H₂O = 36 g; no excess.
  • 100 g CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂: theoretical 56 g CaO; actual 47.6 g → 85% yield.
  • C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH: 0.25 mol ethene → theoretical 11.5 g ethanol; actual 9.2 g → 80% yield.
  • N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: 28 g N₂ (1 mol), 6 g H₂ (3 mol) → stoichiometric; NH₃ = 2 mol = 34 g.

Key Points

  • Limiting reactant = smallest (moles ÷ coefficient); controls all product amounts.
  • Excess reactant = larger quotient; remains after reaction stops.
  • Theoretical yield from limiting reactant only; % yield = (actual/theoretical)×100.
  • Purity correction: pure mass = impure mass × (%/100) BEFORE mole calculation.
  • % yield > 100% = impure/wet product; true yield ≤ 100%.

Summary

The limiting reactant (smallest mole/coefficient) controls theoretical yield; excess reactant remains. Theoretical yield from limiting reactant; % yield = (actual/theoretical)×100. Purity correction (impure mass × %/100) must precede mole calc. Industrial excess optimisation: air in combustion (λ=1.1–1.3), recycle in Haber/Contact processes. Figures: decision tree, ammonia synthesis, recycle loop, air-fuel ratio.
