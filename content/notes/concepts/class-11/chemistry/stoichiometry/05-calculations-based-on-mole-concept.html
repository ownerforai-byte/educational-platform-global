Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Every Calculation Is a Mole Hop

    Mass–mass, mass–volume, mass–particles, solution concentration — all stoichiometric calculations follow one route: convert the given quantity to moles, apply the equation's mole ratio, convert to the required quantity. Master the hop and every numerical becomes bookkeeping.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The universal algorithm

    1. Write the balanced equation.
    2. Convert the given quantity to moles (÷M for mass, ÷22.4 for gas volume at STP, ÷Nₐ for particles, ×V(L) for molarity).
    3. Multiply by the mole ratio (coefficients).
    4. Convert moles of the required species to the asked unit (×M, ×22.4, ×Nₐ, ÷V).

    2.2 Worked numericals

    Q (NEB 2024, 2m): 4 g of NaOH is dissolved in water to make 250 mL of solution. Calculate its molarity. (NaOH = 40)

    • n(NaOH) = 4/40 = 0.1 mol
    • V = 250 mL = 0.25 L
    • M = n/V = 0.1/0.25 = 0.4 M

    Q (NEB-style): What mass of CO₂ is released when 100 g of CaCO₃ is completely decomposed? (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂)

    • n(CaCO₃) = 100/100 = 1 mol → n(CO₂) = 1 mol → m = 1 × 44 = 44 g.

    Q (NEB-style): What volume of H₂ at STP is produced when 6.5 g of Zn reacts with excess HCl? (Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂; Zn = 65)

    • n(Zn) = 6.5/65 = 0.1 mol → n(H₂) = 0.1 mol → V = 0.1 × 22.4 = 2.24 L.

    Q (NEB-style): How many mL of 0.05 M H₂SO₄ neutralise 25 mL of 0.1 M NaOH?

    • 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O; ratio NaOH:H₂SO₄ = 2:1
    • n(NaOH) = 0.1 × 0.025 = 2.5 × 10⁻³ mol → n(H₂SO₄) = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol
    • V = n/M = 1.25 × 10⁻³/0.05 = 0.025 L = 25 mL.

    2.3 Molarity vs molality — similarities and differences

    Similarities: both are concentration units; both use moles of solute; both convert to/from moles in numericals. Differences: molarity = mol solute per litre of solution (M), temperature-dependent (solution expands); molality = mol solute per kg of solvent (m), temperature-independent. Dilution changes molarity but never molality.

    2.4 Mass–mass vs mass–volume stoichiometry

    Similarities: both need the balanced equation and the mole hop. Differences: mass–mass ends with ×M (grams); mass–volume ends with ×22.4 L at STP for gases — and only gases.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To the mole (Topic 4): the hop is only as good as your n = m/M reflex.
    • To limiting reactant (Topic 6): when both reactants are quantified, the limiting one caps the yield.
    • To titration (practicals): M₁V₁/n₁ = M₂V₂/n₂ is this algorithm in solution form.

    3. Applying 22.4 L to non-STP conditions.

Specific Conditions

  • STP Definition: Standard Temperature and Pressure = 0°C (273.15 K) and 1 atm (101.325 kPa). Molar volume 22.4 L applies ONLY at STP. At other conditions use PV = nRT.
  • Ideal Gas Assumption: Molar volume and simple gas stoichiometry assume ideal behavior. Real gases deviate at high pressure or low temperature.
  • Balanced Equation Required: All stoichiometric calculations must start with a correctly balanced chemical equation. Unbalanced equations give wrong mole ratios.
  • Consistent Units: Always convert to moles first, then to target units. Mixing grams and liters without conversion causes errors.

Formulas

    • M = n/V(L) (molarity)
    • m = molality = n solute/kg solvent
    • M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ (dilution)
    • M₁V₁/n₁ = M₂V₂/n₂ (titration, coefficients n)

Key Points

  • Algorithm: balance → moles → ratio → convert. Molarity M = n/V(L). Mass–mass uses M; gas volumes use 22.4 L at STP; particles use Nₐ.

Important Statements

  • "Balanced chemical equations give mole ratios, not mass ratios."

Important Notes

  • Verified PYQ 2024 (2m): molarity of 4 g NaOH in 250 mL = 0.4 M.

Exam Short Tricks

  • "Balance → moles → ratio → unit. Write the four steps and NEB gives method marks even if arithmetic slips."

Exam Notes

  • State units at every step — lost units are the top cause of lost marks in numericals.

Examples

  • 4 g NaOH in 250 mL — 0.1 mol in 0.25 L = 0.4 M (NEB 2024): the standard molarity question.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (2m): 4 g of NaOH is dissolved in water to make 250 mL of solution. Calculate its molarity. (NaOH = 40) — n = 4/40 = 0.1 mol; V = 0.25 L; M = 0.1/0.25 = 0.4 M.

Practice

  • What volume of 0.5 M HCl neutralises 20 mL of 0.5 M NaOH?

Universal Facts

  • Every stoichiometry numerical, from Class 11 to industrial engineering, is the same four-step hop.

Confusion

  • Molarity vs molality: M = mol/L solution; m = mol/kg solvent. Only molality ignores temperature.
  • Dilution: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ conserves moles of solute — volumes are additive only approximately, moles exactly.

Mcs

1.

Number of moles in 36 g water is:

Options
  • 1 mol
  • 2 mol
  • 3 mol
  • 0.5 mol
Answer

B

Explanation

n = m/M = 36/18 = 2 mol.

2.

Volume of 1 mol gas at STP is:

Options
  • 11.2 L
  • 22.4 L
  • 24.5 L
  • 44.8 L
Answer

B

Explanation

Standard molar volume at STP (0°C, 1 atm) = 22.4 L.

3.

Empirical formula of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is:

Options
  • C₆H₁₂O₆
  • CH₂O
  • C₂H₄O₂
  • CHO
Answer

B

Explanation

Simplest ratio: divide all subscripts by 6 → CH₂O.

4.

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, if 4 mol H₂ and 3 mol O₂ react, limiting reagent is:

Options
  • H₂
  • O₂
  • Both equal
  • Neither
Answer

A

Explanation

H₂/O₂ ratio needed = 2:1. Available 4:3. H₂ needs 2 mol O₂ but 3 available → H₂ limits.

5.

Percentage yield is calculated as:

Options
  • Theoretical/Actual × 100
  • Actual/Theoretical × 100
  • (Actual−Theoretical)/Theoretical × 100
  • Actual + Theoretical
Answer

B

Explanation

% yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100.

Summary

All stoichiometric calculations follow one algorithm: balance the equation, convert to moles, apply the coefficient ratio, convert to the required unit. Mass uses n = m/M; gas volume at STP uses 22.4 L/mol; particles use Nₐ; solutions use molarity M = n/V(L) (mol per litre of solution, temperature-dependent) rather than molality (mol per kg solvent, temperature-independent). Worked anchors: 4 g NaOH in 250 mL → 0.4 M (NEB 2024); 100 g CaCO₃ → 44 g CO₂; 6.5 g Zn → 2.24 L H₂ at STP.
