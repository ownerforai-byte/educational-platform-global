Notes

  • Mole Concept Foundation: One mole contains exactly 6.022 × 10²³ entities (Avogadro's number Nₐ). The molar mass M (g/mol) of any substance equals its atomic or molecular mass numerically. This bridges the microscopic (atoms/molecules) and macroscopic (grams) worlds.
  • Mass-to-Moles Conversion: n = m/M where n = moles, m = mass in grams, M = molar mass in g/mol. Example: 36 g water (M = 18 g/mol) → n = 36/18 = 2 mol. This is the most fundamental stoichiometric calculation.
  • Moles-to-Particles Conversion: Number of particles N = n × Nₐ. Example: 0.5 mol CO₂ contains 0.5 × 6.022×10²³ = 3.011×10²³ molecules. Reverse: n = N/Nₐ when given particle count.
  • Molar Volume at STP: One mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at STP (0°C, 1 atm). n = V/22.4 for gases at STP. Example: 5.6 L O₂ at STP → n = 5.6/22.4 = 0.25 mol. Non-STP conditions require PV = nRT.
  • Percentage Composition: % of element = (mass of element in 1 mol compound / molar mass of compound) × 100. Example: In H₂O, %H = (2×1.008/18.015)×100 = 11.19%, %O = (16.00/18.015)×100 = 88.81%. Used to determine empirical formulas from experimental data.
  • Empirical Formula Determination: Step 1: Convert % composition to grams (assume 100 g sample). Step 2: Convert grams to moles for each element. Step 3: Divide all mole values by smallest mole value. Step 4: Multiply by integer if ratios are not whole numbers. Result gives simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
  • Molecular Formula from Empirical: Molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ where n = molecular mass / empirical formula mass. Example: Empirical CH₂O (mass 30), molecular mass 180 → n = 180/30 = 6 → molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose).
  • Stoichiometric Calculations from Balanced Equations: Coefficients give mole ratios. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: 2 mol H₂ reacts with 1 mol O₂ to produce 2 mol H₂O. Convert given quantity to moles → use ratio → convert back to desired units.
  • Mass-Mass Stoichiometry: Given mass of reactant A, find mass of product B. Steps: mass A → moles A (÷M_A) → moles B (×ratio) → mass B (×M_B). Example: How much CO₂ from 100 g CaCO₃? CaCO₃→CaO+CO₂. n(CaCO₃)=100/100=1 mol → n(CO₂)=1 mol → m=44 g.
  • Mass-Volume Stoichiometry (Gases): For reactions involving gases, use molar volume. Example: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. From 6.5 g Zn (0.1 mol), H₂ produced = 0.1 mol = 0.1×22.4 = 2.24 L at STP.
  • Solution Stoichiometry: Molarity M = n/V(L). n = M × V. Use in titration calculations: M₁V₁/n₁ = M₂V₂/n₂ where n₁,n₂ are coefficients. Example: 25 mL 0.1M NaOH neutralizes how many mL of 0.05M H₂SO₄? Ratio 2:1 → V = (0.1×25×1)/(0.05×2) = 25 mL.
  • Limiting Reagent Identification: Calculate moles of each reactant. Divide by coefficient in balanced equation. Smallest value identifies limiting reagent. All product calculations based on limiting reagent only. Excess reagent remains partially unreacted.
  • Theoretical Yield Calculation: Maximum possible product based on limiting reagent and stoichiometry. Actual yield is always less due to incomplete reactions, side reactions, and losses during purification.
  • Percentage Yield: % yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100. Industrial processes aim for >90%. Low yields indicate inefficiency, side reactions, or equilibrium limitations. NEB frequently asks numerical problems combining limiting reagent and % yield.
  • Percentage Purity: % purity = (mass of pure substance / mass of impure sample) × 100. Impure samples require correction before stoichiometric calculations. Example: 80% pure limestone means only 80 g CaCO₃ per 100 g sample participates in reaction.

Specific Conditions

  • STP Definition: Standard Temperature and Pressure = 0°C (273.15 K) and 1 atm (101.325 kPa). Molar volume 22.4 L applies ONLY at STP. At other conditions use PV = nRT.
  • Ideal Gas Assumption: Molar volume and simple gas stoichiometry assume ideal behavior. Real gases deviate at high pressure or low temperature.
  • Balanced Equation Required: All stoichiometric calculations must start with a correctly balanced chemical equation. Unbalanced equations give wrong mole ratios.
  • Consistent Units: Always convert to moles first, then to target units. Mixing grams and liters without conversion causes errors.

Key Points

  • n = m/M is the gateway to all stoichiometric calculations.
  • Always convert to moles before applying mole ratios from balanced equations.
  • 22.4 L/mol applies only to ideal gases at STP.
  • Empirical formula = simplest ratio; molecular formula = actual atom count.
  • Limiting reagent determines maximum product; excess reagent is leftover.
  • % yield compares actual vs theoretical; always ≤ 100%.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: The mole concept provides the quantitative link between atomic-scale events and measurable laboratory quantities.
  • Statement 2: All stoichiometric calculations require conversion through moles as the intermediate step.
  • Statement 3: Percentage composition data enables determination of empirical and molecular formulas.
  • Statement 4: The limiting reagent controls the extent of reaction and maximum product formation.
  • Statement 5: Percentage yield measures the efficiency of a chemical process relative to theoretical maximum.

Important Notes

  • NEB exam pattern: Most stoichiometry numericals follow mass→moles→ratio→moles→mass/volume sequence.
  • Common error: Forgetting to balance the equation before calculating mole ratios.
  • For hydrated salts (e.g., CuSO₄·5H₂O), include water molecules in molar mass calculation.
  • In combustion analysis, carbon converts to CO₂ and hydrogen to H₂O — use these to find C and H content.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Mass↔Moles: divide by M to get moles, multiply by M to get mass.
  • Gas at STP: divide liters by 22.4 for moles.
  • Empirical formula: %→g→mol→divide by smallest→whole number ratio.
  • Limiting reagent: moles/coefficient — smallest wins.
  • % yield = actual/theoretical × 100.

Exam Notes

  • Practice at least 10 mass-mass, 5 mass-volume, and 5 solution stoichiometry problems.
  • Memorize common molar masses: H₂O=18, CO₂=44, NaOH=40, H₂SO₄=98, CaCO₃=100.

Examples

  • Calculate moles in 49 g H₂SO₄: n = 49/98 = 0.5 mol.
  • Volume of 0.25 mol NH₃ at STP: V = 0.25 × 22.4 = 5.6 L.
  • Empirical formula from 40% C, 6.67% H, 53.33% O: C=40/12=3.33, H=6.67/1=6.67, O=53.33/16=3.33 → divide by 3.33 → CH₂O.
  • From 2KClO₃→2KCl+3O₂, how much O₂ from 24.5 g KClO₃? n=24.5/122.5=0.2 mol KClO₃ → 0.3 mol O₂ → 6.72 L at STP.
  • If 10 g Mg reacts with excess HCl (Mg+2HCl→MgCl₂+H₂), theoretical H₂ = 10/24.3 = 0.412 mol = 9.22 L at STP. If actual = 8.5 L, % yield = 8.5/9.22×100 = 92.2%.

Universal Facts

  • Avogadro's number was determined experimentally by Jean Perrin (1908) using Brownian motion, confirming atomic theory.
  • The mole was redefined in 2019 as exactly 6.02214076×10²³ entities, fixing it as an exact constant.
  • Industrial ammonia synthesis (Haber process) achieves only ~15% single-pass yield but recycles unreacted gases for >98% overall conversion.
  • Analytical chemistry relies entirely on stoichiometric calculations for quantitative determination of unknown concentrations.

Confusion

  • Confusing molecular mass with molar mass: Molecular mass is dimensionless (relative); molar mass has units g/mol. Numerically equal but dimensionally different.
  • Using 22.4 L for non-STP conditions: Only valid at 0°C and 1 atm. Room temperature (25°C) gives ~24.5 L/mol.
  • Forgetting diatomic elements: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ exist as molecules. Atomic mass ≠ molecular mass for these.
  • Mixing up empirical and molecular formulas: Empirical is simplest ratio; molecular is actual. They can be identical (H₂O) or different (C₆H₁₂O₆ vs CH₂O).
  • Assuming 100% yield in calculations: Theoretical yield is maximum possible; actual is always less. Never use actual yield to predict reactant needs.

1.

Number of moles in 36 g water is:

Options
  • 1 mol
  • 2 mol
  • 3 mol
  • 0.5 mol
Answer

B

Explanation

n = m/M = 36/18 = 2 mol.

2.

Volume of 1 mol gas at STP is:

Options
  • 11.2 L
  • 22.4 L
  • 24.5 L
  • 44.8 L
Answer

B

Explanation

Standard molar volume at STP (0°C, 1 atm) = 22.4 L.

3.

Empirical formula of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is:

Options
  • C₆H₁₂O₆
  • CH₂O
  • C₂H₄O₂
  • CHO
Answer

B

Explanation

Simplest ratio: divide all subscripts by 6 → CH₂O.

4.

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, if 4 mol H₂ and 3 mol O₂ react, limiting reagent is:

Options
  • H₂
  • O₂
  • Both equal
  • Neither
Answer

A

Explanation

H₂/O₂ ratio needed = 2:1. Available 4:3. H₂ needs 2 mol O₂ but 3 available → H₂ limits.

5.

Percentage yield is calculated as:

Options
  • Theoretical/Actual × 100
  • Actual/Theoretical × 100
  • (Actual−Theoretical)/Theoretical × 100
  • Actual + Theoretical
Answer

B

Explanation

% yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100.
