Theoretical Yield, Experimental Yield and Percentage Yield

Notes

  • 1. Meaning of the three quantities

  • Theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product calculated from the balanced equation using the limiting reactant (assuming complete reaction and no losses). Experimental (actual) yield is the amount of pure product actually isolated in the experiment. Percentage yield compares them: %yield=theoretical yieldactual yield​×100. Related terms: conversion (fraction of reactant consumed), selectivity (fraction converted to the desired product) and atom economy (mass of desired product ÷ mass of all reactants × 100).

  • 2. Why the actual yield is lower than the theoretical yield

  • • The reaction is incomplete — the limiting reactant is not fully consumed in the time allowed.
  • • The reaction is reversible, so it stops at an equilibrium position (this is exactly the case in the Haber process).
  • • Side reactions produce by-products (organic syntheses especially).
  • • Mechanical losses during filtration, decantation, transfer, crystallisation, evaporation and drying, plus product remaining dissolved in the mother liquor (solubility loss).
  • • Product may be volatile, thermally unstable or hygroscopic, and impurities may interfere with the reaction.
  • • Weighing, reading and calculation errors of the experimenter.
  • A yield greater than 100% always indicates experimental error: the product is wet or impure, or unreacted starting material has been weighed as product.

  • 3. Worked examples

  • Example 1: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. From 100 g CaCO₃ the theoretical yield of CaO is 56 g; if 47.6 g is obtained, % yield = 47.6/56 × 100 = 85%.
  • Example 2: C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH. 7.0 g of ethene (0.25 mol) should give 0.25 mol = 11.5 g of ethanol; an actual yield of 9.2 g gives 80%.
  • Example 3: Aspirin synthesis: 5.0 g of salicylic acid (M = 138) gives a theoretical 6.5 g of aspirin (M = 180, 1:1 mole ratio); a student isolating 5.2 g reports (5.2/6.5) × 100 = 80%.

  • 4. Meaning and use of atom economy

  • Percentage yield measures how much of the intended product is recovered, whereas atom economy measures how much of the reactant mass ends up in the desired product (substitution and elimination reactions with by-products have poor atom economy). Green chemistry prefers high atom economy because it minimises waste, an idea quantified by the E-factor (mass of waste ÷ mass of product). Both concepts are needed to judge whether a process is efficient and environmentally acceptable.

  • 5. Applications

  • • Industrial economics: yield determines raw-material cost, plant capacity and profitability. In the Haber process only about 15% of the gas reacts per pass, so unreacted gas is recycled to raise the overall yield; the contact process similarly recycles SO₂.
  • • Laboratory synthesis (aspirin, soap, esters, dyes, polymers): the percentage yield is the standard measure of how successful a procedure was, and it is compared with literature values.
  • • Green chemistry: atom economy and the E-factor (waste ÷ product) supplement % yield to judge environmental performance — a high-yield reaction with a poor atom economy still generates much waste.
  • • Pharmaceutical and food quality control: yield data with assays (purity) are used for batch release, cost estimation and process optimisation, and yield losses indicate equipment or procedural faults.

  • 6. Limitations

  • • Percentage yield does not measure purity — a 100% yield can be an impure or wet product; a separate assay (%, melting point, chromatography) is needed.
  • • The theoretical value is only as good as its assumptions: the correct limiting reactant, the correct balanced equation and a single principal reaction must all be established first.
  • • A value greater than 100% is always an error (impure/wet product, unreacted starting material, weighing mistake) and should never be reported as a good result.
  • • For reversible reactions the theoretical yield calculated from stoichiometry is not attainable at all; the equilibrium yield depends on K, temperature, pressure and recycling — the practical yield must be measured.
  • • Percentage yield says nothing about energy consumption, solvent use, hazards or waste, so it cannot by itself establish that a process is efficient or green; and it cannot be predicted theoretically — it must be determined experimentally.

Confusion

  • ❌ A 100% yield means a pure product. ✅ Purity is a separate property; yields above 100% are the usual sign of an impure or wet product.
  • ❌ Percentage yield is calculated from the reactant taken in excess. ✅ It is calculated from the limiting reactant, which fixes the theoretical yield.
  • ❌ The theoretical yield is what you actually get. ✅ It is the maximum possible amount; the actual yield is always lower for practical reasons.
  • ❌ Yield and atom economy measure the same thing. ✅ Yield is about recovery of product; atom economy is about how much of the reactant mass ends up in the desired product.
  • ❌ Yield can be predicted by stoichiometry alone. ✅ Stoichiometry gives only the theoretical maximum; the actual yield depends on kinetics, equilibrium and technique.

Universal Facts

  • Haber process: about 15% conversion per pass with recycling; the overall yield is raised by recycling unreacted N₂ and H₂.
  • Typical undergraduate yields: aspirin 70–85%, soap 80–90%, esterification (equilibrium-limited) 60–70%.
  • Atom economy of addition reactions is 100%, whereas substitution reactions lose part of the reactant mass as by-product.
  • answer pattern for "Theoretical yield, experimental yield and % yield": define → method → result. Structure earns the marks on 6–10 mark questions.

Examples

  • Problem: 100 g CaCO₃ gives 47.6 g CaO. Find the % yield. Solution: n(CaCO₃) = 1 mol → theoretical CaO = 56 g; % yield = 47.6/56 × 100 = 85%.
  • Problem: 7.0 g of ethene (0.25 mol) is hydrated to give 9.2 g ethanol. Find the % yield. Solution: theoretical ethanol = 0.25 × 46 = 11.5 g; % yield = 9.2/11.5 × 100 = 80%.

Important Notes

  • Always quote the limiting reactant when explaining the theoretical yield — it is the only reactant that fixes it.
  • Mention purity separately: yield ≠ purity, and >100% means an impure or wet sample.
  • For reversible processes (Haber, esterification) state that equilibrium limits the yield and recycling is used to improve the overall figure.
