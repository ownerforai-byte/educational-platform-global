Notes

  • Focus of this tab

  • The law of reciprocal proportions (Richter, 1792; also called the law of equivalent proportions) — statement, the reference-element method, worked verification and its link to equivalent masses.

  • Statement

  • 'The masses of two elements which combine separately with a fixed mass of a third element are either the same as, or simple multiples of, the masses in which they combine with each other.' It is the law that connects compounds sharing a common third element.

  • The standard verification (H as reference)

  • • Water H₂O: H : O = 1 : 8.
  • • Methane CH₄: H : C = 1 : 3, i.e. C : H = 3 : 1 — 3 g C combines with 1 g H.
  • • Carbon dioxide CO₂: C : O = 12 : 32 = 3 : 8.
  • Check: with a fixed mass of H (1 g), the masses of C and O that combine are 3 g and 8 g. The ratio C : O = 3 : 8 — exactly the ratio in which C and O combine in CO₂. The law is verified.

  • Second pattern (oxygen as reference)

  • In SO₂, S : O = 32 : 32 = 1 : 1; in H₂O, H : O = 1 : 8. With a fixed mass of O (say 8 g), H = 1 g and S = 8 g combine. In H₂S the actual ratio is H : S = 1 : 16 = 1 : 16, which is a simple multiple (×2) of the predicted 8 : 1 → the law holds in 'simple multiple' form.

  • Connection to equivalent masses

  • The 'masses combining with a fixed mass of the reference element' are precisely the equivalent masses (H = 1.008, O = 8, C = 3 when valency 4 is chosen...). The law is the logical basis of the equivalent-mass concept and of the classical method of determining equivalent masses by analysis.

  • Limitations

  • It applies to pure compounds and exact analyses; like multiple proportions it fails for berthollides; and it requires the three elements actually to form the relevant binary compounds (e.g. some noble gases form none). It is also the hardest of the laws to verify experimentally because the data must be very accurate.

Confusion

  • ❌ This law compares two compounds of the same two elements. ✅ That is multiple proportions; reciprocal proportions links three elements through two compounds.
  • ❌ The ratio must be identical. ✅ It may be the same or a simple multiple — the 'simple multiple' clause is part of the statement.
  • ❌ The reference element must be hydrogen. ✅ Any common third element works; hydrogen and oxygen are just the textbook choices.

Universal Facts

  • Richter published the law in 1792 — before Dalton's atomic theory (1803).
  • Equivalent masses derived from this law underpin the classical titration and electrochemical (Faraday) calculations.
  • The law is also called the law of equivalent proportions for this reason.

Examples

  • Problem: Given C : H = 3 : 1 in CH₄ and H : O = 1 : 8 in H₂O, predict C : O in carbon's oxide. Solution: fix H = 1 g → C = 3 g, O = 8 g → predicted C : O = 3 : 8. Experiment gives 3 : 8 in CO₂ — verified (and 3 : 4 would be a simple multiple form, as in CO).

Important Notes

  • Keep the three compounds clearly identified: two with the reference element, one with the pair being compared.
  • Remember the 'simple multiple' clause — verification need not be equality.
