Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Measuring the Unevenness of Bonds

    A bond between unlike atoms is rarely purely ionic or purely covalent — it sits somewhere between, and three measurable quantities locate its position: bond length (how close the nuclei sit), ionic character (how far charge has shifted), and dipole moment (the electrical signature of that shift). This topic turns bonding from a picture into numbers.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Bond length

    • Definition: the equilibrium internuclear distance between the centres of two bonded atoms.
    • Measured by X-ray/electron diffraction or spectroscopy; equals the sum of covalent radii of the two atoms (covalent radius = half the internuclear distance in a homonuclear bond).
    • Bond order shrinks length: C–C 154 pm → C=C 134 pm → C≡C 120 pm; N–N 145 pm vs N≡N 110 pm.
    • Atomic size grows length down a group: H–F 92 pm, H–Cl 127 pm, H–Br 141 pm, H–I 160 pm.
    • Shorter bond → stronger bond (higher bond energy): H–F 565 kJ mol⁻¹ > H–Cl 431 > H–Br 364 > H–I 297.

    2.2 Ionic character and electronegativity difference

    • A 100% covalent bond has equal sharing; a 100% ionic bond has full transfer. Real bonds are partially ionic — the electron pair is pulled toward the more electronegative atom.
    • Greater ΔEN → greater ionic character. Pauling's scale: H 2.1, F 4.0, Cl 3.0, Br 2.8, I 2.5, Li 1.0, Na 0.9, Cs 0.7.
    • Hannay–Smyth empirical relation: % ionic character = 16(ΔEN) + 3.5(ΔEN)².
    • HF: ΔEN = 1.9 → 16(1.9) + 3.5(3.61) = 30.4 + 12.6 ≈ 43%.
    • HCl: ΔEN = 0.9 → 14.4 + 2.8 ≈ 17%; HBr ≈ 11%; HI ≈ 5%.
    • No bond is 100% ionic (even CsF retains covalent character); no homonuclear bond is 100% covalent-only in the polar sense — but homonuclear bonds are nonpolar by symmetry.

    2.3 Dipole moment

    • Definition: μ = q × d — the product of the charge separated and the distance of separation; a vector pointing from the positive to the negative end (chemists' convention: the crossbar of δ⁺→δ⁻).
    • Unit: debye (D); 1 D = 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m. A full electron charge across 100 pm gives ≈ 4.8 D (the "unit charge benchmark").
    • Molecular dipole = vector sum of all bond dipoles:
    • Zero despite polar bonds: CO₂ (linear, two equal dipoles cancel), BF₃ (trigonal planar, 120° three-way cancel), CH₄ and CCl₄ (tetrahedral symmetry).
    • Nonzero: H₂O 1.85 D (bent 104.5°), NH₃ 1.47 D (pyramidal), HF 1.91 D, HCl 1.03 D, CH₃Cl 1.87 D, CHCl₃ 1.04 D.
    • NF₃ surprise: same pyramidal shape as NH₃ but only 0.24 D — the N–F bond dipoles point toward F, opposing the lone-pair dipole, so they nearly cancel it. Shape alone does not fix the moment; direction matters.

    2.4 Applications of dipole moments

    • Geometry decisions: isomers with the same formula are told apart — cis-1,2-dichloroethene (1.90 D) vs trans (0 D); ortho-dichlorobenzene polar vs para (0 D).
    • Ionic character ranking: % ionic character ∝ μ(observed)/μ(100% ionic).
    • Polarity → solubility and boiling point downstream (topics 36's hydrogen bonding builds on this).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The HF ionic-character calculation (worked)

    • Observed μ(HF) = 1.91 D; H–F bond length = 92 pm.
    • Hypothetical 100% ionic dipole: μ = q·d = (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)(92 × 10⁻¹² m) = 1.47 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m = 4.42 D.
    • % ionic character = 1.91/4.42 × 100 ≈ 43% — matching the Hannay–Smyth estimate. HF is mostly covalent but strongly polar — the reason it hydrogen-bonds (topic 36).

    4.2 Why symmetry can erase polarity

    • Each C=O bond in CO₂ is strongly polar (C δ⁺, O δ⁻), but the two dipoles are equal and opposite along 180° — vector sum zero. Bend the molecule (as in SO₂, 119°) and cancellation fails → μ = 1.63 D.
    • This is how dipole moments measured molecular shapes historically: CO₂'s zero moment proved it linear before diffraction could.

    4.3 Bond parameters working together

    • Bond length, bond energy, bond order and ionic character move as a system: higher order → shorter → stronger; larger ΔEN → shorter (partial ionic attraction) and stronger, with HF the extreme (92 pm, 565 kJ mol⁻¹).
    • Percentage ionic character correlates with dipole moment — both measure the same charge shift from different directions.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Polar bonds ≠ polar molecule: CO₂ and CCl₄ have polar bonds but zero net moment — symmetry cancels. Polarity of a molecule is a vector question, not a bond question.

  • Dipole direction convention: the chemists' arrow points from positive to negative (crossed tail at δ⁺); physics defines the vector the opposite way. Quote μ magnitudes, not directions, unless the convention is stated.
  • Ionic character is not all-or-nothing: HF is 43% ionic and 57% covalent simultaneously — real bonds are mixtures, which is why Fajan's rules (topic 28) modulate ionic compounds toward covalency.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/02, adapted to this topic): Explain why CO₂ has zero dipole moment while SO₂ does not. — CO₂ is linear (180°): its two equal C=O bond dipoles point in exactly opposite directions and cancel, giving μ = 0. SO₂ is bent (≈119°, lone pair on S): the two S=O dipoles no longer oppose directly; their vector sum plus the lone-pair contribution gives a net moment ≈ 1.63 D. Geometry, not bond polarity alone, decides the molecular dipole.

  • NEB-style (3m): Calculate the percentage ionic character of HF. — μ(observed) = 1.91 D; bond length 92 pm; μ(100% ionic) = q·d = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 92 × 10⁻¹² = 1.47 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m = 4.42 D; % ionic = 1.91/4.42 × 100 ≈ 43%. Cross-check with Hannay–Smyth: ΔEN = 1.9 → 16(1.9) + 3.5(1.9)² ≈ 43%.
  • NEB-style (2m): Arrange HF, HCl, HBr, HI by bond length and bond strength, with reasons. — Length: H–F 92 < H–Cl 127 < H–Br 141 < H–I 160 pm (halogen size grows down the group). Strength runs the reverse: 565 > 431 > 364 > 297 kJ mol⁻¹ — longer bonds overlap less effectively and are weaker; HF's extra strength also reflects its high ionic character (43%).

Confusion

  • Zero dipole ≠ nonpolar bonds: CO₂'s bonds are highly polar; the molecule is nonpolar. State both facts when answering.
  • NF₃ vs NH₃: same shape, opposite dipole logic — in NF₃ the bond dipoles oppose the lone pair's contribution (0.24 D); in NH₃ they reinforce it (1.47 D).

Practice

  • Why does NF₃ have a much smaller dipole moment than NH₃ despite the same pyramidal shape?
  • A bond has length 127 pm and observed dipole 1.03 D. Calculate its percentage ionic character and identify the bond.
  • Explain why para-dichlorobenzene has zero dipole moment while the ortho isomer does not.

Universal Facts

  • Water's 1.85 D moment is why microwave ovens work — the rotating dipole absorbs 2.45 GHz radiation; a symmetric molecule like CO₂ would be transparent.

Summary

Bond parameters turn bonding into measurement. Bond length — the equilibrium internuclear distance, the sum of covalent radii — shrinks as bond order rises (C–C 154 → C≡C 120 pm) and grows as atoms enlarge (H–F 92 → H–I 160 pm), moving inversely with bond strength. Ionic character quantifies how far a heteronuclear bond has slid from pure sharing toward transfer: it scales with electronegativity difference via the Hannay–Smyth relation (HF ≈ 43%, HCl ≈ 17%, HBr ≈ 11%, HI ≈ 5%), and no real bond is 100% of either kind. Dipole moment (μ = q·d, debye = 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m) is the vector sum of all bond dipoles — so symmetric molecules (CO₂, BF₃, CH₄, CCl₄, trans and para isomers) read zero despite polar bonds, while bent H₂O (1.85 D) and pyramidal NH₃ (1.47 D) read large; NF₃'s 0.24 D shows that direction, not shape alone, decides the total. Divide the observed moment by the full-ionic value (q × bond length) and you recover the ionic percentage — one measurement connecting electronegativity, geometry and reactivity.

Central Concept

Bond characteristics — measuring what a bond is like

Branches

Topic

Level 1 — Foundation: bond length and order

Subtopics
1.
Name

Bond length

Points
  • The equilibrium distance between two bonded nuclei
  • Increases with atomic size, decreases with bond order
  • Measured in picometres (pm) or angstroms
2.
Name

Bond order and energy

Points
  • Bond order is the number of electron pairs shared
  • Higher bond order gives shorter, stronger bonds
  • Bond dissociation energy is the energy needed to break one mole of bonds
2.
Topic

Level 2 — Core: polarity measures

Subtopics
1.
Name

Dipole moment

Points
  • Dipole moment mu = charge x distance, measured in debye (D)
  • A polar bond has a non-zero dipole moment
  • A symmetric molecule can have polar bonds but zero net dipole moment
2.
Name

Ionic character

Points
  • Percentage ionic character = (observed dipole / calculated for full charge) x 100
  • HF is about 43% ionic character despite being called a covalent molecule
  • Electronegativity difference drives ionic character
3.
Topic

Level 3 — Upgraded: bond angle and comparisons

Subtopics
1.
Name

Bond angle

Points
  • The angle between two bonds at the central atom
  • Lone pair repulsion compresses bond angles
  • Hybridization sets the base angle: sp 180, sp2 120, sp3 109.5
2.
Name

Ordering questions

Points
  • Bond length order: single > double > triple
  • Bond strength order: triple > double > single
  • Dipole moment: NH3 > NF3 because the lone pair and bond dipoles reinforce in NH3
