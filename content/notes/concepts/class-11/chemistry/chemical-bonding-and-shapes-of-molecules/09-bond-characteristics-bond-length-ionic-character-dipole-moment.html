Notes

  • Bond length: The equilibrium internuclear distance at which the potential energy of a bond is minimized. It depends on: (1) atomic size — larger atoms form longer bonds (C–C: 154 pm, Si–Si: 235 pm), (2) bond order — higher order means shorter bonds (C–C: 154 pm, C=C: 134 pm, C≡C: 120 pm), (3) hybridization — more s-character shortens bonds (sp³ C–H: 109 pm, sp² C–H: 108 pm, sp C–H: 106 pm), (4) resonance — delocalization equalizes bond lengths (benzene C–C: 139 pm, intermediate between single and double).
  • Bond energy (bond enthalpy): The energy required to break one mole of a particular bond in the gas phase, producing gaseous atoms. Typical values: H–H 436 kJ/mol, C–H 413 kJ/mol, C–C 347 kJ/mol, C=C 614 kJ/mol, C≡C 839 kJ/mol, O=O 498 kJ/mol, N≡N 945 kJ/mol. Bond energy increases with bond order and decreases with increasing atomic size. The N≡C bond in N₂ is particularly strong, explaining nitrogen's low reactivity.
  • Bond order: The number of chemical bonds between a pair of atoms. In Lewis terms, bond order = (number of bonding electrons − number of antibonding electrons)/2 in MO theory. Higher bond order → shorter bond length and greater bond energy. For resonance structures, bond order is the average (e.g., O₃ has bond order 1.5, benzene C–C has bond order 1.5).
  • Ionic character percentage: No bond is 100% ionic (except in idealized models). The percent ionic character can be estimated using Pauling's equation: % ionic character = \left[1 - e^{-0.25(\chi_A - \chi_B)^2} ight] \times 100, where χ represents electronegativity. For H–F (χF​−χH​=3.98−2.20=1.78): % ionic ≈ 1 − e^(−0.25×3.17) ≈ 1 − 0.456 ≈ 54.4%. For NaCl (Δχ=2.23): % ionic ≈ 71%.
  • Dipole moment (μ): A vector quantity measuring the separation of positive and negative charges in a molecule. μ=q×d, where q is the magnitude of charge separation and d is the distance between the charges. Unit: Debye (D), where 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m. For a 100% ionic bond with charge separation of one elementary charge (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) at 100 pm: μ=1.602×10−19×100×10−12=4.8 D.
  • Percentage ionic character from dipole moment: % ionic character = μcalculated (100% ionic)​μobserved​​×100. For HCl: μobs​=1.08 D, μcalc​=4.8 D (for 100 pm bond), so % ionic = 4.81.08​×100≈22.5. This confirms HCl is predominantly covalent with partial ionic character.
  • Net dipole moment in polyatomic molecules: The molecular dipole moment is the vector sum of individual bond dipoles. In CO₂ (linear, O=C=O), the two C=O dipoles are equal and opposite → net μ=0. In H₂O (bent, 104.5°), the two O–H dipoles add vectorially → μ=1.85 D. In BF₃ (trigonal planar), the three B–F dipoles cancel → μ=0. In NH₃ (trigonal pyramidal), the three N–H dipoles and the lone pair contribution add up → μ=1.47 D.
  • Factors affecting dipole moment: (1) Electronegativity difference — greater difference → larger bond dipole, (2) Molecular geometry — symmetrical molecules may have zero net dipole despite polar bonds, (3) Lone pairs — contribute significantly to dipole moment (NH₃ > NF₃ despite F being more electronegative, because N's lone pair reinforces the N–F bond dipoles in NH₃ but opposes them in NF₃).
  • Bond length trends in halides: For a given halogen, bond length increases down the group: HF (92 pm) < HCl (127 pm) < HBr (141 pm) < HI (161 pm). This correlates with decreasing bond energy: HF (567) > HCl (431) > HBr (366) > HI (299) kJ/mol. Bond length and bond energy are inversely related — shorter bonds are stronger.
  • Fajans' rules and covalent character in ionic compounds: Small cations with high charge (e.g., Al³⁺, Be²⁺) and large anions (e.g., I⁻, S²⁻) polarize each other, introducing covalent character into supposedly ionic bonds. AlCl₃ is covalent (low melting point, soluble in organic solvents), while NaCl is ionic (high melting point, conducts when molten). This explains why AlCl₃ sublimes at 180°C while NaCl melts at 801°C.
  • Bond length and resonance: In nitrate ion NO₃⁻, all three N–O bonds are equivalent with bond length 124 pm, intermediate between N–O (140 pm) and N=O (115 pm). The bond order is 1.33, confirmed by X-ray diffraction. Similarly, in carbonate CO₃²⁻, all C–O bonds are 129 pm (between C–O single 143 pm and C=O double 120 pm).

  • Bond energy and thermodynamic cycles: Bond energies can be used to estimate reaction enthalpies using Hess's Law: ΔHrxn​≈∑BEreactants​−∑BEproducts​. For combustion of CH₄: break 4×C–H (4×413 = 1652 kJ) + 2×O=O (2×498 = 996 kJ) = 2648 kJ input; form 2×C=O (2×799 = 1598 kJ) + 4×O–H (4×463 = 1852 kJ) = 3450 kJ released.   • Ionic character and solubility: The percent ionic character predicts solubility behavior. NaCl (71% ionic) dissolves readily in water (dielectric constant 80) but not in hexane. AgCl (less ionic due to polarization by Ag⁺) is nearly insoluble in water. This is explained by Fajans' rules — the covalent character introduced by polarization reduces lattice energy dissolution driving force.

Confusion

  • Dipole moment direction: The dipole moment vector points from positive to negative charge (by IUPAC convention in physics), but chemists often draw the arrow with the plus end at the less electronegative atom and a cross at the positive end. Remember: the partial positive (δ+) is on the less electronegative atom, and δ− is on the more electronegative atom.
  • Zero dipole does not mean non-polar bonds: CO₂ and CCl₄ have zero net dipole moments, but their individual bonds are polar. The zero result comes from symmetrical geometry causing vector cancellation. Always distinguish between bond polarity (local) and molecular polarity (global).
  • Bond energy vs. bond length are inversely related but not perfectly: While generally shorter bonds are stronger, there are exceptions. For example, the F–F bond (159 pm, 159 kJ/mol) is anomalously weak compared to Cl–Cl (199 pm, 243 kJ/mol) due to lone pair–lone pair repulsion between the small fluorine atoms. Never assume bond energy monotonically decreases with bond length.
  • Ionic character percentage is approximate: Pauling's equation gives an estimate, not an exact value. The actual ionic character depends on the crystal environment, solvation, and other factors. For instance, gaseous NaCl has ~71% ionic character, but in the solid lattice, the ionic contribution is even more pronounced due to the collective electrostatic environment.
  • Bond order is not always an integer: In resonance structures and molecular orbital theory, bond orders can be fractional (1.5 for benzene C–C, 1.33 for carbonate, 2 for O₂ in MO theory). Fractional bond orders correspond to intermediate bond lengths and energies between the nearest integer values.

Practice

  • Calculate the percent ionic character of the H–F bond given: observed dipole moment = 1.86 D, bond length = 92 pm. (Expected answer: calculated μ=3.336×10−301.602×10−19×92×10−12​≈4.41 D; % ionic = 4.411.86​×100≈42.2.)
  • Arrange the following in order of increasing bond length: C≡C, C=C, C–C, benzene C–C. Explain your ordering using bond order and resonance concepts. (Answer: C≡C 120 pm < C=C 134 pm < benzene 139 pm < C–C 154 pm.)
  • Predict which molecule has the larger dipole moment: NF₃ or NH₃. Explain using vector addition of bond dipoles and the contribution of the lone pair. (Answer: NH₃ has μ=1.47 D > NF₃'s μ=0.23 D. In NH₃, the lone pair dipole reinforces the N–H bond dipoles; in NF₃, the lone pair dipole opposes the N–F bond dipoles because fluorine is more electronegative than nitrogen.)
  • Using bond energies, estimate the enthalpy change for the reaction: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). Given: N≡N = 945 kJ/mol, H–H = 436 kJ/mol, N–H = 391 kJ/mol. (Answer: ΔH=[945+3(436)]−[6(391)]=2253−2346=−93 kJ/mol. Experimental: −92 kJ/mol — excellent agreement!)

Universal Facts

  • The N≡N triple bond in molecular nitrogen (945 kJ/mol) is one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. This extraordinary bond strength is why N₂ is so inert at room temperature and why nitrogen fixation (breaking N≡N) requires extreme conditions or biological enzymes (nitrogenase).
  • The bond length of H₂ (74 pm) is the shortest known covalent bond because hydrogen has the smallest atomic radius. The H–H bond energy (436 kJ/mol) is also remarkably high for such a short bond, reflecting the excellent 1s–1s overlap.
  • The dipole moment of water (1.85 D) is responsible for its extraordinary solvent properties. No other common liquid has a combination of high dipole moment, small size, and hydrogen bonding capability, making water the 'universal solvent' for biological and chemical systems.
  • Bond energy values are average values derived from many compounds. The C–H bond energy of 413 kJ/mol is an average across methane, ethane, propane, etc. The actual C–H bond energy in CH₄ (427 kJ/mol for the first bond) differs from the average because each successive bond removal requires different energy.
  • The relationship between electronegativity difference and ionic character is not linear. Going from Δχ=0.5 to 1.0 roughly doubles the ionic character, but going from 2.0 to 2.5 adds much less. This is because the exponential function e−0.25Δχ2 decays rapidly at first then levels off.
