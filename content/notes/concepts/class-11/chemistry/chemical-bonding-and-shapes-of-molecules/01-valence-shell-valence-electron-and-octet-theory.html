Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Atoms Bother to Bond

    Every bond answers one question: how does an atom reach the stability of a noble gas? Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar…) are chemically inert because their valence shells are complete — ns²np⁶, an octet (or the duet of helium). All other atoms borrow, give, or share electrons to copy that arrangement. Kossel's electron-transfer picture explains ionic bonds; Lewis's electron-sharing picture explains covalent bonds. Both are bookkeeping devices built on the same observation: complete valence shells mean low energy.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Valence shell and valence electrons

    • Valence shell: the outermost occupied shell of an atom (highest n). Valence electrons: the electrons in it — the only electrons that participate in bonding.
    • Examples: Na (2,8,1) has 1 valence electron; Cl (2,8,7) has 7; C (2,4) has 4. Core electrons stay spectators.
    • The valency of an element = electrons it must lose, gain or share to complete its octet (Na: 1; O: 2; N: 3; C: 4; noble gases: 0).

    2.2 Octet rule (Kossel–Lewis, 1916)

    • Statement: atoms tend to acquire an eight-electron outer shell (ns²np⁶) by transferring or sharing electrons.
    • Driving force: a complete octet has the extra stability of the noble-gas configuration — lowest energy, no tendency to react.
    • Success: explains formulae NaCl, MgO, CaCl₂, CCl₄, H₂O, NH₃ and the inertness of He, Ne, Ar.

    2.3 Limitations of the octet rule

    • Incomplete octet: BeCl₂ (4 e⁻ around Be), BF₃ (6 e⁻ around B) — stable despite shortage.
    • Expanded octet: PCl₅ (10 e⁻), SF₆ (12 e⁻), H₂SO₄ (12 e⁻ around S) — possible from the third shell onward.
    • Odd-electron molecules: NO (11 valence e⁻), NO₂ (17), ClO₂ — octet impossible for all atoms.
    • Noble-gas compounds: XeF₂, XeF₄, XeO₃ exist — octet rule cannot explain them.
    • Shape-blind: the octet rule says nothing about molecular geometry — VSEPR and hybridisation are needed for shapes.

    2.4 Duet rule

    • Hydrogen and lithium reach the helium configuration (2 e⁻) — the duet. H₂, LiH, and the two electrons around H in all covalent compounds follow it.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 How the rule generates formulae

    • Na (2,8,1) transfers one electron to Cl (2,8,7): both reach (2,8) and (2,8,8) — Na⁺Cl⁻, formula NaCl.
    • Mg (2,8,2) gives one electron each to two Cl atoms → MgCl₂; Mg gives two to O → MgO.
    • Two Cl atoms each need one electron; they share a pair → Cl₂. C shares four pairs with four Cl → CCl₄.
    • Water: O shares one pair with each of two H atoms → H₂O with two lone pairs left over — which later explain its bent shape.

    4.2 Electron counting in practice

    • Total valence electrons = sum over atoms (adjust for charge: add electrons for anions, subtract for cations).
    • Example CO₂: 4 + 2(6) = 16 electrons → two C=O double bonds, two lone pairs on each O — every atom reaches an octet.
    • Example NH₄⁺: 5 + 4(1) − 1 = 8 electrons → four N–H bonds; N formally has no lone pair.

    4.3 Where the rule fails — and why that is still useful

    • BeCl₂ and BF₃ are electron-deficient and therefore strong Lewis acids — the "failure" predicts reactivity.
    • PCl₅ and SF₆ exist only because the 3d subshell is energetically reachable for third-period atoms; second-period atoms (C, N, O, F) can never expand.
    • The rule's shape-blindness is the opening act for VSEPR theory (topic 32) — octet decides how many pairs, VSEPR decides where they point.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Octet ≠ eight bonds: the octet counts electrons around an atom (bonding pairs counted twice, once per atom), not bonds. In CO₂ carbon owns 8 electrons via two double bonds — but forms only two bonds.

  • Valence electrons vs valency: Cl has 7 valence electrons but valency 1 — valency is 8 − valence electrons (for non-metals), not the count itself.
  • Expanded octets are third-row-only: C, N, O, F (period 2) have no accessible d orbitals; P, S, Cl and beyond can exceed 8. Never write five bonds on carbon.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): State the octet rule and give one limitation with an example. — Atoms tend to acquire eight electrons in their valence shell by transfer or sharing of electrons. Limitation: expanded octet — in SF₆, sulphur is surrounded by 12 electrons (six S–F bonds), which the octet rule cannot accommodate; similarly PCl₅ holds 10 electrons around P.

  • NEB-style (2m): Why are noble gases chemically inert? — Their valence shells are already complete (ns²np⁶; He 1s²). They have no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons — no bond formation lowers their energy — so they exist as monatomic molecules and resist reaction.
  • NEB-style (3m): Explain the formation of MgCl₂ using the octet rule. — Mg (2,8,2) has two excess valence electrons; Cl (2,8,7) is one short of an octet. Mg transfers one electron to each of two Cl atoms → Mg²⁺ (2,8) and two Cl⁻ (2,8,8). Both ions attain neon/argon configurations, and electrostatic attraction between Mg²⁺ and 2Cl⁻ builds the ionic lattice.

Confusion

  • Octet counts electrons, not bonds: CO₂'s carbon has 8 electrons but only 2 bonds (double bonds count four electrons each).
  • Valency vs valence electrons: for non-metals valency = 8 − valence electrons (Cl: 7 valence e⁻, valency 1).

Universal Facts

  • Helium completes its shell with just two electrons — the duet — because n = 1 holds only 1s²; it is the only noble gas whose "octet" is a pair.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): State the octet rule. Explain its application in the formation of NaCl and Cl₂. — The octet rule states that atoms acquire eight electrons in the valence shell by transfer or sharing. NaCl: Na (2,8,1) transfers one electron to Cl (2,8,7) → Na⁺ (2,8) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8), both noble-gas configurations, bound electrostatically. Cl₂: each Cl needs one electron; the two atoms share one pair, giving each Cl seven own + one shared = 8 electrons.
  • NEB-style (2m): Give two molecules in which the central atom has an incomplete octet, with electron counts. — BeCl₂: Be holds 4 electrons (two Be–Cl bonds) — stable but electron-deficient, hence a strong Lewis acid. BF₃: B holds 6 electrons (three B–F bonds) — also electron-deficient; BF₃ is a widely used organic catalyst for this reason.
  • NEB-style (3m): What is the expanded octet? Illustrate with PCl₅ and SF₆. — An expanded octet has more than eight electrons around the central atom, possible only for period-3 elements and beyond where empty d orbitals can accept electron density. PCl₅: five P–Cl bonds = 10 electrons around P (trigonal bipyramidal). SF₆: six S–F bonds = 12 electrons around S (octahedral). Period-2 atoms (C, N, O, F) can never expand because they have no accessible valence d orbitals.

Formulas

    • Valence electrons = group number (main-group elements)
    • Valency (non-metals) = 8 − valence electrons
    • Total valence e⁻ = Σ valence e⁻ (atoms) ± charge (anion +, cation −)

Key Points

  • Valence shell = outermost shell; its electrons do the bonding. Octet rule (Kossel–Lewis 1916): eight electrons in the outer shell via transfer (ionic) or sharing (covalent); duet for H/Li. Limitations: incomplete octet (BeCl₂, BF₃), expanded octet (PCl₅, SF₆ — third period onward), odd-electron molecules (NO, NO₂), noble-gas compounds (XeF₂), and no shape prediction.

Summary

Bonding exists to copy noble-gas stability. The valence shell (outermost shell) supplies the electrons; the octet rule of Kossel and Lewis says atoms reach ns²np⁶ either by transferring electrons (Kossel — ionic bonds, NaCl, MgO) or sharing pairs (Lewis — covalent bonds, Cl₂, CH₄), while hydrogen and lithium settle for the helium duet. The rule explains formulae brilliantly but fails five ways: incomplete octets (BeCl₂ with 4, BF₃ with 6 — which make them Lewis acids), expanded octets (PCl₅ 10, SF₆ 12 — period 3 onward only), odd-electron molecules (NO, NO₂), noble-gas compounds (XeF₂), and total silence on molecular shape. Octet decides how many electrons gather; VSEPR decides where they point.
