Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Molecule That Is Two Structures and Neither

    Ozone's two O–O bonds refuse to behave: experiment says both are 128-ish pm — neither the single-bond 148 pm nor the double-bond 121 pm. No single Lewis structure can draw this. The answer is resonance: the real molecule is a hybrid of two valid dot structures, with the true electron distribution being an average no single drawing captures. The double-headed arrow (↔) between them is chemistry's most misread symbol — it does not mean "flipping between" but "simultaneously both".

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition and conditions

    • Resonance (mesomerism): when a single Lewis structure cannot represent a molecule, two or more valid structures (canonical forms) are drawn, and the actual molecule is their resonance hybrid.
    • Conditions: the canonical forms differ only in the positions of electrons (π bonds and lone pairs) — never in the positions of atoms or σ bonds.
    • The hybrid is more stable than any single canonical form: the stabilisation is the resonance energy.

    2.2 The standard set

    • O₃: two forms, double bond left ↔ double bond right; both O–O bonds equal (≈128 pm).
    • CO₃²⁻: three forms, the C=O rotating among the three C–O positions; all three C–O bonds identical (≈129 pm).
    • Benzene C₆H₆: two Kekulé forms; all six C–C bonds equal (139 pm — between single 154 and double 134); resonance energy ≈ 150 kJ mol⁻¹.
    • SO₂ / SO₃: S–O links drawn partly as dative/double hybrids; all S–O bonds equal and intermediate in length.
    • NO₃⁻, NO₂⁻: three and two equivalent forms respectively.

    2.3 Resonance energy and stabilisation

    • Resonance energy = energy of the most stable canonical form − energy of the actual hybrid. Benzene: the hydrogenation of cyclohexene releases 120 kJ mol⁻¹; "cyclohexatriene" would predict 360 kJ mol⁻¹, but benzene releases only ≈208 kJ mol⁻¹ — the shortfall (≈150 kJ mol⁻¹) is resonance energy.
    • Consequences: benzene resists addition (prefers substitution), ozone's bonds are equal, the carbonate anion's charge is spread over three oxygens — each a direct experimental footprint.

    2.4 Rules for judging canonical forms

    • More covalent bonds, more full octets, less formal charge → greater contribution.
    • Negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom → greater contribution.
    • Equivalent structures contribute equally (O₃'s two forms are equal partners).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Ozone — the cleanest example

    • Structure I: O=O–O⁻ (FC: +1, 0, −1); Structure II: the mirror image.
    • Neither matches reality: measured O–O = 128 pm, between single (148 pm) and double (121 pm).
    • The hybrid gives each bond bond order 1.5 and spreads the −1 charge half on each terminal oxygen.
    • Ozone's reactivity (powerful oxidant) reflects the partial charge separation the hybrid describes.

    4.2 Carbonate — charge spreading in an anion

    • Three equivalent forms: the C=O occupies position 1, 2 or 3 in turn.
    • Hybrid: all three C–O bonds identical (129 pm), bond order 1⅓, each oxygen carrying −⅔ of the charge.
    • Consequence in the lab: all three C–O bonds of CO₃²⁻ respond identically — the anion has no "special" double-bond oxygen.

    4.3 Benzene — the resonance that rewrote organic chemistry

    • Two Kekulé forms with alternating double bonds; the hybrid has six equal C–C bonds (139 pm).
    • Resonance energy ≈ 150 kJ mol⁻¹ explains: benzene's unusual resistance to addition, its substitution chemistry, and its lower heat of hydrogenation than three isolated double bonds would predict.
    • The π electrons form a continuous ring cloud above and below the plane — delocalisation made visible.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    The molecule does not oscillate between forms: resonance is a static averaging — the hybrid exists all the time; the canonical forms are bookkeeping, never real intermediates.

  • Atoms never move in resonance: only π electrons and lone pairs shift; any "structure" with a relocated H or O is a different molecule (a tautomer), not a resonance form.
  • More forms ≠ more stability automatically: quality (octets, charge placement) matters; equivalent forms contribute equally regardless of count.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): What is resonance? Explain the resonance in ozone with bond-order consequences. — Resonance: when one Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule, two or more canonical forms are drawn and the real molecule is their hybrid. Ozone: O=O–O⁻ ↔ ⁻O–O=O. Both forms have the same atom positions and differ only in the π bond's side. The hybrid gives each O–O bond order 1.5 — hence both bonds measure 128 pm, between single (148) and double (121), and the negative charge is shared equally by the two terminal oxygens.

  • NEB-style (2m): Why are all three C–O bonds in the carbonate ion identical? — CO₃²⁻ has three equivalent resonance forms with the C=O on each oxygen in turn. The hybrid delocalises the π bond and the two negative charges over all three oxygens, giving each C–O bond order 1⅓ — all three measure 129 pm.
  • NEB-style (3m): Define resonance energy and estimate it for benzene from hydrogenation data. — Resonance energy is the extra stability of the hybrid over the most stable single canonical form. Cyclohexene hydrogenates releasing ≈120 kJ mol⁻¹ per double bond; three isolated bonds would release ≈360 kJ mol⁻¹, but benzene releases only ≈208 kJ mol⁻¹. The difference, ≈150 kJ mol⁻¹, is benzene's resonance energy — the energy price of its reluctance to undergo addition.

Confusion

  • ↔ is not ⇌: resonance is not an equilibrium between interconverting structures — there is no flipping, only one hybrid.
  • Equal bond lengths are the proof, not a coincidence: whenever measured bonds sit between single and double values, think resonance — that is the experimental signature.

Practice

  • Why does benzene undergo electrophilic substitution rather than addition?
  • Draw the three resonance forms of the nitrate ion and give each N–O bond order.
  • Explain why the two S–O bonds in SO₂ are equal although one is drawn as dative.

Universal Facts

  • Ozone's partial positive central oxygen and partial negative terminals make it both a strong oxidant and a dipolar molecule — resonance chemistry with weather-scale consequences (the ozone layer).

Estimation: hydrogenating one C=C in cyclohexene releases ≈120 kJ mol⁻¹; a hypothetical "cyclohexatriene" with three isolated double bonds would release ≈360 kJ mol⁻¹. Formulas

    • Resonance energy = E(best canonical) − E(hybrid)
    • Bond order (O₃ hybrid) = 1.5; (CO₃²⁻) = 1⅓; (benzene C–C) = 1.5
    • FC = V − (N + ½B) — used to rank canonical forms

Key Points

  • Resonance: single Lewis structure insufficient → two or more canonical forms (electrons move, atoms never) → real molecule is the more stable hybrid. Examples: O₃ (bond order 1.5, 128 pm), CO₃²⁻ (three forms, 1⅓), benzene (two Kekulé, 139 pm, resonance energy ≈150 kJ mol⁻¹), SO₂, NO₃⁻. Resonance energy = stability of hybrid over best single form. Best forms: most bonds, full octets, least FC, negative FC on EN atom.

Summary

When one dot structure cannot tell the truth, chemistry draws several and averages them. Resonance lets π electrons and lone pairs — never atoms — take multiple positions: ozone's two forms give both O–O bonds bond order 1.5 (measured 128 pm), the carbonate's three forms equalise all C–O bonds at 129 pm with −⅔ charge on each oxygen, and benzene's two Kekulé forms build a six-membered π cloud with every C–C at 139 pm and a resonance energy of ≈150 kJ mol⁻¹ — read straight off the hydrogenation shortfall. The hybrid, not any canonical form, is the real molecule: more stable than any single drawing, less eager to add across "double bonds", and identifiable in the lab by the fingerprint of equal, intermediate bond lengths. The double-headed arrow means "simultaneously both", never "flipping between".
