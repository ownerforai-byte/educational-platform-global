Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Bond That Bends Instead of Breaks

    Metals conduct, gleam, bend and hammer without shattering — behaviour no ionic or covalent model can explain. The electron sea (band) model can: metal atoms surrender their valence electrons to a delocalised cloud that binds the remaining cations electrostatically and flows freely. One picture, four properties — and the reason copper wires exist.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The electron sea model

    • Structure: a lattice of metal cations (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ …) fixed in position, immersed in a "sea" or "gas" of delocalised valence electrons shared by the whole crystal.
    • The metallic bond: the electrostatic attraction between the positive cation lattice and the negative delocalised electron cloud. It is non-directional — unlike covalent bonds, no particular angle is favoured.
    • Why metals have low ionisation energies: group 1 and 2 elements (and Al) lose valence electrons easily; the energy cost of delocalisation is repaid by the lattice stabilisation of many cation–electron interactions at once.

    4.1 Band-theory refinement (elementary)

    • In a crystal, the valence atomic orbitals of ~10²³ atoms merge into continuous energy bands; the valence band is filled, the conduction band above it is empty or partly filled.
    • Conductors (Cu, Ag): conduction band partly filled or overlapping the valence band — electrons move freely under a field.
    • Insulators: large gap between filled valence band and empty conduction band (diamond ≈ 5.5 eV).
    • Semiconductors (Si, Ge): small gap (≈1 eV) — thermal energy promotes a few electrons; conductivity rises with temperature (opposite to metals).

    2.2 Properties explained by the model

    • Electrical conductivity: delocalised electrons drift under an applied potential — the best conductors (Ag, Cu, Al) have the loosest hold on valence electrons.
    • Thermal conductivity: the same mobile electrons carry kinetic energy through the lattice.
    • Lustre: free electrons absorb and re-emit light of all visible frequencies at the surface — the metallic sheen.
    • Malleability and ductility: the non-directional bond means cation layers can slide past one another without breaking the bonding (unlike ionic crystals, where sliding brings like charges together and the crystal shatters).
    • High melting points (generally): strong cation–electron attraction; trend follows charge density — Na 98 °C < Mg 650 < Al 660 (with Al's 3+ core binding three electrons per atom). Mercury is the liquid exception (29.8 °C) — a relativistic/electronic-configuration curiosity.
    • Alloying: atoms of similar radius substitute in the lattice (brass Cu/Zn, steel Fe/C) — non-directional bonding tolerates foreign atoms, which is why metals but not salts form continuous solid solutions easily.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Metallic vs ionic — the sliding test

    • Both are lattices of positive cores with mobile negative charge (electrons vs anions), but geometry decides failure mode. Ionic: sliding a layer aligns Na⁺ against Na⁺ → repulsion → brittle fracture. Metallic: sliding a Cu layer still leaves every cation bathed in the same electron sea → bends, stays bonded. This single contrast answers the classic "why is a metal malleable but a salt crystal not".
    • Conductivity contrast: metals conduct as solids (electrons); ionic solids conduct only when molten or dissolved (ions then mobile).

    4.2 Trend reasoning with the model

    • Melting point across period 3: Na (1 valence e⁻, 1+ core) 98 °C → Mg (2 e⁻, 2+) 650 °C → Al (3 e⁻, 3+) 660 °C — more delocalised electrons and higher cation charge strengthen the sea's grip.
    • Conductivity ranking: Ag > Cu > Au > Al — one electron loosely held per atom suffices; transition metals add d-electron contribution.
    • Alloy hardness: steel's interstitial carbon blocks slip planes; substitutional Sn in bronze distorts the lattice — both raise strength by impeding the very sliding that makes pure metals soft.

    4.3 Where the model stops (elementary)

    • The simple electron sea explains conduction and malleability but not why some metals are magnetic (Fe, Co, Ni) or why Hg is liquid — band theory with electron correlations handles those; the syllabus stops at the elementary band picture.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    The cations do not move during conduction: only the electron sea flows; the lattice stays fixed. (In electrolysis the ions move — different process.)

  • Metallic bonds are not covalent: no localized shared pairs between specific atoms — the electrons are collective property of the crystal.
  • Low mp metals exist: Na (98 °C), Hg (liquid) — "metals have high mp" is a trend, not a law; the trend tracks valence electrons and cation charge.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Define the metallic bond and explain malleability, conductivity and lustre using the electron sea model. — Bond: the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of metal cations and a cloud of delocalised valence electrons. Malleability: the bond is non-directional; when cation layers slide under stress, the electron sea immediately re-adjusts and bonding persists — the metal deforms instead of fracturing (ionic crystals shatter because sliding brings like charges face-to-face). Conductivity: the delocalised electrons are mobile; an applied potential drives them through the crystal (electrical), and their motion carries kinetic energy (thermal). Lustre: free electrons absorb and instantly re-emit light across the visible spectrum, giving the characteristic sheen.

  • NEB-style (2m): Explain the melting-point rise Na (98 °C) → Mg (650 °C) → Al (660 °C). — Moving across period 3 the valence electron count rises (1 → 2 → 3) and the cation core charge rises (Na⁺ → Mg²⁺ → Al³⁺) while the ionic radius shrinks. More delocalised electrons per atom and stronger cation–electron attraction mean more energy is needed to disrupt the lattice — melting point and hardness climb together.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why do metals form alloys so readily while ionic crystals do not? — The metallic bond is non-directional and tolerant: foreign atoms of similar radius simply substitute into the cation lattice (brass: Zn in Cu) or squeeze into interstitial sites (steel: C in Fe), with the electron sea adjusting. Ionic crystals demand strict charge and size balance — substituting Na⁺ by a differently charged ion breaks stoichiometry and electrostatics, so salts form only limited solid solutions.

Confusion

  • Conduction is electron flow, not ion flow: in a copper wire the Cu²⁺-core lattice never migrates; in molten NaCl it is the ions that carry current — keep the two mechanisms separate.
  • "Electron sea" is a model, not a photograph: it is the elementary picture; band theory is its quantum refinement — cite both for full marks on "explain metallic bonding".

Practice

  • Explain why the electrical conductivity of silicon increases with temperature while that of copper decreases.
  • Account for the hardness of steel compared with pure iron using the interstitial model.

Universal Facts

  • Mercury is liquid at room temperature (29.8 °C mp) — the only metal — used in thermometers and barometers precisely because its electron sea still conducts while the lattice barely coheres.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Define the metallic bond and use the electron sea model to explain malleability, electrical conductivity and lustre of metals. — Definition: a metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between a three-dimensional lattice of metal cations and a cloud of delocalised valence electrons (the "electron sea") that belongs to the whole crystal. Malleability: the bond is non-directional. When a hammer blow slides one layer of cations past another, the delocalised electrons instantly re-adjust and every cation remains surrounded by negative charge — the deformation is absorbed without breaking the bond, so metals bend and flatten. (In an ionic crystal the same slide brings like charges face to face; repulsion shatters it.) Electrical conductivity: the valence electrons are not bound to any atom; an applied potential difference sets them drifting through the lattice, constituting current — the cations themselves stay fixed. Lustre: the free electrons absorb incident light of all visible frequencies and re-emit it at the surface, producing the characteristic metallic sheen.
  • NEB-style (2m): Explain the rise in melting point from sodium (98 °C) through magnesium (650 °C) to aluminium (660 °C). — Across period 3 each element contributes more valence electrons to the sea (1, 2 and 3 respectively) and forms a cation of higher charge (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺) with a smaller radius. The strength of the metallic bond therefore grows — more electrons per atom attracting a more highly charged, more closely packed cation lattice — so progressively more energy is needed to disrupt the lattice, and melting point (with hardness) climbs from Na through Mg to Al.
  • NEB-style (2m): Silicon's conductivity rises with temperature but copper's falls. Explain using band theory. — Silicon (semiconductor, gap ≈ 1 eV): at low temperature the conduction band is nearly empty; heating promotes electrons across the small gap into the conduction band, so the number of charge carriers grows exponentially and conductivity increases. Copper (conductor): the conduction band is already partly filled with abundant free electrons; heating increases lattice vibrations, which scatter the electrons and impede their drift — carrier number is unchanged while mobility drops, so conductivity decreases. The sign of the temperature response separates semiconductors from metals.

Formulas

    • mp trend (period 3 metals): Na 98 < Mg 650 < Al 660 °C
    • Band gaps: conductor ≈ 0; Si ≈ 1 eV; diamond ≈ 5.5 eV
    • Conductivity ranking: Ag > Cu > Au > Al

Key Points

  • Metallic bond = cation lattice + delocalised valence electrons; non-directional. Explains: conduction (mobile electrons), thermal conduction, lustre (re-emission), malleability/ductility (layers slide, sea re-adjusts — ionic crystals shatter instead), high mp trend Na < Mg < Al. Band theory: valence/conduction bands; conductors overlapping, semiconductors ≈1 eV gap (Si, Ge), insulators ≈5.5 eV (diamond); semiconductor conductivity rises with temperature. Alloys: substitutional (brass) or interstitial (steel); non-directional bonding tolerates them.

Summary

The metallic bond is the crystal-scale bargain: metal atoms, with low ionisation energies, surrender their valence electrons into a delocalised sea that electrostatically binds the fixed cation lattice — non-directional, collective, and forgiving. That forgiveness is the property engine: layers of cations slide under a hammer and the sea re-adjusts (malleability, where ionic crystals shatter); the same mobile electrons drift under a potential (conductivity, Ag > Cu > Au > Al) and carry heat; they absorb and re-emit all visible light (lustre); and their count and cation charge set the melting ladder Na 98 → Mg 650 → Al 660 °C. Band theory refines the picture: overlapping or partly filled bands conduct, a ≈1 eV gap makes silicon a semiconductor whose conductivity rises with temperature, and diamond's 5.5 eV gap insulates. Non-directionality also welcomes guests — substitutional brass, interstitial steel — turning soft metals into engineering alloys. One delocalised cloud, four properties and an industry.
