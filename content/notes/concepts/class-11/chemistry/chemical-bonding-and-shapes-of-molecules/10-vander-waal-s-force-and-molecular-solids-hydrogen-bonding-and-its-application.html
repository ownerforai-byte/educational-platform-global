Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Weak Forces That Hold Matter Together

    Covalent bonds build molecules; intermolecular forces decide whether those molecules are gas, liquid or solid. Weakest to strongest: London dispersion → Debye (dipole-induced) → Keesom (dipole-dipole) → hydrogen bond. The hydrogen bond — 5 to 40 kJ mol⁻¹, an order of magnitude above the rest — is the one that sets water's boiling point at 100 °C, holds DNA's double helix together, and gives ice a structure that floats (NEB 2024's verified question).

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 van der Waals forces — the three tiers

    • London dispersion forces (instantaneous dipole–induced dipole): electron clouds flicker; a momentary dipole induces one in the neighbour. Present in all molecules; the only force in nonpolar ones (N₂, noble gases). Strength grows with polarisability — more electrons, bigger cloud: I₂ (solid) vs Cl₂ (gas); boiling points climb down the halogen group.
    • Debye forces (dipole–induced dipole): a permanent dipole polarises a neighbouring nonpolar molecule (HCl … Ar).
    • Keesom forces (dipole–dipole): permanent dipoles align (HCl … HCl).
    • All three are weak (≈0.4–4 kJ mol⁻¹ per interaction), short-range (∝ 1/r⁶), and collectively called van der Waals forces.

    2.2 Molecular solids

    • Solids whose units are molecules held by intermolecular forces only: nonpolar (I₂, dry ice CO₂, naphthalene, solid noble gases — dispersion only) and polar (ice, solid HCl, glucose — plus dipole/H-bond terms).
    • Consequences: soft, low melting (often below 300 °C), volatile, electrical insulators, soluble in nonpolar (or matching-polarity) solvents. Contrast: ionic/ metallic/ covalent-network solids melt hundreds to thousands of degrees higher.

    2.3 The hydrogen bond

    • Definition: the attraction between a hydrogen bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (F, O, N) and a lone pair on another such atom: X–H ⋯ Y, X, Y = F, O, N.
    • Requirements: H must carry a large δ⁺ (only F, O, N pull hard enough); Y must have a lone pair and high electronegativity; small size permits close approach.
    • Strength: 5–40 kJ mol⁻¹ per bond — far weaker than covalent (H–O 463 kJ mol⁻¹) but ~10× the strongest dispersion contact.
    • Types: intermolecular (H₂O ⋯ H₂O; HF chains; ethanol) and intramolecular (o-nitrophenol's O–H ⋯ O within one molecule — which reduces association between molecules and lowers its boiling point relative to para).

    2.4 Consequences to quote

    • Water's anomaly: H₂O boils at 100 °C; H₂S (same group, no H-bonds) at −60 °C. Each water can form up to four H-bonds (two donor H, two lone-pair acceptors) — a 3-D network that costs enormous energy to break (NEB 2024).
    • HF 19.5 °C vs HCl −85 °C; NH₃ −33 °C vs PH₃ −87.7 °C — the same story on the F and N rows.
    • Ice floats: in ice each O is tetrahedrally H-bonded to 4 neighbours → open hexagonal lattice with ≈11% empty space; density 0.92 g cm⁻³ < water 1.00. On melting, some H-bonds collapse and molecules pack closer — a rare solid-less-dense-than-liquid case.
    • Biology: the DNA double helix is zipped by A=T (2 H-bonds) and G≡C (3 H-bonds) pairs; proteins fold on backbone H-bonds.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/02): what is hydrogen bonding? Why does water boil so high?

    • Definition (section 2.3) with the X–H ⋯ Y sketch and the F/O/N restriction.
    • Why water boils high: every H₂O can donate two H-bonds (its two O–H hydrogens) and accept two (oxygen's two lone pairs) — a four-connected 3-D network. Vaporising water means breaking a large fraction of this network, so the energy cost is enormous: bp 100 °C. The sulphur analogue H₂S, with no H-bonding (S is large, only weakly electronegative), relies on weak dispersion + dipole forces alone and boils at −60 °C — a 160 °C gap purely from hydrogen bonding.

    4.2 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/04, cross-topic): NH₃ boils higher than PH₃

    • NH₃ (−33 °C) hydrogen-bonds (N: small, EN 3.0, one lone pair); PH₃ (−87.7 °C) cannot (P larger, EN 2.1, H–P barely polar). Shape and bonding together: the N–H ⋯ N network in liquid ammonia raises its boiling point ≈55 °C above phosphine's.

    4.3 The dispersion ladder — why I₂ is solid and Cl₂ is gas

    • Same bonding family, same nonpolarity; only size differs. I₂ (53 electrons) is highly polarisable — its flickering cloud induces strong correlated dipoles → solid at room temperature. Cl₂ (17 electrons) barely polarisable → gas. Boiling points: F₂ −188 °C, Cl₂ −34 °C, Br₂ 59 °C, I₂ 184 °C. Dispersion strength is a size story.

    4.4 Intramolecular vs intermolecular — the nitrophenol test

    • o-Nitrophenol: O–H ⋯ O=N within the same molecule (six-membered chelate ring) — the H-bond is "used up" internally, molecules stay unassociated → steam-volatile, lower bp.
    • p-Nitrophenol: the groups are too far apart for intramolecular bonding; molecules H-bond to each other → associated, higher bp, not steam-volatile. Same formula, different intermolecular architecture.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Hydrogen bonding needs F, O, N — not just any H: C–H hydrogens are barely positive (C EN 2.5); CH₄ cannot hydrogen-bond. Chlorine, though EN 3.0, is too large for the close approach — HCl's "H-bond" is negligible.

  • Intramolecular H-bonds lower, not raise, boiling points: the bond is spent inside the molecule, leaving less for intermolecular association (o- vs p-nitrophenol).
  • Dispersion forces are not "nothing": they exist in every molecule, including water — they are simply dwarfed by the H-bond network there.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/02): What is hydrogen bonding? Explain why water has an unusually high boiling point. — Hydrogen bonding is the electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative small atom (F, O or N) and a lone pair on another electronegative atom: X–H ⋯ Y. In water each molecule donates two H-bonds via its O–H hydrogens and accepts two via oxygen's lone pairs, building a tetrahedral 3-D network. Boiling requires breaking much of this network; the energy cost pushes the boiling point to 100 °C, whereas non-hydrogen-bonded H₂S boils at −60 °C. The same effect gives HF (19.5 °C) and NH₃ (−33 °C) their anomalously high boiling points.

  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/04): Ammonia has a higher boiling point than phosphine. Explain. — NH₃: N is small and electronegative (3.0); N–H bonds are strongly polar; ammonia molecules link through N–H ⋯ N hydrogen bonds, so extra energy is needed to separate them — bp −33 °C. PH₃: P is larger and barely electronegative (2.1); P–H is nearly nonpolar and no H-bonding is possible — only weak dispersion/dipole forces hold the molecules — bp −87.7 °C. The ≈55 °C difference is the hydrogen-bond signature.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is ice less dense than liquid water? — In ice, every oxygen is tetrahedrally hydrogen-bonded to four neighbours, producing an open hexagonal lattice with ≈11% unoccupied space (density 0.92 g cm⁻³). Melting collapses a fraction of the H-bonds, letting molecules pack closer (1.00 g cm⁻³) — which is why ice floats and lakes freeze from the top.

Confusion

  • H-bond strength vs covalent strength: an H-bond is ~10× weaker than the O–H covalent bond (5–40 vs 463 kJ mol⁻¹) — boiling breaks H-bonds, not covalent bonds; water does not decompose at 100 °C.
  • "van der Waals" as umbrella: the term covers dispersion + Debye + Keesom; the hydrogen bond is usually treated separately because it is far stronger and directional.

Practice

  • Why is the boiling point of ethanol (78 °C) far above that of its isomer dimethyl ether (−24 °C)?
  • Account for the boiling-point climb F₂ → I₂ using dispersion-force reasoning.
  • Explain why o-nitrophenol is steam-volatile but p-nitrophenol is not.

Universal Facts

  • Gecko feet and the surface tension of water both trace to intermolecular attractions; capillary rise in soil and xylem is cohesion (H-bonds) plus adhesion.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (3m): What is hydrogen bonding? Explain why water has an unusually high boiling point. — Definition: a hydrogen bond is the electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (F, O or N) and a lone pair on another electronegative atom — written X–H ⋯ Y. It is weaker than a covalent bond (≈5–40 kJ mol⁻¹) but roughly ten times stronger than ordinary dipole or dispersion contacts. Water: each H₂O molecule has two O–H hydrogens to donate and two lone pairs on oxygen to accept — up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule, forming a tetrahedral three-dimensional network throughout the liquid. To vaporise water, a large fraction of this network must be broken, so the energy required is very large and the boiling point is pushed to 100 °C. Its group neighbour H₂S cannot hydrogen-bond (sulphur is large and only weakly electronegative) and boils at −60 °C — a 160 °C gap attributable almost entirely to hydrogen bonding. HF (19.5 °C) and NH₃ (−33 °C) show the same anomaly.
  • NEB 2024 (2m): Ammonia has a higher boiling point than phosphine (PH₃). Explain on the basis of molecular shape and bonding. — Both molecules are pyramidal with a lone pair, but the bonding differs: in NH₃ nitrogen is small and highly electronegative (3.0), the N–H bonds are strongly polar, and neighbouring molecules link through N–H ⋯ N hydrogen bonds — breaking these costs extra energy, so NH₃ boils at −33 °C. In PH₃ phosphorus is larger and much less electronegative (2.1); P–H is nearly nonpolar, no hydrogen bonding is possible, and only weak dispersion/dipole forces hold the molecules — bp −87.7 °C. The ≈55 °C difference is the hydrogen-bond signature of ammonia.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is ice less dense than liquid water, and what follows for aquatic life? — In ice, every oxygen atom is tetrahedrally hydrogen-bonded to four neighbouring oxygens, producing an open hexagonal lattice with about 11% empty space; density ≈ 0.92 g cm⁻³. On melting, part of the H-bond network collapses and molecules pack more closely — liquid water is denser (1.00 g cm⁻³), so ice floats. Consequence: ponds and lakes freeze from the surface downward; the floating ice layer insulates the water beneath, letting fish and aquatic plants survive winter — a small density anomaly with ecosystem-scale consequences.

Formulas

    • H-bond energy: 5–40 kJ mol⁻¹ (vs O–H covalent 463)
    • Boiling points: H₂O 100, H₂S −60, HF 19.5, HCl −85, NH₃ −33, PH₃ −87.7 °C
    • Ice density 0.92 g cm⁻³ < water 1.00
    • Halogen bp: F₂ −188, Cl₂ −34, Br₂ 59, I₂ 184 °C

Key Points

  • Intermolecular ladder: London dispersion (universal, ∝ polarisability/size) < Debye < Keesom < hydrogen bond (5–40 kJ mol⁻¹, X–H ⋯ Y with F/O/N). Molecular solids: soft, low mp, insulators (I₂, dry ice, naphthalene). Water: 4-connected H-bond network → bp 100 °C vs H₂S −60 °C (NEB 2024); HF 19.5 vs HCl −85; NH₃ −33 vs PH₃ −87.7 (NEB 2024). Ice: open tetrahedral lattice, 0.92 g cm⁻³, floats. o-Nitrophenol intramolecular → steam-volatile; para intermolecular → high bp. Dispersion ladder: F₂ −188 → I₂ 184 °C.

Summary

Between molecules, the forces are a ladder: London dispersion (universal, growing with electron count — the reason I₂ is solid and Cl₂ gaseous), Debye and Keesom dipole terms, and above them the hydrogen bond — X–H ⋯ Y where X and Y are small, electronegative F, O or N, worth 5–40 kJ mol⁻¹. Molecular solids (I₂, dry ice, naphthalene) are soft, volatile insulators because only these weak forces bind them. Hydrogen bonding's fingerprints are the boiling-point anomalies: water's four-connected network lifts H₂O to 100 °C against H₂S's −60 °C (NEB 2024), HF to 19.5 °C against HCl's −85 °C, NH₃ to −33 °C against PH₃'s −87.7 °C (NEB 2024). The same network freezes into ice's open tetrahedral lattice — 0.92 g cm⁻³, floating, lake-insulating. And architecture matters as much as presence: o-nitrophenol spends its H-bond internally (steam-volatile) while para associates externally (high bp), and DNA stores its code in two-versus-three H-bond pairs. Weak forces, large consequences.

  • H-bond strength vs covalent strength: an H-bond is ~10× weaker than the O–H covalent bond (5–40 vs 463 kJ mol⁻¹) — boiling breaks H-bonds, not covalent bonds; water does not decompose at 100 °C.
  • "van der Waals" as umbrella: the term covers dispersion + Debye + Keesom; the hydrogen bond is usually treated separately because it is far stronger and directional.
Key Points
  • Intermolecular ladder: London dispersion (universal, ∝ polarisability/size) < Debye < Keesom < hydrogen bond (5–40 kJ mol⁻¹, X–H ⋯ Y with F/O/N). Molecular solids: soft, low mp, insulators (I₂, dry ice, naphthalene). Water: 4-connected H-bond network → bp 100 °C vs H₂S −60 °C (NEB 2024); HF 19.5 vs HCl −85; NH₃ −33 vs PH₃ −87.7 (NEB 2024). Ice: open tetrahedral lattice, 0.92 g cm⁻³, floats. o-Nitrophenol intramolecular → steam-volatile; para intermolecular → high bp. Dispersion ladder: F₂ −188 → I₂ 184 °C.
Important Concepts
  • Two anomalous-property generators: (1) H-bond count per molecule — water's four-connectivity beats HF's chains and NH₃'s lower donor capacity, which is why water's bp rise is the largest of the trio; (2) intramolecular H-bonds subtract from intermolecular association.
Examples
  • DNA's zipper — A=T held by 2 H-bonds, G≡C by 3: weak individually, but thousands in sequence give a stable double helix that still unzips for replication — hydrogen bonding doing information storage.
Universal Facts
  • Gecko feet and the surface tension of water both trace to intermolecular attractions; capillary rise in soil and xylem is cohesion (H-bonds) plus adhesion.
Important Statements
  • "Hydrogen bonding is the electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O, N) and a lone pair of another electronegative atom."

Practice Questions
  • NEB 2024 (3m): What is hydrogen bonding? Explain why water has an unusually high boiling point. — Definition: a hydrogen bond is the electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (F, O or N) and a lone pair on another electronegative atom — written X–H ⋯ Y. It is weaker than a covalent bond (≈5–40 kJ mol⁻¹) but roughly ten times stronger than ordinary dipole or dispersion contacts. Water: each H₂O molecule has two O–H hydrogens to donate and two lone pairs on oxygen to accept — up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule, forming a tetrahedral three-dimensional network throughout the liquid. To vaporise water, a large fraction of this network must be broken, so the energy required is very large and the boiling point is pushed to 100 °C. Its group neighbour H₂S cannot hydrogen-bond (sulphur is large and only weakly electronegative) and boils at −60 °C — a 160 °C gap attributable almost entirely to hydrogen bonding. HF (19.5 °C) and NH₃ (−33 °C) show the same anomaly.
  • NEB 2024 (2m): Ammonia has a higher boiling point than phosphine (PH₃). Explain on the basis of molecular shape and bonding. — Both molecules are pyramidal with a lone pair, but the bonding differs: in NH₃ nitrogen is small and highly electronegative (3.0), the N–H bonds are strongly polar, and neighbouring molecules link through N–H ⋯ N hydrogen bonds — breaking these costs extra energy, so NH₃ boils at −33 °C. In PH₃ phosphorus is larger and much less electronegative (2.1); P–H is nearly nonpolar, no hydrogen bonding is possible, and only weak dispersion/dipole forces hold the molecules — bp −87.7 °C. The ≈55 °C difference is the hydrogen-bond signature of ammonia.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is ice less dense than liquid water, and what follows for aquatic life? — In ice, every oxygen atom is tetrahedrally hydrogen-bonded to four neighbouring oxygens, producing an open hexagonal lattice with about 11% empty space; density ≈ 0.92 g cm⁻³. On melting, part of the H-bond network collapses and molecules pack more closely — liquid water is denser (1.00 g cm⁻³), so ice floats. Consequence: ponds and lakes freeze from the surface downward; the floating ice layer insulates the water beneath, letting fish and aquatic plants survive winter — a small density anomaly with ecosystem-scale consequences.
