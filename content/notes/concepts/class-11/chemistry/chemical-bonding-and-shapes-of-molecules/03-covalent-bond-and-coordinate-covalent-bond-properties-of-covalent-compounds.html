Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Bond of Shared Ownership

    Between two identical non-metal atoms there is no transfer — only sharing. Two chlorine atoms each bring one electron; the pair sits between both nuclei and pulls on each equally: the covalent bond. When one atom supplies both electrons, the link is a coordinate (dative) bond — chemically identical once formed, but with a revealing origin story. This topic builds the bond, its multiple varieties, and the property profile of covalent compounds.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Covalent bond — definition and formation

    • Definition: the bond formed by the mutual sharing of one or more electron pairs between atoms, each contributing one electron per pair.
    • H₂: two H atoms share one pair; each nucleus attracts the pair — energy minimum at 74 pm, bond energy 436 kJ mol⁻¹.
    • Cl₂: each Cl (2,8,7) contributes one electron → shared pair completes both octets.
    • Forces: attraction (nucleus–opposite electron cloud) versus repulsion (nucleus–nucleus, electron–electron) balance at the bond length.

    2.2 Multiple bonds

    • Single bond (–): one shared pair — H₂, Cl₂, CH₄ (C–H).
    • Double bond (=): two shared pairs — O₂, CO₂ (O=C=O), ethene C₂H₄.
    • Triple bond (≡): three shared pairs — N₂, ethyne C₂H₂. Triple > double > single in bond energy, and shorter in length.

    2.3 Coordinate (dative) bond

    • Definition: a bond in which the shared pair is supplied by one atom only (the donor); the acceptor contributes none.
    • Conditions: donor has a lone pair; acceptor has an empty orbital.
    • Examples: NH₄⁺ (N donates its lone pair to H⁺), H₃O⁺ (H₂O donates to H⁺), NH₃·BF₃ (N→B), O₃ (one O→O link), SO₂/SO₃ (drawn with S→O links), metal complexes (Ni(CO)₄).
    • Key point: once formed, a coordinate bond is indistinguishable from an ordinary covalent bond — same strength, same length. It is conventionally drawn as an arrow (→) from donor to acceptor.

    2.4 Properties of covalent compounds

    • Low melting/boiling points: forces between molecules are weak van der Waals/dipole forces (I₂ sublimes; CH₄ bp −161 °C) — the covalent bonds themselves are strong, but they are intramolecular.
    • Non-conductors: no free ions or electrons (exceptions: graphite, HCl(aq) which ionises in water).
    • Solubility: dissolve in non-polar (organic) solvents; generally insoluble in water — "like dissolves like".
    • Molecular, not lattices of ions: exist as discrete molecules with definite shapes; show isomerism (directional bonds permit structural variety).
    • Reactions are slow: covalent bonds must be broken and rearranged via molecular collisions — unlike the lightning-fast ion recombinations.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Energy profile of H₂ formation

    • As two H atoms approach: attraction lowers potential energy; below ~74 pm, nucleus–nucleus repulsion dominates and energy climbs steeply.
    • The minimum (−436 kJ mol⁻¹ relative to separated atoms) is the bond energy; the internuclear distance there is the bond length. The same logic underlies every covalent bond.

    4.2 Coordinate bond anatomy — NH₄⁺

    • N in NH₃ has one lone pair; H⁺ offers an empty 1s orbital.
    • N donates the pair: N→H⁺ creates the fourth N–H link; all four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ become equivalent (identical length/strength) — the origin is invisible in the final ion.
    • Same recipe: H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺ (hydronium — the species that carries "H⁺" in water); NH₃ + BF₃ → NH₃·BF₃ adduct (N donates, B accepts — filling boron's deficient octet).

    4.3 Why "low melting point" does not mean "weak bond"

    • Diamond (covalent, network solid) melts above 3500 °C; iodine (covalent, molecular solid) sublimes at 184 °C. The difference is what holds between molecules: diamond is one giant covalent network; I₂ molecules cling by weak dispersion forces. Quote both to show the distinction between intramolecular and intermolecular forces.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Coordinate bond ≠ weaker bond: after formation it is electronically identical to any covalent bond; only the bookkeeping of origin differs.

  • Intermolecular vs intramolecular: low boiling points reflect weak forces between molecules; the covalent bonds within each molecule remain strong.
  • H⁺ never travels free in water: it immediately coordinate-bonds to H₂O forming H₃O⁺ — writing bare H⁺(aq) is a shorthand, not a structure.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2024, 2m): Distinguish between ionic and covalent bonds with one example of each. — Ionic: complete transfer (NaCl); covalent: mutual sharing (H₂O). Property contrast: mp/bp, conductivity, solubility as tabulated in section 5.

  • NEB-style (3m): What is a coordinate covalent bond? Explain the formation of NH₄⁺ and H₃O⁺. — A coordinate bond is a shared pair donated by a single atom. In NH₄⁺, nitrogen's lone pair is donated to H⁺ (empty 1s), creating a fourth N–H link; all four bonds become equivalent. In H₃O⁺, the water oxygen donates its lone pair to H⁺, giving a dative O→H bond on top of the two ordinary O–H bonds.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why are covalent compounds generally non-conductors of electricity? — They contain no free ions and no free electrons; the shared pairs are locked between specific nuclei. (Exceptions to mention: aqueous HCl ionises; graphite conducts via delocalised electrons.)

Confusion

  • Origin vs identity: a coordinate bond looks identical to a covalent bond after formation — the arrow is history, not a property.
  • Diamond vs iodine: both covalent, yet diamond melts above 3500 °C and iodine at 184 °C — network vs molecular solid; intermolecular forces set the difference.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (2m): Distinguish between ionic and covalent bonds with one example of each. — Ionic bond: complete electron transfer from metal to non-metal; oppositely charged ions attract electrostatically in a lattice — NaCl. Covalent bond: mutual sharing of electron pairs between non-metal atoms — H₂O. Consequences: ionic compounds are high-melting, molten/aqueous conductors, water-soluble; covalent compounds are low-melting, non-conducting molecular species, generally organic-soluble.
  • NEB-style (3m): Define coordinate covalent bond. Describe the formation of the ammonium ion and the hydronium ion. — A coordinate (dative) bond forms when one atom supplies both electrons of the shared pair: the donor must own a lone pair, the acceptor an empty orbital. NH₄⁺: ammonia's nitrogen has a lone pair; it donates it to H⁺ (bare proton, empty 1s orbital) → N→H⁺; the ion carries four equivalent N–H bonds and one positive charge. H₃O⁺: water's oxygen donates a lone pair to H⁺ → O→H⁺; the hydronium ion carries two ordinary O–H bonds, one dative O→H bond, and one positive charge — it is the actual form of "H⁺" in aqueous acid.
  • NEB-style (3m): Explain three properties of covalent compounds, each with its cause. — (a) Low melting and boiling points: the forces between molecules are weak van der Waals/dipole attractions; melting separates molecules without touching the strong C–H or O–H bonds inside them. (b) Non-conduction: no free ions and no delocalised electrons — shared pairs are fixed between particular nuclei (graphite and ionising acids like HCl are the standard exceptions). (c) Solubility in organic, not aqueous, solvents: "like dissolves like" — non-polar molecular solutes interact well with non-polar solvents; water's hydrogen-bond network does not accommodate them (sugar and ethanol are the polar-covalent exceptions that do dissolve).

Key Points

  • Covalent bond = mutual sharing of electron pairs (single/double/triple; energy rises, length falls with multiplicity). Coordinate bond = shared pair from one atom (donor→acceptor): NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, NH₃·BF₃; identical to covalent once formed. Properties of covalent compounds: low mp/bp (weak intermolecular forces), non-conducting, organic-solvent soluble, molecular, isomerism shown, slow reactions.

Summary

Where transfer is impossible, sharing takes over. Two chlorine atoms each contribute one electron to a pair both nuclei pull on — the covalent bond, with its single (H₂), double (CO₂) and triple (N₂) grades trading length for strength. A variant where one atom donates the whole pair — the coordinate bond — builds NH₄⁺ from NH₃ + H⁺ and H₃O⁺ from H₂O + H⁺, yet becomes electronically identical to any covalent bond once formed. Covalent compounds are molecular: low melting points (weak intermolecular forces, not weak bonds — diamond proves the point at 3500 °C), non-conducting (no free carriers), organic-soluble ("like dissolves like"), capable of isomerism (directional bonds), and slow to react (bonds must be broken, not just recombined). Ionic vs covalent is the NEB 2024 distinction question; the coordinate-bond trio NH₄⁺/H₃O⁺/NH₃·BF₃ is the stock 3m answer.
