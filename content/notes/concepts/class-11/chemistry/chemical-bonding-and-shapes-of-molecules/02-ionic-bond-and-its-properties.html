Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Bond of Full Transfer

    When the electronegativity gap is a canyon — metal meets non-metal — electrons are not shared but surrendered. Sodium hands its 3s electron to chlorine; the resulting Na⁺ and Cl⁻ lock into a three-dimensional lattice by sheer electrostatic attraction. That attraction is the ionic (electrovalent) bond. Its consequences cascade through chemistry: high melting points, molten-state conductivity, water solubility — and, through Fajan's rules, the admission that no ionic bond is perfectly ionic.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Formation mechanism

    • Electron transfer: metal atoms (low IE) lose electrons → cations; non-metal atoms (high EA) gain them → anions.
    • Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻; Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8). Both reach noble-gas configurations.
    • Coulombic attraction between oppositely charged ions, in all directions, builds the crystal lattice — NaCl is a cubic array of alternating ions, not discrete NaCl molecules.

    2.2 Conditions favouring ionic bond formation

    • Low ionisation energy of the metal (Cs, K, Na — IE₁ 376, 419, 496 kJ mol⁻¹).
    • High negative electron affinity of the non-metal (Cl −349 kJ mol⁻¹).
    • High lattice energy — the larger the energy released packing ions into the lattice, the more stable the compound.
    • Consequence: ionic compounds are typical of Group 1/2 metals with Group 17/16 non-metals (NaCl, MgO, CaF₂).

    2.3 Properties rooted in the lattice

    • Hard, brittle crystalline solids: displacement of a layer brings like charges face-to-face → repulsion cracks the crystal.
    • High melting/boiling points: strong, non-directional electrostatic forces must be broken throughout the lattice (NaCl mp 801 °C; MgO mp 2852 °C).
    • Conduct in molten/aqueous state only: ions are fixed in the solid but mobile once freed; solid ionic crystals do not conduct.
    • Soluble in polar solvents (water): dipole–ion attractions prise ions from the lattice; insoluble in non-polar solvents (benzene).
    • No isomerism, fast ionic reactions: no directional bonds; reactions are simply ion encounters in solution.

    2.4 Fajan's rules — covalent character in ionic bonds

    • A cation's positive charge polarises the anion's electron cloud; the distorted, shared-looking cloud is covalent character.
    • Covalent character increases with: small cation, large anion, high charge on either ion, and cations with pseudo-noble-gas (18-electron) configurations (Cu⁺ vs Na⁺).
    • Classic ordering: LiCl more covalent than NaCl (Li⁺ smaller, polarises Cl⁻ more); LiI most covalent of the lithium halides (I⁻ largest).

    4.1 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): ionic vs covalent — the distinction

    BasisIonic bondCovalent bond
    MechanismComplete electron transferSharing of electron pairs
    Particles formedIons (Na⁺, Cl⁻)Molecules (H₂O, CH₄)
    DirectionalityNon-directional electrostatic pullDirectional, between specific atoms
    Melting pointHigh (NaCl 801 °C)Low to moderate (H₂O 0 °C solid, CH₄ −182 °C)
    ConductivityMolten/aqueous onlyNon-conducting (except graphite/electrovalent exceptions)
    SolubilityPolar solventsNon-polar solvents (generally)
    ExampleNaCl, MgOCl₂, H₂O, CO₂

    One example each: NaCl (transfer: Na loses 3s¹ to Cl) and H₂O (sharing: one pair per O–H bond).

    4.2 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Fajan's rules and LiCl vs NaCl

    • Rules: covalent character rises with (i) smaller cation, (ii) larger anion, (iii) higher charges, (iv) 18-electron cation configuration.
    • LiCl vs NaCl: Li⁺ (76 pm) is much smaller than Na⁺ (102 pm), so Li⁺ has greater polarising power — it distorts Cl⁻'s cloud more, giving LiCl higher covalent character (evidenced by LiCl's solubility in organic solvents such as ethanol and acetone, and its lower melting point than NaCl).

    4.3 Why brittleness and conductivity follow from geometry

    • The lattice is a 3D checkerboard; slide it one ion across and Cl⁻ meets Cl⁻ — repulsion shatters the crystal. Hardness (resists scratching) and brittleness (cleaves under shear) come from the same force.
    • In the solid, ions vibrate about fixed lattice points — no charge carrier movement, no conduction. Melt or dissolve: ions migrate to electrodes — conduction, and electrolysis becomes possible.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Ionic bond is not directional: an Na⁺ attracts every neighbouring Cl⁻ equally — the "bond" is the whole lattice's attraction, not a pair-specific link. That is why formula units (NaCl) are not molecules.

  • Covalent character in ionic bonds is normal, not anomalous: Fajan's rules mean every "ionic" compound has some electron distortion; 100% ionic exists only as an idealisation.
  • Solid ionic compounds do not conduct: conduction requires mobile charge carriers; in the solid the ions are locked in place — the common exam trap.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2024, 2m): Distinguish between ionic and covalent bonds with one example of each. — Ionic bond: complete transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal, giving oppositely charged ions held by electrostatic attraction (NaCl). Covalent bond: mutual sharing of one or more electron pairs between non-metal atoms (H₂O). Key consequences: ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct when molten; covalent compounds have low melting points and are non-conducting.

  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024, 3m): State Fajan's rules. How do they explain the covalent character of LiCl vs NaCl? — Covalent character increases with small cation size, large anion size, high ionic charges and cations of 18-electron configuration. Li⁺ (76 pm) is smaller than Na⁺ (102 pm) — greater charge density, stronger polarising power — so Li⁺ distorts the chloride cloud more than Na⁺ does, making LiCl more covalent: it dissolves in organic solvents and melts lower than NaCl.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is MgO's melting point (2852 °C) far higher than NaCl's (801 °C)? — Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ carry double the charge of Na⁺/Cl⁻ and are smaller, so the electrostatic attraction (and lattice energy, ≈ −3795 kJ mol⁻¹) is far greater — more energy is needed to disrupt the lattice.

Confusion

  • Formula unit ≠ molecule: NaCl is a formula unit of an infinite lattice, not a discrete molecule — "molecule of NaCl" is a mark-losing phrase.
  • Polarisation direction: the cation polarises (distorts) the anion — small/high-charge cation pulls, large anion is pulled.

Practice

Universal Facts

  • Magnesium oxide's melting point (2852 °C) is so high that MgO lines furnaces — doubly charged, small ions build the strongest common lattice.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (2m): Distinguish between ionic and covalent bonds with one example of each. — Ionic bond: formed by complete transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal; the resulting oppositely charged ions attract electrostatically in all directions, building a crystal lattice — example NaCl (Na gives its 3s¹ electron to Cl). Covalent bond: formed by mutual sharing of electron pairs between atoms — example H₂O (one shared pair per O–H bond). Ionic compounds: high mp, conduct when molten/aqueous, polar-soluble. Covalent compounds: low mp, non-conducting, often non-polar-soluble.
  • NEB 2024 (3m): State Fajan's rules and use them to compare the covalent character of LiCl and NaCl. — Rules: (i) smaller cation → more polarising; (ii) larger anion → more easily polarised; (iii) higher charges on either ion → more polarisation; (iv) cations with 18-electron (pseudo-noble-gas) configurations polarise more than 8-electron ones of equal size. Application: Li⁺ (76 pm) vs Na⁺ (102 pm) — the smaller Li⁺ has higher charge density and distorts the Cl⁻ electron cloud more strongly, so LiCl has greater covalent character than NaCl. Evidence: LiCl dissolves in organic solvents (ethanol, acetone) and melts about 200 °C below NaCl.
  • NEB-style (3m): Explain the following properties of NaCl on the basis of its bonding: (a) high melting point, (b) conduction in molten state only, (c) brittleness. — (a) Strong, non-directional electrostatic attraction runs throughout the 3D lattice; melting requires disrupting the entire array (801 °C). (b) In the solid, ions are fixed at lattice points — no mobile carriers. Molten NaCl has free Na⁺ and Cl⁻ that migrate under a field, carrying current. (c) Sliding one lattice layer displaces like charges into contact; the sudden repulsion cleaves the crystal along a plane.

Formulas

    • Polarising power ∝ charge² / radius (ionic potential φ = z/r)
    • Coulomb energy ∝ |q₁q₂| / r (lattice attraction basis)
    • Li⁺ 76 pm < Na⁺ 102 pm < K⁺ 138 pm (polarising ladder)

Key Points

  • Ionic bond = electrostatic attraction between ions formed by electron transfer (metal low IE + non-metal high EA + high lattice energy). Properties: hard brittle crystals, high mp/bp, conduct only when molten/aqueous, soluble in polar solvents, fast ionic reactions. Fajan's rules: covalent character ↑ with small cation, large anion, high charges, 18-e⁻ cation — LiCl more covalent than NaCl.

Summary

When a low-ionisation-energy metal meets a high-electron-affinity non-metal, electrons transfer completely: Na gives its 3s electron to Cl, and the Na⁺/Cl⁻ pair assembles into a three-dimensional lattice whose electrostatic attraction is the ionic bond. That geometry explains everything: hardness yet brittleness (layer slip brings like charges together), high melting points (NaCl 801 °C, MgO 2852 °C — charge and size set the lattice energy), conduction only when molten or dissolved (mobile ions needed), and solubility in polar solvents (ion–dipole prize ions out). Fajan's rules then admit reality: small, highly charged cations distort anion clouds — LiCl is measurably more covalent than NaCl (smaller Li⁺, organic-solvent solubility). No bond is perfectly ionic; the ionic–covalent contrast is a continuum, and the exam's favourite probes are the NEB 2024 pair — the distinction table and Fajan's LiCl comparison.
