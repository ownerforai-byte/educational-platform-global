  • 1. Topic Introduction — Geometry from Repulsion

    Electron pairs are all negatively charged — they repel. VSEPR (valence shell electron pair repulsion) turns that single fact into molecular architecture: the pairs around a central atom spread as far apart as possible, and the geometry they form is the molecule's shape. One counting rule predicts linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal and octahedral shapes — and the lone-pair corrections predict every bent and pyramidal exception NEB has ever asked (BeCl₂, H₂S, NH₃, CH₄, PCl₅, SF₆ — NEB 2022, 2020, 2024).

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Postulates

    1. The shape of a molecule is set by the total number of electron pairs in the valence shell of the central atom.
    2. The pairs repel one another and adopt the arrangement of minimum repulsion / maximum separation.
    3. Repulsion strength order: lone pair–lone pair (lp–lp) > lone pair–bond pair (lp–bp) > bond pair–bond pair (bp–bp) — lone pairs occupy more space (held by one nucleus only, spread wider).
    4. Multiple bonds count as one domain but repel more strongly than single bonds.
    5. The molecular shape is named by atom positions only; lone pairs distort angles but stay invisible in the shape name.

    2.2 The five parent geometries

    DomainsElectron geometryAngleExample (0 LP)
    2Linear180°BeCl₂, CO₂
    3Trigonal planar120°BF₃, SO₂*
    4Tetrahedral109.5°CH₄
    5Trigonal bipyramidal120°/90°PCl₅
    6Octahedral90°SF₆

    *SO₂ has one lone pair — bent, ≈119°.

    2.3 Lone-pair corrections (the exam's favourite)

    • CH₄ (4 BP): tetrahedral, 109.5°. NH₃ (3 BP + 1 LP): pyramidal, 107°. H₂O (2 BP + 2 LP): bent, 104.5°. Each lone pair squeezes the angle down.
    • BeCl₂ (2 BP): linear 180°. H₂S (2 BP + 2 LP): bent, but only ≈92° — sulphur's large, diffuse lone pairs and long S–H bonds compress far below water's angle.
    • PCl₅ (5 BP): trigonal bipyramidal — two axial (90° to three equatorial) and three equatorial (120° apart); axial bonds are longer (219 pm) than equatorial (204 pm).
    • SF₆ (6 BP): octahedral, all F–S–F 90°.
    • CO₂ (2 domains, both double bonds): linear 180° — multiple bonds still count once.

    2.4 Why NH₃ is pyramidal while CH₄ is tetrahedral (verified PYQ)

    • Both central atoms carry four electron domains — the electron geometry is tetrahedral for both.
    • CH₄'s four domains are all bond pairs: atoms occupy all four corners → tetrahedral, 109.5°.
    • NH₃'s fourth domain is a lone pair: it repels the three N–H pairs more strongly (lp–bp > bp–bp), pushing them together → pyramidal, 107°. The lone pair is real but nameless in the shape.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/04): postulates of VSEPR + shape of CH₄

    • Quote the five postulates (section 2.1), then: CH₄'s carbon has four bond pairs, no lone pairs; minimum repulsion places them at the corners of a tetrahedron — H–C–H = 109.5°, tetrahedral shape.

    4.2 Verified PYQ (NEB 2022/02): shapes of PCl₅, SF₆, BeCl₂, H₂S

    • PCl₅: five bond pairs → trigonal bipyramidal; three equatorial P–Cl at 120°, two axial at 90° to them; axial bonds longer (more repulsion from three equatorial neighbours).
    • SF₆: six bond pairs → octahedral; all angles 90°.
    • BeCl₂: two bond pairs → linear, 180° (gas phase; Be's octet is incomplete).
    • H₂S: two bond pairs + two lone pairs → bent, ≈92° — same domain count as H₂O but sulphur's bigger lone pairs and longer bonds give a much smaller angle.

    4.3 Verified PYQ (NEB 2020): ammonia — shape, hybridisation, angle

    • NH₃: three N–H bonds + one lone pair → pyramidal; sp³ hybridised; H–N–H ≈ 107°.
    • Why less than methane's 109.5°: the single lone pair repels the three bond pairs more strongly than they repel each other, squeezing the H–N–H angle down by 2.5°.

    4.4 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/04): H₂O bent vs CO₂ linear

    • H₂O: O carries two bond pairs + two lone pairs (four domains) → tetrahedral electron geometry, atoms in a bent (V) shape, 104.5°.
    • CO₂: C carries two double-bond domains, no lone pairs → linear, 180°.
    • The contrast is not about oxygen "in the middle" — it is the lone-pair count on the central atom that bends one and straightens the other.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Electron geometry ≠ molecular shape: NH₃'s electron geometry is tetrahedral but its shape is pyramidal — the shape name counts atoms only.

  • Multiple bonds count once: CO₂ has two domains (not four) — each double bond is one region of electron density.
  • H₂S is not "mini-water": same domain count, but 92° vs 104.5° — larger, more diffuse lone pairs on sulphur and longer S–H bonds change the balance; never copy water's angle.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2022): Predict the shape, hybridisation and bond angle of (a) PCl₅ (b) SF₆ (c) BeCl₂ (d) H₂S. — (a) PCl₅: 5 BP → trigonal bipyramidal, sp³d, 120°/90°. (b) SF₆: 6 BP → octahedral, sp³d², 90°. (c) BeCl₂: 2 BP → linear, sp, 180°. (d) H₂S: 2 BP + 2 LP → bent, ≈92°, with s-p overlap (little hybridisation on S).

  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/04): Predict the shapes of BF₃ and PCl₅ using VSEPR theory. Give their hybridisation. — BF₃: three bond pairs, no lone pair → trigonal planar, 120°, sp². PCl₅: five bond pairs → trigonal bipyramidal (3 equatorial at 120°, 2 axial at 90°), sp³d.
  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024/02): Why is NH₃ pyramidal while CH₄ is tetrahedral, though both have four electron pairs? — Both have tetrahedral electron geometry. In CH₄ all four pairs are bond pairs and all four corners carry H atoms → tetrahedral, 109.5°. In NH₃ one corner is a lone pair that repels the three bond pairs more strongly (lp–bp > bp–bp), compressing H–N–H to 107° and leaving a pyramidal shape — the lone pair occupies space but is not an atom, so it never appears in the shape name.

  • Domains, not bonds: double bonds are one domain each — counting CO₂ as four domains gives a false tetrahedral answer.
  • Lone pairs shape the molecule but never name it: "pyramidal" and "bent" describe atom positions; the lone pair is the invisible puppeteer.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2022 (3m): Predict the shape, hybridisation and bond angle of (a) PCl₅ (b) SF₆ (c) BeCl₂ (d) H₂S using VSEPR theory. — (a) PCl₅: five bond pairs, no lone pair → trigonal bipyramidal (sp³d); three equatorial P–Cl at 120°, two axial at 90°; axial bonds longer (219 pm) than equatorial (204 pm). (b) SF₆: six bond pairs → octahedral (sp³d²), all F–S–F = 90°. (c) BeCl₂: two bond pairs → linear (sp), 180°. (d) H₂S: two bond pairs + two lone pairs → bent (V-shaped), ≈92° — sulphur's large lone pairs and long S–H bonds compress the angle far below water's 104.5°.
  • NEB 2020 (3m): Using VSEPR theory, explain the shape of ammonia, its hybridisation and bond angle. Why is its bond angle less than that of methane? — Nitrogen in NH₃ carries three bond pairs and one lone pair — four electron domains with tetrahedral electron geometry (sp³ hybridisation). The three H atoms occupy three tetrahedral corners, giving a pyramidal shape with H–N–H ≈ 107°. The angle is less than methane's 109.5° because the lone pair, held by one nucleus only, spreads wider and repels each N–H bond pair more strongly (lp–bp > bp–bp), squeezing the three bonds together by about 2.5°.
  • NEB 2024 (3m): Explain why H₂O is bent while CO₂ is linear. — CO₂: carbon has two electron domains (two C=O double bonds — each counts once) and no lone pairs; minimum repulsion puts the domains 180° apart → linear. H₂O: oxygen has two O–H bond pairs plus two lone pairs — four domains with tetrahedral electron geometry; the two hydrogen atoms occupy only two corners, so the molecular shape is bent (V-shaped) with H–O–H ≈ 104.5°. The deciding variable is the lone-pair count on the central atom: two lone pairs bend water, none leave carbon dioxide straight — and the two lone pairs also make H₂O strongly polar while CO₂'s equal dipoles cancel.

Summary

Electron pairs repel; geometry is the truce. VSEPR counts the domains around the central atom — bonding pairs plus lone pairs, multiple bonds counting once — and spreads them to minimum repulsion: two domains linear (BeCl₂, CO₂), three trigonal planar (BF₃), four tetrahedral (CH₄), five trigonal bipyramidal (PCl₅, axial 219 pm vs equatorial 204 pm), six octahedral (SF₆). Lone pairs repel hardest — lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp — so they compress angles and rename shapes: NH₃ pyramidal at 107° (NEB 2020, 2024), H₂O bent at 104.5°, H₂S bent at ≈92°, PH₃ at ≈93°. The two NEB 2024 contrasts carry the logic: NH₃ is pyramidal while CH₄ is tetrahedral because one of four domains is a lone pair; H₂O is bent while CO₂ is linear because oxygen's lone pairs bend what carbon's absent lone pairs leave straight. Domain count → geometry → lone-pair correction → shape and angle: four lines that answer every shape question NEB has asked.

Practice Questions
  • NEB 2022 (3m): Predict the shape, hybridisation and bond angle of (a) PCl₅ (b) SF₆ (c) BeCl₂ (d) H₂S using VSEPR theory. — (a) PCl₅: five bond pairs, no lone pair → trigonal bipyramidal (sp³d); three equatorial P–Cl at 120°, two axial at 90°; axial bonds longer (219 pm) than equatorial (204 pm). (b) SF₆: six bond pairs → octahedral (sp³d²), all F–S–F = 90°. (c) BeCl₂: two bond pairs → linear (sp), 180°. (d) H₂S: two bond pairs + two lone pairs → bent (V-shaped), ≈92° — sulphur's large lone pairs and long S–H bonds compress the angle far below water's 104.5°.
  • NEB 2020 (3m): Using VSEPR theory, explain the shape of ammonia, its hybridisation and bond angle. Why is its bond angle less than that of methane? — Nitrogen in NH₃ carries three bond pairs and one lone pair — four electron domains with tetrahedral electron geometry (sp³ hybridisation). The three H atoms occupy three tetrahedral corners, giving a pyramidal shape with H–N–H ≈ 107°. The angle is less than methane's 109.5° because the lone pair, held by one nucleus only, spreads wider and repels each N–H bond pair more strongly (lp–bp > bp–bp), squeezing the three bonds together by about 2.5°.
  • NEB 2024 (3m): Explain why H₂O is bent while CO₂ is linear. — CO₂: carbon has two electron domains (two C=O double bonds — each counts once) and no lone pairs; minimum repulsion puts the domains 180° apart → linear. H₂O: oxygen has two O–H bond pairs plus two lone pairs — four domains with tetrahedral electron geometry; the two hydrogen atoms occupy only two corners, so the molecular shape is bent (V-shaped) with H–O–H ≈ 104.5°. The deciding variable is the lone-pair count on the central atom: two lone pairs bend water, none leave carbon dioxide straight — and the two lone pairs also make H₂O strongly polar while CO₂'s equal dipoles cancel.
