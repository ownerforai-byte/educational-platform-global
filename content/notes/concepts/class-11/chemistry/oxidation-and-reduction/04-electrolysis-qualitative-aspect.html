Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Forcing Reactions Backwards with Electricity

    Spontaneous redox runs a galvanic cell; electricity can also drive non-spontaneous redox — that is electrolysis. Pass a current through molten NaCl and sodium metal appears at one electrode, chlorine gas at the other; pass it through CuSO₄ solution and copper plates out. Which ion discharges first is not chance: the preferential discharge rule and the electrochemical series decide, and the electrode products of the classic electrolyses are stock exam answers.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The electrolytic cell

    • Electrolysis: chemical decomposition by passage of direct current through an electrolyte (molten or aqueous).
    • Cathode: the negative electrode in electrolysis — reduction happens here (cations migrate to it, gain electrons).
    • Anode: the positive electrode — oxidation happens here (anions migrate, lose electrons).
    • Memory hook: AN OX / RED CAT, with polarity inverted from a galvanic cell (electrolysis cathode is −, anode +).
    • Energy conversion: electrical → chemical (the reverse of a galvanic cell).

    2.2 Preferential discharge theory

    • When several ions crowd the electrodes, the one discharged first is the one requiring less energy — related to position in the electrochemical series and concentration.
    • At the cathode (cations): discharge ease follows the series upward from the bottom: Cu²⁺ > H⁺ > Pb²⁺ > Fe²⁺ > Zn²⁺ > Na⁺ > Ca²⁺ > K⁺ (low-series cations like Cu²⁺ grab electrons most readily; alkali metals resist).
    • At the anode (anions): discharge ease: OH⁻ (gives O₂) > I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ and SO₄²⁻/NO₃⁻ (rarely discharged — water oxidises instead).
    • Electrode material matters: an inert anode (Pt, graphite) is unchanged; a reactive anode (Cu, Ag) dissolves itself instead of discharging anions.

    2.3 The classic electrolyses

    • Molten NaCl (inert electrodes): cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (silvery metal beads); anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (green-yellow gas). Overall: 2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂.
    • Aqueous CuSO₄ with Pt electrodes: cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (pink-brown deposit); anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (OH⁻ beats SO₄²⁻). Solution turns acidic as Cu²⁺ depletes.
    • Aqueous CuSO₄ with copper electrodes: the anode dissolves: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻, replenishing exactly what the cathode plates out — solution concentration constant. This is the basis of copper refining and electroplating.
    • Acidulated water (dilute H₂SO₄, Pt): cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂; anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻; overall 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (volume ratio 2 : 1 — Hoffmann voltameter).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why polarity flips between cell types

    • In a galvanic cell the anode (oxidation) is negative because the spontaneous chemistry pushes electrons out; in electrolysis the external battery imposes the flow — the electrode connected to the battery's negative terminal pulls cations and forces reduction, so it is the cathode. Same chemistry (AN OX / RED CAT), opposite polarity.

    4.2 The discharge order in action

    • Brine (aqueous NaCl): cathode — H⁺ beats Na⁺ (hydrogen evolves, not sodium); anode — Cl⁻ beats OH⁻ at high Cl⁻ concentration (chlorine evolves). Products: H₂, Cl₂, and NaOH left in solution — the chlor-alkali industry.
    • Dilute Na₂SO₄ with Pt: only water electrolyses — H₂ and O₂ in 2:1; the salt is a spectator carrying current.

    4.3 Applications to quote

    • Electrorefining of copper: impure anode dissolves; pure Cu plates the cathode; Ag/Au fall as anode mud.
    • Electroplating: object made cathode in a salt bath of the plating metal (Ag plating from AgCN bath).
    • Extraction: reactive metals (Na, Mg, Al) are won by electrolysis of their molten compounds precisely because chemical reductants cannot beat them — series logic again.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Cathode is negative — but only in electrolysis: in a galvanic cell the cathode is positive. The process (reduction at cathode) never changes; the sign does. Always state the cell type.

  • Na⁺ rarely discharges from water: in aqueous NaCl the hydrogen comes from water/H⁺, not sodium — molten salt is required to win sodium metal.
  • Anode is where oxidation happens even if the anode itself is the victim: a copper anode oxidises into solution — still oxidation, still the anode.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe the electrolysis of molten NaCl with inert electrodes, giving the electrode reactions. — Molten NaCl contains free Na⁺ and Cl⁻. On passing current, Na⁺ migrates to the cathode (negative) and is reduced: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (molten silvery sodium collects). Cl⁻ migrates to the anode (positive) and is oxidised: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (greenish-yellow chlorine gas bubbles off). Overall: 2NaCl(l) → 2Na(l) + Cl₂(g) — electrical energy drives a non-spontaneous decomposition, the industrial route to sodium metal.

  • NEB-style (3m): Compare the electrolysis of aqueous CuSO₄ using platinum electrodes versus copper electrodes. — With Pt: cathode reduces Cu²⁺ → Cu (deposit); anode oxidises OH⁻ (from water) rather than SO₄²⁻: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻. Copper leaves the solution and oxygen escapes, so blue colour fades and acidity rises. With Cu: the anode itself oxidises: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻, dissolving copper at exactly the rate the cathode deposits it — Cu²⁺ concentration stays constant, no gas evolves. The Cu-anode version is the working principle of copper refining and electroplating.
  • NEB-style (2m): State the preferential discharge order at the cathode and give one consequence. — Discharge ease at the cathode rises down the electrochemical series: Cu²⁺ > H⁺ > Pb²⁺ > Fe²⁺ > Zn²⁺ > Na⁺ > Ca²⁺ > K⁺. Consequence: in aqueous NaCl, H⁺ (from water) discharges instead of Na⁺, so hydrogen — not sodium — evolves at the cathode; sodium metal can only be won from molten NaCl.

Confusion

  • Anions do not always reach the anode's oxidation: SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻ and F⁻ resist discharge — water's OH⁻ is oxidised instead, so oxygen, not the anion's element, appears.
  • Concentration bends the rules: in concentrated brine Cl⁻ out-competes OH⁻ at the anode even though OH⁻ is nominally easier to discharge — the textbook order assumes comparable concentrations.

Universal Facts

  • Aluminium exists in aircraft only because electrolysis (Hall–Héroult, molten Al₂O₃ in cryolite) can force the reverse of thermite's favourite reaction — electricity beating the series.

Examples

  • Electrorefining copper — an impure anode dissolves, 99.99% copper plates the cathode, silver and gold collect as "anode mud" worth more than the copper itself.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Describe the electrolysis of molten sodium chloride using inert electrodes, with electrode reactions. — Molten NaCl is an ionic liquid containing mobile Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. When a direct current passes, Na⁺ migrates to the cathode (negative electrode) and is reduced: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na — molten sodium metal collects beneath the surface. Cl⁻ migrates to the anode (positive electrode) and is oxidised: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ — greenish-yellow chlorine gas evolves. Adding the halves (electrons cancel) gives the overall decomposition 2NaCl(l) → 2Na(l) + Cl₂(g). Electrical energy drives this non-spontaneous redox — the electrolytic route is the only practical source of sodium metal, since no chemical reductant can do the job.
  • NEB-style (3m): Compare the electrolysis of aqueous copper sulphate using platinum electrodes with that using copper electrodes. — Platinum (inert) electrodes: at the cathode Cu²⁺ is preferentially discharged — Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (pink-brown deposit). At the anode, sulphate resists discharge, so hydroxide ions from water are oxidised: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ — oxygen gas evolves. Copper leaves the solution while no Cu²⁺ returns, so the blue colour fades and the solution becomes acidic (excess H⁺). Copper electrodes: the anode is no longer inert — copper itself oxidises: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻, dissolving at exactly the rate the cathode plates copper out. Cu²⁺ concentration stays constant, no gas evolves, and the cell simply transports copper from anode to cathode — the principle of copper refining (impure anode, pure cathode, Ag/Au anode mud) and of electroplating.
  • NEB-style (2m): State the preferential discharge order of cations at the cathode and explain one practical consequence. — The ease of discharge at the cathode follows the electrochemical series from bottom (most easily reduced) upward: Cu²⁺ > H⁺ > Pb²⁺ > Fe²⁺ > Zn²⁺ > Na⁺ > Ca²⁺ > K⁺ — ions of low-series metals accept electrons most readily; alkali-metal ions resist hardest. Consequence: in the electrolysis of aqueous NaCl (brine), H⁺ from water discharges in preference to Na⁺, so hydrogen gas — not sodium — evolves at the cathode, while chlorine forms at the anode and NaOH accumulates in solution (the chlor-alkali process). Sodium metal can only be obtained by electrolysing molten NaCl, where no competing H⁺ exists.

    • Water electrolysis: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (2 : 1 by volume)

Key Points

  • Electrolysis: current decomposes molten/aqueous electrolyte; electrical → chemical. Cathode − (reduction, cations); anode + (oxidation, anions); AN OX/RED CAT with polarity opposite to galvanic. Summary

Electrolysis is redox driven backwards: a battery forces electrons into the cathode (negative), where cations are reduced, and pulls them from the anode (positive), where anions — or the anode itself — are oxidised; same AN OX/RED CAT chemistry as a galvanic cell, opposite polarity. Which ion discharges first obeys the preferential discharge rule: at the cathode the order runs Cu²⁺ > H⁺ > Pb²⁺ > Fe²⁺ > Zn²⁺ > Na⁺ > K⁺, at the anode OH⁻ > I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > SO₄²⁻ — which is why brine gives hydrogen and chlorine (the chlor-alkali process), why CuSO₄ with platinum plates copper and bubbles oxygen while its blue fades, and why swapping in a copper anode dissolves metal at exactly the rate the cathode plates it — the engine of copper refining and electroplating. Molten NaCl, with no water to compete, finally surrenders sodium metal; dilute H₂SO₄ simply splits water 2 : 1. The discharge ladder is the electrochemical series wearing work clothes — the third job of one ranking.

  • "Electrolysis is the process of chemical decomposition of an electrolyte by the passage of electricity: reduction occurs at the cathode and oxidation at the anode."

Important Notes

  • No verified PYQ on electrolysis exists in the current bank (the 2024 set covers redox concepts, ON rules, balancing, the series, H₂O₂ and fluorine); all items above are NEB-style practice.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Polarity: "electrolysis cathode = minus". Discharge ladder: "Cu H Pb Fe Zn Na K" at the cathode; "OH I Br Cl SO4 F" at the anode. Classic products table: molten NaCl → Na/Cl₂; CuSO₄/Pt → Cu/O₂; CuSO₄/Cu → Cu/Cu²⁺; H₂SO₄ → 2:1.

Exam Notes

  • For each electrolysis answer, give three things: migrating ions, both half-equations, and the observed product at each electrode — one missing half-equation costs the bulk of the marks.
