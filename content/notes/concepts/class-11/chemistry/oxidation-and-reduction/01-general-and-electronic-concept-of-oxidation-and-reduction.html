Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Electron Accounting for Every Reaction

    Burning, rusting, respiration, plating and batteries all share one bookkeeping system: oxidation–reduction (redox). The modern definition is electronic — oxidation is electron loss, reduction is electron gain — and every redox reaction is a transfer: one species loses what another gains, simultaneously. Master the two roles (oxidising agent, reducing agent) and the electrochemical series that ranks them, and half of applied chemistry becomes predictable.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The electronic concept

    • Oxidation: loss of electrons — Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻; Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
    • Reduction: gain of electrons — Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu; Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻.
    • The two always occur together — electrons lost must be gained somewhere; a half-reaction alone cannot happen.
    • Memory aid: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

    2.2 The three generations of definitions

    GenerationOxidationReduction
    Classical (oxygen/hydrogen)Addition of O; removal of HAddition of H; removal of O
    ElectronicLoss of electronsGain of electrons
    Modern (oxidation number)Increase in oxidation numberDecrease in oxidation number
    • The classical view fails on reactions with no oxygen or hydrogen (Zn + CuSO₄); the oxidation-number view covers everything.

    2.3 Agents and couples

    • Oxidising agent (oxidant): the species that accepts electrons — it oxidises the other reactant and is itself reduced (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, O₂, F₂, HNO₃).
    • Reducing agent (reductant): the species that donates electrons — it reduces the other reactant and is itself oxidised (H₂, C, CO, SO₂, metals, H₂S).
    • Redox couple: the oxidised/reduced pair of one element, e.g. Zn²⁺/Zn, MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺.

    2.4 The electrochemical series

    • Elements ranked by their tendency to lose electrons (standard electrode potentials): Li, K, Ba, Ca, Na … Zn … Fe … H … Cu … Ag … F₂ at the top of oxidising power.
    • A higher metal displaces a lower metal's ion from solution: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu (NEB 2024) — zinc is the stronger reductant, copper ion the stronger oxidant.
    • Fluorine is the strongest oxidising agent among the halogens (NEB 2024): smallest size, highest electronegativity (4.0), most positive reduction potential (F₂ + 2e⁻ → 2F⁻, +2.87 V), so it accepts electrons most eagerly; oxidising power falls F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.

    2.5 Special redox behaviours

    • Auto-oxidation–reduction (disproportionation): one species is simultaneously oxidised and reduced — Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O (chlorine to −1 and +1); 2Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Cu.
    • Dual behaviour — hydrogen peroxide (NEB 2022 and 2024): H₂O₂'s oxygen sits at −1, between O₂ (0) and H₂O (−2), so it can go either way:
    • As oxidising agent: H₂O₂ + 2Fe²⁺ + 2H⁺ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O (O: −1 → −2).
    • As reducing agent: H₂O₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl + O₂ (O: −1 → 0).
    • In itself: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ is disproportionation.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): define the agents; identify each in Zn + CuSO₄

    • Oxidising agent: accepts electrons and is reduced. Reducing agent: donates electrons and is oxidised.
    • Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (loss — zinc is oxidised, so zinc is the reducing agent); Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (gain — copper ion is reduced, so CuSO₄/Cu²⁺ is the oxidising agent).

    4.2 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): why zinc displaces copper

    • In the electrochemical series Zn sits above Cu — zinc has a greater tendency to lose electrons ( stronger reductant, E° Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V vs Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V).
    • The electron transfer Zn → Cu²⁺ is therefore spontaneous: zinc dissolves as Zn²⁺ while copper deposits as the familiar red-brown coat. Any metal above another in the series displaces the lower one from its salt solution.

    4.3 Verified PYQ (NEB 2022 + 2024): hydrogen peroxide's double life

    • The −1 oxidation state of oxygen in H₂O₂ is the pivot: it can climb to 0 (oxidised — H₂O₂ acts as reducing agent) or sink to −2 (reduced — H₂O₂ acts as oxidising agent).
    • Balanced equations (section 2.5) show both roles; in acid, permanganate even uses H₂O₂ as a reductant: 2MnO₄⁻ + 5H₂O₂ + 6H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5O₂ + 8H₂O.

    4.4 Spotting redox without electrons — the oxidation-number audit

    • Assign oxidation numbers to every atom on both sides; any element whose number changes marks the redox pair: increase = oxidation, decrease = reduction, no change = spectator.
    • This audit is the gateway to topic 40's balancing methods.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    The agent does the opposite of its name: an oxidising agent does not get oxidised — it causes oxidation and is itself reduced. Role and fate are opposite; exams punish swapping them.

  • Oxidation number is not charge: Mn is +7 in KMnO₄ but no Mn⁷⁺ ion floats in the solution — oxidation numbers are bookkeeping, not ionic charges.
  • Disproportionation needs an intermediate state: the element must sit between two accessible states (Cl at 0 between −1 and +1; O in H₂O₂ at −1 between −2 and 0) — F₂ cannot disproportionate because −1 is its floor.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Define oxidising and reducing agents. Identify each in Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. — An oxidising agent accepts electrons and is itself reduced; a reducing agent donates electrons and is itself oxidised. Zinc loses two electrons (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻): it is oxidised and is the reducing agent. Copper(II) ions gain those electrons (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu): they are reduced, so CuSO₄ (Cu²⁺) is the oxidising agent.

  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): What is the electrochemical series? Why does zinc displace copper from CuSO₄? — The electrochemical series arranges elements in order of their standard electrode potentials — tendency to be reduced (equivalently, inverse tendency to be oxidised). Zinc lies well above copper (E° Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V vs Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V), so zinc is the stronger electron donor. When zinc metal meets Cu²⁺ ions, electrons flow spontaneously from Zn to Cu²⁺: zinc dissolves as Zn²⁺ and copper deposits — Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. Any metal above another displaces the lower from its salt solution.
  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2022 + 2024): Hydrogen peroxide acts as both oxidising and reducing agent. Explain with one reaction of each. — Oxygen in H₂O₂ is at −1, intermediate between 0 (O₂) and −2 (H₂O), so H₂O₂ can move either way. As oxidising agent (O reduced −1 → −2): H₂O₂ + 2Fe²⁺ + 2H⁺ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O — iron(II) is oxidised to iron(III). As reducing agent (O oxidised −1 → 0): H₂O₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl + O₂ — chlorine is reduced to chloride while H₂O₂ liberates oxygen. Its own decomposition, 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂, is disproportionation.
  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Why is fluorine the strongest oxidising agent among the halogens? — Fluorine is the smallest and most electronegative halogen (4.0) with the most positive reduction potential (F₂ + 2e⁻ → 2F⁻, E° = +2.87 V): it accepts electrons most readily and its small F⁻ ions are strongly hydrated, adding thermodynamic drive. Oxidising power therefore falls down the group — F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ — making fluorine unbeatable as an oxidant.

Confusion

  • Agent vs fate: the oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised — name the role from what happens to the other species.
  • Displacement direction: the metal higher in the series displaces the lower metal's ion — students often reverse it; Zn (higher) displaces Cu, never the reverse.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (2m): Define oxidising and reducing agents. Identify each in Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. — An oxidising agent is a species that accepts electrons and is itself reduced in the process; a reducing agent is a species that donates electrons and is itself oxidised. In the reaction, zinc metal loses two electrons (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻), so zinc is oxidised and acts as the reducing agent; the copper(II) ions from CuSO₄ gain those electrons (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu), so they are reduced and CuSO₄ (Cu²⁺) acts as the oxidising agent. Sulphate is a spectator.
  • NEB 2024 (3m): What is the electrochemical series? Explain why zinc displaces copper from copper sulphate solution. — The electrochemical series is the arrangement of elements in order of their standard electrode potentials, i.e. their tendency to be reduced (equivalently, the inverse of their tendency to be oxidised). Metals high in the series (Li, K, Na, Zn …) are strong reducing agents; metals low (Cu, Ag, Au) are weak. Zinc displaces copper because Zn stands above Cu (E° Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V against Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V): zinc has the greater tendency to lose electrons. Electrons therefore flow spontaneously from zinc to Cu²⁺ ions — the zinc dissolves as Zn²⁺ while copper deposits on it: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. A general rule follows: any metal higher in the series displaces a lower metal from its salt solution.
  • NEB 2022/2024 (3m): Hydrogen peroxide can act both as an oxidising and a reducing agent. Explain with one reaction of each type. — In H₂O₂ oxygen is at −1, an intermediate state between 0 (in O₂) and −2 (in H₂O), so H₂O₂ can be reduced or oxidised. As oxidising agent — its oxygen is reduced from −1 to −2 while it oxidises the other reactant: H₂O₂ + 2Fe²⁺ + 2H⁺ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O (iron(II) to iron(III)). As reducing agent — its oxygen is oxidised from −1 to 0 while it reduces the other reactant: H₂O₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl + O₂ (chlorine to chloride, oxygen gas liberated). Its spontaneous decomposition 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ is disproportionation, showing both behaviours at once.
  • NEB 2024 (2m): Why is fluorine the strongest oxidising agent among the halogens? — Fluorine combines the smallest atomic size, the highest electronegativity (4.0) and the most positive standard reduction potential (F₂ + 2e⁻ → 2F⁻, E° = +2.87 V) of all the halogens, so it accepts electrons more eagerly than Cl₂, Br₂ or I₂. Its small, strongly hydrated fluoride ions add extra thermodynamic driving force. Oxidising power therefore decreases down the group — F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ — leaving fluorine as the strongest oxidising agent, able even to oxidise water.

Formulas

    • Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu (E°: −0.76 vs +0.34 V)
    • H₂O₂ as oxidant: H₂O₂ + 2Fe²⁺ + 2H⁺ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O
    • H₂O₂ as reductant: H₂O₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl + O₂
    • 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ (disproportionation)
    • Halogen oxidising power: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂

Key Points

  • OIL RIG: oxidation = loss of electrons/ON increase; reduction = gain/ON decrease; both simultaneous. Oxidising agent accepts e⁻ (is reduced): KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, F₂, O₂. Reducing agent donates e⁻ (is oxidised): Zn, H₂, CO, C. Electrochemical series ranks reduction tendency; higher metal displaces lower's ion (Zn + CuSO₄; E° −0.76 vs +0.34 V). F₂ strongest oxidising halogen (EN 4.0, E° +2.87 V); order F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Disproportionation: one element both oxidised and reduced (Cl₂ + 2OH⁻; 2H₂O₂). H₂O₂ dual: oxidant (Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺) and reductant (Cl₂ → Cl⁻ + O₂).

Summary

Redox is electron bookkeeping: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain (OIL RIG), and because electrons are conserved the two always run together — one species climbs in oxidation number while another falls. The oxidising agent accepts electrons and is itself reduced (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, O₂, F₂); the reducing agent donates them and is itself oxidised (Zn, H₂, CO, C) — role and fate are always opposite. The electrochemical series ranks these tendencies by standard electrode potential, so a higher metal displaces a lower one from its salt: zinc (−0.76 V) throws copper (+0.34 V) out of CuSO₄, and fluorine — smallest, most electronegative, +2.87 V — stands unbeaten as the strongest oxidising halogen. Special cases complete the picture: disproportionation splits one element into two states (Cl₂ in alkali; 2H₂O₂), and hydrogen peroxide lives at the −1 pivot, oxidising Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ with one hand and reducing Cl₂ to chloride with the other. Three generations of definitions — oxygen/hydrogen, electrons, oxidation numbers — nest inside each other, and the third one governs the balancing methods of the next topic.

As reducing agent (O oxidised −1 → 0): H₂O₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl + O₂ — chlorine is reduced to chloride while H₂O₂ liberates oxygen. Its own decomposition, 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂, is disproportionation.
  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Why is fluorine the strongest oxidising agent among the halogens? — Fluorine is the smallest and most electronegative halogen (4.0) with the most positive reduction potential (F₂ + 2e⁻ → 2F⁻, E° = +2.87 V): it accepts electrons most readily and its small F⁻ ions are strongly hydrated, adding thermodynamic drive. Oxidising power therefore falls down the group — F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ — making fluorine unbeatable as an oxidant.
Confusion
  • Agent vs fate: the oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised — name the role from what happens to the other species.
  • Displacement direction: the metal higher in the series displaces the lower metal's ion — students often reverse it; Zn (higher) displaces Cu, never the reverse.
Key Points
  • OIL RIG: oxidation = loss of electrons/ON increase; reduction = gain/ON decrease; both simultaneous. Oxidising agent accepts e⁻ (is reduced): KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, F₂, O₂. Reducing agent donates e⁻ (is oxidised): Zn, H₂, CO, C. Electrochemical series ranks reduction tendency; higher metal displaces lower's ion (Zn + CuSO₄; E° −0.76 vs +0.34 V). F₂ strongest oxidising halogen (EN 4.0, E° +2.87 V); order F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Disproportionation: one element both oxidised and reduced (Cl₂ + 2OH⁻; 2H₂O₂). H₂O₂ dual: oxidant (Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺) and reductant (Cl₂ → Cl⁻ + O₂).
Important Concepts
  • The intermediate-oxidation-state rule powers H₂O₂'s dual behaviour and all disproportionation: an element between two accessible states can move both ways; an element at an extreme (F at −1 floor) cannot.
Examples
  • The copper coin trick — an iron nail dropped in CuSO₄ comes out copper-plated: Fe (higher in the series) displaces Cu²⁺; the same logic that corrodes pipes and runs the classic displacement practical.
Universal Facts
  • Respiration is a redox relay: glucose is oxidised (C from 0 toward +4) while O₂ is reduced (0 → −2 in water) — the same electron accounting as a zinc-copper cell, at 37 °C.
Important Statements
  • "Oxidation is the loss of electrons and reduction is the gain of electrons; the two processes occur simultaneously in a redox reaction."

Practice Questions
  • NEB 2024 (2m): Define oxidising and reducing agents. Identify each in Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. — An oxidising agent is a species that accepts electrons and is itself reduced in the process; a reducing agent is a species that donates electrons and is itself oxidised. In the reaction, zinc metal loses two electrons (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻), so zinc is oxidised and acts as the reducing agent; the copper(II) ions from CuSO₄ gain those electrons (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu), so they are reduced and CuSO₄ (Cu²⁺) acts as the oxidising agent. Sulphate is a spectator.
  • NEB 2024 (3m): What is the electrochemical series? Explain why zinc displaces copper from copper sulphate solution. — The electrochemical series is the arrangement of elements in order of their standard electrode potentials, i.e. their tendency to be reduced (equivalently, the inverse of their tendency to be oxidised). Metals high in the series (Li, K, Na, Zn …) are strong reducing agents; metals low (Cu, Ag, Au) are weak. Zinc displaces copper because Zn stands above Cu (E° Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V against Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V): zinc has the greater tendency to lose electrons. Electrons therefore flow spontaneously from zinc to Cu²⁺ ions — the zinc dissolves as Zn²⁺ while copper deposits on it: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. A general rule follows: any metal higher in the series displaces a lower metal from its salt solution.
  • NEB 2022/2024 (3m): Hydrogen peroxide can act both as an oxidising and a reducing agent. Explain with one reaction of each type. — In H₂O₂ oxygen is at −1, an intermediate state between 0 (in O₂) and −2 (in H₂O), so H₂O₂ can be reduced or oxidised. As oxidising agent — its oxygen is reduced from −1 to −2 while it oxidises the other reactant: H₂O₂ + 2Fe²⁺ + 2H⁺ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O (iron(II) to iron(III)). As reducing agent — its oxygen is oxidised from −1 to 0 while it reduces the other reactant: H₂O₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl + O₂ (chlorine to chloride, oxygen gas liberated). Its spontaneous decomposition 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ is disproportionation, showing both behaviours at once.
  • NEB 2024 (2m): Why is fluorine the strongest oxidising agent among the halogens? — Fluorine combines the smallest atomic size, the highest electronegativity (4.0) and the most positive standard reduction potential (F₂ + 2e⁻ → 2F⁻, E° = +2.87 V) of all the halogens, so it accepts electrons more eagerly than Cl₂, Br₂ or I₂. Its small, strongly hydrated fluoride ions add extra thermodynamic driving force. Oxidising power therefore decreases down the group — F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ — leaving fluorine as the strongest oxidising agent, able even to oxidise water.
