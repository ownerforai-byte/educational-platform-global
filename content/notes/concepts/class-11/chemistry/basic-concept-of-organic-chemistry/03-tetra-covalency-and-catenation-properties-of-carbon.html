Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Four Bonds and a Chain

    Two properties of carbon build the entire organic world: tetravalency (four bonds per atom) and catenation (self-linking into chains and rings). This topic shows where the fourth bond comes from (the excited state), why C–C bonds are strong enough for unlimited chains, and what catenation costs the neighbours — silicon tries and fails.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Tetravalency — the excited-state story

    • Carbon: 1s² 2s² 2p² — ground state has only 2 unpaired electrons, which would suggest valency 2.
    • On excitation, one 2s electron jumps to the empty 2p orbital: 2s¹ 2p³ — 4 unpaired electrons → valency 4.
    • The excitation cost (~406 kJ/mol) is repaid many times over by forming two extra strong bonds; that is why carbon is tetravalent in virtually every compound.
    • The four bonds are equivalent in methane (via hybridisation — sp³, treated fully in bonding) — four identical C–H bonds.

    2.2 Catenation — self-linking

    • Catenation: the property of an element's atoms to link with each other forming chains and rings.
    • Carbon is the champion: small size + high C–C bond energy (≈ 348 kJ/mol) → stable chains of any length (from ethane's two carbons to polymer chains of hundreds of thousands).
    • Chains may be straight, branched or cyclic; each carbon still bonds to H or other groups with its remaining valencies.

    2.3 Why the neighbours fail

    • Silicon (same group) catenates weakly: Si–Si ≈ 226 kJ/mol, so silanes run to only a few units and are reactive; silicon prefers Si–O bonds (the silicate minerals).
    • Boron, sulphur catenate to limited rings/chains; nitrogen barely at all (N–N weak, lone-pair repulsion).
    • Only carbon combines tetravalency + strong self-bonds + bonds to H, O, N, halogens — the full organic toolkit.

    2.4 Consequences

    • Isomerism: same formula, many skeletons (C₄H₁₀ has two; C₅H₁₂ three; the count explodes upward).
    • Allotropes (diamond, graphite, fullerenes) display carbon's bonding versatility in elemental form.
    • Functional groups can be hung on any position of any chain — the combinatorial engine of the tens of millions.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The energy ledger of tetravalency

    • Excitation costs ~406 kJ/mol but buys two extra bonds; a C–H bond releases ~413 kJ/mol, so the investment repays itself within the reaction that forms the compound. Thermodynamics, not mystery, makes carbon four-handed.

    4.2 Chain stability and the bond-energy ladder

    • C–C ≈ 348 kJ/mol vs Si–Si ≈ 226 kJ/mol: the silicon chain earns less per link while the atoms are bigger and bonds longer, so attack (by water, oxygen) wins easily. Carbon's small radius holds its shared pairs close — strong, unreactive backbones.

    4.3 Isomerism grows explosively

    • C₄H₁₀: 2 isomers (butane, isobutane). C₅H₁₂: 3. C₆H₁₄: 5. C₁₀H₂₂: 75. C₂₀H₄₂: over 300,000. Catenation + branching = the combinatorial root of organic chemistry's size.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Ground state does not decide valency: carbon's ground state predicts valency 2, but bonding happens through the excited state — the excitation energy is an investment repaid by bond formation.

  • Catenation is not the same as allotropy: catenation is self-linking in compounds (chains/rings); allotropes are different elemental forms — related ideas, different words.
  • Tetravalency is not tetrahedral geometry by definition: four bonds is the valency; the tetrahedral shape comes from sp³ hybridisation minimising repulsion — valency and shape are separate statements.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Describe tetravalency of carbon. How does the excited state explain the four valency of carbon? — Carbon's ground-state electronic configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p², with only two unpaired 2p electrons — apparently valency 2. Before bonding, one of the 2s electrons is promoted to the empty 2p orbital, giving the excited state 2s¹ 2p³ with four unpaired electrons. Carbon now forms four covalent bonds — its tetravalency (CH₄, CCl₄, and every organic skeleton). Why the promotion pays: excitation costs about 406 kJ/mol, but forming two additional strong bonds (each C–H ≈ 413 kJ/mol) releases far more energy, so the excited-state bonding is energetically favourable — the atom borrows energy to earn more. In methane the four bonds are equivalent (sp³ hybridisation), pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron.

  • NEB-style (3m): What is catenation? Why does carbon show maximum catenation? — Catenation is the ability of atoms of an element to link with one another, forming chains and rings. Carbon shows the maximum catenation because: (i) the C–C bond is very strong (≈ 348 kJ/mol) — among the strongest element–element single bonds; (ii) carbon is small, so the shared electron pairs are close to the nuclei and well held; (iii) carbon is tetravalent, so each atom in the chain still has bonds available for hydrogen or functional groups. Hence carbon forms stable straight, branched and cyclic chains of unlimited length — the basis of the millions of organic compounds. Silicon, the next element in the group, catenates only weakly (Si–Si ≈ 226 kJ/mol), so its hydrides stop at a few units.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why does silicon show poor catenation compared with carbon? — Silicon atoms are larger, so the Si–Si bond is longer and weaker (≈ 226 kJ/mol vs 348 kJ/mol for C–C); the shared pairs are farther from the nuclei and easily attacked. Silicon chains (silanes) are therefore limited to a few units and are reactive, whereas carbon's small size and strong self-bonds support chains of unlimited length.

Confusion

  • ❌ All atoms are identical in an element. ✅ Isotopes have same protons but different neutrons.
  • ❌ Atoms are indivisible. ✅ Atoms consist of protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • ❌ Electrons orbit in fixed paths like planets. ✅ Electrons exist in probability clouds (orbitals).
  • ❌ Heisenberg uncertainty means we cannot measure position and momentum precisely simultaneously — not that we lack good instruments.
  • ❌ de Broglie wavelength applies only to light. ✅ All matter has wave nature; detectable mainly for small masses like electrons.
  • ❌ Quantum number l can be any integer. ✅ l ranges from 0 to n−1.
  • ❌ Magnetic quantum number ml determines energy. ✅ ml determines orbital orientation, not energy (in absence of magnetic field).
  • ❌ Spin quantum number ms can be ±½ or any value. ✅ ms is always +½ or −½.
  • ❌ Aufbau principle means filling from bottom to top of the diagram. ✅ Fill lowest-energy orbitals first: 1s→2s→2p→3s→3p→4s→3d→4p→...
  • ❌ Pauli exclusion allows two electrons per orbital with any spin. ✅ Two electrons in same orbital must have opposite spins.
  • ❌ Hund's rule means pair electrons before spreading. ✅ Electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly first with parallel spins.
  • ❌ The mole is just a number like dozen. ✅ 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ particles (Avogadro constant) — a bridge between atomic and macroscopic scales.
  • ❌ Molar mass and molecular mass are the same thing. ✅ Molecular mass is in amu; molar mass is in g/mol — numerically equal but different units.
  • ❌ Limiting reagent is the reactant with smallest mass. ✅ Limiting reagent is the one that produces the least product (determined by moles, not mass).
  • ❌ % yield can exceed 100%. ✅ % yield >100% indicates impure product or incomplete drying.
  • ❌ Molarity and molality are the same. ✅ Molarity = mol/L solution (changes with T); Molality = mol/kg solvent (temperature-independent).
  • ❌ A strong acid completely ionizes in water. ✅ Strong acids (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄) are ~100% ionized; weak acids partially ionize.
  • ❌ pH can be negative. ✅ pH < 0 is possible for concentrations >1M (e.g., 2M HCl has pH ≈ −0.3).
  • ❌ Kc changes when concentration changes. ✅ Kc is constant at constant temperature; only temperature changes K.
  • ❌ Adding catalyst shifts equilibrium position. ✅ Catalyst speeds both forward and backward reactions equally; no shift in equilibrium.
  • ❌ High pressure always favors products. ✅ High pressure favors side with fewer gas moles (Le Chatelier).
  • ❌ Exothermic reactions are spontaneous. ✅ Spontaneity depends on ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, not just ΔH.
  • ❌ Endothermic reactions cannot be spontaneous. ✅ Endothermic reactions can be spontaneous if TΔS > ΔH (e.g., ice melting above 0°C).
  • ❌ ΔH and ΔU are always the same. ✅ ΔH = ΔU + PΔV; they differ when gas moles change.
  • ❌ Heat (q) and work (w) are state functions. ✅ q and w are path functions; only ΔU, ΔH, ΔS, ΔG are state functions.
  • ❌ Oxidation and reduction happen separately. ✅ Oxidation and reduction always occur together (redox).
  • ❌ Oxidizing agent gets oxidized. ✅ Oxidizing agent accepts electrons and gets reduced.
  • ❌ Reducing agent gets reduced. ✅ Reducing agent donates electrons and gets oxidized.
  • ❌ Faraday's laws apply only to metals. ✅ Faraday's laws apply to any substance deposited/dissolved at electrodes.
  • ❌ Real gases obey PV=nRT at all conditions. ✅ Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature.
  • ❌ van der Waals constants a and b are same for all gases. ✅ a (attraction) and b (volume) are gas-specific.
  • ❌ Surface tension increases with temperature. ✅ Surface tension decreases as temperature increases.
  • ❌ Viscosity decreases with temperature for liquids. ✅ Viscosity of liquids decreases with T; gases increase with T.
  • ❌ Colligative properties depend on nature of solute. ✅ Colligative properties depend only on number of particles, not identity.
  • ❌ Raoult's law applies to all solutions. ✅ Raoult's law is exact for ideal solutions; approximate for dilute real solutions.
  • ❌ Osmotic pressure is a colligative property. ✅ Yes — π = CRT depends on solute particle concentration.
  • ❌ All carbon compounds are organic. ✅ CO, CO₂, carbonates, and carbides are inorganic despite containing carbon.

Summary

Carbon's chemistry rests on two properties. Tetravalency: the ground state 1s²2s²2p² has only two unpaired electrons, but promotion to the excited state 2s¹2p³ provides four; the ~406 kJ/mol excitation cost is repaid twice over by two extra strong bonds, so carbon forms four equivalent covalent bonds everywhere (sp³ tetrahedral in methane). Catenation: carbon's small size and strong C–C bond (≈348 kJ/mol) let its atoms link into straight, branched and cyclic chains of unlimited length, while silicon's weak Si–Si bond (≈226 kJ/mol) limits silanes to a few units. Together the two properties generate the isomer explosion (C₄H₁₀: 2; C₁₀H₂₂: 75; C₂₀H₄₂: >300,000) and the functional-group variety that make organic chemistry the largest branch of the subject.

Additionally, the same molecular formula can be arranged into several different skeletons — isomerism (butane has 2 isomers, pentane 3, decane 75, and the numbers grow explosively). Unlimited chain lengths × many functional groups × many isomeric arrangements = the tens of millions of known organic compounds.
