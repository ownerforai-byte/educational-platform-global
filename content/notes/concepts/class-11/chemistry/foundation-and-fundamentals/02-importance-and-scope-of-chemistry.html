  • Importance and scope of chemistry

    1. Introduction

    If the last topic defined chemistry, this one measures its footprint — and the footprint covers the whole economy. The rice that feeds Nepal grew on urea chemistry; the house that shelters it stands on cement chemistry; the child reading this was likely vaccinated, diagnosed or dosed through medicinal chemistry; the phone in her pocket charges a lithium-ion cell. Importance is chemistry's value to life and livelihood; scope is the range of fields it reaches and the careers it opens.

    Nepal's numbers tell it plainly: cement plants at Hetauda and Udayapur calcine limestone by the kiln-reaction; NARC field stations test fertilizer doses plot by plot; paper, soap, brewing, biogas and hydropower chemicals employ thousands. The same science also warns — plastics in rivers, pesticide residues, air pollution — which is why green chemistry closes this topic.

    What you will learn: (i) chemistry in agriculture, industry, medicine and daily life; (ii) chemistry and the environment (both directions); (iii) the scope: pure-to-applied spectrum and careers; (iv) Nepal-specific examples for every sector.

    Learning outcomes. You can: name two chemical inputs/outputs per sector with a Nepal example; explain fertilizer, drug and cement chemistry in one line each; and argue both the promise and the pollution of chemistry.

  • 2. Concepts and definitions

    Chemistry in agriculture

    1. Fertilizers. Urea CO(NH₂)₂ (46% N), DAP (N + P), muriate of potash (K) replace the N-P-K crops remove; NARC trials fix doses per crop and soil — yield chemistry, plot by plot.
    2. Pesticides and protection. Insecticides, fungicides, herbicides guard the harvest; the same molecules demand waiting periods and protective gear — benefit and hazard in one bottle.
    3. Soil chemistry. pH testing decides lime vs gypsum amendment; saline soils are reclaimed by chemistry before a seed is sown.
    4. Animal husbandry. Mineral mixture for dairy cattle, silage fermentation for winter fodder — applied biochemistry in the shed.

    Chemistry in industry

    1. Cement. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (calcination, ~1400°C), then clinker + gypsum → cement; Hetauda/Udayapur kilns run this equation at industrial scale.
    2. Glass, paper, soap, textiles. Sand + soda → glass; bamboo/sabai grass → paper pulp; oil + NaOH → soap; dyeing/finishing chemistry colours the garment sector.
    3. Metals and fuels. Ore → metal (extraction chemistry, next unit's metallurgy); petroleum refining → fuels, plastics, fertilisers — one barrel, a hundred products.
    4. Assumptions box. Industrial equations assume continuous feed, stated T/P/catalyst, and purity specs — classroom equations omit the engineering margins.

    Chemistry in medicine and daily life

    1. Drugs. Antibiotics (penicillin blocks wall-building), antimalarials (artemisinin), ORS salts, vaccines — each a molecule with a tested dose.
    2. Diagnostics. Blood-glucose strips (enzyme electrodes), pregnancy kits (antibody chemistry), X-ray contrast media — analysis at the bedside.
    3. Kitchen and home. Fermentation (gundruk, curd, jaad), baking (NaHCO₃ → CO₂ lift), cleaning (surfactants), water treatment (chlorination, alum) — a household runs dozens of reactions daily.
    4. Scope and careers. Pure (research, teaching) → applied (QC chemist, pharmacist, agronomist, environmental analyst, forensic chemist) — the spectrum Table 1 maps.

  • 3. Reaction and equation block — sector equations with conditions

    1. Fertilizer (urea hydrolysis in soil). CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O →(urease enzyme) 2NH₃ + CO₂; ammonia → ammonium plants absorb. Condition: soil moisture + urease — why urea is broadcast before rain, not onto dry dust.
    2. Cement (calcination). CaCO₃(s) →(Δ ~1400°C kiln) CaO(s) + CO₂(g). Condition: rotary-kiln heat; the CO₂ is the industry's carbon footprint — green-chemistry problem statement included.
    3. Soap (saponification). Oil/fat + NaOH →(warm, stir) soap + glycerol. Condition: exact alkali dose — excess burns skin, deficit leaves grease.
    4. Quantitative reading. Each equation doubles as a mass budget (next topics' stoichiometry): 100 kg CaCO₃ → 56 kg CaO + 44 kg CO₂ — industry plans in these ratios.

  • 5. Worked examples and applications

    1. Urea dose. A NARC trial recommends 100 kg urea/ha for wheat: 100 × 0.46 = 46 kg nitrogen per hectare — percentage composition (next topic) deciding the harvest.
    2. Cement budget. 100 kg limestone → 56 kg quicklime + 44 kg CO₂: the kiln's mass budget and its carbon bill in one ratio.
    3. ORS rescue. One sachet in a litre of clean water replaces the salts and glucose diarrhoea removes — formulation chemistry saving children daily.
    4. Soap choice. Hard water (Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺) wastes soap as scum; detergents keep working — water chemistry deciding the product.
    5. Biogas vs firewood. A 6 m³ digester gives ~3 hours of flame with no smoke: fermentation chemistry against indoor air pollution and forest pressure.
    6. Pesticide waiting period. Spray chemistry protects the crop; the 7–14 day harvest gap protects the eater — same molecule, two rules.
    7. Paper and forests. Sabai grass → pulp → paper runs on NaOH/Na₂S cooking; community-forest supply makes it renewable — industry inside ecology.

    6. Key terms and statements

    • Importance — chemistry's value: food, shelter, health, livelihood.
    • Scope — chemistry's reach: sectors, fields, careers it opens.
    • Fertilizer N-P-K — nitrogen (leaf), phosphorus (root/flower), potassium (health); urea 46% N.
    • Calcination — carbonate → oxide + CO₂ by heat (cement's core step).
    • Saponification — fat/oil + alkali → soap + glycerol.
    • Green chemistry — design that prevents waste and hazard: atom economy, renewables, safer solvents.
    • QC / QA chemist — quality control/assurance: industry's analytical gatekeepers.

    8. Summary and self-check

    Chemistry's importance is food (urea/DAP/potash, pesticides with waiting periods, soil pH), shelter and goods (cement calcination at ~1400°C, glass, paper, soap, metals), health (antibiotics, ORS, diagnostics) and daily life (fermentation, baking, cleaning, chlorination) — with the green balance (biogas, IPM, refills) answering its pollution. Its scope runs pure→applied into careers from QC chemist to forensic analyst, all mapped on the 118-element periodic territory.     Common confusions. Fertilizer feeds plants, not soil fertility alone (organic matter still matters) · pesticide = benefit + hazard, never one side · green chemistry prevents waste, it doesn't just treat it · scope (reach) vs importance (value) — the question words differ.

Confusion

  • Fertilizer supplies nutrients; it does not replace organic matter — both build soil.
  • Scope is reach (sectors/careers); importance is value (food/health/livelihood) — answer the word asked.
