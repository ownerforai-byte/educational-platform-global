Notes

  • 1. Meaning and statement

  • Werner Heisenberg (1927) showed that for a microscopic particle it is impossible to determine simultaneously and exactly both its position and its momentum: Δx⋅Δp≥4πh​, or Δx⋅Δv≥4πmh​. Other conjugate pairs obey the same pattern: ΔE⋅Δt≥4πh​ and ΔL⋅Δθ≥4πh​.

  • 2. Why it happens

  • To locate an electron we must 'illuminate' it with radiation of wavelength comparable to its size; such a photon collides with the electron and changes its momentum by an unknown amount, while using longer-wavelength radiation blurs the position. The disturbance is a property of matter, not an experimental defect — the γ-ray microscope is the standard illustration.

  • 3. Consequence: the concept of probability

  • A definite orbit needs exact r and p simultaneously, which the principle forbids. The electron's location can only be described by a wave function ψ, where |ψ|² dV is the probability of finding the electron in volume dV — the electron-cloud or orbital picture. The radial probability peak for the 1s orbital lies at 0.529 Å: the same number Bohr obtained, now interpreted as a most-probable distance instead of a fixed radius.

  • 4. Applications

  • • Atomic stability and size: an electron confined to a nucleus (Δx ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m) would need Δp ≈ 10⁻¹⁹ kg m s⁻¹, i.e. KE of the order 10¹⁰ eV — impossible; hence electrons cannot sit inside nuclei and atoms are ≈10⁻¹⁰ m across, which also explains why the electron does not fall into the nucleus.
  • • Natural line width: an excited state living Δt ≈ 10⁻⁸ s has ΔE ≥ h/4πΔt, so spectral lines are broadened; the same relation explains tunnelling and virtual-particle processes.
  • • Zero-point energy of confined particles, resolution limits of electron microscopes, quantum-dot behaviour and NMR line widths all follow from the principle, and all quantum-mechanical predictions (average radii, orbital shapes) are probabilistic.

  • 5. Limitations and cautions

  • The relation matters only for microscopic particles — for a 1 g body the product is utterly negligible, so classical mechanics holds. It forbids simultaneous exact knowledge, not knowledge of one quantity: perfect momentum knowledge leaves position completely undefined. Interpretations differ (Copenhagen versus measurement-disturbance readings), so it is safest to state it as a property of quantum ensembles; it is not a statement about instrument precision and does not mean measurement is impossible.
  • Fact. P(E) = favourable/total. Additive law: P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B). If mutually exclusive, P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B).
  • Fact. Multiplicative: P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B|A); independence: P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B).
  • Fact. Complement: P(A') = 1 − P(A). For discrete: ΣP = 1; expected value E(X) = Σxᵢpᵢ.

  • Focus of this tab

  • The three standard forms of the principle and the two numerical patterns NEB asks (electron in an atom; electron in the nucleus). The probability interpretation is in the main note.

  • The three forms

  • • Position–momentum: ΔxΔp≥4πh​, or with velocity ΔxΔv≥4πmh​.
  • • Energy–time: ΔEΔt≥4πh​ — an excited state living Δt has a minimum energy spread ΔE, which broadens spectral lines (natural line width).
  • • Angular momentum–angle: ΔLΔθ≥4πh​.

  • Numerical pattern 1 — electron localised to atomic size

  • Δx = 10⁻¹⁰ m: Δv ≥ h/(4πmΔx) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴/(4π × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 10⁻¹⁰) ≈ 5.8 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹. This is comparable with the electron's own speed (2.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹), so a definite orbit is impossible — the standard argument against Bohr orbits.

  • Numerical pattern 2 — electron inside the nucleus

  • Δx = 10⁻¹⁴ m (nuclear size): Δp ≈ h/(4πΔx) ≈ 5.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ kg m s⁻¹ → KE ≈ (Δp)²/2m ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ J ≈ 10⁹ eV. Nuclear binding energies are ~10⁷ eV, so the electron cannot be confined in the nucleus — proof that electrons are not nuclear constituents (β-particles are created at emission).

  • Relation to the probability concept

  • Because r and p cannot both be sharp, the electron's state is described by ψ with |ψ|² = probability density. The 1s radial probability peaks at 0.529 Å — Bohr's radius reappears as the most probable distance. 'Electron cloud', boundary surfaces and atomic radii averages all follow from this probabilistic description.

  • Limits of the principle

  • It matters only for microscopic particles: for a 1 g body at Δx = 10⁻¹⁰ m, Δv ≈ 10⁻²⁴ m s⁻¹ — utterly negligible, so classical mechanics remains valid for everyday objects.

Confusion

  • ❌ The principle is due to clumsy instruments. ✅ It is fundamental; better instruments cannot beat h/4π.
  • ❌ It says nothing about the electron can be known. ✅ Either quantity may be exact; only the product of uncertainties is bounded below.
  • ❌ It applies equally to a moving cricket ball. ✅ For macroscopic bodies the uncertainty product is negligible, so classical physics applies.
  • ❌ The principle proves electrons do not exist in atoms. ✅ It replaces fixed orbits by probability distributions (orbitals) described by |ψ|².
  • ❌ ΔE·Δt ≥ h/4π is a different law. ✅ It is the energy–time form of the same principle, explaining natural line broadening.
  • ❌ The principle sets a limit on both quantities individually. ✅ Only the product of the two uncertainties is bounded; either one alone can be exact.
  • ❌ ΔE·Δt means energy conservation is violated. ✅ It permits transient fluctuations within ΔE for times shorter than Δt — the basis of virtual processes and tunnelling.
  • ❌ A better microscope can beat the limit. ✅ The limit is fundamental, not technical.

Universal Facts

  • Heisenberg published the principle in 1927 and received the 1932 Nobel Prize in Physics.
  • ħ = h/2π = 1.054 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; the bound is often written Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2.
  • The most probable distance of the 1s electron is 0.529 Å — the value Bohr called the first-orbit radius.
  • P(E) = favourable/total. Additive law: P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B). If mutually exclusive, P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B).
  • Multiplicative: P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B|A); independence: P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B).
  • Complement: P(A') = 1 − P(A). For discrete: ΣP = 1; expected value E(X) = Σxᵢpᵢ.
  • ħ = h/2π = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; the bound is often written Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2 (numerically equal to h/4π).
  • Natural line width of an excited state (Δt ≈ 10⁻⁸ s): ΔE ≈ 10⁻⁸ eV — the origin of the intrinsic sharpness limit of spectral lines.
  • Tunnelling (STM, α-decay) is the practical use of the energy–time form.

Examples

  • Problem: Minimum velocity uncertainty for an electron localised within 10⁻¹⁰ m. Solution: Δv ≥ h/(4πmΔx) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴/(4π × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 10⁻¹⁰) ≈ 5.8 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹ — comparable to the electron's own speed, so the orbit picture collapses.
  • Problem: Can an electron be confined in a nucleus of radius 10⁻¹⁵ m? Solution: Δp ≈ h/(4πΔx) ≈ 5.3 × 10⁻²⁰ kg m s⁻¹ → KE ≈ (Δp)²/2m ≈ 10¹⁰ eV, far beyond nuclear binding energies; β-particles are therefore created during decay, not stored in the nucleus.
  • Problem: Δx = 5 × 10⁻¹¹ m for an electron — find Δv. Solution: Δv = h/(4πmΔx) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴/(4π × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 5×10⁻¹¹) ≈ 1.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ — over half the orbital speed, confirming the orbit picture's collapse.

Important Notes

  • Write both forms: Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π and Δx·Δv ≥ h/4πm — questions often ask about velocity.
  • Use the principle to justify atomic size (~10⁻¹⁰ m) and stability; these are standard 3-mark applications.
  • State clearly that the limit is fundamental, not instrumental.
  • Write the form the question asks for (momentum or velocity); mixing m into the denominator wrongly is the common error.
  • Conclude numericals with the physical meaning (no definite orbit / no electron in the nucleus).
