Notes

  • 1. Meaning of an orbital

  • An orbital is the three-dimensional region around the nucleus in which the probability of finding the electron is maximum (the ~90–95% boundary-surface region) for a given energy state. It comes from the wave function ψ of the Schrödinger equation, and |ψ|² is the probability density. An orbital has no sharp boundary and does not describe a path.

  • 2. Orbital vs orbit

  • • Orbit (Bohr): fixed two-dimensional circular path with definite radius and energy.
  • • Orbital (quantum mechanics): three-dimensional probability distribution — spherical (s) or directional (p, d) — with no definite path and no sharp edge.

  • 3. Shape of s orbitals

  • • Spherical and hence non-directional; present in every shell from n = 1.
  • • Size and energy increase with n (1s < 2s < 3s); the radial probability distribution shows (n − 1) maxima, the outermost being the most probable distance (0.529 Å for 1s).
  • • (n − 1) spherical nodes and no nodal plane; 1s has no node.

  • 4. Shape of p orbitals

  • • Dumbbell (peanut) shaped — two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus with a nodal plane through the nucleus where probability is zero.
  • • They appear from n = 2 as three orbitals at 90° to one another: px, py, pz (m = −1, 0, +1).
  • • The three are degenerate in a free atom and split in a magnetic field (Zeeman effect).
  • • Each p orbital holds 2 electrons, so a p subshell holds 6; the shapes are identical, only the orientation differs.

  • 5. Nodes, penetration and degeneracy

  • Nodes are zero-probability regions: radial = n − l − 1, angular (nodal planes) = l, total = n − 1. Because s orbitals penetrate closer to the nucleus than p orbitals of the same shell, they are less shielded and lower in energy — the reason for the ns < np < nd order in multi-electron atoms.

  • 6. Applications

  • • Bonding and molecular shape: the directionality of p orbitals explains the 90° H–S–H angle, the VSEPR shapes, and the σ (end-on) versus π (side-on) classification of bonds.
  • • Hybridisation: mixing s and p orbitals (sp, sp², sp³) explains the linear BeF₂, trigonal-planar BF₃ and tetrahedral CH₄ geometries.
  • • Periodic table: three p orbitals per subshell (capacity 6) explain the six-membered p-block and the period lengths 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32.
  • • Explains paramagnetism (degenerate orbitals occupied singly per Hund's rule) and the spherical electron cloud of the noble gases.

  • 7. Limitations

  • Orbital pictures are boundary-surface diagrams at a chosen probability level — the electron is not confined to the drawn shape and no motion is shown. Exact shapes strictly hold only for one-electron systems; electron correlation and bonding perturb them in real atoms and molecules; d and f orbitals are far more complex (this syllabus limits us to s and p); and orbitals cannot be observed directly — only electron density can.

  • Focus of this tab

  • Spin quantum number and the box-arrow notation, mapped onto the shell structure — the drawing skills needed for configuration and orbital-diagram questions.

  • The spin quantum number

  • mₛ = +½ (↑) or −½ (↓) for every electron, independent of n, l, m. Spin angular momentum is √(3/4)ħ. Consequences: an orbital holds at most 2 electrons (Pauli); a shell holds 2n²; a subshell holds 2(2l + 1).

  • Box notation rules

  • • One box per orbital; label the subshell above (2p: three boxes).
  • • Aufbau: fill orbitals in increasing (n + l) order.
  • • Hund: in degenerate boxes, one up-arrow per box before any pairing.
  • • Pauli: two arrows in one box must be opposite (↑↓).

  • Shell mapping (putting orbitals into shells)

  • K shell: 1s (2 e⁻). L: 2s, 2p (2 + 6 = 8). M: 3s, 3p, 3d (2 + 6 + 10 = 18). N: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f (2 + 6 + 10 + 14 = 32). Filling order across shells: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p …

  • Worked box diagrams

  • • N (Z = 7): 1s ↑↓ | 2s ↑↓ | 2p ↑ ↑ ↑ → 3 unpaired electrons, paramagnetic.
  • • O (Z = 8): 2p ↑↓ ↑ ↑ → 2 unpaired.
  • • Cr (Z = 24): [Ar] 3d ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ with 4s ↑ — the half-filled exception.

  • What the diagrams predict

  • Unpaired electrons → paramagnetism, μ = √(u(u + 2)) BM; paired → diamagnetic. The diagram also shows why carbon forms two covalent bonds after promotion (two half-filled p orbitals) and why the noble gases are inert (all boxes paired).
  • The orbit–orbital distinction in tabular form plus the s-orbital details (radial probability distribution) that the main note covers briefly.

  • Orbit versus orbital (six-row table)

  • • Origin: Bohr's model / Schrödinger's equation.
  • • Nature: fixed 2D circular path / 3D probability region.
  • • Definiteness: exact radius and momentum / neither is definite (Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π).
  • • Number: one orbit per n / n² orbitals per shell.
  • • Direction: non-directional concept / s spherical, p directional.
  • • Status: contradicted by experiment / the accepted modern picture.

  • The s orbital in detail

  • • Shape: sphere — electron density depends only on distance r, not direction (non-directional, hence s orbitals give no directionality in bonding).
  • • Radial probability distribution: the chance of finding the electron between r and r + dr peaks at the most probable distance — 0.529 Å for 1s. For 2s the curve has two maxima separated by a radial node where probability is exactly zero.
  • • Nodes: 1s none; 2s one spherical node at r = 2a₀ ≈ 1.06 Å; 3s two; in general (n − 1) spherical nodes. There are no nodal planes for s.
  • • Size and energy: both increase with n (1s < 2s < 3s), and higher s orbitals penetrate closer to the nucleus than p of the same shell — the origin of the ns < np energy order.

  • Why s orbitals matter chemically

  • Spherical symmetry makes s orbitals the 'all-direction' bond formers (H uses 1s in every bond), the mixing component of every hybrid (sp, sp², sp³), and the reason the noble-gas electron clouds (filled s and p) are spherically symmetric — the chemical basis of their inertness.

Confusion

  • ❌ Orbit and orbital mean the same thing. ✅ Orbit = fixed 2D path; orbital = 3D probability region.
  • ❌ A p orbital has two lobes, so it holds two electrons in each lobe. ✅ The lobes together form one orbital, which holds a pair of electrons of opposite spin.
  • ❌ s orbitals become dumbbell-shaped in higher shells. ✅ All s orbitals stay spherical; only size and nodes change with n.
  • ❌ px, py and pz have different energies in an isolated atom. ✅ They are degenerate; a magnetic or ligand field is needed to split them.
  • ❌ The electron stays inside the drawn boundary. ✅ The surface is a probability contour; there is a small chance of finding it outside.
  • ❌ Arrows in the same box may point the same way. ✅ Pauli forbids it — opposite spins only.
  • ❌ Pairing starts in the first p box. ✅ Hund's rule: each degenerate box gets one electron first.
  • ❌ Spin affects the shape of orbitals. ✅ Spin changes capacity and magnetism, not the shape given by l.
  • ❌ 2s is just a bigger 1s. ✅ 2s also has an inner radial node and a second probability maximum — qualitatively different.
  • ❌ The electron sits on the surface of the drawn sphere. ✅ The sphere is a probability boundary; the density decays continuously outward.
  • ❌ s orbitals can be directional after hybridisation. ✅ Pure s is spherical; directionality appears only in hybrid orbitals (sp, sp², sp³).

Universal Facts

  • The most probable distance of the 1s electron is 0.529 Å (52.9 pm).
  • A 2p orbital has one nodal plane through the nucleus; 3p has one nodal plane plus one radial node.
  • Three p orbitals at 90° explain the 90° bond angle in H₂S (pure p bonding).
  • answer pattern for "Orbitals and shape of s and p orbitals only": define → method → result. Structure earns the marks on 6–10 mark questions.
  • Nitrogen's three unpaired 2p electrons explain its triple-bond tendency and paramagnetism.
  • Box diagrams are also called orbital diagrams; arrows denote mₛ = ±½.
  • The 4s orbital fills before 3d but empties first in ion formation.
  • The 1s radial probability maximum lies at exactly a₀ = 0.529 Å.
  • The 2s radial node lies at r = 2a₀ ≈ 1.06 Å — probability is zero there.
  • s orbitals are the only subshells present in every shell (n = 1 to ∞).

Examples

  • Problem: Total nodes in a 3p orbital? Solution: radial = n − l − 1 = 1; angular = l = 1; total = n − 1 = 2.
  • Problem: How many electrons fill all p orbitals of the n = 3 shell? Solution: 2l + 1 = 3 orbitals × 2 = 6 electrons.
  • Problem: Draw the box diagram for V (Z = 23). Solution: [Ar] 4s ↑↓, 3d ↑ ↑ ↑ → three unpaired d electrons, μ ≈ √15 ≈ 3.87 BM.
  • Problem: Compare the probability of finding the 1s electron at r = a₀ and r = 0. Solution: the radial probability 4πr²|ψ|² vanishes at r = 0 (the r² factor) and peaks at a₀ — even though |ψ|² itself is largest at the nucleus; the two curves answer different questions.

Important Notes

  • Quote the node formulas (n − l − 1 radial, l angular) — a recurring numerical point.
  • Describe orbitals as 'three-dimensional regions of maximum electron-finding probability', never as paths.
  • Stay within s and p shapes for this syllabus; d and f are mentioned only as more complex.
  • Always draw boxes for degenerate orbitals separately; merging them loses the Hund's rule marks.
  • Relate unpaired-electron count directly to magnetic behaviour in the answer.
  • Distinguish |ψ|² (probability density, maximal at the nucleus for 1s) from radial probability (maximal at a₀) — a classic subtlety.
  • Use the six-row table form for the orbit-vs-orbital question.
