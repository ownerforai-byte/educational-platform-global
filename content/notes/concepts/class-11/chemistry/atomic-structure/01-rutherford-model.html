Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Experiment That Found the Nucleus

    Thomson's plum-pudding model (1904) pictured the atom as diffuse positive charge with embedded electrons. Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden (1909–1911) fired α-particles (He²⁺, ~5.5 MeV) at gold foil ~4 × 10⁻⁵ cm thick and got a result the pudding model could not survive: a few α-particles bounced straight back. The atom has a nucleus.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The set-up (state the four parts)

    1. α-source: radium in a lead block with a collimator → narrow α-beam.
    2. Target: gold foil ~4 × 10⁻⁵ cm (≈1000 atoms thick) — gold can be beaten very thin and has a heavy nucleus (Z = 79).
    3. Detector: rotatable ZnS screen viewed through a microscope — each α-hit gives a scintillation.
    4. Enclosure: evacuated so air does not scatter the beam.

    2.2 Observations → conclusions (the exam's 3m chain)

    ObservationConclusion
    ≈99.86% of α-particles passed straight throughThe atom is mostly empty space
    ≈1 in 20,000 deflected by more than 90°; a very few bounced back (≈180°)A tiny, heavy, positively charged nucleus carries nearly all the mass
    Scattering follows N(θ) ∝ 1/sin⁴(θ/2)Large-angle scattering is rare but real — Coulomb repulsion off a point-like core

    2.3 The nuclear model

    • The atom is neutral and largely empty; almost all mass sits in a nucleus of radius ~10⁻¹⁵ m against atomic radius ~10⁻¹⁰ m — a 1 : 100,000 linear ratio, ~10⁻¹⁵ in volume, with nuclear density ~10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
    • Electrons revolve in circular orbits with the Coulomb attraction supplying the centripetal force.
    • The nuclear charge is characteristic of the element — the origin of atomic number, proved by Moseley (1913).
    • Distance of closest approach: r₀ = 2Ze²/(4πε₀·KE) ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ m for 5 MeV α on gold — an experimental upper limit on nuclear size.

    2.4 Limitations (exam-critical)

    1. Stability not explained: a revolving, accelerating electron must radiate continuously (Maxwell) and spiral into the nucleus in ~10⁻⁸ s — yet atoms are stable.
    2. Line spectra not explained: an inward spiral would give a continuous range of frequencies; hydrogen gives sharp discrete lines.
    3. No electronic arrangement: nothing about shells or energies — periodicity and valency unexplained.
    4. No quantisation: circular orbits are assumptions; no orbital radii or energies can be calculated.
    5. Ignores the neutron (isotopes unexplained) and electron–electron repulsion (fails for multi-electron atoms).

    2.5 Similarities and differences — Thomson's model vs Rutherford's model

    Similarities: both are pre-quantum classical models; both place electrons inside a positive framework; both try to keep the atom electrically neutral. Differences: Thomson spreads positive charge throughout the atom (pudding); Rutherford concentrates it in a tiny nucleus (1 : 100,000). Thomson cannot explain large-angle scattering; Rutherford explains it but cannot explain stability or line spectra. Thomson's atom is mostly filled; Rutherford's is mostly empty.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Bohr (Topic 14): Bohr fixes exactly the two failures — stability and line spectra — by adding quantisation.
    • To atomic number (Topic 24): Moseley's X-ray work turned Rutherford's nuclear charge into Z.
    • To nuclear chemistry: the nucleus Rutherford found later yields the proton (1919) and neutron (1932).

  • 4. Exam Orientation and Previous Year Questions (NEB)

    4.1 Verified PYQs from the solved-paper bank

    No verified PYQ for this exact topic in the bank. Practise observations→conclusions and limitations as NEB-style practice.

    4.2 Likely question forms

    1. Describe the α-scattering experiment with observations and conclusions (3–5m).
    2. Write four limitations of Rutherford's model (3m).
    3. Why was gold foil used? (2m)

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Quoting "1 in 20,000" as "1 in 2000".
    2. Saying the atom is "completely empty" — it is mostly empty; the nucleus is there.
    3. Listing limitations without the stability argument (the one NEB wants).

Confusion

  • Scattering source: deflection comes from Coulomb repulsion between the positive α-particle and the positive nucleus — not from electron collisions (electrons are too light).
  • Atomic radius vs nuclear radius: 10⁻¹⁰ m vs 10⁻¹⁵ m — the nucleus is 100,000× smaller in linear size but carries ~100% of the mass.

Practice

Grammar

Notes
  • Nucleus (noun): the dense central core of an atom. Plural: nuclei.
  • Alpha particle (noun phrase): helium nucleus (He²⁺) emitted in radioactive decay.
  • Scattering (noun/gerund): deflection of particles from their path. Rutherford scattering is the classic experiment.
  • Atomic model (noun phrase): a theoretical representation of atom structure.
  • Limitation (noun): a weakness or shortcoming of a theory/model.
  • Quantization (noun): restricting values to discrete levels; the key step from Rutherford to Bohr.
Important Statements
  • In scientific English, 'model' means a simplified representation, not a miniature replica.
  • The phrase 'mostly empty space' is a relative statement — 99.9% of the volume, not 99.9% of the mass.
  • Rutherford's experiment 'probed' atomic structure — to probe means to investigate by testing.

Summary

Rutherford's α-scattering experiment (1909–1911) on ~4 × 10⁻⁵ cm gold foil destroyed Thomson's pudding model: 99.86% of α-particles passed straight through (the atom is mostly empty space) while ~1 in 20,000 deflected >90° or bounced back — proof of a tiny (10⁻¹⁵ m), heavy, positive nucleus carrying nearly all the mass, with electrons revolving around it under Coulomb attraction. The model fails because a revolving electron should radiate and collapse in ~10⁻⁸ s, and because a spiral would emit continuous rather than line spectra — the two failures Bohr's quantised model repairs. Thomson spread charge through the atom; Rutherford concentrated it — and found the nucleus.
