Rutherford's Atomic Model and its Limitations

Relevance

Notes

  • 1. Meaning of the experiment

  • Thomson's plum-pudding model (1904) treated the atom as diffuse positive charge with electrons embedded in it and could not explain large-angle deflection of fast α-particles (He²⁺, ≈5.5 MeV). Rutherford with Geiger and Marsden (1909–1911) probed atomic structure by α-scattering — the experiment that established the nuclear atom.

  • 2. Set-up

  • Radium in a lead block with a collimator gives a narrow α-beam; the target is gold foil ≈4 × 10⁻⁵ cm thick (≈1000 atoms) because gold beats very thin and has a heavy nucleus (Z = 79); a rotatable ZnS screen viewed through a microscope records each scintillation; the apparatus is evacuated.

  • 3. Observations

  • • ≈99.86% of α-particles passed straight through — the atom is mostly empty space.
  • • ≈1 in 20 000 was deflected by more than 90°, and a very few bounced back (θ ≈ 180°).
  • • Scattering obeys N(θ) ∝ 1/sin⁴(θ/2); large-angle scattering is rare but real.

  • 4. Conclusions (nuclear model)

  • • Atom is neutral and largely empty; a tiny, heavy, positive nucleus carries almost all the mass.
  • • Nuclear radius ≈10⁻¹⁵ m against atomic radius ≈10⁻¹⁰ m (1 : 100 000); nuclear volume ≈10⁻¹⁵ of atomic volume, nuclear density ≈10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
  • • Electrons revolve in circular orbits, the Coulomb attraction of the nucleus giving the centripetal force.
  • • The positive charge is characteristic of the element — the origin of atomic number, later proved by Moseley (1913).

  • 5. Quantitative results

  • • Distance of closest approach (KE → electrostatic PE): r₀ = 2Ze²/(4πε₀·KE) ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ m for 5 MeV α on gold — an experimental upper limit on nuclear size.
  • • Rutherford scattering law N(θ) ∝ 1/sin⁴(θ/2) explains why back-scattering is the rarest event.

  • 6. Applications

  • • Founded nuclear physics: led to the proton (1919) and neutron (1932) and to nuclear fission/reactor technology; Rutherford backscattering spectroscopy (RBS) still analyses thin films industrially.
  • • Underpins Moseley's law and the atomic-number basis of the modern periodic table, and is the classic example of deducing structure statistically (probability) instead of by direct observation.

  • 7. Limitations (exam-critical)

    1. Stability not explained: a revolving electron is continuously accelerated, so by Maxwell's theory it must radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus in ≈10⁻⁸ s — yet atoms are stable.
    1. Line spectra not explained: the inward spiral would emit a continuous range of frequencies, while hydrogen gives sharp discrete lines.
    1. No electronic arrangement: nothing about shells, electron numbers or energies, so periodicity and valency remain unexplained.
    1. Purely classical: circular orbits and a point nucleus are assumptions; without quantisation the model gives no orbital radii or energies.
    1. Ignores the neutron (so isotopes are unexplained) and neglects electron–electron repulsion, so it fails for multi-electron atoms; no account of bonding or Zeeman/Stark splitting.

  • 8. Exam pointers

  • Observations + conclusions and the two main defects (stability, discrete spectra) are standard 4–5 mark NEB questions; numericals use r₀, nuclear radius/density and the atom : nucleus volume ratio.

Confusion

  • ❌ The atom is 'totally empty'. ✅ It is mostly empty space, but ≈99.9% of the mass lies in the nucleus.
  • ❌ α-particles are electrons. ✅ They are helium nuclei (+2e, ≈7300 times heavier than an electron), so electrons cannot deflect them appreciably.
  • ❌ The model failed because α-particles were absorbed. ✅ Scattering itself was explained well; the model failed on stability and line spectra.
  • ❌ Atom radius = nuclear radius. ✅ 10⁻¹⁰ m versus 10⁻¹⁵ m.

Practice

  • Describe the α-scattering experiment with a labelled diagram; list observations and conclusions.
  • Use Maxwell's electromagnetic theory to explain why Rutherford's model could not account for atomic stability.
  • State the limitations of the nuclear model and explain why Bohr's model became necessary.
  • Calculate the distance of closest approach of a 5 MeV α-particle to a gold nucleus (Z = 79).
  • Estimate the ratio of nuclear volume (r ≈ 7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m) to atomic volume (r ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) for gold.

Universal Facts

  • α-scattering experiment: 1909–1911; Geiger and Marsden counted ≈1 flash in 10⁴ at large angles; gold foil ≈4 × 10⁻⁵ cm thick.
  • Rutherford received the 1908 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work on radioactivity.
  • Nuclear density is nearly the same for all nuclei because R = R₀A^(1/3), R₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.

Examples

  • Problem: Find r₀ for a 5.0 MeV α-particle on gold (Z = 79). Solution: KE = 5.0 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ J; r₀ = (2 × 9×10⁹ × 79 × (1.6×10⁻¹⁹)²)/(8.0 × 10⁻¹³) ≈ 4.55 × 10⁻¹⁴ m. Since r₀ is larger than the nuclear radius, the nucleus acts as a point charge and the α-particle is never captured.

Important Notes

  • Quote the radiating-electron instability (collapse in ≈10⁻⁸ s) and the continuous-versus-line-spectrum defect first.
  • Never claim Rutherford's model explained the hydrogen spectrum — repairing that was Bohr's achievement (1913).

Grammar

Notes
  • Nucleus (noun): the dense central core of an atom. Plural: nuclei.
  • Alpha particle (noun phrase): helium nucleus (He²⁺) emitted in radioactive decay.
  • Scattering (noun/gerund): deflection of particles from their path. Rutherford scattering is the classic experiment.
  • Atomic model (noun phrase): a theoretical representation of atom structure.
  • Limitation (noun): a weakness or shortcoming of a theory/model.
  • Quantization (noun): restricting values to discrete levels; the key step from Rutherford to Bohr.
Important Statements
  • In scientific English, 'model' means a simplified representation, not a miniature replica.
  • The phrase 'mostly empty space' is a relative statement — 99.9% of the volume, not 99.9% of the mass.
  • Rutherford's experiment 'probed' atomic structure — to probe means to investigate by testing.
