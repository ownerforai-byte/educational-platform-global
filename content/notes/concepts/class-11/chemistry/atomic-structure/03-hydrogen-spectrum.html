Notes

  • 1. Meaning of 'spectrum'

  • A spectrum is the ordered display of the wavelengths in a beam of radiation after dispersion by a prism or grating. An emission spectrum is bright lines on a dark background (excited atoms emitting); an absorption spectrum is dark lines on a continuous background (cool atoms removing their own characteristic wavelengths). Atomic spectra are line spectra — band spectra come from molecules, continuous spectra from incandescent solids.

  • 2. Origin of the hydrogen spectrum

  • Each line is one electron transition: the electron falls from n₂ to n₁ and emits one photon, hν = E₂ − E₁ = 13.6(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) eV for hydrogen. Since the levels are discrete, only discrete wavelengths appear — hence a line spectrum rather than a continuous one.

  • 3. The spectral series of hydrogen

  • • Lyman (n₁ = 1): to the ground level — ultraviolet; first line 121.6 nm, series limit 91.2 nm.
  • • Balmer (n₁ = 2): the only visible series — Hα 656.3 nm (red), Hβ 486.1 nm, Hγ 434.0 nm, Hδ 410.2 nm; limit 364.6 nm.
  • • Paschen (n₁ = 3), Brackett (n₁ = 4) and Pfund (n₁ = 5): infrared.
  • • Lines within a series crowd together and converge at the series limit; beyond it the spectrum becomes continuous because the atom is ionised.

  • 4. Rydberg equation and the Rydberg constant

  • νˉ=λ1​=RZ2(n12​1​−n22​1​),n2​>n1​ with R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ = 109 678 cm⁻¹ for hydrogen (Z = 1). Bohr's theory derived R = 2π²me⁴/h³c, turning Rydberg's empirical formula into theory — the model's greatest success.

  • 5. Applications

  • • Spectral analysis identifies elements: each element has a unique line pattern, used in chemistry labs, metallurgy, forensics and pollution monitoring.
  • • Astronomy: composition, temperature and motion (Doppler shift) of stars are read from hydrogen lines; the 21 cm hydrogen line maps interstellar gas.
  • • Technology: atomic emission/absorption spectroscopy (AAS, ICP), flame tests, He–Ne gas lasers (632.8 nm) and spectrometer calibration all rest on hydrogen-like energy levels.
  • • Frank–Hertz and discharge-tube experiments confirm discrete energy levels and the Bohr energy expression.

  • 6. Limitations of this treatment

  • The Bohr/Rydberg picture gives series positions but not line fine structure (Hα is really a doublet), relative intensities, or splitting in magnetic (Zeeman) and electric (Stark) fields; beyond hydrogen it needs multi-electron and relativistic corrections.

  • The five series at a glance

  • • Lyman (n₁ = 1): UV. First line 121.6 nm (2→1); series limit 91.2 nm. Discovered by Lyman, 1906.
  • • Balmer (n₁ = 2): visible. Hα 656.3 nm (3→2), Hβ 486.1 nm (4→2), Hγ 434.0 nm (5→2), Hδ 410.2 nm (6→2); limit 364.6 nm. Discovered by Balmer, 1885.
  • • Paschen (n₁ = 3): near IR. First line 1875 nm (4→3); limit 820.4 nm. Discovered 1908.
  • • Brackett (n₁ = 4): IR. First line 4051 nm (5→4); limit 1458 nm. Discovered 1922.
  • • Pfund (n₁ = 5): far IR. First line 7460 nm (6→5); limit 2279 nm. Discovered 1924. (A sixth, Humphreys, n₁ = 6, lies in the far IR.)

  • Counting lines

  • • From a single excited level n: the number of different lines = n(n − 1)/2 (e.g. n = 4 → 6 lines: 3 Lyman + 2 Balmer + 1 Paschen).
  • • In one series, from level n to fixed n₁: n − n₁ lines (e.g. Balmer lines down from n = 4: two lines).

  • Line spacing within a series

  • Successive lines crowd together and converge at the series limit (n₂ → ∞): νˉlimit​=R/n12​. The Balmer limit 364.6 nm marks the end of visible lines; beyond it the atom ionises and the spectrum becomes continuous.

  • Wavelength ↔ energy conversions

  • ΔE(eV)=λ(nm)1240​ and ΔE=13.6(n12​1​−n22​1​). Every line position follows from these two relations; the wavelength of any hydrogen-like ion scales as 1/Z² (He⁺ lines are 4× more energetic, shifted to ¼ wavelength).
  • How the Rydberg equation follows from Bohr's theory, what the Rydberg constant means, and the standard numerical patterns built on it.

  • Derivation (short form)

  • Photon energy for a jump n₂ → n₁: hν=E2​−E1​=h22π2me4Z2​(n12​1​−n22​1​). With ν=c/λ and νˉ=1/λ=ν/c: νˉ=RZ2(n12​1​−n22​1​) where R=h3c2π2me4​=1.097×107 m−1. The empirical Balmer/Rydberg formula thus emerged from theory — Bohr's greatest success.

  • Meaning of R

  • R is the wave number of the hydrogen series limit (n₁ = 1, n₂ → ∞): νˉlimit​=R, λ = 91.2 nm. Because m appears in R, the constant is slightly isotope-dependent (reduced-mass correction): R(²H) ≈ R(¹H) × 1.00027 — the basis of measuring deuterium in heavy water spectroscopically.

  • Numerical patterns

  • • Wavelength of a given line: pick n₁ (series) and n₂ (upper level); compute νˉ, invert for λ.
  • • Series limit: set n₂ → ∞ → νˉ=RZ2/n12​.
  • • First line of a series: n₂ = n₁ + 1.
  • • Identification: given λ and a series, solve for n₂; given two lines, form their difference to get a third line (Ritz combination principle).
  • • Hydrogen-like ions: multiply by Z² (He⁺ Balmer lines are 4× the wave number of hydrogen's).

  • Units trap

  • R is quoted both as 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ and 109 677 cm⁻¹. Convert before mixing: 1 cm⁻¹ = 100 m⁻¹; λ(nm) = 10⁷/ν̄(cm⁻¹).

Confusion

  • ❌ The Balmer series lies in the ultraviolet. ✅ Balmer (n₁ = 2) is visible; Lyman (n₁ = 1) is UV and Paschen onward are IR.
  • ❌ A ground-state hydrogen atom can emit a line. ✅ Emission needs excitation first; the ground state can only absorb.
  • ❌ Within a series the lines get farther apart. ✅ They crowd together and converge at the series limit.
  • ❌ Absorption lines mean the atom lost energy. ✅ The atom absorbs exactly the photons matching its level gaps, so those wavelengths are missing from the transmitted beam.
  • ❌ Every line requires n₂ − n₁ = 1. ✅ Any n₂ > n₁ gives a line; the number of lines from level n is n(n − 1)/2.
  • ❌ The Balmer series starts from n = 1. ✅ Balmer lines end at n₁ = 2; the lower level defines the series, not the upper one.
  • ❌ The number of lines from level n is n − 1. ✅ That counts one series only; the total is n(n − 1)/2.
  • ❌ The series limit is the strongest line. ✅ It is the convergence point where lines merge into the ionisation continuum.
  • ❌ R is an empirical fitted constant with no origin. ✅ Bohr derived it from m, e, h and c — a theoretical prediction confirmed by experiment.
  • ❌ ν̄ is the frequency. ✅ It is the wave number (1/λ); frequency ν = c·ν̄.
  • ❌ The same R applies unchanged to He⁺. ✅ Multiply by Z² (and apply the reduced-mass correction for precision).

Universal Facts

  • Hα = 656.3 nm (red) is the first Balmer line; Lyman limit = 91.2 nm; Balmer limit = 364.6 nm.
  • R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ = 109 678 cm⁻¹ for hydrogen.
  • The Balmer series was discovered first (1885) because it alone lies in the visible region.
  • answer pattern for "Spectrum of hydrogen atom": define → method → result. Structure earns the marks on 6–10 mark questions.
  • Balmer (1885) found the visible series before the electron was discovered — a purely empirical formula at first.
  • The Lyman limit 91.2 nm marks the ionisation threshold of ground-state hydrogen.
  • Hα at 656.3 nm is the reddest strong line in astronomy — used to map hydrogen nebulae.
  • R was known experimentally to 6 figures before Bohr — the theory matched without fitting.
  • The reduced-mass shift of the D line (²H) versus H line identified deuterium in 1932.
  • Ritz's combination principle: the wave number of one line equals the sum/difference of two others sharing a level.

Examples

  • Problem: Wavelength of the first Balmer line (n = 3 → 2). Solution: 1/λ = 1.097×10⁷(1/4 − 1/9) = 1.524×10⁶ m⁻¹ → λ = 656 nm (Hα).
  • Problem: Energy of the photon for n = 4 → 2. Solution: ΔE = 13.6(1/4 − 1/16) = 2.55 eV; λ = 1240/2.55 ≈ 486 nm (Hβ).
  • Problem: Lines from n = 5 to ground. Solution: 5(5−1)/2 = 10 lines — 4 Lyman, 3 Balmer, 2 Paschen, 1 Brackett.
  • Problem: Shortest Balmer wavelength. Solution: the series limit, n₂ → ∞: λ = 1/(R/4) = 4/R = 364.6 nm.
  • Problem: λ of the Hβ line (4 → 2). Solution: ν̄ = 1.097×10⁷(1/4 − 1/16) = 1.097×10⁷ × 0.1875 = 2.057×10⁶ m⁻¹ → λ = 486 nm.
  • Problem: Wave number of the Paschen limit. Solution: ν̄ = R/9 = 1.22×10⁶ m⁻¹ → λ = 820.4 nm.

Important Notes

  • Remember the series-to-region mapping: Lyman UV, Balmer visible, Paschen/Brackett/Pfund IR — a standard one-mark question.
  • Convert cm⁻¹ to m⁻¹ (× 100) before using R in SI; unit slips are the commonest error here.
  • Memorise the three visible-region Balmer wavelengths (656, 486, 434 nm) — they recur in numericals.
  • Series is named by its lower level n₁; the upper level varies.
  • Keep R in one unit system throughout a calculation; the cm⁻¹↔m⁻¹ factor of 100 is the classic slip.
  • For He⁺, Li²⁺ multiply by Z² before inverting for λ.
