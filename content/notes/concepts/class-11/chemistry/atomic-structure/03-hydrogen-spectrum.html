Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Fingerprint That Saved Bohr

    Rutherford's model could not explain why atomic hydrogen emits discrete coloured lines instead of a continuous glow. Bohr (1913) turned that failure into a triumph: if electrons occupy only quantised orbits, an electron dropping from a higher orbit to a lower one emits a photon carrying exactly the energy gap — hν = E₂ − E₁. Each gap is fixed, so each line is fixed. The hydrogen spectrum is therefore the experimental proof of quantisation, and the model that predicts its wavelengths to 5 significant figures is the model NEB examines every year.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Energy of the nth orbit

    • Eₙ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J / n² = −13.6 eV / n² (n = 1, 2, 3, …).
    • The negative sign means the electron is bound; E₁ = −13.6 eV is the ground state, E∞ = 0 is ionisation.
    • Energy gaps shrink as n grows — levels crowd together near the ionisation limit.

    2.2 The transition rule

    Photon emitted when the electron falls from n₂ (upper) to n₁ (lower):

    hν = E₂ − E₁ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)

    2.3 The Rydberg equation (wavelength form)

    1/λ = R (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ (Rydberg constant), n₂ > n₁.

    2.4 The five spectral series (memorise the table — straight 3m)

    Seriesn₁ (lower)n₂ (upper)Spectral regionSeries limit
    Lyman12, 3, 4, …Ultraviolet91.2 nm
    Balmer23, 4, 5, …Visible364.6 nm
    Paschen34, 5, 6, …Infrared1875 nm
    Brackett45, 6, 7, …Infrared4051 nm
    Pfund56, 7, 8, …Infrared (far)7460 nm

    Only Balmer lies in the visible region — the four visible lines are called Hα (656.3 nm, red), Hβ (486.1 nm), Hγ (434.0 nm), Hδ (410.2 nm).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why lines, not a rainbow

    Classical physics: an orbiting electron radiates continuously, spirals inward, and the spectrum should be continuous. Bohr: orbits are stationary — no radiation while the electron stays put. Radiation happens only during a jump, and the jump energy is quantised, so the photon wavelength is one fixed value per jump. Millions of atoms making the same jump produce one bright line; different jumps produce different lines — a line spectrum.

    4.2 Emission vs absorption

    • Emission spectrum: excited atoms (flame, discharge tube) release photons falling down — bright lines on a dark background.
    • Absorption spectrum: white light passes through cool hydrogen gas; atoms remove exactly the photons whose energy matches an upward jump — dark lines on a continuous background at the same wavelengths.
    • Solar spectrum's Fraunhofer dark lines are absorption lines of elements in the solar atmosphere — the same physics reads the composition of stars.

    4.3 Worked numerical — NEB 2022 (3m, verified PYQ)

    Q: Using Eₙ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸/n² J, calculate the energy of the electron in the second orbit of hydrogen and the wavelength of the photon emitted when the electron falls from the third to the second orbit. Express the wavelength in nm.

    Step 1 — Energy of the second orbit: E₂ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ / 2² = −5.45 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

    Step 2 — Energy released (3 → 2): ΔE = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ (1/2² − 1/3²) = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ × (1/4 − 1/9) = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ × 5/36 = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

    Step 3 — Wavelength (λ = hc/ΔE): λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3 × 10⁸) / 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ m ≈ 656 nm — the red Hα line of the Balmer series.

    4.4 Worked numerical — series limit

    Q: Find the shortest wavelength of the Lyman series. Series limit: n₂ → ∞, so 1/λ = R(1/1² − 0) = R → λ = 1/R = 1/1.097 × 10⁷ = 9.12 × 10⁻⁸ m = 91.2 nm (extreme UV).

    4.5 Hydrogen-like species

    For He⁺, Li²⁺ (one electron only), the equation becomes Eₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² eV and 1/λ = RZ²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) — the spectrum shifts by the factor Z², which is why He⁺ lines appear at λ/4 of the hydrogen positions.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Series limit vs first line: the limit (n₂ → ∞) is the shortest wavelength; the first line (smallest gap, e.g. 3 → 2) is the longest — students swap them.

  • Balmer vs Lyman direction: Lyman needs jumps down to n = 1 (from any higher level); Balmer needs jumps down to n = 2. A 3 → 2 jump is Balmer even though it starts at n = 3.
  • Rydberg sign convention: 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) with n₁ the lower level; writing the terms the other way gives a negative 1/λ — physically meaningless.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): Why does hydrogen produce a line spectrum rather than a continuous spectrum? Because Bohr orbits are quantised and stationary: radiation is emitted only in jumps between fixed levels, so each photon has one definite energy — discrete lines, not a continuum.

  • NEB-style (3m): Calculate the wavelength of the Hα line (3 → 2). 1/λ = 1.097 × 10⁷ (1/4 − 1/9) = 1.097 × 10⁷ × 5/36 = 1.524 × 10⁶ m⁻¹ → λ = 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ m = 656.3 nm.
  • NEB-style (3m): The second line of the Balmer series (4 → 2). 1/λ = 1.097 × 10⁷ (1/4 − 1/16) = 1.097 × 10⁷ × 3/16 → λ = 486.1 nm (Hβ, blue-green).

Confusion

  • Orbit vs jump: energy is possessed in an orbit but radiated only during a jump between orbits — the orbit itself emits nothing.
  • n₁ vs n₂: in the Rydberg equation n₁ is the lower (final) level and n₂ the upper (initial) level; reversing them gives a negative result.
