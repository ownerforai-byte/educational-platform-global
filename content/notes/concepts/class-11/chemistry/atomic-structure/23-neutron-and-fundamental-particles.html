Notes

  • Focus of this tab

  • Chadwick's discovery of the neutron and the complete subatomic-particle data table that the nuclear atom needs (electron, proton, neutron with charge, mass, discoverer and location).

  • Why a third particle had to exist

  • Mass problem: helium (Z = 2) should weigh 2 protons ≈ 2 u, but weighs 4 u. Charge problem: nitrogen (A = 14, Z = 7) would need 7 electrons inside the nucleus under a proton-only model, making the atom's magnetism and the electron's confinement impossible (uncertainty: electrons cannot be confined to 10⁻¹⁵ m). A neutral, massive particle was required.

  • Chadwick's experiment (1932)

  • Polonium's α-particles hit beryllium and released a highly penetrating, neutral radiation: 9Be+4He→12C+1n. The radiation knocked protons out of paraffin wax; by conservation of momentum and energy the unknown particle's mass was calculated as ≈1.15–1.16 u — close to the proton's. Being neutral, it was unaffected by E/B fields and could pass through centimetres of lead.

  • The fundamental particles table (memorise)

  • • Electron (e⁻): charge −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (−1 unit), mass 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg = 0.000549 u = 1/1837 of H; discovered by J. J. Thomson (1897); location: revolving in orbitals outside the nucleus.
  • • Proton (p): +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (+1), 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.00728 u; Goldstein's rays/identified by Thomson, named by Rutherford (1919–20); location: nucleus.
  • • Neutron (n): 0, 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.00867 u (heaviest of the three); Chadwick (1932); location: nucleus. Free neutrons β-decay (half-life ≈ 11 min) because they are slightly heavier than a proton plus an electron.

  • What the trio explains

  • Isotopes (same Z, different N), radioactivity (nuclear instability), nuclear stability (N/Z ratio ≈ 1 for light nuclei, rising to ≈1.5 for heavy), fission/fusion energy, and the notation ᴬZX that summarises every nuclide.
  • Mass number A, the isotope concept it makes possible, and the average-atomic-mass calculation — the accounting that sits on top of the fundamental particles.

  • Mass number

  • A = number of protons + number of neutrons = Z + N. It is always a whole number because it counts particles; the atomic mass measured for an element is usually not a whole number because it is a weighted average over isotopes (e.g. Cl = 35.45 u). Notation ᴬZX places A upper-left, Z lower-left.

  • Isotopes (same Z, different A)

  • • Hydrogen: ¹H protium, ²H deuterium (D, in heavy water D₂O), ³H tritium (radioactive) — the only element whose isotopes have separate names.
  • • Carbon: ¹²C (98.9%, the amu standard), ¹³C (NMR), ¹⁴C (dating). Chlorine: ³⁵Cl (75%), ³⁷Cl (25%). Uranium: ²³⁵U (fissile, 0.7%), ²³⁸U (99.3%).
  • • Chemical properties are almost identical (same electron configuration); physical properties differ with mass (density, diffusion rate, spectra — the D line shift identified deuterium).

  • Average atomic mass (the numerical pattern)

  • Average = Σ(abundance × isotopic mass). Cl: 0.75 × 35 + 0.25 × 37 = 35.5 u. Ne: 0.905 × 20 + 0.0027 × 21 + 0.0923 × 22 ≈ 20.18 u. Working backwards: given the average and one abundance, the second isotopic mass can be found.

  • Separation and uses

  • Because isotopes differ only in mass, separation uses mass-dependent processes: gaseous diffusion (UF₆) or centrifugation for ²³⁵U, electrolysis/distillation for D₂O. Uses: ¹⁴C dating, ¹³¹I thyroid therapy, ⁶⁰Co radiotherapy, D₂O as a neutron moderator, tracer studies in metabolism.

  • Relation to Dalton's theory

  • Isotopes violate Dalton's postulate that all atoms of an element are identical in mass; the nuclear model plus the neutron explains them — same proton count, different neutron count.

Confusion

  • ❌ The neutron is lighter than the proton. ✅ It is heavier (1.675 vs 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) — hence free-neutron β-decay.
  • ❌ The neutron was discovered before the proton was named. ✅ Proton named 1919–20; neutron 1932.
  • ❌ Neutrons carry a small charge. ✅ Exactly zero charge; that is why they penetrate deeply and leave no ion tracks.
  • ❌ Mass number is the atomic mass. ✅ A is a whole-number particle count; atomic mass is the weighted average in u.
  • ❌ Isotopes have different chemical properties. ✅ Nearly identical chemically; they differ in mass and nuclear stability.
  • ❌ ²³⁵U and ²³⁸U are isobars. ✅ They are isotopes (same Z = 92); isobars need equal A.

Universal Facts

  • Chadwick received the 1935 Nobel Prize for the neutron; it made the fission chain reaction conceivable.
  • Neutron half-life outside the nucleus ≈ 10.7 min.
  • The neutron's magnetic moment is not zero (−1.91 nuclear magnetons) despite zero charge — evidence of internal quark structure.
  • ¹²C defines the unified mass unit: 1 u = 1/12 mass of a ¹²C atom = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • Heavy water (D₂O) is used as moderator-cum-coolant in CANDU reactors.
  • Uranium enrichment to ≈3–5% ²³⁵U is what reactor fuel requires (weapons need ≈90%).

Examples

  • Problem: An atom has Z = 6 and A = 14. Give p, n, e and its isotope name. Solution: 6 protons, 8 neutrons, 6 electrons — carbon-14 (¹⁴C), the dating isotope.
  • Problem: An element of average mass 10.8 consists of isotopes 10 and 11. Find abundances. Solution: let x be the 10-isotope fraction: 10x + 11(1 − x) = 10.8 → x = 0.2 → 20% mass-10 (boron).

Important Notes

  • The table (charge, mass, discoverer, location) is a guaranteed short-question — memorise all four columns.
  • Use the uncertainty principle (electron cannot fit in the nucleus) as the conceptual argument for the neutron.
  • Keep 'mass number' (integer count) and 'atomic mass' (average in u) strictly separate.
  • Attach the isotope discussion to Dalton's limitation — it is the standard link.
  • The Neutron — Discovery & Fundamental Particle Characteristics is based on fundamental principles.
