Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Where the Quantum Model Still Breaks

    Bohr's model rescued the hydrogen atom from Rutherford's collapse problem and predicted the hydrogen spectrum brilliantly. But it was a hybrid: classical circular orbits bolted onto quantum conditions. As soon as the model faced finer spectrographs, multi-electron atoms, or the Zeeman effect, cracks appeared. Knowing why Bohr fails is the bridge to the quantum mechanical model — and a standing NEB 3m question.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts — The Defects (state any five for 3m)

    1. Fails for multi-electron atoms. The model works only for one-electron species (H, He⁺, Li²⁺). With two or more electrons, electron–electron repulsion breaks the simple Coulomb-plus-quantisation picture — the energy depends on more than n.
    2. No explanation of fine structure. High-resolution spectrographs show each Balmer line (e.g. Hα) is a close doublet, not a single line. Bohr's model predicts one line per transition — it has no sub-levels (no l, no spin).
    3. No explanation of the Zeeman effect. In a magnetic field, spectral lines split into several components; Bohr's circular orbits have no mechanism for this (needs magnetic quantum number mₗ).
    4. No explanation of the Stark effect. Lines also split in an electric field — again unexplained.
    5. Orbits are physically unreal. The model gives precise position and momentum of the electron simultaneously — forbidden by Heisenberg's uncertainty principle; the modern picture is a probability orbital, not a track.
    6. Cannot explain the dual nature of matter. de Broglie's wave mechanics shows why only certain orbits are stable (a whole number of wavelengths must fit the circumference) — Bohr quantisation was an arbitrary postulate, not a consequence.
    7. No rule for the intensity (relative brightness) of spectral lines — the model predicts wavelengths, not how strong each line should be.
    8. Circular orbits only. Real angular-momentum states include elliptical shapes (Sommerfeld's extension) — Bohr's circles are a special case.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why multi-electron atoms break the model

    Bohr's energy Eₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² assumes the electron feels only the nucleus. With two electrons, each also feels the repulsion of the other, and the electrons screen each other from the nuclear charge. The energy then depends on the electron's shape and shielding, not just on n — a single quantum number cannot describe the levels, and the predicted spectrum is wrong.

    4.2 Why fine structure defeats circular orbits

    Bohr's orbit is a circle: one radius, one energy per n. But an electron also has angular-momentum shape (l) and spin (s); relativistic and spin effects shift the energy slightly depending on l. Result: one Bohr line is actually several lines a fraction of a nanometre apart — fine structure. Bohr's n alone cannot produce it.

    4.3 Why the orbit itself is unphysical

    The model treats the electron as a particle with an exactly known position (the orbit) and momentum — but Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π forbids knowing both. Sommerfeld tried ellipses; the real cure was Schrödinger's wave mechanics: the electron is a standing wave of probability (orbital), and Bohr's quantisation emerges automatically because only waves that fit the orbit constructively survive.

    4.4 What Bohr got right (keep this — examiners ask the contrast)

    • Quantised energy levels (validated by the hydrogen spectrum).
    • The energy-gap rule hν = E₂ − E₁.
    • Ionisation energy 13.6 eV and the Rydberg constant.
    • The concept of stationary states.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Zeeman vs Stark: Zeeman = magnetic field splitting; Stark = electric field splitting. Both are line splittings, both unexplained by Bohr — but the fields differ.

  • Fine structure vs Zeeman: fine structure appears without any field (spin/relativistic splitting); Zeeman needs an external magnetic field.
  • "Bohr failed completely" — no. It succeeded for one-electron species and introduced quantised levels; its defects are specific and each maps to a missing quantum number.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): "Bohr's model could not explain the line spectrum of an atom containing more than one electron." Justify. — The model's energy formula assumes each electron feels only the nuclear Coulomb pull. In multi-electron atoms, mutual repulsion and shielding make the energy depend on more than n; one quantum number cannot assign the levels, so predicted lines do not match observed spectra.

  • NEB-style (2m): What is the Zeeman effect? — The splitting of a spectral line into several components when the emitting atoms are placed in a magnetic field; Bohr's model cannot explain it because its orbits carry no magnetic quantum number.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is Bohr's orbit incompatible with the uncertainty principle? — A definite orbit means exact position and exact momentum simultaneously; Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π forbids this, so the electron must be described as a probability orbital instead.

Confusion

  • Field pairings: Zeeman → magnetic field; Stark → electric field. Mixing them loses the 2m mark.
  • Fine structure is field-free: it is intrinsic (spin + relativistic) splitting, not a Zeeman effect.

Summary

Bohr's model is a classical–quantum hybrid, and its defects are the seams: it works only for one-electron species because it ignores electron–electron repulsion and shielding; it cannot explain fine structure (no l or s), the Zeeman and Stark line splittings (no mₗ), or line intensities; and its exact orbits violate the uncertainty principle, which replaces the electron track with a probability orbital. de Broglie's wave mechanics later derived Bohr's quantisation naturally — a whole number of wavelengths must fit the orbit.
