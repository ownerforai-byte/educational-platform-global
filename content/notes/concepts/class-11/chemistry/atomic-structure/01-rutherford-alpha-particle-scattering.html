Notes

  • Focus of this tab

  • A close-up of the α-scattering experiment itself: the apparatus, the counting data, and the two quantitative results (distance of closest approach and the scattering law) that NEB numericals are built on. The full model and its limitations are in the main note.

  • Apparatus, quantity by quantity

  • • α-source: radium C (polonium) in a lead block — lead absorbs all radiation except the fine beam escaping the collimator hole; α-particles emerge with KE ≈ 5.5 MeV and speed ≈ 1.6 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹.
  • • Foil: gold, ≈ 4 × 10⁻⁵ cm (≈ 10⁻⁷ m) thick ≈ 1000 atomic layers; gold is chosen because it is the most malleable metal and Z = 79 gives maximum deflection (∝ Z).
  • • Detector: circular ZnS screen; every α-hit gives a scintillation counted through a low-power microscope that can rotate about the foil through 0°–180°.
  • • Enclosure evacuated so α-particles are not absorbed by air (their range in air is only a few cm).

  • The counting data (Geiger–Marsden)

  • • Nearly all α-particles (≈ 99.86%) pass straight through — no deflection.
  • • About 1 in 8000 is deflected by more than 90°; about 1 in 10⁴–10⁶ comes straight back (θ ≈ 180°).
  • • The probability of scattering at angle θ falls as sin⁻⁴(θ/2) — from 0° to 180° the count falls by about 10 orders of magnitude.

  • Distance of closest approach (numerical pattern)

  • At the turning point all kinetic energy is stored as electrostatic potential energy: 21​mv2=4πε0​r0​2Ze2​, giving r0​=4πε0​(KE)2Ze2​. For 5.5 MeV α on gold r₀ ≈ 4.1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m — about 40 nuclear radii, so the α-particle never touches the nucleus and the nucleus acts as a point charge. This number is an upper limit of the nuclear size, which is why the experiment 'measures' the nucleus without seeing it.

  • Impact parameter (the other half of the story)

  • The scattering angle is fixed by the impact parameter b (the perpendicular distance the α-particle's initial line would pass from the nucleus): b=KEkZe2​cot2θ​. Head-on approach (b = 0) gives θ = 180° (back-scattering); large b gives tiny θ — the geometric origin of the sin⁻⁴ law.

  • What each observation proves

  • • Undeflected majority → atom mostly empty space.
  • • Large-angle deflection → a concentrated positive charge much heavier than the α-particle (a light electron cannot turn a 7300-times-heavier projectile).
  • • Back-scattering → the positive charge and mass are concentrated in a volume 10⁻¹⁵ of the atom.

Confusion

  • ❌ The foil must be thick for good scattering. ✅ It must be extremely thin (≈1000 atoms) so an α-particle meets at most a few nuclei; a thick foil would absorb the particles.
  • ❌ Electrons deflect the α-particles. ✅ Electron mass is ≈1/7300 of the α-mass, so momentum transfer is negligible; only the nucleus can scatter.
  • ❌ r₀ is the nuclear radius. ✅ It is the distance of closest approach — an upper limit; the nuclear radius is ≈10⁻¹⁵ m, ten times smaller.

Universal Facts

  • ≈1 in 8000 α-particles was deflected by more than 90°; the count falls by ~10 orders of magnitude from 0° to 180°.
  • For 5.5 MeV α on gold, r₀ ≈ 4.1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m ≈ 40 nuclear radii.
  • Scattering probability scales as Z² of the foil and as 1/(KE)² of the α-particle.

Examples

  • Problem: α-particles of 4 MeV approach silver (Z = 47). Find r₀. Solution: r₀ = 2kZe²/KE = (2 × 9×10⁹ × 47 × (1.6×10⁻¹⁹)²)/(4 × 1.6×10⁻¹³) ≈ 3.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m — the measurement is a nucleus-size upper limit, not the radius itself.

Important Notes

  • State clearly that r₀ is an upper limit to nuclear size — a favourite distinction question.
  • Connect each observation to its conclusion; the marks are for the inference chain, not the apparatus list.
