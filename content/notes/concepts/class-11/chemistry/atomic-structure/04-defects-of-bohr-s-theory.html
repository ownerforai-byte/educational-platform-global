  • Defects of Bohr's theory

    1. Topic Introduction

    Bohr's atomic model (1913) was a triumph — it explained the hydrogen spectrum perfectly and introduced quantization to atomic physics. But it was not the final word. Within a decade, experimental evidence piled up that the model could not explain. The defects of Bohr's theory are not just historical footnotes; they are the signposts that pointed the way to quantum mechanics.

    Why it matters. Understanding why Bohr's model fails is the best way to understand why quantum mechanics succeeds. Each defect maps directly to a quantum mechanical concept: stability → stationary states; spectra → quantization; multi-electron atoms → Pauli exclusion; fine structure → spin; Zeeman effect → magnetic quantum number; uncertainty principle → orbitals.

    What you will learn: (i) five major defects of Bohr's theory with physical reasons; (ii) why the model fails for multi-electron atoms; (iii) how Heisenberg's uncertainty principle destroys the concept of a Bohr orbit; (iv) why Bohr cannot explain Zeeman/Stark effects; (v) how Sommerfeld and Schrödinger corrected the model; (vi) the Bohr-Bury scheme and its limitations.

    Learning outcomes. You can: list all defects with one-line physical reasons; explain why Bohr fails for helium but works for He⁺; show how uncertainty principle contradicts fixed orbits; distinguish Zeeman and Stark effects; write Bohr-Bury configurations and identify exceptions (Cr, Cu); state which defect each later theory repaired.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding

    What the defects are

    The defects are the experimental and theoretical gaps between Bohr's predictions and reality. They fall into five categories: (1) stability vs radiation paradox; (2) multi-electron atom failure; (3) fine structure absence; (4) external field splitting failure; (5) uncertainty principle violation.

    Why they exist

    Bohr's model was a hybrid — classical mechanics (circular orbits) plus one quantum postulate (mvr = nħ). It worked for hydrogen because hydrogen is the simplest atom (one electron, no electron-electron repulsion). Every additional electron introduces effects the model cannot handle: repulsion, screening, exchange energy, spin coupling.

    How they manifest

    • Helium spectrum: Bohr predicts He⁺ perfectly but fails completely for neutral He.
    • Fine structure: Single lines predicted by Bohr are actually doublets (Na D-lines 589.0/589.6 nm).
    • Zeeman effect: One line splits into three in magnetic field; Bohr has no magnetic quantum number.
    • Stark effect: Electric field splits lines; Bohr has no orientation quantum number.
    • Uncertainty principle: Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π forbids definite position AND momentum simultaneously.

    Where they appear

    Helium spectrum, alkali metal doublets (Na, K), Zeeman/Stark splitting experiments, electron diffraction (wave nature), Stern-Gerlach experiment (spin), multi-electron atom chemistry.

    What their components are

    Experimental observations (spectral splitting, failure for He), theoretical contradictions (uncertainty principle, classical radiation), later corrections (Sommerfeld elliptical orbits, Schrödinger equation, spin, Pauli exclusion).

    How components interact

    Each defect points to a missing quantum number: multi-electron failure → Pauli exclusion (4th quantum number); fine structure → spin (s); Zeeman effect → magnetic (mₗ); Stark effect → spatial orientation; uncertainty → orbital concept replaces orbit.

    What makes them biologically/physically important

    Chemical periodicity arises from electron configurations (defect: Bohr couldn't explain it). Spectroscopy diagnostics (Zeeman/Stark) measure magnetic/electric fields in stars/plasmas. Electron diffraction (wave nature) enables electron microscopy. Spin (defect: no spin in Bohr) underlies MRI, ESR, spintronics.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Defect 1: Failure for multi-electron atoms

    Bohr's theory was derived for hydrogen-like ions (one electron). For neutral helium (2 electrons), the model fails because:

    • Electron-electron repulsion: The two electrons repel each other; neither sees full nuclear charge Z=2.
    • Screening/shielding: Each electron partially screens the other from the nucleus; effective nuclear charge Z_eff < Z.
    • Exchange energy: Quantum mechanical exchange interaction lowers energy for parallel spins (Hund's rule).
    • No electron correlation: Electrons avoid each other; independent particle approximation fails.

    Result: Bohr's energy formula Eₙ = -13.6Z²/n² fails completely for He. The actual ground state energy of He is -79.0 eV, not -54.4 eV (which would be 2 × -27.2 eV for two independent electrons in n=1).

    4.2 Defect 2: Fine structure (spin-orbit coupling)

    Bohr predicts single spectral lines for each transition. Experimentally, many lines are doublets or triplets:

    • Na D-lines: 589.0 nm and 589.6 nm (Δλ = 0.6 nm) — 3p → 3s transition split by spin-orbit coupling.
    • Hydrogen fine structure: H-α (656.3 nm) is actually a close doublet (2p₃/₂ → 2s₁/₂ and 2p₁/₂ → 2s₁/₂).
    • Physical origin: Electron's spin magnetic moment interacts with the magnetic field it sees from its orbital motion around the nucleus. This spin-orbit coupling adds/subtracts energy depending on relative orientation.
    • Bohr's failure: No spin in the model; all angular momentum is orbital (L = mvr). No spin-orbit coupling possible.

    4.3 Defect 3: Zeeman effect (magnetic field splitting)

    When a light source is placed in a magnetic field, spectral lines split:

    • Normal Zeeman effect: Single line splits into THREE components (σ⁻, π, σ⁺) — polarized light.
    • Anomalous Zeeman effect: More than three components (due to spin).
    • Bohr's failure: No magnetic quantum number (mₗ) in the model. Bohr's circular orbits have no preferred orientation in space. The splitting requires magnetic quantum number mₗ = -l, -l+1, ..., +l.
    • Physical origin: Magnetic moment of orbiting electron interacts with external B field: ΔE = -μ·B = mₗμ_B B.

    4.4 Defect 4: Stark effect (electric field splitting)

    In an electric field, spectral lines split:

    • Linear Stark effect: Proportional to field strength (for hydrogen n=2 level).
    • Quadratic Stark effect: Proportional to E² (for non-degenerate levels).
    • Bohr's failure: No quantum number for spatial orientation of orbit. Stark splitting requires the electron's charge distribution to have a dipole moment that can align with the field.
    • Physical origin: Interaction of atomic dipole moment with external E field: ΔE = -d·E.

    4.5 Defect 5: Heisenberg's uncertainty principle violation

    Bohr's model assigns each electron a definite orbit with exact radius rₙ and velocity vₙ. This means:

    • Position known exactly: Δx = 0
    • Momentum known exactly: Δp = 0
    • Product: Δx·Δp = 0

    Heisenberg's principle: Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π (or ħ/2)

    Consequence: The concept of a definite electron orbit is fundamentally impossible. Electrons do not have trajectories; they have probability distributions (orbitals, ψ²). The uncertainty principle is the fundamental reason Bohr's orbits must be replaced by orbitals.

    4.6 Defect 6: Classical radiation paradox

    According to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, any accelerating charged particle radiates energy. An electron in circular orbit has centripetal acceleration a = v²/r. It should continuously lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in ~10⁻¹¹ seconds.

    Bohr's fix: Postulate 1 — electrons in stationary states do not radiate. This is an ad hoc assumption with no classical justification. It works but doesn't explain WHY.

    Quantum mechanical resolution: Stationary states are eigenstates of the Hamiltonian. No radiation because there is no time-varying dipole moment in a stationary state (ψ*ψ is time-independent).

    4.7 Defect 7: No explanation for chemical bonding

    Bohr's model describes isolated atoms with circular orbits. It has:

    • No concept of orbital overlap
    • No hybridization (sp, sp², sp³)
    • No molecular orbitals
    • No valence bond theory
    • No explanation for covalent, ionic, metallic bonding

    Result: The entire field of molecular structure and chemical bonding is outside Bohr's scope.

    4.8 Sommerfeld's extension (elliptical orbits)

    Arnold Sommerfeld (1915–1916) extended Bohr's model:

    • Elliptical orbits: Electron moves in ellipses with nucleus at one focus.
    • Two quantum numbers: n (principal, energy) and k (azimuthal, shape). Later renamed n and l.
    • Relativistic correction: Electron speed varies in elliptical orbit; mass increases near perihelion → precession of orbit → fine structure splitting.
    • Success: Explained fine structure of hydrogen partially (but not fully; spin needed).
    • Limitation: Still couldn't explain multi-electron atoms, Zeeman/Stark fully, chemical bonding.

    4.9 Bohr-Bury scheme (electron configuration rules)

    Before quantum numbers, Bohr and Bury (1921) proposed empirical filling rules:

    • Maximum electrons per shell: 2n² (n=1→2, n=2→8, n=3→18, n=4→32)
    • Octet rule: Outermost shell holds max 8 electrons (except K shell: 2)
    • Filling order: K, L, M, N... (shells fill sequentially)

    Examples:

    • Ca (Z=20): 2, 8, 8, 2 (Bohr-Bury) vs actual [Ar]4s²
    • Cr (Z=24): 2, 8, 13, 1 vs actual [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ (exception!)
    • Cu (Z=29): 2, 8, 18, 1 vs actual [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ (exception!)

    Limitations:

    • Fails for transition metals (4s fills before 3d)
    • Cannot explain Cr, Cu exceptions (half-filled/filled d-subshell stability)
    • No explanation for Hund's rule, Pauli exclusion
    • No concept of subshells (s, p, d, f) — just shells

  • 6. Chemical Processes and Mechanisms

    Process: Zeeman effect measurement (magnetic field diagnostic)

    1. Definition: Measuring magnetic field strength by observing spectral line splitting.
    2. Location: Solar observatories, laboratory plasma diagnostics, stellar spectroscopy.
    3. Requirements: Spectrograph with high resolution, known spectral line, calibration.
    4. Components: Telescope/spectrograph, polarimeter, calibration lamp, magnetic field source.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Observe spectral line without field (reference); (ii) Apply magnetic field; (iii) Observe splitting pattern (normal: 3 lines; anomalous: more); (iv) Measure separation Δλ; (iv) Calculate B = Δλ / (C·λ₀²·g) where C = e/4πmₑc, g = Landé factor.
    6. Result: Magnetic field strength and direction.
    7. Significance: Remote magnetic field measurement in stars, sunspots, interstellar medium.

    Process: Stark broadening diagnostics (density/temperature)

    1. Definition: Using electric field broadening of spectral lines to infer plasma conditions.
    2. Location: White dwarf atmospheres, laboratory plasmas, inertial confinement fusion.
    3. Requirements: High-resolution spectrograph, known Stark broadening coefficients.
    4. Components: Spectrograph, plasma source, Stark broadening tables (e.g., for H-β, H-γ).
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Observe line profile (H-β, H-γ); (ii) Fit Stark broadening profile (Lorentzian + Gaussian); (iii) Extract electron density nₑ from width; (iv) Combine with temperature from line ratios.
    6. Result: Electron density, temperature, ionization fraction.
    7. Significance: Primary diagnostic for high-density plasmas (white dwarfs, ICF).

    Process: Electron diffraction (wave nature proof)

    1. Definition: Diffraction of electrons by crystal lattice proves wave nature (de Broglie).
    2. Location: Electron microscope, LEED (Low Energy Electron Diffraction), surface science labs.
    3. Requirements: Electron gun (50-200 eV), single crystal target, detector.
    4. Components: Electron gun, UHV chamber, crystal (Ni, Cu, Si), phosphor screen/detector.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Accelerate electrons to known energy E; (ii) Compute λ = h/√(2mE); (iii) Aim at crystal; (iv) Observe diffraction rings/spots; (v) Measure angles; (vi) Verify Bragg's law: nλ = 2d sinθ.
    6. Result: Diffraction pattern confirms λ = h/p; measures interplanar spacing d.
    7. Significance: Direct proof of matter waves; basis of electron microscopy and surface crystallography.

  • 10. Topic Mastery Check

    Important definitions

    1. Multi-electron failure — Bohr assumes single electron; screening, exchange, correlation ignored.
    2. Fine structure — Spectral lines split into doublets/triplets by spin-orbit coupling.
    3. Zeeman effect — Magnetic field splits spectral lines (normal: 3 lines; anomalous: spin adds more).
    4. Stark effect — Electric field splits spectral lines (linear for degenerate, quadratic otherwise).
    5. Uncertainty principle violation — Bohr assumes exact r, v; Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π forbids this.
    6. Classical radiation paradox — Orbiting electron should radiate and spiral in (Maxwell).
    7. No chemical bonding — No orbital overlap, hybridization, MO theory in Bohr model.
    8. Bohr-Bury scheme — 2n² per shell, octet rule; fails for transition metals (Cr, Cu exceptions).
    9. Sommerfeld model — Elliptical orbits, azimuthal quantum number k (→l), relativistic fine structure.

    Key concepts to remember

    • 7 major defects: multi-e⁻ failure, fine structure, Zeeman, Stark, uncertainty, radiation, bonding.
    • Each defect → missing QM concept (Pauli, spin, mₗ, uncertainty, stationary states, VB/MO, Pauli).
    • Fine structure = spin-orbit coupling (Na D-lines 589.0/589.6 nm).
    • Zeeman = B-field splits lines (needs mₗ, spin); Stark = E-field splits lines.
    • Uncertainty principle Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π forbids exact orbits; orbitals (ψ²) replace orbits.
    • Bohr-Bury: 2n² per shell, octet rule; Cr = 3d⁵4s¹, Cu = 3d¹⁰4s¹ (half/filled d stability).
    • Sommerfeld: elliptical orbits, k quantum number (→l), relativistic precession → fine structure.

    Important classifications

    • Defects by missing QM concept: Multi-e⁻ (Pauli), fine structure (spin), Zeeman (mₗ), Stark (orientation), uncertainty (orbitals), radiation (stationary states), bonding (VB/MO).
    • Spectral effects: Fine structure (doublets), Zeeman (splitting in B), Stark (splitting in E).
    • Configuration rules: Bohr-Bury (2n², octet) → Pauli + Aufbau + Hund (modern).

    Common conceptual confusions

    • Bohr's model works for all atoms — NO (fails for multi-electron).
    • Fine structure is the same as Zeeman effect — NO (fine structure = intrinsic spin-orbit; Zeeman = external B field).
    • Bohr-Bury scheme is the modern configuration rule — NO (fails for transition metals; modern = Pauli + Aufbau + Hund).
    • Zeeman and Stark effects are the same — NO (Zeeman = magnetic field, Stark = electric field).
    • Uncertainty principle is just measurement limitation — NO (fundamental property of nature; Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2).
    • Sommerfeld model fully explained fine structure — NO (only partial; spin needed for full explanation).

    7. Write the Bohr-Bury configuration of Fe (Z=26) and explain the anomaly.

    Answers

    1. Seven defects: multi-e⁻ failure, fine structure, Zeeman, Stark, uncertainty, radiation, no bonding.
    2. Multi-electron: screening reduces Z_eff; electron-electron repulsion; exchange energy; correlation.
    3. Fine structure: spin-orbit coupling splits lines (Na D-lines 589.0/589.6 nm).
    4. Normal Zeeman: 3 lines (σ⁻, π, σ⁺) from mₗ; Anomalous: spin adds more components.
    5. Bohr: exact r, v (Δx=0, Δp=0); Uncertainty: Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π forbids exact values.
    6. Bohr-Bury: 2n² per shell, octet rule; Cr = 3d⁵4s¹, Cu = 3d¹⁰4s¹ (half/filled d stability).
    7. Sommerfeld: elliptical orbits, azimuthal quantum number k (→l), relativistic precession.
    8. de Broglie: λ = h/mv; standing wave nλ = 2πr → mvr = nħ. For n=1: λ = h/mv₁; 2πr₁ = 2πa₀ = h/mv₁ ✓.
    9. Uncertainty: Δx = r₁ = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m → Δp ≥ ħ/2Δx = 1.05×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s; v₁ = 2.18×10⁶ m/s → p = mv = 1.99×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s; Δp/p ≈ 50% — orbit not exact.
    10. Cr = 3d⁵4s¹ (half-filled d⁵ exchange energy); Cu = 3d¹⁰4s¹ (filled d¹⁰).
    11. mvr = nħ from nλ = 2πr with λ = h/mv → n(h/mv) = 2πr → mvr = nħ. Defect: assumes classical orbit.
    12. Uncertainty: exact r → Δx=0 → Δp=∞; exact v → Δp=0 → Δx=∞. Standing wave still has finite width.

    PYQ Solutions

    Q: State the defects of Bohr's theory, giving the physical reason for each. (2 marks) Answer: (1) Multi-electron failure: screening, electron-electron repulsion, exchange energy not included. (2) Fine structure: lines are doublets due to spin-orbit coupling (Na D-lines). (3) Zeeman effect: B-field splits lines; needs magnetic quantum number mₗ. (4) Stark effect: E-field splits lines; needs spatial orientation. (5) Uncertainty principle: exact orbit forbidden (Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π). (6) Classical radiation: orbiting electron should radiate and collapse. (7) No chemical bonding: no orbital overlap, hybridization. Keywords: screening, spin-orbit, mₗ, orientation, Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π, radiation, orbital overlap.

    Q: Why does Bohr's theory fail for multi-electron atoms? Use screening and electron–electron repulsion. (3 marks) Answer: In multi-electron atoms, inner electrons screen outer electrons from full nuclear charge (Z_eff < Z). Electrons repel each other (electron-electron repulsion), so energy is not simply -13.6Z²/n². Exchange energy (parallel spins lower energy) and correlation also contribute. Bohr's single-electron formula ignores all these. Keywords: screening (Z_eff < Z), electron-electron repulsion, exchange energy, correlation.

    Q: How does Heisenberg's uncertainty principle destroy the concept of a Bohr orbit? (3 marks) Answer: Bohr orbit assumes electron has exact position (radius r) and exact momentum (velocity v), so Δx = 0, Δp = 0, giving Δx·Δp = 0. Heisenberg's principle states Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π (≈ ħ/2), so exact position and momentum cannot be simultaneously known. Bohr's definite circular orbit is impossible; electrons exist as probability clouds (orbitals, ψ²). Keywords: exact r and v, Δx=0 Δp=0, Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π, forbidden, orbitals (ψ²).

    Q: Distinguish the Zeeman and Stark effects and explain why Bohr's model cannot explain them. (3 marks) Answer: Zeeman: magnetic field splits spectral lines (normal: 3 lines from mₗ = -l...+l; anomalous: spin adds more). Stark: electric field splits lines (linear for degenerate, quadratic otherwise). Bohr model has no magnetic quantum number mₗ and no spatial orientation quantum number, so it cannot account for splitting by external fields. Keywords: Zeeman = B field, mₗ; Stark = E field, orientation; Bohr lacks mₗ and orientation.

    Q: Describe the modifications by Sommerfeld and Schrödinger that corrected Bohr's model. (5 marks) Answer: Sommerfeld (1915): elliptical orbits with azimuthal quantum number k (later l); relativistic precession explains fine structure partially. Schrödinger (1926): wave equation; electrons as orbitals (ψ²) not orbits; quantum numbers n, l, mₗ, s; stationary states = eigenstates; uncertainty principle built-in; chemical bonding via orbital overlap. Sommerfeld's k → l; Schrödinger's n, l, mₗ, s complete the quantum number set. Keywords: elliptical orbits, k→l, relativistic fine structure, wave equation, orbitals ψ², n/l/mₗ/s, stationary states, uncertainty built-in, orbital overlap.

    Q: State the Bohr–Bury scheme with the 2n² and octet rules. (2 marks) Answer: Maximum electrons in nth shell = 2n² (K=2, L=8, M=18, N=32). Octet rule: outermost shell holds max 8 electrons (except K shell: 2). Filling order: shells sequentially K, L, M, N. Keywords: 2n², octet rule, sequential filling.

    Q: Write the Bohr–Bury configuration of calcium (Z = 20) and point out where the scheme needs 4s before 3d. (3 marks) Answer: Ca: K=2, L=8, M=8, N=2 (2,8,8,2). Actual: [Ar]4s². Bohr-Bury fills M shell (3d) before N (4s), but 4s has lower energy (n+l = 4) than 3d (n+l = 5), so 4s fills first. Bohr-Bury misses this (n+l) rule. Keywords: 2,8,8,2; 4s before 3d; n+l rule (4+0 < 3+2).

    Q: Why does the scheme fail for chromium and copper? (2 marks) Answer: Cr (Z=24): Bohr-Bury gives 2,8,13,1; actual [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. Cu (Z=29): Bohr-Bury gives 2,8,18,1; actual [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Reason: exactly half-filled (d⁵) and fully-filled (d¹⁰) subshells gain extra stability from exchange energy and symmetry. Keywords: half-filled d⁵, fully-filled d¹⁰, exchange energy, symmetry, Cr 3d⁵4s¹, Cu 3d¹⁰4s¹.

    Q: Show how the standing-wave condition nλ = 2πr leads to Bohr's quantisation of angular momentum. (3 marks) Answer: de Broglie: λ = h/mv. Standing wave around orbit: nλ = 2πr. Substitute λ: n(h/mv) = 2πr → mvr = n·h/2π = nħ. This is Bohr's quantisation condition mvr = nħ. Keywords: λ = h/mv, nλ = 2πr, mvr = nħ, standing wave.

    Q: Verify numerically that exactly one de Broglie wavelength fits the first Bohr orbit of hydrogen. (3 marks) Answer: n=1: r₁ = a₀ = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m. Circumference = 2πr₁ = 3.32×10⁻¹⁰ m. v₁ = 2.18×10⁶ m/s. λ = h/mv = 6.626×10⁻³⁴/(9.11×10⁻³¹×2.18×10⁶) = 3.32×10⁻¹⁰ m. Matches circumference exactly → one wavelength fits. Keywords: a₀ = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m, 2πr = 3.32×10⁻¹⁰ m, λ = h/mv = 3.32×10⁻¹⁰ m, match.

    Q: Why was mvr = nh/2π considered a defect of the theory? (2 marks) Answer: The quantisation condition was postulated ad hoc with no theoretical justification in Bohr's model. It works for hydrogen but has no basis in classical mechanics and fails for multi-electron atoms. In quantum mechanics, angular momentum is √[l(l+1)]ħ, not nħ, and arises from boundary conditions on the wave function, not a postulated orbit condition. Keywords: ad hoc, no classical basis, QM gives √[l(l+1)]ħ, not nħ, boundary conditions.

    Q: How does the uncertainty principle still limit the standing-wave picture? (3 marks) Answer: The standing wave still assumes a definite orbit radius (Δx ≈ r). For n=1, r₁ = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m, so Δx ≈ 5.3×10⁻¹¹ m. Uncertainty principle gives Δp ≥ ħ/2Δx = 1.05×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s. Actual momentum p = mv₁ = 1.99×10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s. Relative uncertainty Δp/p ≈ 53% — the electron's momentum is not well-defined, so the standing wave still has a spread in momentum and position; it is not a precise orbit. Keywords: Δx ≈ r₁, Δp ≥ ħ/2Δx, Δp/p ≈ 50%, standing wave still has momentum spread.

Confusion

  • ❌ Bohr's model works for all atoms. ✅ Fails for multi-electron atoms (He, Li, etc.).
  • ❌ Fine structure is the same as Zeeman effect. ✅ Fine structure = intrinsic spin-orbit splitting; Zeeman = external B-field splitting.
  • ❌ Bohr-Bury scheme is the modern electron configuration rule. ✅ Fails for transition metals (4s before 3d, Cr/Cu exceptions); modern = Pauli + Aufbau + Hund.
  • ❌ Zeeman effect and Stark effect are the same. ✅ Zeeman = magnetic field (B); Stark = electric field (E).
  • ❌ Heisenberg's uncertainty principle is a limitation of measurement instruments. ✅ Fundamental property of nature; Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2 always.
  • ❌ Sommerfeld model fully explained fine structure. ✅ Only partial; spin needed for full explanation.
  • ❌ mvr = nħ is the exact quantum mechanical angular momentum. ✅ QM gives √[l(l+1)]ħ; only Bohr's circular orbits give nħ.
  • ❌ de Broglie waves justify Bohr's orbits completely. ✅ Uncertainty principle still gives Δp/p ≈ 50% for n=1; standing wave is not a precise orbit.
  • ❌ Bohr's postulate of no radiation has classical justification. ✅ Ad hoc; Maxwell says accelerating charge radiates. QM: stationary states = eigenstates, no dipole oscillation.
