Notes

  • 1. Meaning

  • Solving the Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom yields a set of numbers that completely describe the state of an electron. These are the quantum numbers — effectively the 'address' of an electron in an atom. Four numbers are needed: principal (n), azimuthal or angular-momentum (l), magnetic (m or mₗ) and spin (s or mₛ).

  • 2. Principal quantum number (n)

  • Determines the shell (energy level) and, in effect, the size: r ∝ n². Values n = 1, 2, 3, … ∞; the shell letters are K, L, M, N …; maximum capacity of a shell = 2n² electrons (n = 1 → 2, n = 2 → 8, n = 3 → 18, n = 4 → 32). It corresponds to Bohr's orbit number but is now a quantum label, not a path.

  • 3. Azimuthal quantum number (l)

  • Determines the subshell and the shape of the orbital. Values l = 0, 1, 2, 3 … (n − 1), i.e. (n − 1) subshells per shell; the letters are s (l = 0), p (1), d (2), f (3). Orbital angular momentum = l(l+1)​2πh​. Within a shell the energies follow the order s < p < d < f, and a subshell holds 2(2l + 1) electrons (s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14).

  • 4. Magnetic quantum number (mₗ)

  • Determines the orientation of the orbital in space (equivalently the component of angular momentum along a chosen axis). Values run from −l through 0 to +l, i.e. (2l + 1) values: one s orbital, three p orbitals (px, py, pz), five d orbitals, seven f orbitals. All orbitals of a subshell are degenerate (equal energy) in a free atom and split in a magnetic field — the origin of the Zeeman effect.

  • 5. Spin quantum number (mₛ)

  • Describes the intrinsic spin of the electron; values +1/2 (↑) or −1/2 (↓). Introduced from the Stern–Gerlach experiment and the doublet structure of spectra (Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit, 1925). Because of it each orbital holds at most two electrons, giving the total capacities 2, 6, 10, 14 and 2n² per shell.

  • 6. Orbitals, subshells and shells at a glance

  • n = 1: 1s (2e⁻, 1 orbital). n = 2: 2s + 2p (8e⁻, 4 orbitals). n = 3: 3s + 3p + 3d (18e⁻, 9 orbitals). n = 4: 4s + 4p + 4d + 4f (32e⁻, 16 orbitals). In general, orbitals in a shell = n², electrons = 2n², and an orbital is written n l (e.g. 3p).

  • 7. Applications

  • • They define electronic configuration, the order of filling, and hence the position of an element in the periodic table and its block (s, p, d, f).
  • • They predict magnetic behaviour: unpaired electrons (Hund's rule follows from spin) give paramagnetism; fully paired species are diamagnetic.
  • • They explain spectral fine structure, the Zeeman and Stark effects, spin-orbit coupling, MRI/NMR (nuclear spin), lasers and selection rules.
  • • They let us test validity of a given set (e.g. l = n is forbidden) and count orbitals, unpaired electrons and quantum states in a subshell.

  • 8. Limitations

  • Quantum numbers describe one electron at a time (not a pair or an atom as a whole); they do not give an electron's position, only the probability; the exact energies for multi-electron atoms need Z_eff and electron–electron interaction; mₛ comes from relativistic Dirac theory and is not contained in the simple Schrödinger solution; and spin–orbit coupling requires the total angular-momentum quantum number J, which is beyond Bohr's picture.

  • Where the quantum numbers come from mathematically and the counting metrics (orbitals, capacities, designations) in one table.

  • Origins

  • • n, l and m arise as separation constants when the Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom is solved in spherical coordinates — the radial equation gives n, the angular equations give l and m. They are not assumptions; they are forced by the boundary conditions.
  • • mₛ does not come from the Schrödinger equation: it enters through the relativistic Dirac equation (1928) and was inferred earlier (1925, Uhlenbeck–Goudsmit) from the Stern–Gerlach experiment and spectral doublets.

  • The metrics table

  • Shell n: orbitals n², electrons 2n² (K 1/2, L 4/8, M 9/18, N 16/32). Subshell l: orbitals 2l + 1 (s 1, p 3, d 5, f 7), electrons 2(2l + 1) (s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14). Nodes: radial n − l − 1, angular l, total n − 1. Orbital designation nl (1s, 2p, 3d, 4f).

  • Energy order within a shell

  • For hydrogen all subshells of a shell are degenerate (E depends only on n). In multi-electron atoms penetration makes Ens​<Enp​<End​<Enf​, and across shells the (n + l) Madelung order governs filling — the two facts the Bohr–Bury scheme could not express.

  • Counting patterns (exam drills)

  • • Electrons with n = 4, l = 2 → 4d: 2(2·2+1) = 10.
  • • Orbitals with n = 3 → 9 = 3² (1 s + 3 p + 5 d).
  • • Electrons with n = 3 and m = 0 → 6 (one each in 3s, 3p, 3d, doubled by spin).
  • • Unpaired electrons in a subshell from Hund's rule → paramagnetism; fully paired → diamagnetism.
  • How (n, l, m) triples map onto orbital designations, and what the spin quantum number adds — including the box-and-arrow notation used in configuration questions.

  • The designation matrix

  • • l = 0 → s, 1 orbital (m = 0): 1s, 2s, 3s, 4s …
  • • l = 1 → p, 3 orbitals (m = −1, 0, +1): 2p, 3p, 4p … each written px, py, pz.
  • • l = 2 → d, 5 orbitals (m = −2 … +2): 3d, 4d … (dxy, dyz, dxz, dx²−y², dz²).
  • • l = 3 → f, 7 orbitals (m = −3 … +3): 4f, 5f …
  • The full designation combines n and the subshell letter (3p) or, fully, (n, l, m) — e.g. (3, 1, 0) is the 3pz orbital.

  • The spin quantum number

  • mₛ = +½ (↑) or −½ (↓). Electron spin is an intrinsic angular momentum with magnitude √(3/4)·h/2π = √3/2 ħ — it is not literally a spinning ball, but the name survives. Evidence: Stern–Gerlach (silver atoms split into two beams) and doublet structure of alkali spectra. With spin, each orbital (defined by n, l, m) holds exactly two electrons of opposite spin — Pauli's exclusion principle.

  • Box-and-arrow notation (configuration drawing)

  • Draw one box per orbital; fill per Aufbau/Pauli/Hund. Nitrogen: 2p ↑ ↑ ↑ (three boxes, parallel arrows); oxygen: 2p ↑↓ ↑ ↑. The notation encodes unpaired-electron counts, hence magnetic behaviour: N has 3 unpaired (paramagnetic), Ne has 0 (diamagnetic).

  • Magnetic behaviour from the matrix

  • Count unpaired electrons u: spin-only magnetic moment μ = √(u(u + 2)) BM. Fe³⁺ (3d⁵): u = 5 → μ ≈ 5.92 BM; Cu²⁺ (3d⁹): u = 1 → 1.73 BM; Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰): u = 0 → diamagnetic.
  • The four quantum numbers through one concrete example — the 2p electron of carbon — the fastest route to the 'write the quantum numbers' question. Definitions and the full metric table are in the main note and the metrics tab.

  • The example worked through

  • Carbon (Z = 6): 1s² 2s² 2p². Take a 2p electron: n = 2 (L shell, second energy level); l = 1 (p subshell, dumbbell shape); m = −1, 0 or +1 (which of px, py, pz the orbital is); mₛ = +½ or −½ (spin). Hund's rule puts the two 2p electrons in different p orbitals with parallel spins, so a valid full set for the first is n = 2, l = 1, m = −1, mₛ = +½ and for the second n = 2, l = 1, m = 0, mₛ = +½.

  • What each number fixes

  • • n: shell, size and energy (r ∝ n²; capacity 2n²).
  • • l: subshell and shape (s spherical, p dumbbell, d clover, f complex); angular momentum √(l(l+1))·h/2π; permitted values 0 … n − 1.
  • • m: orientation in space; 2l + 1 values; explains Zeeman splitting.
  • • mₛ: spin ±½; Pauli exclusion follows — no two electrons share all four numbers.

  • The validity ladder (check in this order)

  • n ≥ 1 → l ≤ n − 1 → |m| ≤ l → mₛ = ±½. So (n = 2, l = 2) is invalid (l = n), (n = 3, l = 2, m = −3) is invalid (|m| > l), and (n = 2, l = 1, m = 0, mₛ = ±½) is valid.

  • Degeneracy and pairing

  • The three p orbitals are degenerate in a free atom; a magnetic field splits them (Zeeman). Filling follows Hund's rule: singly with parallel spins first — the origin of carbon's two unpaired 2p electrons and hence its divalent/paramagnetic behaviour.

Confusion

  • ❌ n determines shape and l determines size. ✅ n → size/energy (shell); l → shape (subshell).
  • ❌ l can equal n. ✅ l = 0 to n − 1, so 1s has l = 0 and 2p has l = 1.
  • ❌ mₗ is the spin quantum number. ✅ mₗ gives orientation; spin is mₛ = ±1/2.
  • ❌ A p subshell holds 3 electrons. ✅ It has 2l + 1 = 3 orbitals, so it holds up to 6 electrons.
  • ❌ Orbital angular momentum is l(h/2π). ✅ It is √(l(l+1))h/2π.
  • ❌ mₛ comes from solving the Schrödinger equation. ✅ Only n, l, m do; spin needs the Dirac equation.
  • ❌ 2n² counts orbitals. ✅ It counts electrons; orbitals in a shell number n².
  • ❌ All subshells of a shell have equal energy in every atom. ✅ True only for hydrogen; multi-electron atoms order them by penetration.
  • ❌ Spin means the electron literally spins on its axis. ✅ Spin is an intrinsic quantum property; the classical picture is only an analogy.
  • ❌ The five d orbitals have five different energies in a free atom. ✅ They are degenerate; ligand or magnetic fields split them.
  • ❌ A full designation needs only n and l. ✅ A complete state needs all four numbers (n, l, m, mₛ).
  • ❌ mₛ determines the orbital orientation. ✅ mₗ gives orientation; mₛ gives spin.
  • ❌ l can take the value n. ✅ l runs 0 to n − 1.
  • ❌ Two 2p electrons of carbon must have opposite spins. ✅ Hund's rule: parallel spins in different degenerate orbitals first.

Universal Facts

  • s, p, d, f stand for sharp, principal, diffuse, fundamental — the old spectroscopic series names.
  • Shell capacities: K 2, L 8, M 18, N 32 (2n²); subshell capacities: s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14.
  • Spin was proposed in 1925 (Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit) after the Stern–Gerlach experiment (1922).
  • The 4f subshell (l = 3) holds 14 electrons — the width of the lanthanide series.
  • Stern–Gerlach (1922) split a silver atomic beam into two — the first spin evidence.
  • Degeneracy of the n = 2 level in hydrogen: 4 orbitals (2s + three 2p).
  • The five d orbitals carry the spectroscopic labels dxy, dyz, dxz, dx²−y², dz².
  • Silver's single unpaired 5s electron produced exactly two Stern–Gerlach beams.
  • Pauli stated his exclusion principle in 1925, before spin was formally introduced.
  • Carbon's two unpaired 2p electrons make atomic carbon paramagnetic.
  • The letters s, p, d, f come from the old spectroscopic terms sharp, principal, diffuse, fundamental.
  • Pauli's exclusion principle is the reason any orbital holds at most two electrons.

Examples

  • Problem: Maximum number of electrons with n = 4 and l = 2? Solution: 4d subshell: 2(2l + 1) = 2(5) = 10 electrons.
  • Problem: How many electrons in an atom can have n = 3, m = 0? Solution: For n = 3: 3s (m = 0 only), 3p (m = 0 available), 3d (m = 0 available) → 1 + 2 + 2 = 6 electrons.
  • Problem: Count orbitals with n = 4, l = 3 and the electrons they hold. Solution: 2l + 1 = 7 orbitals (4f), holding 14 electrons; nodes: radial 0, angular 3.
  • Problem: Give the designation of an electron with (n, l, m, mₛ) = (4, 2, −1, +½). Solution: n = 4, l = 2 → 4d; m = −1 selects one of the five d orbitals; mₛ = +½ — an up-spin electron in a 4d orbital.
  • Problem: How many electrons in an atom can have n = 3 and m = 0? Solution: 3s (1 orbital, m = 0), 3p (one of three orbitals has m = 0), 3d (one of five) → 1 + 2 + 2 = 6 electrons.

Important Notes

  • Remember the two counting rules: 2l + 1 orbitals per subshell and 2n² electrons per shell.
  • Check validity sets in the order n ≥ 1, l ≤ n − 1, |m| ≤ l, mₛ = ±1/2 — a favourite one-mark question.
  • Memorise the three counting numbers: 2l + 1 (orbitals), 2(2l + 1) (subshell electrons), 2n² (shell electrons).
  • Attribute spin to Dirac/Stern–Gerlach, not to Schrödinger.
  • Use box notation whenever a configuration question asks about unpaired electrons or magnetism.
  • Pauli's principle is stated in terms of the four quantum numbers — quote all four.
  • Answer 'write the quantum numbers' questions by naming the element and configuration first, then the set per electron.
  • Check validity with the ladder n → l → m → mₛ.
