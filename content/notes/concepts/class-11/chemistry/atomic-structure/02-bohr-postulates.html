Notes

  • 1. Meaning — why a new model was needed

  • Rutherford's nuclear model could not explain (i) why the revolving electron does not fall into the nucleus and (ii) why hydrogen gives discrete spectral lines. In 1913 Niels Bohr combined classical mechanics with Planck's quantum theory for the hydrogen atom and other one-electron species (He⁺, Li²⁺).

  • 2. Postulates of Bohr's model (state them in order)

  • • Stationary orbits: the electron revolves only in certain permitted circular orbits (stationary states) of definite energy and does not radiate while in such an orbit.
  • • Quantisation of angular momentum: only orbits with mvr = nh/2π are allowed, n = 1, 2, 3, … (n = principal quantum number).
  • • Frequency condition: energy is absorbed or emitted only during a jump between orbits, hν = |E₂ − E₁| (emission on falling, absorption on rising).
  • • Force condition: the Coulomb attraction provides the centripetal force, mv²/r = kZe²/r² — used with the quantisation rule to find r and E.

  • 3. Results derived from the postulates

  • • Orbit radius: rn​=0.529Zn2​ Å; the first Bohr orbit of hydrogen (0.529 Å = 52.9 pm) is the Bohr radius.
  • • Orbital speed: vn​=2.18×106nZ​ m s⁻¹ (for H, ground state = c/137, so the motion is non-relativistic).
  • • Energy: En​=−13.6n2Z2​ eV = −2.18×10−18n2Z2​ J, with KE=−En​ and PE=2En​; total energy is negative because the zero of energy is at infinite separation.
  • • Rydberg constant follows from the model: R=h3c2π2me4​=1.097×107 m⁻¹.

  • 4. Applications

  • • Hydrogen spectrum: gives the wave number of every line, νˉ=RZ2(n12​1​−n22​1​), and identifies the Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett and Pfund series.
  • • Ionisation energy/enthalpy: IE of H = 13.6 eV = 1312 kJ mol⁻¹; for He⁺ (Z = 2) it is 54.4 eV, and so on for Li²⁺, Be³⁺.
  • • Atomic size and stability: explains radii of the order 10⁻¹⁰ m growing as n², and shows why the atom does not collapse — stationary orbits do not radiate.
  • • Spectra of hydrogen-like ions (He⁺, Li²⁺) and energy-level diagrams still used in spectroscopy and astrophysics for highly ionised plasmas.

  • 5. Successes and the edge of validity

  • Successes: atomic stability, hydrogen line spectrum and its series limits, the Rydberg constant, ionisation energy and orbital radii. It stops working for more than one electron and cannot explain fine structure, line intensities or the Zeeman and Stark effects (see 'Defects of Bohr's Theory').
  • Fact. Bohr model: electron in fixed circular orbits; angular momentum mvr = nh/2π; energy En = −13.6 eV·Z²/n² (for H, Z=1).
  • Fact. Rydberg/Balmer series: 1/λ = R(H)(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²). Absorption/emission = ΔE = hν between levels.
  • Fact. Four quantum numbers: n (shell), l (subshell, 0…n−1), mₗ (orientation, −l…+l), mₛ (spin ±½). Orbitals: s (spherical), p (dumbbell).
  • Fact. Filling order (Aufbau, n+l rule): 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d…; Hund's rule: maximise unpaired spin before pairing; Pauli: no two electrons share all four quantum numbers.

  • The four workhorse formulas

  • Radius: rn​=0.529Zn2​ Å. Speed: vn​=2.18×106nZ​ m s⁻¹. Energy: En​=−13.6n2Z2​ eV =−1312n2Z2​ kJ mol⁻¹. Wave number: νˉ=RZ2(n12​1​−n22​1​), R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹.

  • Dependences to memorise

  • • r ∝ n²/Z (larger orbit for larger n, smaller for larger Z).
  • • v ∝ Z/n (fastest in the innermost orbit of the heaviest one-electron ion).
  • • E ∝ −Z²/n² (energy spacing shrinks as 1/n³ in difference form: ΔE ≈ 2 × 13.6 Z²/n³ for adjacent high levels).
  • • λ of a line ∝ n³/(Z²) roughly for adjacent-level transitions.

  • Standard numerical patterns

  • • Radius of the nth orbit of a one-electron ion: substitute n and Z (He⁺ Z = 2, Li²⁺ Z = 3).
  • • Ionisation energy: IE = 13.6 Z²/n² eV (from the level n to ∞). For H ground state 13.6 eV = 1312 kJ mol⁻¹; for He⁺ (n = 1, Z = 2) 54.4 eV.
  • • Excitation energy: ΔE = 13.6 Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) eV; convert with λ(nm) = 1240/ΔE(eV).
  • • Velocity ratio: v(n₁, Z₁)/v(n₂, Z₂) = (Z₁/n₁)/(Z₂/n₂).

  • Two results worth memorising

  • • The ground-state speed of the H electron is c/137 ≈ 2.19 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ — non-relativistic, validating the model.
  • • The energy of the nth level approaches 0 as n → ∞, and the level spacing → 0: that is why spectral lines crowd toward the series limit.
  • The four postulates, stated cleanly with the physical meaning of each — the fastest way to secure full marks on the 'state the postulates' question. Derivations and applications are in the main note and the companion application tab.

  • Postulate 1 — stationary orbits

  • The electron in a hydrogen atom revolves only in certain fixed circular orbits around the nucleus. While in such an orbit it does not emit or absorb energy, although it is accelerating. Each orbit is a stationary state with a definite energy. Meaning: this postulate suspends classical electrodynamics inside the atom.

  • Postulate 2 — quantisation of angular momentum

  • Only those orbits are permitted for which the angular momentum is an integral multiple of h/2π: mvr=2πnh​, n = 1, 2, 3, … The integer n is the principal quantum number. Meaning: the allowed orbits are discrete because the angular momentum comes in packets of ħ.

  • Postulate 3 — frequency (energy) condition

  • Energy is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between orbits: hν=E2​−E1​ (emission when it falls to a lower level, absorption when it is raised). Meaning: this is exactly Planck's quantum hypothesis applied to atoms, and it is what produces sharp spectral lines.

  • Postulate 4 — force condition

  • The Coulomb attraction of the nucleus provides the centripetal force for the circular motion: rmv2​=r2kZe2​. Meaning: the mechanics is classical; only the allowed states are quantum — a hybrid (semi-classical) model.

  • Vocabulary attached to the postulates

  • Ground state: lowest energy orbit (n = 1). Excited state: any higher orbit. Ionisation: complete removal of the electron (n = ∞). Binding energy: −Eₙ, the energy holding the electron. All definitions follow directly from the postulates.
  • How Bohr's frequency condition produces the two kinds of line spectra, why absorption lines and emission lines fall at the same wavelengths, and where each spectrum is used. Complements the main postulates tab.

  • Emission spectrum (bright lines)

  • Atoms are excited — by a flame, discharge tube, or collision — to higher levels n₂. The electron falls back to a lower level n₁ and emits one photon per jump with hν=E2​−E1​. The spectrum is a set of bright lines on a dark background: each line = one allowed transition. In a discharge tube both atoms and electrons collide, so many levels are populated and several series appear at once.

  • Absorption spectrum (dark lines)

  • Cool gas (ground-state atoms) is placed in front of a continuous white-light source. The atoms absorb only those photons whose energy exactly equals a level gap from the populated (usually ground) state: the transmitted light is missing those wavelengths — dark lines on a bright continuum. Fraunhofer's solar lines are exactly this: the cool solar atmosphere absorbing against the hot photosphere.

  • Why the lines coincide

  • The same energy difference E₂ − E₁ is involved in both directions, so absorption and emission lines fall at identical wavelengths (Kirchhoff's law). The pattern differs, though: absorption requires the lower level to be populated, so absorption lines start mainly from the ground state (only the Lyman series in cold hydrogen gas), while emission can populate every series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen …).

  • Energy-level diagram language

  • Vertical arrows up = absorption; arrows down = emission. The ground level is the zero-height reference; higher levels crowd together as n grows; the n = ∞ limit is the ionisation continuum. Every allowed jump must obey the selection Δn = any (Bohr) — quantum mechanics later restricts it by Δl = ±1.

  • Applications

  • • Chemical analysis: every element's line pattern is unique → qualitative and quantitative spectroscopy (flame tests, AAS, ICP).
  • • Astronomy: composition, temperature and Doppler velocity of stars from line positions and shifts; redshift of galaxies.
  • • Lasers: population inversion and stimulated emission between discrete levels (He–Ne 632.8 nm).
  • • Neon signs, fireworks and street lamps: line colours of excited atoms (Ne red-orange, Na yellow D-line 589 nm).
  • Why Bohr energies are negative, what the zero of energy means, and the three energy relations (E, KE, PE) that follow from the force condition.

  • Where the negative sign comes from

  • Bohr takes the zero of energy at infinite separation — the electron just free of the atom, at rest. Any bound orbit is below that reference, so all bound-state energies are negative: En​=−13.6Z2/n2 eV. The magnitude |E| is the binding energy — exactly the energy that must be supplied to free the electron (ionisation).

  • The three relations

  • From the Coulomb force condition, rmv2​=r2kZe2​ gives KE=2rkZe2​ and PE=−rkZe2​. Therefore: KE = −E and PE = 2E = −2|E|, with E = KE + PE. Check for hydrogen ground state: E = −13.6 eV, KE = +13.6 eV, PE = −27.2 eV. As n grows: E → 0⁻, KE → 0, PE → 0⁻ (a loosely bound, slow electron).

  • Reading the numbers

  • • n = 1: E = −13.6 eV (most tightly bound, v = c/137).
  • • n = 2: E = −3.4 eV; n = 3: −1.51 eV; n = 4: −0.85 eV — level differences shrink as 1/n², hence the converging spectral series.
  • • n = ∞: E = 0 — ionisation; the electron is free.

  • Why the sign matters in chemistry

  • Ionisation energy = |Eₙ| (positive number supplied); electron affinity and bond energies follow the same convention (energy released on going to a bound state is positive when reported as 'released'). Mixing the two sign conventions is the standard source of error — always state which convention you use.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'Stationary orbit' means the electron is at rest. ✅ The electron moves, but the orbit is stationary in energy — it does not radiate.
  • ❌ Bohr orbits and orbitals are the same. ✅ Orbits are fixed 2D circular paths of definite radius/energy; orbitals are 3D probability regions from the Schrödinger equation.
  • ❌ A bound electron has positive energy. ✅ Its energy is negative (zero at infinite separation).
  • ❌ Bohr's model works for all atoms. ✅ Only for one-electron species: H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺.
  • ❌ The electron radiates while revolving in an allowed orbit. ✅ Radiation occurs only during a jump.
  • ❌ rₙ ∝ n/Z. ✅ rₙ = 0.529 n²/Z Å — the n-dependence is squared.
  • ❌ Ionisation energy from level n is 13.6 Z²/n² eV multiplied by n. ✅ It is exactly 13.6 Z²/n² eV (removal from that level to ∞).
  • ❌ Speed increases with n. ✅ v decreases as 1/n; outer electrons move slower.
  • ❌ Stationary orbit means the electron is at rest. ✅ It revolves, but the orbit is stationary in energy (non-radiating).
  • ❌ Bohr's postulates were derived from classical mechanics. ✅ They were imposed; only the force condition is classical.
  • ❌ The frequency condition applies to the electron's own motion. ✅ It concerns the emitted/absorbed photon only.
  • ❌ Absorption lines are produced when atoms emit energy. ✅ They appear when atoms remove matching photons from a continuous beam.
  • ❌ Absorption and emission lines occur at different wavelengths. ✅ They coincide; only the pattern/populated series differ.
  • ❌ A ground-state hydrogen atom can emit any Balmer line. ✅ Emission requires prior excitation; a ground-state atom can only absorb (Lyman lines).
  • ❌ Negative energy means the calculation is wrong. ✅ It is a bound state referenced to infinite separation.
  • ❌ KE is also negative. ✅ KE = −E is positive (½mv²); only total E and PE are negative.
  • ❌ The electron loses mass when bound. ✅ Mass–energy differences are negligible here (13.6 eV vs 511 keV rest mass).

Universal Facts

  • Bohr radius a₀ = 0.529 Å = 52.9 pm; ground-state speed of the H electron = 2.18 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ = c/137.
  • Ionisation energy of H = 13.6 eV = 1312 kJ mol⁻¹; ionisation energy of He⁺ = 54.4 eV.
  • Ground-state energy of hydrogen = −13.6 eV, and level spacing decreases as 1/n² increases.
  • Bohr model: electron in fixed circular orbits; angular momentum mvr = nh/2π; energy En = −13.6 eV·Z²/n² (for H, Z=1).
  • Rydberg/Balmer series: 1/λ = R(H)(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²). Absorption/emission = ΔE = hν between levels.
  • Four quantum numbers: n (shell), l (subshell, 0…n−1), mₗ (orientation, −l…+l), mₛ (spin ±½). Orbitals: s (spherical), p (dumbbell).
  • r₁(H) = 0.529 Å; r₁(He⁺) = 0.265 Å; r₁(Li²⁺) = 0.176 Å — all scale as 1/Z.
  • IE(H) = 13.6 eV; IE(He⁺) = 54.4 eV; IE(Li²⁺) = 122.4 eV — the Z² scaling in action.
  • v₁(H) = c/137 = 2.19 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹.
  • Bohr's model was published in 1913 — one year after Bohr joined Rutherford in Manchester.
  • The postulates apply strictly to one-electron species: H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺.
  • Postulate 2 was later derived by de Broglie (standing matter waves) — turning an assumption into a consequence.
  • The Na D-line at 589 nm is the classic emission/absorption pair — street lamps and the sodium flame test share it.
  • Fraunhofer catalogued over 570 solar absorption lines in 1814, decades before Bohr explained them.
  • Neon signs glow red-orange from Ne emission lines — pure line spectra in commercial use.
  • Hydrogen ground state: E = −13.6 eV, KE = +13.6 eV, PE = −27.2 eV.
  • The virial theorem for Coulomb systems (2KE + PE = 0) is the general reason behind PE = 2E.
  • IE of hydrogen in molar units is 1312 kJ mol⁻¹ — the value measured calorimetrically.

Examples

  • Problem: Radius and energy of the electron in the n = 2 orbit of Li²⁺. Solution: r₂ = 0.529 × 2²/3 = 0.705 Å = 70.5 pm; E₂ = −13.6 × 3²/2² = −30.6 eV.
  • Problem: Energy to excite H from n = 1 to n = 3? Solution: ΔE = 13.6(1 − 1/9) = 12.09 eV; λ = 1240/12.09 ≈ 102.6 nm (Lyman series).
  • Problem: Energy to excite He⁺ from n = 1 to n = 2. Solution: ΔE = 13.6 × 2² × (1 − 1/4) = 13.6 × 4 × 0.75 = 40.8 eV — three times the corresponding H value, because of the Z² factor.
  • Problem: Why do the postulates make the atom stable? Solution: in a stationary orbit (postulate 1) the electron accelerates without radiating, so it neither loses energy nor spirals in — stability is postulated, not explained classically.
  • Problem: Which line does a hydrogen atom absorb when illuminated with 102.6 nm light? Solution: ΔE = 1240/102.6 = 12.09 eV = 13.6(1 − 1/n₂²) → n₂ = 3: the Lyman-β absorption line (1 → 3).
  • Problem: Photon energy to move H from n = 2 to n = ∞? Solution: 0 − (−3.4) = 3.4 eV — the second ionisation step of the excited atom; the photon must supply at least 3.4 eV (λ ≈ 364.6 nm, the Balmer limit).

Important Notes

  • Quote all four postulates; the marks sit on 'stationary orbit', 'mvr = nh/2π' and 'hν = E₂ − E₁'.
  • Negative total energy signals a bound state — always mention that the zero of energy is taken at infinite separation.
  • Keep Z = 1 for hydrogen: forgetting Z is the commonest numerical error in rn, vn and En.
  • Never forget Z in r, v, E for ions — substitute Z = 2, 3, 4 explicitly.
  • Negative E means a bound electron; report ionisation energy as a positive number.
  • Quote the four postulates in order; examiners look for 'stationary orbit', 'mvr = nh/2π' and 'hν = E₂ − E₁' verbatim.
  • Say 'one-electron species' when quoting applicability — the commonest precision slip.
  • Draw the energy-level diagram with arrows up (absorption) and down (emission) — it is nearly a guaranteed mark.
  • State the population condition for absorption lines (lower level must be occupied).
  • Always define the zero of energy (infinite separation) when explaining negative energies.
  • Report ionisation energy as a positive magnitude; keep Eₙ negative.
