Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Quantisation Saves the Atom

    In 1913 Niels Bohr repaired Rutherford's model by adding one idea: the electron's angular momentum is quantised. Only certain orbits exist, no radiation is emitted inside them, and light appears only when the electron jumps between orbits. The model works exactly for hydrogen and one-electron ions (He⁺, Li²⁺).

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The postulates (state in order — NEB 3m)

    1. Stationary orbits: the electron revolves only in certain permitted circular orbits (stationary states) of fixed energy and does not radiate while in one.
    2. Quantisation of angular momentum: only orbits satisfying mvr = nh/2π (n = 1, 2, 3, …) are allowed.
    3. Frequency condition: radiation is absorbed or emitted only during a jump: hν = |E₂ − E₁| (emission on falling, absorption on rising).
    4. Force condition: Coulomb attraction supplies the centripetal force, mv²/r = kZe²/r² — combined with postulate 2 to derive r and E.

    2.2 The derived results (memorise the three)

    QuantityFormulaHydrogen ground state
    Orbit radiusrₙ = 0.529 n²/Z Å0.529 Å = 52.9 pm (Bohr radius)
    Orbital speedvₙ = 2.18 × 10⁶ Z/n m s⁻¹c/137 (non-relativistic)
    EnergyEₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² eV = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ Z²/n² J−13.6 eV = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J

    KE = −Eₙ and PE = 2Eₙ; total energy is negative because the zero is taken at infinite separation (a bound electron has less energy than a free one).

    2.3 Worked numericals

    Q (NEB 2022, 3m): Using Eₙ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸/n² J, calculate the energy of the electron in the second orbit of hydrogen.

    • E₂ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸/(2)² = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸/4 = −5.45 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

    Q (NEB-style): Calculate the radius of the third Bohr orbit of He⁺ (Z = 2).

    • r₃ = 0.529 × 9/2 = 2.38 Å

    Q (NEB-style): Find the ionisation energy of He⁺ (Z = 2).

    • IE = 13.6 × Z² = 13.6 × 4 = 54.4 eV

    2.4 Applications

    1. Hydrogen spectrum: wavenumber ν̄ = RZ²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ — identifies the Lyman (UV), Balmer (visible), Paschen, Brackett and Pfund (IR) series.
    2. Ionisation energy: H = 13.6 eV = 1312 kJ mol⁻¹; He⁺ = 54.4 eV; Li²⁺ = 122.4 eV (scales as Z²).
    3. Atomic stability: stationary orbits do not radiate — the collapse problem is solved.
    4. Energy-level diagrams still used in spectroscopy, lasers and astrophysics (highly ionised plasmas).

    2.5 Validity and limits

    The model succeeds for hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions but fails for multi-electron atoms (ignores electron–electron repulsion), cannot explain fine structure (doublets), the Zeeman and Stark effects, or the dual wave nature of the electron — de Broglie and Schrödinger take over from here.

    2.6 Similarities and differences — Rutherford's model vs Bohr's model

    Similarities: both place a tiny positive nucleus at the centre with electrons revolving around it; both use Coulomb attraction as the centripetal force; both are circular-orbit pictures. Differences: Rutherford allows any orbit and continuous radiation (→ collapse predicted); Bohr restricts orbits to mvr = nh/2π with no radiation inside an orbit (→ stability). Rutherford explains nothing about spectra; Bohr reproduces the hydrogen line spectrum quantitatively. Rutherford is purely classical; Bohr mixes classical mechanics with Planck's quantum condition.

    2.7 Similarities and differences — orbit (Bohr) vs orbital (quantum model)

    Similarities: both describe where the electron may be found around the nucleus; both carry definite energies in their models. Differences: an orbit is a fixed circular path (Bohr, definite trajectory); an orbital is a 3-D probability region (quantum model, ~90% probability cloud). Orbits violate the uncertainty principle; orbitals are its direct consequence.

  • 3. Conceptual Relationships

    • To Rutherford (Topic 13): Bohr keeps the nucleus and quantises the orbits.
    • To hydrogen spectrum (Topic 15): the frequency condition generates every spectral series.
    • To de Broglie (Topic 17): the wave view explains why mvr = nh/2π — n wavelengths must fit the orbit's circumference.

Confusion

  • Orbit vs orbital: orbit = Bohr's fixed circular path; orbital = quantum probability region — different models, different words.
  • Emission vs absorption: falling jump emits hν; rising jump absorbs hν — the frequency condition covers both.

Summary

Bohr's model (1913) keeps Rutherford's nucleus but quantises the electron: stationary non-radiating orbits exist only where mvr = nh/2π; light is emitted or absorbed only in jumps (hν = |E₂ − E₁|); Coulomb force supplies the centripetal force. The results — rₙ = 0.529 n²/Z Å, vₙ = 2.18 × 10⁶ Z/n m s⁻¹, Eₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² eV — explain atomic stability, reproduce the hydrogen spectrum through ν̄ = RZ²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), and give ionisation energies scaling as Z² (H 13.6 eV; He⁺ 54.4 eV). E₂ of hydrogen is −5.45 × 10⁻¹⁹ J (NEB 2022). Unlike Rutherford's any-orbit picture, Bohr's quantised orbits forbid radiation inside them; unlike the later orbital, an orbit is a definite path, not a probability cloud.
