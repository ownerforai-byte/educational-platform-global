  • Spectrum of hydrogen atom

    1. Topic Introduction

    The hydrogen spectrum is the Rosetta Stone of atomic physics. When hydrogen gas in a discharge tube is excited, it emits light at discrete wavelengths — a line spectrum. These lines are not random; they fall into distinct series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, Pfund) that Bohr's model predicted with stunning accuracy. The hydrogen spectrum is the primary experimental evidence that energy levels in atoms are quantized.

    Why it matters. Every spectroscopy technique — from stellar classification to environmental monitoring — traces its roots to understanding the hydrogen spectrum. The Balmer series in visible light is the standard calibration for spectrographs; the Lyman series in UV probes interstellar hydrogen; the 21 cm line (hyperfine transition) maps the Milky Way.

    What you will learn: (i) origin of line spectrum from quantized energy levels; (ii) five spectral series of hydrogen with regions, formulas, and limits; (iii) Rydberg formula and its derivation from Bohr's model; (iv) calculation of wavelengths, wave numbers, and photon energies; (v) emission vs absorption spectra; (v) applications in astronomy and spectroscopy.

    Learning outcomes. You can: identify the five spectral series and their regions; compute wavelengths for any transition using Rydberg formula; calculate wave numbers and photon energies; explain emission vs absorption spectra; solve numerical problems on series limits and transitions.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding

    What the hydrogen spectrum is

    The hydrogen spectrum is the set of discrete wavelengths emitted or absorbed when hydrogen electrons jump between quantized energy levels. It consists of five named series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, Pfund) corresponding to transitions ending at n_f = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 respectively.

    Why it exists

    Electrons in hydrogen occupy quantized energy levels Eₙ = -13.6/n² eV. When an electron jumps from a higher level nᵢ to a lower level n_f, it emits a photon of energy ΔE = Eᵢ - E_f = hν = hc/λ. Since only specific energy differences are possible, only specific wavelengths appear — a line spectrum.

    How it works

    Excitation (electric discharge, heat, photon absorption) promotes electrons to higher levels. De-excitation returns them to lower levels, emitting photons of specific energies. The set of all possible transitions from all nᵢ > n_f to a given n_f forms a spectral series.

    Where it occurs

    Hydrogen discharge tubes, stellar atmospheres (chromosphere/corona), interstellar medium (Lyman-alpha forest), planetary nebulae, laboratory spectrometers, hydrogen masers.

    What its components are

    Quantized energy levels (Eₙ = -13.6/n² eV), transitions (nᵢ → n_f), photon energy (ΔE = 13.6(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) eV), wavelength (1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²)), spectral series (named by n_f).

    How components interact

    Energy levels define possible transitions; transitions define photon energies; photon energies define wavelengths; wavelengths group into series by common n_f. The Rydberg constant R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹ unifies the description.

    What makes it biologically/astronomically important

    Stellar classification: Balmer line strength classifies A-type stars. Cosmology: Lyman-alpha forest maps intergalactic hydrogen. 21 cm line maps galactic rotation. Hydrogen maser clocks define the second. Fluorescence microscopy uses H-alpha (656.3 nm) for cellular imaging.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Origin of the hydrogen line spectrum

    Hydrogen electrons occupy quantized energy levels: Eₙ = -13.6/n² eV (n = 1, 2, 3...)

    When an electron transitions from nᵢ to n_f (nᵢ > n_f):

    • Emission: Electron drops from nᵢ to n_f → photon emitted
    • Absorption: Electron rises from n_f to nᵢ → photon absorbed

    Photon energy: ΔE = Eᵢ - E_f = 13.6(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) eV Wavelength: 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) where R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹ Wave number: ν̃ = 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) m⁻¹ Photon energy: E = hc/λ = hcR(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) = 13.6(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) eV

    4.2 The five spectral series of hydrogen

    First few lines of each series:

    • Lyman (UV): Ly-α (2→1) 121.6 nm, Ly-β (3→1) 102.6 nm, Ly-γ (4→1) 97.3 nm, Ly-δ (5→1) 95.0 nm, limit 91.2 nm
    • Balmer (Visible): H-α (3→2) 656.3 nm red, H-β (4→2) 486.1 nm cyan, H-γ (5→2) 434.0 nm violet, H-δ (6→2) 410.2 nm violet, limit 364.6 nm
    • Paschen (IR): Pa-α (4→3) 1875 nm, Pa-β (5→3) 1282 nm, Pa-γ (6→3) 1094 nm, limit 820.4 nm
    • Brackett (IR): Br-α (5→4) 4051 nm, Br-β (6→4) 2625 nm, limit 1458 nm
    • Pfund (Far IR): Pf-α (6→5) 7458 nm, Pf-β (7→5) 4653 nm, limit 2279 nm

    4.3 Rydberg formula and its forms

    Standard form (wavelength): 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) where R = 1.097373×10⁷ m⁻¹

    For hydrogen-like ions (Z > 1): 1/λ = R·Z²(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²)

    Wave number form (spectroscopist's preference): ν̃ = 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) cm⁻¹ (R = 109737 cm⁻¹)

    Photon energy form: E (eV) = 13.6(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) E (J) = hcR(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²)

    4.4 Worked examples

    Example 1: Wavelength of H-α (Balmer α, 3→2) 1/λ = R(1/2² - 1/3²) = R(1/4 - 1/9) = 5R/36 λ = 36/(5R) = 36/(5×1.097×10⁷) = 6.563×10⁻⁷ m = 656.3 nm

    Example 2: Wavelength of Lyman-β (3→1) 1/λ = R(1/1² - 1/3²) = R(1 - 1/9) = 8R/9 λ = 9/(8R) = 9/(8×1.097×10⁷) = 1.026×10⁻⁷ m = 102.6 nm

    Example 3: Series limit of Paschen series (n_f=3, nᵢ→∞) 1/λ = R(1/3² - 0) = R/9 λ = 9/R = 9/1.097×10⁷ = 8.204×10⁻⁷ m = 820.4 nm

    Example 4: Wave number of H-γ (5→2) ν̃ = R(1/4 - 1/25) = R(0.25 - 0.04) = 0.21R = 23047 cm⁻¹

    Example 5: Photon energy of H-δ (6→2) E = 13.6(1/4 - 1/36) = 13.6(9/36 - 1/36) = 13.6×8/36 = 3.02 eV

    Example 6: Number of spectral lines from n=6 → ground state N = n(n-1)/2 = 6×5/2 = 15 lines

    4.5 Emission vs Absorption spectra

    AspectEmission spectrumAbsorption spectrum
    AppearanceBright lines on dark backgroundDark lines on bright continuum

    ConditionHot, low-pressure gas emittingCool gas in front of continuum source
    Hydrogen exampleDischarge tube, stellar chromosphereStellar photosphere, interstellar clouds
    Lines presentAll possible transitions nᵢ → n_fOnly n_f → nᵢ where n_f is populated
    Hydrogen caseAll series seenUsually only Lyman (ground state n=1 populated)

    4.5 Series limit and ionization energy

    Series limit (nᵢ → ∞) for each series:

    • Lyman limit: 91.2 nm (13.6 eV) — ionization from n=1
    • Balmer limit: 364.6 nm (3.4 eV) — ionization from n=2
    • Paschen limit: 820.4 nm (1.51 eV) — ionization from n=3
    • Brackett limit: 1458 nm (0.85 eV) — ionization from n=4
    • Pfund limit: 2279 nm (0.54 eV) — ionization from n=5

    Ionization energy from level n_f: IE = 13.6/n_f² eV

    4.6 Worked numericals

    1. H-α (3→2) wavelength: 656.3 nm (red)
    2. H-β (4→2) wavelength: 486.1 nm (cyan)
    3. H-γ (5→2) wavelength: 434.0 nm (violet)
    4. H-δ (6→2) wavelength: 410.2 nm (violet)
    5. Lyman-α (2→1): 121.6 nm (UV)
    6. Lyman limit: 91.2 nm (13.6 eV)
    7. Balmer limit: 364.6 nm (3.4 eV)
    8. Paschen limit: 820.4 nm (1.51 eV)
    9. Wave number of H-γ: ν̃ = R(1/4 - 1/25) = 0.21R = 23047 cm⁻¹
    10. Photon energy of 4→1: ΔE = 13.6(1 - 1/16) = 12.75 eV
    11. Lines from n=6 de-excitation: 6×5/2 = 15 lines

  • 6. Chemical Processes and Mechanisms

    Process: Emission spectrum production (discharge tube)

    1. Definition: Production of hydrogen emission spectrum by electrical excitation.
    2. Location: Low-pressure hydrogen gas discharge tube with electrodes.
    3. Requirements: High voltage (~1000-5000 V), low pressure (~0.1-1 Torr), H₂ gas.
    4. Components: Glass tube, electrodes, vacuum pump, H₂ supply, high-voltage supply, spectrometer.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Evacuate tube, fill with H₂ at low pressure; (ii) Apply high voltage → electrical discharge; (iii) Electrons collide with H₂ molecules → dissociation + ionization; (iv) H atoms excited to n=2,3,4... by electron impact; (v) Excited H atoms de-excite → emit photons at characteristic wavelengths; (vi) Light passes through spectrometer → line spectrum recorded.
    6. Result: Bright lines at Lyman, Balmer, Paschen... wavelengths on dark background.
    7. Significance: Classic laboratory demonstration of quantized energy levels; fingerprint of hydrogen.

    Process: Absorption spectrum production (stellar atmosphere)

    1. Definition: Production of hydrogen absorption spectrum by cool gas in front of hot continuum.
    2. Location: Stellar photosphere (cool outer layers), interstellar clouds, planetary atmospheres.
    3. Requirements: Hot continuum source (stellar interior), cooler absorbing gas (H atoms in n=1), spectrometer.
    4. Components: Continuum source, absorbing gas layer, spectrometer.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Hot stellar interior emits blackbody continuum; (ii) Light passes through cooler outer atmosphere (photosphere/chromosphere); (iii) H atoms in ground state (n=1) absorb Lyman series photons; (iv) Specific wavelengths removed from continuum → dark absorption lines; (v) Spectrum shows dark Lyman lines on bright continuum.
    6. Result: Dark Lyman lines on bright continuum; Balmer absorption only if n=2 populated (hot stars).
    7. Significance: Stellar spectra are absorption spectra; Lyman lines ubiquitous, Balmer strong in A-type stars.

    Process: Spectral line analysis (astronomical spectroscopy)

    1. Definition: Measuring wavelengths, intensities, profiles of spectral lines to infer physical conditions.
    2. Location: Telescopes with spectrographs (optical, UV, IR, radio).
    3. Requirements: Telescope, spectrograph, detector (CCD), calibration source, reduction software.
    4. Components: Telescope, slit, grating/prism, detector, wavelength calibration lamp.
    5. Sequential steps: (i) Collect light from source; (ii) Disperse by grating/prism; (iii) Detect on CCD; (iv) Calibrate wavelength (Th-Ar lamp); (v) Measure line positions (λ), intensities (I), widths (Δλ); (vi) Infer temperature (Balmer decrement), density (Stark broadening), velocity (Doppler shift), magnetic field (Zeeman splitting).
    6. Result: Physical diagnostics of astronomical object.
    7. Significance: Spectroscopy is the primary tool of astrophysics; hydrogen lines are the most abundant.

  • 8. Conceptual Relationships

    Within this topic

    • Energy levels → transitions → photons → wavelengths → series. Complete chain from quantum levels to observed lines.
    • Rydberg formula unifies all series: same formula, different n_f.
    • Emission and absorption are two sides of the same transitions.

    To other units

    • Bohr model (previous topic): Bohr's energy levels directly predict the hydrogen spectrum.
    • Defects of Bohr (next topic): Bohr's formula works perfectly for hydrogen but fails for multi-electron atoms.
    • Quantum mechanical model: Fine structure (spin-orbit) splits lines Bohr predicts as single.
    • Chemical spectroscopy: H-α filter used in solar telescopes; atomic absorption for metals uses similar principles.
    • Astrophysics: Lyman-alpha forest, 21 cm line, hydrogen recombination lines — all rooted in hydrogen spectrum.

    To real life

    • Star classification: A0 stars show strongest Balmer lines; used for spectral typing.
    • Quasar absorption lines: Lyman-alpha forest maps intergalactic hydrogen at high redshift.
    • 21 cm line: Hyperfine splitting of n=1 (not electronic transition) maps galactic hydrogen.
    • Medical physics: Hydrogen spectroscopy in MRI (proton NMR) uses spin transitions, not electronic.
    • Environmental: Hydrogen sensors use spectroscopy for leak detection.

  • 10. Topic Mastery Check

    Important definitions

    1. Line spectrum — discrete wavelengths emitted/absorbed by an element.
    2. Spectral series — set of lines sharing common final level n_f.
    3. Rydberg formula — 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²); R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹.
    4. Wave number — ν̃ = 1/λ (cm⁻¹); E = hcν̃.
    5. Series limit — nᵢ→∞ transition; shortest wavelength in series.
    6. Lyman series — n_f=1, UV, limit 91.2 nm.
    7. Balmer series — n_f=2, visible, limit 364.6 nm.
    8. Paschen series — n_f=3, IR, limit 820.4 nm.
    9. Brackett series — n_f=4, IR, limit 1458 nm.
    10. Pfund series — n_f=5, far IR, limit 2279 nm.

    Key concepts to remember

    • Hydrogen spectrum = line spectrum from quantized energy levels Eₙ = -13.6/n² eV.
    • Five series: Lyman (n_f=1, UV), Balmer (n_f=2, visible), Paschen (n_f=3, IR), Brackett (n_f=4, IR), Pfund (n_f=5, far IR).
    • Rydberg formula: 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²); R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹.
    • Series limits: Lyman 91.2 nm, Balmer 364.6 nm, Paschen 820.4 nm.
    • Emission: excited gas → bright lines; Absorption: cool gas + continuum → dark lines.
    • H-α (656.3 nm) = strongest visible line; Lyman-α (121.6 nm) = strongest UV.

    Important classifications

    • By n_f: Lyman (1), Balmer (2), Paschen (3), Brackett (4), Pfund (5).
    • By region: UV (Lyman), Visible (Balmer), Near IR (Paschen), Mid IR (Brackett), Far IR (Pfund).
    • Emission vs Absorption: emission = bright lines on dark; absorption = dark lines on bright.
    • Hydrogen-like: same formula with Z² factor: 1/λ = RZ²(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²).

    Common conceptual confusions

    • Balmer series is UV — NO, Balmer is VISIBLE (n_f=2).
    • All series are visible — NO, only Balmer is in visible; Lyman UV, others IR.
    • Series limit is the first line — NO, limit is nᵢ→∞ (shortest wavelength).
    • Absorption shows all series — NO, usually only Lyman (ground state n=1 populated).
    • Rydberg formula works for all atoms — NO, only hydrogen-like (Z² factor for H-like ions).
    • H-α is the series limit — NO, H-α is FIRST line (3→2); limit is nᵢ→∞.

    PYQ Solutions

    Q: Explain the origin of the line spectrum of hydrogen using Bohr's postulates. (2 marks) Answer: Bohr's postulate 1: electrons in stationary states don't radiate. Postulate 3: when electron jumps between discrete states, photon emitted with hν = Eᵢ - E_f. Since energy levels are discrete (Eₙ = -13.6/n²), ΔE is discrete → line spectrum. Keywords: stationary states, discrete levels, hν = Eᵢ - E_f, discrete photons.

    Q: Write the Rydberg equation and compute the wavelength of the first Balmer line. (3 marks) Answer: 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²), R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹. For Balmer first line (3→2): 1/λ = R(1/4 - 1/9) = 5R/36 → λ = 36/5R = 656.3 nm. Keywords: Rydberg formula, R = 1.097×10⁷, 5R/36, 656.3 nm.

    Q: Name the hydrogen series lying in the visible region and give the regions of the others. (2 marks) Answer: Balmer series (n_f=2) lies in visible region. Lyman (n_f=1) — UV; Paschen (n_f=3) — IR; Brackett (n_f=4) — IR; Pfund (n_f=5) — Far IR. Keywords: Balmer = visible; Lyman = UV; Paschen/Brackett/Pfund = IR.

    Q: Calculate the wave number and photon energy for the n = 4 → 2 transition in hydrogen. (3 marks) Answer: ν̃ = R(1/2² - 1/4²) = R(1/4 - 1/16) = 3R/16 = 20568 cm⁻¹. E = hcν̃ = 13.6(1/4 - 1/16) = 13.6×3/16 = 2.55 eV. Keywords: 3R/16, 20568 cm⁻¹, 2.55 eV.

    Q: How many spectral lines are possible when an excited hydrogen atom de-excites from n = 5? (2 marks) Answer: N = n(n-1)/2 = 5×4/2 = 10 lines. Keywords: n(n-1)/2, 10 lines.

Confusion

  • ❌ Balmer series is in UV. ✅ Balmer (n_f=2) is VISIBLE; Lyman (n_f=1) is UV.
  • ❌ All hydrogen spectral lines are visible. ✅ Only Balmer (n_f=2) is in visible; Lyman=UV, Paschen/Brackett/Pfund=IR.
  • ❌ Series limit is the first line of the series. ✅ Series limit is nᵢ→∞ (shortest wavelength); first line is longest wavelength.
  • ❌ Absorption spectrum shows all five series. ✅ Usually only Lyman series (n=1 populated); Balmer absorption only in hot stars.
  • ❌ Rydberg formula works for all elements. ✅ Only for hydrogen-like ions (1/λ = RZ²(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²)).
  • ❌ H-α is the series limit of Balmer series. ✅ H-α (3→2) is the FIRST line (longest λ); series limit is nᵢ→∞ (364.6 nm).
  • ❌ All spectral lines have equal intensity. ✅ Intensities follow transition probabilities; H-α strongest in Balmer.
  • ❌ 21 cm line is an electronic transition. ✅ 21 cm is hyperfine spin-flip in n=1, not an electronic transition between n levels.

Universal Facts

  • Rydberg formula: 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²); R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹.
  • Five series: Lyman (n_f=1, UV), Balmer (n_f=2, visible), Paschen (n_f=3, IR), Brackett (n_f=4, IR), Pfund (n_f=5, far IR).
  • Series limits: Lyman 91.2 nm, Balmer 364.6 nm, Paschen 820.4 nm, Brackett 1458 nm, Pfund 2279 nm.
  • H-α=656.3 nm, H-β=486.1 nm, H-γ=434.0 nm, H-δ=410.2 nm (Balmer visible).
  • Lyman-α=121.6 nm (UV); Ly-β=102.6 nm; Ly-γ=97.3 nm.

Examples

  • H-α (3→2): 656.3 nm red — solar chromosphere, star formation tracer.
  • H-β (4→2): 486.1 nm cyan — temperature diagnostic in nebulae.
  • Lyman-α (2→1): 121.6 nm — intergalactic medium probe (Lyman-alpha forest).
  • Balmer limit 364.6 nm — convergence of visible lines.

Formulas

  • 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) where R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹
  • ν̃ = 1/λ = R(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²) cm⁻¹ (R = 109737 cm⁻¹)
  • E (eV) = 13.6(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²)
  • E (J) = hc/λ = hcR(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²)
  • For hydrogen-like ions: 1/λ = RZ²(1/n_f² - 1/nᵢ²)
  • Series limit (nᵢ→∞): 1/λ = R/n_f²; λ_limit = n_f²/R
  • Number of spectral lines from de-excitation n → 1: n(n-1)/2
  • Photon energy: E = hν = hc/λ = hcν̃
