Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — When Gases Stop Being Ideal

    Real molecules have size and attraction — the two things the kinetic theory waved away. Compress a gas enough and the molecules' own volume matters; cool it enough and attraction condenses it. Van der Waals patched PV = nRT with two corrections, and the conditions of maximum deviation (high pressure, low temperature) follow from exactly those two failures (NEB 2024).

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The two idealisations and how they fail

    • Negligible molecular volume: at high pressure the molecules are squeezed into a significant fraction of the container — free volume is less than V. Correction: (V − nb).
    • Negligible intermolecular attraction: at low temperature the molecules are slow enough that attraction pulls inbound molecules back, softening their wall impact — measured pressure is less than ideal. Correction: +(an²/V²) — the "attractive drag" term.

    2.2 Van der Waals equation

    • (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT (for n moles); per mole: (P + a/V²)(V − b) = RT.
    • a: attraction correction — larger for easily liquefied, polarisable gases (NH₃, CO₂, H₂O ≫ He, H₂); units atm·L²/mol².
    • b: volume (co-volume) correction — larger for bulky molecules; roughly 4× the actual molecular volume per mole; units L/mol.

    2.3 Conditions of maximum deviation (NEB 2024)

    • High pressure: molecules crowd; b-term dominates → observed V > ideal V (positive deviation, Z > 1).
    • Low temperature: molecules slow; attraction dominates → observed P < ideal P (negative deviation, Z < 1).
    • High T and low P: both corrections shrink — ideal behaviour recovered; every gas is ideal in the dilute-hot limit.

    2.4 Compressibility factor Z

    • Z = PV/nRT; Z = 1 ideal; Z < 1 attraction wins (easier to compress); Z > 1 repulsion/volume wins (harder to compress).
    • H₂ and He (a ≈ 0) show Z > 1 at practically all pressures — attraction too weak to ever pull Z below 1.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): when does a real gas deviate most; explain with van der Waals

    • Most deviation: high pressure and low temperature (near condensation). At high P the molecules' own volume is no longer negligible against the compressed V — the free space is (V − nb), so the observed volume exceeds the ideal prediction (Z > 1). At low T the molecules move slowly enough for attraction to act: incoming molecules are pulled back by the bulk, hitting the walls with less momentum, so the observed pressure is less than ideal — van der Waals writes this as an added term a(n/V)², giving (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. At high T and low P both corrections vanish and PV = nRT is recovered.

    4.2 The a-table: attraction ranks the gases

    Gasa (atm·L²/mol²)Character
    H₂O5.46Strong H-bonding
    NH₃4.17Polar, H-bonding
    CO₂3.59Polarisable, liquefies easily
    O₂1.36Nonpolar
    H₂0.244Tiny, barely attracts
    He0.034Nearly ideal
    • Liquefaction ease tracks a: NH₃ and CO₂ compress to liquids at room temperature (a large); He resists liquefaction down to 4 K (a tiny).

    4.3 Boyle temperature

    • Each gas has a temperature where the a and b effects cancel over a pressure range — Z stays ≈ 1: the gas behaves most ideally (e.g. N₂ ≈ 323 K, He ≈ 23 K).

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Deviation has two directions: high pressure inflates volume (Z > 1) while low temperature deflates pressure (Z < 1) — "deviates" does not mean one sign.

  • a and b are gas-specific constants, not universal: R is universal; a and b must be looked up per gas — quoting R's value for a is a mark-losing slip.
  • Liquefaction needs attraction, not just pressure: a large a means the gas can be liquefied near room temperature; a tiny a (He) demands extreme cooling first.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Under what conditions does a real gas deviate most from ideal behaviour? Explain using van der Waals' equation. — Conditions: high pressure and low temperature. Van der Waals modified the ideal equation to (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT, where a corrects for intermolecular attraction and b for finite molecular volume. At high pressure the molecules occupy an appreciable fraction of the container, so the free volume is less than the measured V — the (V − nb) term — and the gas is harder to compress than ideal (Z > 1). At low temperature the molecules move slowly enough for attraction to matter: each molecule approaching the wall is pulled back by the others, so it strikes with less momentum and the observed pressure falls short — compensated by the added term a(n/V)² (Z < 1). At high temperature and low pressure both corrections become negligible and the gas obeys PV = nRT.

  • NEB-style (2m): Why do H₂ and He show Z > 1 at almost all pressures? — Their a values are tiny (0.244 and 0.034 atm·L²/mol²): intermolecular attraction is too weak to ever make the observed pressure fall below ideal, so the volume (b) effect dominates and Z stays above 1 — they are the most "permanently ideal" gases.
  • NEB-style (2m): Account for the observation that NH₃ liquefies under pressure at room temperature while N₂ does not. — NH₃'s large a (4.17) reflects strong dipole–dipole (and hydrogen-bond) attraction: compression alone lets attraction condense it. N₂'s a is small (1.39) with weak London forces, so room-temperature compression cannot liquefy it — it must first be cooled below its critical-region temperatures.

Confusion

  • b is roughly four times the actual molecular volume, not equal to it: the excluded sphere around each molecule doubles its radius — a detail examiners like to probe.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (3m): Under what conditions does a real gas deviate most from ideal behaviour? Explain using van der Waals' equation. — A real gas deviates most at high pressure and low temperature. Van der Waals corrected the ideal equation for the two failed idealisations: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT, where a accounts for intermolecular attraction and b for the finite volume of the molecules. At high pressure, the molecules' own volume becomes a significant fraction of the container, so the volume actually available for motion is (V − nb) — less than the measured V — and the gas is harder to compress than the ideal law predicts (compressibility factor Z > 1). At low temperature, molecular speeds drop until intermolecular attraction becomes effective: a molecule nearing the wall is pulled backwards by the attraction of the bulk, strikes with reduced momentum, and the observed pressure is lower than ideal — compensated by the added term a(n/V)² (Z < 1). At high temperature and low pressure both corrections vanish, and the gas behaves ideally (Z ≈ 1). The deviation is therefore largest exactly where the gas approaches condensation — high P, low T.
  • NEB-style (2m): Explain why hydrogen and helium show positive deviation (Z > 1) at almost all pressures. — The van der Waals attraction constant a of H₂ (0.244) and He (0.034) is extremely small: their molecules are tiny, light and nearly non-polarisable, so intermolecular attraction never becomes strong enough to pull the observed pressure below the ideal value. The only operative correction is therefore the volume term b — the excluded space around each molecule — which makes the gas harder to compress than ideal, so Z = PV/nRT stays greater than 1 at essentially all pressures. They approach ideality only at very low pressures where even the b effect fades.
  • NEB-style (2m): Ammonia liquefies under pressure at room temperature but nitrogen does not. Explain using van der Waals constants. — Ammonia's attraction constant a = 4.17 atm·L²/mol² is among the largest for common gases because its polar N–H bonds and hydrogen bonding create strong intermolecular attraction; applying pressure alone brings the molecules close enough for this attraction to condense them. Nitrogen's a = 1.39 is much smaller (nonpolar, weak London forces only), so at room temperature compression cannot overcome the molecules' thermal kinetic energy — N₂ must be cooled far down (to about 77 K at 1 atm) before liquefaction becomes possible. The contrast is a direct read-out of the a values.

Summary

Real gases break the kinetic theory's two idealisations, and van der Waals priced each break: molecular volume shrinks the free space to (V − nb), and intermolecular attraction softens wall impacts, adding a(n/V)² to the pressure — together, (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. The constants are fingerprints: a ranks stickiness (H₂O 5.46 > NH₃ 4.17 > CO₂ 3.59 > O₂ 1.36 > H₂ 0.244 > He 0.034) and predicts liquefaction ease; b runs about four times the true molecular volume. Deviation peaks at high pressure (volume term dominates, Z > 1, harder to compress) and low temperature (attraction dominates, Z < 1, easier to compress), while hot dilute gases of any species slide back to Z = 1 — and H₂ and He, with attraction too feeble to matter, ride above 1 everywhere. The compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT turns the story into one number, and the Boyle temperature marks where the two errors cancel.
