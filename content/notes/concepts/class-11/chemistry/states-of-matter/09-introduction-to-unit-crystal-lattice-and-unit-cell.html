Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Address System of Solids

    Crystalline solids are not random piles of atoms: they are three-dimensional ordered arrays whose every particle sits at a mathematically defined address. Two ideas describe that order completely — the crystal lattice (the infinite scaffolding of points) and the unit cell (the smallest repeating box from which the whole crystal can be rebuilt by stacking). Every crystal property — shape, cleavage, anisotropy — traces back to this geometry.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Crystalline vs amorphous solids (the backdrop)

    PropertyCrystallineAmorphous
    Particle arrangementLong-range regular repeating patternShort-range order only, random
    MeltingSharp melting pointSoftens over a range (glass)
    AnisotropyAnisotropic (properties vary with direction)Isotropic (same in all directions)
    ExamplesNaCl, diamond, CuSO₄Glass, plastic, rubber

    2.2 Crystal lattice

    • A crystal lattice is a regular three-dimensional arrangement of points in space, each point representing one particle (atom, ion or molecule) of the crystal.
    • Each lattice point has identical surroundings — walking from any point in the same direction meets the same pattern at the same spacing.
    • The smallest repeat distance along each axis and the angles between axes define the lattice parameters (a, b, c, α, β, γ).

    2.3 Unit cell

    • The unit cell is the smallest three-dimensional portion of a crystal lattice which, when repeated in all three directions, generates the entire lattice.
    • It is the "brick" of the crystal wall: one brick, translated endlessly, builds the whole structure.
    • Types of unit cells: primitive (simple) — particles only at the corners; and centred — additional particles at body centre (body-centred) or face centres (face-centred).
    • Seven crystal systems are distinguished by lattice parameters: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, hexagonal, rhombohedral (trigonal), triclinic.

    2.4 Coordination number

    • The coordination number of a particle = the number of nearest neighbours touching it. In a simple cubic lattice each particle touches 6 neighbours (4 in its plane, 1 above, 1 below).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why the unit cell is the crystal's "DNA"

    • Take one unit cell, translate it along a, b and c by its own dimensions millions of times, and the entire crystal appears — edges, faces, cleavage planes and all. That is why crystallographers describe a whole crystal by one cell: the cell carries the full symmetry information.
    • Cell dimensions are measured by X-ray diffraction (Bragg's law nλ = 2d sinθ): the spacing d between lattice planes is read from diffraction angles.

    4.2 Counting particles in a cubic unit cell

    • Corner particle: shared by 8 cells → contributes 1/8.
    • Face-centre particle: shared by 2 cells → contributes 1/2.
    • Body-centre particle: belongs wholly to one cell → contributes 1.
    • Simple cubic: 8 × 1/8 = 1 particle per cell.
    • Body-centred cubic (bcc): 8 × 1/8 + 1 = 2 particles.
    • Face-centred cubic (fcc): 8 × 1/8 + 6 × 1/2 = 4 particles.

    4.3 Relation between edge length and radius (cubic cells)

    • Simple cubic: atoms touch along the edge → r = a/2.
    • bcc: atoms touch along the body diagonal √3a → r = √3a/4.
    • fcc: atoms touch along the face diagonal √2a → r = a/(2√2).

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Lattice vs unit cell: the lattice is the infinite pattern of points; the unit cell is one repeat box of it. Saying "the unit cell is the lattice" loses the mark — one is the map, the other one tile of the map.

  • Lattice points are positions, not atoms: a lattice point marks where a particle sits; the particle may be an atom, a molecule or a whole ion pair. Do not write "lattice points are atoms".
  • Shared particles are not counted whole: a corner atom belongs to eight neighbouring cells, so only 1/8 of it lies in any one cell — counting it as 1 gives impossible particle counts.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Define crystal lattice and unit cell. How are they related? — A crystal lattice is the regular three-dimensional arrangement of points in space such that each point has identical surroundings, each point representing the position of one particle of the crystal. A unit cell is the smallest three-dimensional portion of that lattice which, when repeated again and again in the three directions of the lattice, generates the complete crystal. Relation: the unit cell is the building block — the crystal is the unit cell translated millions of times along the three axes, just as a wall is built by stacking identical bricks; hence the geometry of one cell (edge lengths a, b, c and angles α, β, γ) determines the entire crystal's shape and symmetry.

  • NEB-style (2m): Calculate the number of particles per unit cell in simple cubic, body-centred and face-centred cubic cells. — Simple cubic: 8 corner particles × 1/8 = 1. Body-centred: 8 × 1/8 (corners) + 1 (body centre, wholly inside) = 2. Face-centred: 8 × 1/8 + 6 face centres × 1/2 = 1 + 3 = 4. The fractional contributions arise because corner particles are shared by 8 cells and face-centre particles by 2.
  • NEB-style (2m): Distinguish crystalline and amorphous solids with two points and one example each. — (1) Order: crystalline solids have long-range repeating order (NaCl, diamond); amorphous solids have only short-range order (glass, rubber). (2) Melting: crystalline solids melt sharply at a fixed temperature; amorphous solids soften gradually over a range. Bonus: crystalline solids are anisotropic, amorphous isotropic.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Define crystal lattice and unit cell. How are they related? — Crystal lattice: the regular three-dimensional arrangement of points in space in which each point has identical surroundings, every point representing the site of one particle (atom, ion or molecule) of the crystal; for example the sodium chloride lattice. Unit cell: the smallest three-dimensional portion of the crystal lattice which, when repeated again and again in the three directions of the lattice, produces the complete crystal lattice; its size and shape are fixed by the lattice parameters a, b, c and the angles α, β, γ. Relation: the unit cell is the fundamental building block of the lattice — repeating (translating) one unit cell along the three crystallographic axes builds the entire crystal, exactly as stacking identical bricks builds a wall; therefore the geometry of a single unit cell determines the external shape, symmetry and properties of the whole crystal.
  • NEB-style (2m): Calculate the number of particles per unit cell in simple cubic, body-centred cubic and face-centred cubic unit cells. — Simple cubic: there are 8 particles at the corners, each shared by 8 unit cells, so each contributes 1/8; total = 8 × 1/8 = 1 particle. Body-centred cubic: 8 corners × 1/8 = 1, plus one particle at the body centre belonging wholly to the cell; total = 2 particles. Face-centred cubic: 8 corners × 1/8 = 1, plus 6 particles at the face centres each shared by 2 cells contributing 1/2 each = 3; total = 1 + 3 = 4 particles.
  • NEB-style (2m): Distinguish between crystalline and amorphous solids with two points and one example each. — (1) Arrangement: crystalline solids possess a long-range, regularly repeating three-dimensional pattern of particles — e.g. NaCl, diamond, CuSO₄ — while amorphous solids have only short-range order with an irregular arrangement — e.g. glass, rubber, plastic. (2) Melting behaviour: crystalline solids melt sharply at a definite temperature because the whole lattice breaks down at once, whereas amorphous solids soften gradually over a range of temperature. Additionally, crystalline solids are anisotropic (physical properties differ with direction) while amorphous solids are isotropic.

Summary

A crystal lattice is the infinite, perfectly regular three-dimensional array of points — identical surroundings at every point — on which a crystalline solid is built, while the unit cell is its smallest repeating box: translate that one box (with lattice parameters a, b, c and angles α, β, γ) millions of times along the three axes and the whole crystal appears, which is why the cell is called the crystal's DNA. Counting particles uses sharing fractions — corner 1/8, face centre 1/2, body centre 1 — giving 1, 2 and 4 particles per cell for simple cubic, body-centred and face-centred cubic lattices, with radius–edge relations r = a/2, √3a/4 and a/(2√2) respectively and coordination number 6 in the simple cube. The seven crystal systems (cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, hexagonal, rhombohedral, triclinic) classify all crystals by their parameters, and X-ray diffraction via Bragg's law nλ = 2d sinθ measures the cell dimensions themselves. The backdrop contrast: crystalline solids show sharp melting points and anisotropy; amorphous solids soften over a range and are isotropic.
