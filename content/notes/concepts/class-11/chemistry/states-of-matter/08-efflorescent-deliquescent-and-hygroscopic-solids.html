Notes

  • 1. Water of crystallisation — the concept behind the three classes

  • Water of crystallisation is water chemically combined in a fixed stoichiometric ratio inside a crystal lattice (not merely adsorbed). Examples: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue vitriol), Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda), Na₂SO₄·10H₂O (Glauber's salt), MgCl₂·6H₂O, CaSO₄·2H₂O (gypsum), FeSO₄·7H₂O. It is usually driven off by heating, and the loss of mass gives the number of water molecules per formula unit (gravimetric determination); the anhydrous salt often differs in colour and crystal shape.

  • 2. Efflorescent solids

  • Efflorescent substances lose their water of crystallisation (wholly or partly) spontaneously in dry air and crumble to a powder, because the vapour pressure of the hydrate exceeds the water-vapour pressure of the atmosphere. Examples: Na₂SO₄·10H₂O → Na₂SO₄ (white powder); Na₂CO₃·10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·H₂O; CuSO₄·5H₂O fades from blue towards pale blue/white; alum. Hot, dry weather speeds it up, so such salts must be stored in sealed containers.

  • 3. Deliquescent and hygroscopic solids

  • Deliquescent solids absorb moisture from the air, dissolve in it and form a saturated solution (a puddle) — CaCl₂, MgCl₂, NaOH, KOH, FeCl₃·6H₂O, ZnCl₂. Hygroscopic substances absorb or adsorb moisture and become damp without dissolving — concentrated H₂SO₄, P₄O₁₀, CaO (quicklime), CuO, silica gel, glycerol. Hygroscopic materials are used as drying agents/desiccants (silica-gel sachets, conc. H₂SO₄ in desiccators, quicklime in packaging). Whether a salt deliquesces depends on humidity: the air must exceed its deliquescence relative humidity (NaCl ≈ 75% RH, CaCl₂ ≈ 30% RH), so a 'deliquescent' salt may simply effloresce or remain unchanged in dry air.

  • 4. Crystallization and crystal growth

  • Crystallisation is the formation of a pure solid in crystalline form from a solution, melt or vapour: the solution is saturated and then supersaturated by cooling or slow evaporation, after which nucleation begins and crystal growth follows. Good crystals need slow, steady growth — slow cooling, a clean solution, a suitable solvent, undisturbed conditions and sometimes a seed crystal. Large single crystals are grown by slow evaporation (sugar, alum, KDP), by the Czochralski pulling technique from a melt (silicon and germanium for electronics), by zone refining, or from the vapour (sublimation and chemical vapour transport).

  • 5. Applications

  • • Recrystallisation is the standard laboratory purification technique; soluble impurities stay behind in the mother liquor.
  • • Industrial crystallisation produces salt, sugar, urea, fertilisers and pharmaceutical crystals, where crystal habit and size control tablet manufacture and food texture.
  • • Single-crystal growth supplies semiconductor wafers, quartz oscillators, laser and detector crystals and gemstones; crystallisation is also used in water softening (scale removal) and freeze-drying.
  • • Desiccants and drying agents (silica gel, CaCl₂, P₄O₁₀, H₂SO₄) protect instruments and goods from moisture and are essential for analytical weighing, while anti-caking agents stop efflorescent salts from caking.

  • 6. Limitations

  • • The three terms describe behaviour, not a rigorous classification: the same substance can deliquesce in humid air and effloresce in dry air (Na₂SO₄ and Na₂CO₃ hydrates both do), because humidity, temperature and the hydrate's vapour pressure decide the outcome.
  • • Efflorescence may stop at a lower hydrate rather than reach the anhydrous salt, so equations assuming complete dehydration can be wrong.
  • • Deliquescence occurs only above the substance's critical relative humidity; below it the solid merely adsorbs a little water.
  • • Determining water of crystallisation by heating fails if the substance decomposes or hydrolyses (MgCl₂·6H₂O loses HCl and water when heated strongly) or if the sample also picks up atmospheric moisture.
  • • Crystallisation cannot remove impurities that co-crystallise as mixed (isomorphous) crystals, is slow, and always loses some product in the mother liquor, lowering the yield.

Confusion

  • ❌ Efflorescent, deliquescent and hygroscopic mean the same. ✅ Efflorescent solids lose water to the air, deliquescent solids absorb water and dissolve, hygroscopic solids absorb water and become damp.
  • ❌ Deliquescence happens in any weather. ✅ It requires air humidity above the deliquescence relative humidity (≈30% for CaCl₂, ≈75% for NaCl).
  • ❌ Water of crystallisation is moisture sticking to a crystal. ✅ It is chemically combined in a definite ratio and forms part of the crystal structure.
  • ❌ Any solid that becomes damp is deliquescent. ✅ Hygroscopic solids only become moist; deliquescent solids dissolve into a solution.
  • ❌ Fast cooling gives the best crystals. ✅ Slow, undisturbed growth gives larger, purer crystals; rapid cooling gives many small crystals.

Universal Facts

  • CuSO₄·5H₂O turns white on heating and blue again on adding water — the classic reversible hydration test.
  • Deliquescence relative humidity: about 30% for CaCl₂ and 75% for NaCl.
  • Silica gel can absorb roughly 40% of its own mass of water and is regenerated by heating.

Examples

  • Problem: 2.86 g of hydrated sodium carbonate loses 1.62 g on strong heating; the anhydrous salt has M = 106 g mol⁻¹. Find n. Solution: anhydrous mass = 1.24 g → 0.0117 mol; water = 1.62/18 = 0.09 mol → n = 0.09/0.0117 ≈ 7.7, i.e. ≈ 8 (illustrating that heating experiments need care, since washing soda is normally the decahydrate).
  • Problem: Why is CaCl₂ unsuitable for drying ammonia? Solution: CaCl₂ is deliquescent but forms the addition compound CaCl₂·8NH₃, so it would remove ammonia as well; conc. H₂SO₄ or quicklime is used instead.

Important Notes

  • Keep one example of each class ready: efflorescent — Na₂SO₄·10H₂O; deliquescent — CaCl₂; hygroscopic — conc. H₂SO₄ or silica gel.
  • Tie efflorescence and deliquescence to humidity: both depend on the vapour-pressure balance between hydrate and atmosphere.
