Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Solids That Fight the Air

    Some solids stay dry forever, some soak the air dry, some crumble into solution without a drop of water added. Efflorescent, hygroscopic and deliquescent solids sit at three points on one water-vapour gradient — and knowing which is which saves experiments, reagents and exam marks.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The three behaviours

    • Efflorescent: a hydrated solid that loses its water of crystallisation to the air at room temperature, crusting to a powder. Examples: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda), Na₂SO₄·10H₂O (Glauber's salt), CuSO₄·5H₂O (partially, to the trihydrate), MgSO₄·7H₂O. Condition: the salt's aqueous tension (vapour pressure of its hydrate) exceeds atmospheric water-vapour pressure.
    • Hygroscopic: a solid that absorbs moisture from the air and stays physically damp — surface adsorption, the solid does not dissolve. Examples: NaOH (solid), quicklime CaO, anhydrous CaCl₂ (mildly), silica gel, conc. H₂SO₄ (a hygroscopic liquid), P₂O₅.
    • Deliquescent: a solid that absorbs so much moisture it dissolves in the absorbed water, forming a concentrated solution. Examples: CaCl₂·6H₂O, MgCl₂·6H₂O, ZnCl₂, FeCl₃, NaOH (fully deliquescent), KOH. Condition: the solution formed has vapour pressure below atmospheric water-vapour pressure, so it keeps drawing water.

    2.2 Water of crystallisation

    • Water molecules fixed in the crystal lattice in definite stoichiometric ratios: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue), FeSO₄·7H₂O (green vitriol), Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda), CaSO₄·2H₂O (gypsum), MgSO₄·7H₂O (Epsom salt).
    • Crystallisation: crystals grow from saturated solutions (or melts) as solvent evaporates or cools — nucleation followed by growth; seed crystals direct the habit.
    • Heating removes water of crystallisation: blue CuSO₄·5H₂O → white CuSO₄ + steam; re-wetting restores the blue — the classic reversible hydration test.

    2.3 The vapour-pressure logic (the deciding rule)

    • Compare the vapour pressure of the solid's saturated solution (or hydrate) with the partial pressure of water vapour in the air:
    • Solid VP > air moisture → solid dries the air around it → efflorescence (loses water).
    • Solid VP < air moisture → air feeds the solid → deliquescence (dissolves) or hygroscopicity (adsorbs without dissolving).
    • Deliquescence vs hygroscopicity is thus a matter of degree and solubility: deliquescent salts are highly soluble; hygroscopic ones adsorb without forming a solution (or form one too slowly).

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why washing soda crumbles and CaCl₂ puddles

    • Na₂CO₃·10H₂O's hydrate vapour pressure exceeds the water vapour normally in air, so it effloresces to the monohydrate/powder. CaCl₂·6H₂O's saturated solution has vapour pressure below air's moisture content, so water flows inward until the salt dissolves completely — a puddle of concentrated solution. Two directions, one rule.

    4.2 The blue–white copper cycle

    • CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue) —heat→ CuSO₄ (white) + 5H₂O; CuSO₄ —water→ blue again. Anhydrous CuSO₄ is therefore a water-detecting agent (turns blue in presence of water) — the standard test in organic chemistry practicals.

    4.3 Desiccators and drying agents

    • Desiccators pack hygroscopic/deliquescent agents (silica gel, CaCl₂, P₂O₅, conc. H₂SO₄) to hold a dry atmosphere for cooling and storing hygroscopic samples. Choice by strength: silica gel (mild, reusable), CaCl₂ (moderate), P₂O₅/conc. H₂SO₄ (strong).

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Hygroscopic vs deliquescent — the solution decides: both absorb water; only the deliquescent salt dissolves in what it absorbs. CaO absorbs (stays solid, becomes Ca(OH)₂ by reaction); CaCl₂·6H₂O dissolves.

  • Efflorescence needs a hydrate: anhydrous solids cannot effloresce — there is no lattice water to lose.
  • Water of crystallisation is not "wetness": it is stoichiometric, structural water — CuSO₄·5H₂O is a dry, powdery crystal holding five waters per formula unit inside its lattice.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Define efflorescent, hygroscopic and deliquescent substances with two examples of each. — Efflorescent: hydrated salts that spontaneously lose their water of crystallisation to the atmosphere at ordinary temperature, crumbling to a powder — e.g. washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, Glauber's salt Na₂SO₄·10H₂O. Hygroscopic: substances that absorb moisture from the air without dissolving in it — e.g. quicklime CaO, silica gel, anhydrous P₂O₅, solid NaOH (partially). Deliquescent: substances that absorb moisture so abundantly that they dissolve in it, forming a solution — e.g. calcium chloride CaCl₂·6H₂O, magnesium chloride MgCl₂·6H₂O, ferric chloride FeCl₃, KOH.

  • NEB-style (2m): Explain efflorescence and deliquescence in terms of vapour pressure. — Each behaviour follows from comparing the vapour pressure of the solid (or its saturated solution) with the partial pressure of water vapour in the air. If the hydrate's vapour pressure is greater than the air's moisture pressure, water flows out of the crystal — efflorescence (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O). If the saturated solution's vapour pressure is less than the air's moisture pressure, water flows into the solid until it dissolves — deliquescence (CaCl₂·6H₂O). Water always migrates from higher to lower vapour pressure.
  • NEB-style (2m): Anhydrous copper sulphate is used to test for water. Why? — Anhydrous CuSO₄ is white; on contact with water it re-forms the blue pentahydrate CuSO₄·5H₂O. The sharp, reversible colour change (white → blue) signals water's presence even in small amounts — the standard test that a distillate or organic liquid is dry or wet.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Define efflorescent, hygroscopic and deliquescent substances, giving two examples of each. — Efflorescent substances are hydrated salts that spontaneously lose their water of crystallisation to the atmosphere at ordinary temperature, crumbling into a lower hydrate or powder — e.g. washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) and Glauber's salt (Na₂SO₄·10H₂O). Hygroscopic substances absorb moisture from the air and remain damp solids without dissolving — e.g. quicklime (CaO) and silica gel (also anhydrous P₂O₅, concentrated H₂SO₄). Deliquescent substances absorb moisture so abundantly that they dissolve in the absorbed water, forming a concentrated solution — e.g. calcium chloride (CaCl₂·6H₂O) and potassium hydroxide (KOH) (also MgCl₂·6H₂O, FeCl₃, ZnCl₂).
  • NEB-style (2m): Explain efflorescence and deliquescence on the basis of vapour pressure. — Water vapour always migrates from a region of higher vapour pressure to lower. Efflorescence: the vapour pressure of the hydrated crystal is higher than the partial pressure of water vapour in the surrounding air, so the crystal loses its lattice water to the air and crumbles (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O). Deliquescence: the vapour pressure of the substance's saturated solution is lower than the moisture pressure of the air, so water vapour keeps condensing onto the solid, diluting the surface solution, which never reaches equilibrium until the whole solid has dissolved (CaCl₂·6H₂O). Hygroscopicity is the intermediate case — moisture is adsorbed but the solid never dissolves.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why is anhydrous copper sulphate used to test for the presence of water? — Anhydrous copper sulphate is white; when it contacts water it re-forms the hydrated salt CuSO₄·5H₂O, which is blue: CuSO₄ (white) + 5H₂O → CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue). The colour change is sharp, rapid and reversible, so a drop of any liquid that contains water turns the white powder blue — the standard laboratory test for water (used, for example, to check whether an organic distillate is dry).

Summary

Three solids, one gradient of water vapour. Efflorescent hydrates sit above the air's moisture pressure and bleed their lattice water out — washing soda and Glauber's salt powder themselves on the shelf. Hygroscopic solids sip moisture and stay whole — quicklime, silica gel, P₂O₅, the desiccator's residents. Deliquescent salts drink past the saturation point and dissolve into their own drink — CaCl₂·6H₂O, MgCl₂·6H₂O, KOH, FeCl₃, ending as puddles. The deciding rule is vapour pressure: water always flows high → low, so a hydrate with higher VP than the air dries out while a salt whose saturated solution has lower VP never stops absorbing. Inside the lattice, water of crystallisation is stoichiometric architecture — five waters in blue vitriol, ten in washing soda, two in gypsum — and the blue→white→blue copper cycle turns that hidden water into the standard test for moisture itself.
