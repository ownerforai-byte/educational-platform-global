Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Four Laws, One Gas

    Boyle, Charles, Avogadro, Dalton and Graham each measured one slice of gas behaviour; together they form the toolkit for every gas numerical. This topic carries the heaviest PYQ load in the unit — a 2024 set alone tested Boyle's law, Graham's law, and Dalton's law with aqueous tension — so the worked numericals below are the exam's own patterns.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Boyle's law (T, n constant)

    • V ∝ 1/P, or P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ — doubling the pressure halves the volume.
    • Graph: P vs V is a hyperbola; PV = constant is a straight horizontal line.

    2.2 Charles's law (P, n constant)

    • V ∝ T (absolute), or V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ — every gas extrapolates to zero volume at −273.15 °C, the origin of the absolute scale.
    • V_t = V₀(1 + t/273) at constant pressure.

    2.3 Avogadro's law

    • V ∝ n at constant T and P — equal volumes of all gases at the same T and P contain equal numbers of molecules; molar volume = 22.4 L at STP (0 °C, 1 atm).

    2.4 Combined gas equation and Dalton's law

    • P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ — the workhorse for "state changed" problems.
    • Dalton's law of partial pressures: total pressure of a non-reacting gas mixture equals the sum of the partial pressures each gas would exert alone: P(total) = p₁ + p₂ + p₃ …
    • Aqueous tension: a gas collected over water is saturated with water vapour; the water vapour contributes its own pressure, so P(dry gas) = P(total) − aqueous tension at the collection temperature (NEB 2024).

    2.5 Graham's law of diffusion

    • Rate of diffusion ∝ 1/√M at constant T and P; for two gases r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁).
    • Diffusion = spreading through a porous space; effusion = escape through a tiny hole — the same law governs both.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Boyle's law numerical — 2 L at 3 atm expands to 1 atm

    • Boyle: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → 3 × 2 = 1 × V₂ → V₂ = 6 L (isothermal expansion).

    4.2 Verified PYQ (NEB 2021): combined law — 500 cm³ at 27 °C to 127 °C

    • Kelvin first: 300 K → 400 K. V₂ = V₁T₂/T₁ = 500 × 400/300 = 666.7 cm³ (pressure constant per the question).

    4.3 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Graham's law — hydrogen 4× faster than unknown gas

    • r(H₂)/r(x) = √(M(x)/M(H₂)) → 4 = √(M(x)/2) → M(x) = 16 × 2 = 32 — the gas is oxygen (or an M = 32 species).

    4.4 Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): Dalton's law and aqueous tension

    • State the law (section 2.4). Why subtract aqueous tension: the collected gas is a mixture of the dry gas and water vapour; by Dalton's law each component contributes its own pressure, so the dry gas's share is total minus the water vapour's contribution (aqueous tension, e.g. 17.5 mmHg at 20 °C). Using the wet reading would overestimate the dry gas pressure.

    4.5 Diffusion numerical template

    • Time ratio form: t₁/t₂ = √(M₁/M₂) (equal volumes); volume ratio form uses r = V/t. Oxygen–hydrogen: r(H₂)/r(O₂) = √(32/2) = 4.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Celsius must become Kelvin before any gas-law algebra: using 27 °C directly in Charles's law gives nonsense; add 273 first — the single most common numerical error.

  • Aqueous tension is subtracted, not the other way: the water vapour adds pressure to the wet gas; the dry gas's share is what remains.
  • Diffusion rate ratios invert with molar mass: the lighter gas is faster — writing r ∝ √M reverses the law.

  • 7. Exam Application

    Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): State Graham's law. Hydrogen diffuses 4 times faster than an unknown gas; find its molar mass. — Graham's law: at constant temperature and pressure, the rate of diffusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass; r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁). Given r(H₂)/r(x) = 4: 4 = √(M(x)/2) → M(x) = 16 × 2 = 32 g/mol — the unknown gas is oxygen.

  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): State Dalton's law of partial pressures. Why is aqueous tension subtracted when a gas is collected over water? — Dalton's law: the total pressure exerted by a mixture of non-reacting gases equals the sum of the partial pressures each gas would exert if it alone occupied the vessel. A gas collected over water is saturated with water vapour, so the measured pressure is the sum of the dry gas's pressure and the aqueous tension (water vapour's own partial pressure at that temperature). Since only the dry gas is wanted, its true pressure is P(dry) = P(wet) − aqueous tension; ignoring the correction overstates the gas pressure and every downstream calculation.
  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2021): A gas occupies 500 cm³ at 27 °C and 1 atm. Find its volume at 127 °C (pressure constant). — Convert: 300 K → 400 K. Charles's law: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ → V₂ = 500 × 400/300 = 666.7 cm³.
  • Verified PYQ (NEB 2024): 2 L of gas at 3 atm expands isothermally to 1 atm. Find the new volume. — Boyle's law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → V₂ = (3 × 2)/1 = 6 L.

Confusion

  • STP means 0 °C and 1 atm (molar volume 22.4 L) — not room temperature; quoting 24 L at "STP" confuses NTP/room conditions.
  • Boyle's law needs constant temperature, Charles's needs constant pressure: mixing the constraints (or forgetting which variable is held) derails the choice of equation.

Practice

  • A gas occupies 600 cm³ at 750 mmHg; find the volume at 1.5 atm (T constant).
  • Oxygen effuses through a pinhole in 200 s; how long does SO₂ take for the same volume?
  • A gas collected over water at 25 °C reads 745 mmHg (aqueous tension 23.8 mmHg); find the dry gas pressure.

Formulas

    • P₁V₁ = P₂V₂; V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂; P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂
    • P(total) = p₁ + p₂ + …; P(dry) = P(wet) − aqueous tension
    • r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁); t₁/t₂ = √(M₁/M₂)
    • Molar volume at STP = 22.4 L

Universal Facts

  • Airplane cabins are pressurised to about 0.75 atm — Boyle's law sets how much air passengers carry per breath, and Dalton's law says the oxygen share of that thinner air is what matters.

Examples

  • The rising dough of bread — CO₂ from yeast obeys Charles's law: oven heat expands each bubble in exact T proportion, doubling the dough's gas volume from 30 °C to 180 °C... wait — from 303 K to 453 K, a 1.5× lift.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2024 (2m): 2 L of a gas at 3 atm pressure expands isothermally. What volume will it occupy at 1 atm? — Isothermal means temperature is constant, so Boyle's law applies: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Substituting: 3 atm × 2 L = 1 atm × V₂, giving V₂ = 6/1 = 6 L. When pressure falls to one-third, volume triples — the inverse proportionality of Boyle's law.
  • NEB 2021 (2m): A sample of gas occupies 500 cm³ at 27 °C and 1 atm. Calculate the volume at 127 °C at the same pressure. — Convert temperatures to Kelvin first: T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K; T₂ = 127 + 273 = 400 K. Pressure is constant, so Charles's law applies: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. Therefore V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 500 × 400/300 = 666.7 cm³ (approximately 667 cm³). The volume grows in direct proportion to absolute temperature.
  • NEB 2024 (2m): State Graham's law of diffusion. Hydrogen diffuses 4 times faster than an unknown gas under identical conditions; find the molar mass of the unknown gas. — Graham's law: at constant temperature and pressure, the rate of diffusion (or effusion) of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its density or molar mass: r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁). Given r(H₂)/r(x) = 4 and M(H₂) = 2: 4 = √(M(x)/2). Squaring: 16 = M(x)/2, so M(x) = 32 g/mol — the unknown gas is oxygen (or another species of molar mass 32).
  • NEB 2024 (3m): State Dalton's law of partial pressures. Why is aqueous tension subtracted when a gas is collected over water? — Dalton's law: the total pressure exerted by a mixture of non-reacting gases is the sum of the partial pressures each gas would exert if it alone occupied the container at the same temperature: P(total) = p₁ + p₂ + p₃ + … Aqueous tension: a gas collected over water becomes saturated with water vapour, so the measured pressure is a mixture pressure — the dry gas plus the water vapour. By Dalton's law each component contributes its own partial pressure, and the water vapour's contribution at the collection temperature is the aqueous tension. The dry gas's true pressure is therefore P(dry) = P(wet) − aqueous tension; omitting the correction would credit the water vapour's pressure to the gas and inflate all calculations (e.g. molar masses from vapour-density data).

Summary

Five laws, one toolkit. Boyle (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂) compresses isothermally — 3 atm on 2 L relaxes to 6 L at 1 atm. Charles (V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂) demands Kelvin: 500 cm³ at 300 K swells to 666.7 cm³ at 400 K, and every gas extrapolates to nothing at −273.15 °C. Avogadro equalises molecules per litre (22.4 L at STP). Dalton adds partial pressures — and its immediate corollary, the aqueous-tension correction, is why every gas-over-water reading loses its water-vapour share. Graham runs diffusion on mass: rates ∝ 1/√M, so hydrogen's 4× head start over an unknown unmasks M = 32. The combined equation P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ chains the state-change problems, and all of it collapses into PV = nRT in the next topic. Kelvin first, constraints identified, square roots squared — the laws are generous to the careful.

By Dalton's law each component contributes its own partial pressure, and the water vapour's contribution at the collection temperature is the aqueous tension. The dry gas's true pressure is therefore P(dry) = P(wet) − aqueous tension; omitting the correction would credit the water vapour's pressure to the gas and inflate all calculations (e.g. molar masses from vapour-density data).

Important Concepts

  • All four partial laws are special cases of PV = nRT (topic 45); recognising which variables are constant in a problem selects the right law instantly.

Important Statements

  • "The total pressure exerted by a mixture of non-reacting gases is equal to the sum of the partial pressures which each gas would exert if it alone occupied the whole volume."

Exam Short Tricks

  • "Kelvin before anything". Boyle: multiply, divide (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂). Graham: heavier is slower; square both sides. Over water: subtract aqueous tension. STP = 0 °C, 1 atm, 22.4 L.

Exam Notes

  • State the law in words before the equation — both carry marks. For Graham's numericals, show the squaring step; for aqueous tension, name the temperature at which the tension applies.
