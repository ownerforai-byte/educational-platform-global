Notes

  • 1. Meaning

  • The gas laws are the experimentally established relations between the pressure (P), volume (V), absolute temperature (T) and amount (n) of a fixed mass of gas. They hold for an ideal gas, i.e. at low pressure and high temperature where molecular volume and intermolecular forces are negligible.

  • 2. Boyle's law (1662)

  • At constant temperature the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure: V ∝ 1/P, so PV = constant (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂). Graphically, P versus V is a rectangular hyperbola and P versus 1/V is a straight line through the origin; each curve is called an isotherm. Molecular meaning: compressing the gas raises the number of collisions per unit area per unit time, which increases pressure.

  • 3. Charles' law (1787; published 1802)

  • At constant pressure the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature: V ∝ T, so V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (isobar). A V–t(°C) plot is a straight line meeting the temperature axis at −273.15 °C, the origin of the absolute (Kelvin) scale. The same logic at constant volume gives the pressure law (Gay-Lussac): P ∝ T, P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ (isochore).

  • 4. Avogadro's law (1811)

  • Equal volumes of all gases under the same conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules: V ∝ n at constant T and P. Consequences: molar volume of any gas at STP = 22.4 dm³ (22.4 L) and at RTP ≈ 24 dm³; 1 mol contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules; vapour density and molecular mass can be calculated from gas volumes.

  • 5. Combined gas equation and the ideal gas equation

  • Combining Boyle, Charles and Avogadro for a fixed mass: T1​P1​V1​​=T2​P2​V2​​ and finally PV=nRT, where R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 2 cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. R is the work done by one mole of an ideal gas per kelvin of expansion at constant pressure (universal gas constant).

  • 6. Dalton's law of partial pressures (1801)

  • The total pressure of a mixture of non-reacting gases is the sum of the partial pressures each gas would exert alone in the same volume: P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …; and P_i = x_i P_total where x_i is the mole fraction. Applications: gases collected over water (P_dry = P_total − aqueous tension), scuba and anaesthetic gas mixtures, and vapour-pressure corrections in the laboratory.

  • 7. Graham's law of diffusion (1833)

  • The rate of diffusion (or effusion) of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its density or molar mass at the same T and P: r2​r1​​=d1​d2​​​=M1​M2​​​. Applications: separation of isotopes (²³⁵UF₆/²³⁸UF₆ diffusion), leak detection, determination of molar mass, and explaining why a balloon filled with H₂ deflates faster than one with O₂.

  • 8. Applications

  • • Calculating unknown P, V, T in experiments and industry (gas cylinders, LPG pressure, tyre pressure with temperature change, weather balloons, airbags).
  • • Dalton's law is used for gases collected over water, in scuba-diving and anaesthesia gas mixtures, and in atmospheric humidity calculations; the combined gas equation underlies gas-flow and compression-ratio engineering.
  • • Graham's law is used industrially to separate isotopes (²³⁵UF₆ from ²³⁸UF₆) and in the laboratory to determine molar masses and to detect leaks; Avogadro's law is the basis of molar-volume calculations and hence of all volumetric analysis of gases.

  • 9. Limitations

  • • All the laws are ideal-gas laws: they are obeyed closely only at low pressure and high temperature; real gases deviate (and the deviation is strongest near the critical temperature, which is why gases cannot be liquefied merely by compression above T_c).
  • • Each law needs a fixed mass of gas and holds P, or V, or T constant — applying two at once to a changing mass gives wrong answers.
  • • Dalton's law applies only to non-reacting, non-associated ideal gases; it fails for reacting mixtures (NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl) and for gases that dissolve appreciably in the liquid present.
  • • Graham's law is strictly valid for gases at equal temperature and pressure and for effusion through a small hole; it needs correction for high pressures, for heavier/polyatomic molecules and for diffusion through porous media.
  • • Avogadro's law assumes equal molecular behaviour, though real molecules of different size and polarity behave differently; and the combined equation cannot represent a real gas — the van der Waals equation is required.
  • Fact. Ideal gas equation PV = nRT; from kinetic theory P = ⅓ (Nm/m)⟨c²⟩, so PV = ⅓N m⟨c²⟩.
  • Fact. r.m.s. speed c_rms = √(3RT/M); mean kinetic energy per mole = 3RT/2, per molecule = 3k_BT/2.
  • Fact. Real gases deviate near condensation; Van der Waals: (P + a n²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.

Confusion

  • ❌ Boyle's law means PV is always constant. ✅ Only for a fixed mass at constant temperature; PV changes when T or n changes.
  • ❌ Doubling the Celsius temperature doubles the volume. ✅ Charles' law uses kelvin: 10 °C → 40 °C is a 293 → 313 K change, not a ×4 change.
  • ❌ Dalton's law works for any gas mixture. ✅ It requires non-reacting gases; NH₃ and HCl react, so partial pressures are not additive.
  • ❌ Graham's law compares diffusion rates directly with molar masses. ✅ Rates compare as the inverse square root of molar mass (or density).
  • ❌ Gases obey PV = nRT exactly. ✅ Real gases obey it only at low P and high T; deviation is measured by the compressibility factor Z.

Universal Facts

  • Molar volume of any gas = 22.4 L at STP (0 °C, 1 atm) and ≈24 L at RTP (25 °C, 1 bar).
  • R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 1.987 cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • Absolute zero = −273.15 °C = 0 K; Charles' plot extrapolates to zero volume there.
  • Ideal gas equation PV = nRT; from kinetic theory P = ⅓ (Nm/m)⟨c²⟩, so PV = ⅓N m⟨c²⟩.
  • r.m.s. speed c_rms = √(3RT/M); mean kinetic energy per mole = 3RT/2, per molecule = 3k_BT/2.
  • Real gases deviate near condensation; Van der Waals: (P + a n²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.

Examples

  • Problem: 500 cm³ of a gas at 27 °C and 700 mm Hg — find the volume at STP. Solution: V₂ = P₁V₁T₂/(P₂T₁) = (700 × 500 × 273)/(760 × 300) ≈ 419 cm³.
  • Problem: Ratio of diffusion rates of H₂ and O₂. Solution: r(H₂)/r(O₂) = √(32/2) = 4, i.e. hydrogen diffuses four times faster.

Important Notes
