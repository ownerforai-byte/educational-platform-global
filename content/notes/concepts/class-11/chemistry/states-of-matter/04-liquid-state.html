Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The In-Between State

    Liquids hold a shapeless volume: molecules touch but keep trading places. Four measurable properties — vapour pressure, boiling point, surface tension, viscosity — all trace back to the same balance of attraction and thermal motion, and each one has a favourite exam question.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Evaporation and condensation

    • Evaporation: surface molecules with above-average kinetic energy escape the liquid; it occurs at all temperatures, faster with higher T, larger surface, lower humidity and air movement.
    • Condensation: the reverse — vapour molecules return to the liquid.
    • Cooling by evaporation: the escaping molecules carry away the highest-energy fraction, lowering the average — sweating, earthen-pot cooling, ether's chill on skin.

    2.2 Vapour pressure and boiling point

    • Vapour pressure: the pressure exerted by a vapour in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid in a closed vessel — characteristic of the liquid and the temperature, independent of liquid amount or vessel volume.
    • Boiling point: the temperature at which vapour pressure equals the external pressure; bubbles form throughout the bulk. At 1 atm water boils at 100 °C; on Everest (~0.5 atm) at ~70 °C; in a pressure cooker (~2 atm) at ~120 °C.
    • Boiling point rises with external pressure, falls in vacuum.

    2.3 Surface tension

    • Surface tension (γ): the force per unit length acting along a liquid surface, arising because surface molecules are pulled inward by unbalanced attraction — the surface behaves like a stretched membrane.
    • Units: N/m (SI) or dyne/cm. Water: 0.072 N/m at 25 °C — the highest of common liquids except mercury.
    • Consequences: spherical drops (minimum surface area), capillary rise, meniscus, detergents lowering γ to wet surfaces, camels' feet and insects skating on water.
    • Falls with rising temperature (attraction outpaced by motion) and with dissolved soaps/detergents/salts (most salts raise it).

    2.4 Viscosity

    • Viscosity (η): the internal friction between layers of a flowing liquid — resistance to flow. Units: poise (CGS), Pa·s (SI); water ≈ 1 cP at 20 °C, glycerine ≈ 1000× water, honey, molasses similarly.
    • Falls with rising temperature (opposite of gases, where viscosity rises with T): cooking oil pours easily when warm.
    • Measured by Ostwald viscometer / Stokes' falling sphere; hydrogen-bonded liquids (glycerol, sulphuric acid) are the most viscous.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Dynamic equilibrium in a closed flask

    • Molecules leave and return at equal rates; the vapour pressure is then constant — evaporation hasn't stopped, it has been matched. Open the flask and equilibrium is lost, evaporation continues to dryness.

    4.2 Why boiling depends on external pressure

    • A bubble can only grow if its internal vapour pressure can push back the surrounding liquid column plus atmosphere. Vapour pressure = external pressure is the exact threshold; hence the altitude and pressure-cooker effects, and vacuum evaporation of heat-sensitive materials (sugar refining, milk powder).

    4.3 Surface tension's molecular story

    • A bulk molecule is pulled equally in all directions; a surface molecule feels a net inward pull — the surface contracts to the minimum area (sphere). Energy cost per new area is the surface energy; surfactants halve it by wedging between water molecules.

    4.4 Viscosity's molecular story

    • Flow means layers sliding; attraction between molecules of adjacent layers resists the slide. Stronger intermolecular forces → higher viscosity; heat loosens the grip → viscosity falls. (Gases: momentum transfer between flying molecules — viscosity rises with T.)

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Vapour pressure exists below the boiling point too: every liquid in a closed flask has a vapour pressure at room temperature; boiling merely marks where it equals the atmosphere.

  • Viscosity and surface tension are different properties: viscosity resists flow (bulk), surface tension resists area increase (surface) — glycerine is viscous, mercury has huge surface tension; the two don't track each other.
  • Temperature effects oppose between liquids and gases: liquid viscosity falls with T, gas viscosity rises — molecular pictures differ (attraction vs momentum flight).

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): Define vapour pressure. Why does it not depend on the amount of liquid? — Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by the vapour in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid in a closed container. Once equilibrium is reached, the rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation, and this rate depends only on the liquid's identity and the temperature — not on how much liquid is present (as long as some liquid remains). Doubling the liquid area speeds the approach to equilibrium but not the final pressure.

  • NEB-style (2m): Water boils at about 70 °C on Mount Everest but needs about 120 °C in a pressure cooker. Explain. — Boiling occurs when the liquid's vapour pressure equals the external pressure. On Everest the atmospheric pressure is roughly half an atmosphere, so water's vapour pressure reaches that value at only ~70 °C — food cooks slowly because the water is cooler. In a pressure cooker the trapped steam raises the external pressure to about 2 atm, so boiling is delayed until ~120 °C — hotter water, faster cooking. Same liquid, different boiling points, entirely an external-pressure effect.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why do liquids cool when they evaporate? — Evaporation selectively removes the fastest (highest-kinetic-energy) molecules from the surface. The molecules left behind have a lower average kinetic energy, so the liquid's temperature falls — the heat taken is the latent heat of vaporisation, drawn from the remaining liquid. Sweating, earthen pots and ether's chill on skin all exploit this.

Confusion

  • Boiling vs evaporation: boiling is bulk-wide, at a fixed T set by external pressure; evaporation is surface-only, at any T — the comparison table is the answer frame.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (2m): Define vapour pressure of a liquid. Why is it independent of the amount of liquid taken? — The vapour pressure of a liquid is the pressure exerted by its vapour when in dynamic equilibrium with the liquid in a closed container: molecules evaporate from the surface at the same rate as vapour molecules condense back. Because the equilibrium is dynamic, the pressure depends only on the nature of the liquid (its intermolecular attraction) and the temperature — not on the quantity of liquid. More liquid only increases the evaporation rate temporarily, reaching the same equilibrium pressure sooner; as long as any liquid remains, the final vapour pressure is identical.
  • NEB-style (2m): Water boils at about 70 °C on Mount Everest but at about 120 °C inside a pressure cooker. Explain. — A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure. On Mount Everest the atmospheric pressure is only about half an atmosphere, so water's vapour pressure reaches the external value at roughly 70 °C — boiling starts early but the water is too cool to cook food quickly. Inside a pressure cooker the escaping steam raises the pressure to about 2 atm, so vapour pressure cannot match it until about 120 °C — the water boils later but hotter, cooking faster. The same liquid, the same property, two external pressures: boiling point is a pressure-dependent temperature, not a fixed property of water.
  • NEB-style (2m): Why does evaporation cause cooling of the remaining liquid? — Molecules in a liquid share a distribution of kinetic energies. Evaporation is selective: only the fastest, highest-energy molecules at the surface escape. Removing them lowers the average kinetic energy of the molecules that remain, and since temperature measures that average, the liquid cools. The energy carried away is the latent heat of vaporisation, drawn from the liquid itself — which is why sweating cools the body, water evaporating from an earthen pot cools its contents, and ether dropped on skin feels freezing.

Summary

A liquid is a tug-of-war frozen mid-pull: molecules touch but keep trading partners. Evaporation lets the fastest surface molecules escape at any temperature — and their departure is why sweat cools. In a closed flask, escape and return balance into dynamic equilibrium, and the vapour's steady pressure is a property of liquid-plus-temperature alone. Boiling is that pressure catching up with the atmosphere: equal pressures at 100 °C in Kathmandu's kitchens, ~70 °C on Everest, ~120 °C in a pressure cooker — the boiling point is an appointment with the external pressure, not a fixed date. Surface tension is the unbalanced inward pull on surface molecules contracting the surface to a sphere (water 0.072 N/m, mercury nearly seven times more), powering capillary rise and defeated by detergents. Viscosity is the friction of sliding layers — glycerine's thousand-fold drag over water, loosened by heat in liquids (though gases stiffen). Heat shifts the whole family in one direction: vapour pressure up, boiling point up (with external P), surface tension and viscosity down.
