Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Tug-of-War Inside Every Atom

    Every periodic trend — radius, ionisation energy, electron affinity, metallic character — is one contest: the nuclear charge pulling electrons in versus inner-electron shielding pushing the outer experience out. The score of that contest is the effective nuclear charge (Z_eff). Define it, compute it, and the whole of Unit 4's trend section becomes derivation instead of memorisation.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Nuclear charge (Z)

    • Z = number of protons in the nucleus = the element's atomic number.
    • It is the true pull available: +Z on every electron (Coulomb attraction).

    2.2 Shielding (screening) effect

    Inner-shell electrons sit between the nucleus and the valence electrons, repelling the outer electrons and cancelling part of the nuclear pull. The outer electron feels less than +Z — it is shielded.

    2.3 Effective nuclear charge (Z_eff)

    Z_eff = Z − σ (σ = screening constant, the shielding contribution of the intervening electrons).

    • Example, Na (Z = 11): the single 3s valence electron is shielded by 10 inner electrons → Z_eff ≈ 11 − 10 = +1 (simple estimate) — Na's valence electron is held loosely, hence low ionisation energy and high reactivity.
    • Example, Cl (Z = 17): 7 valence electrons shield each other poorly (same shell) → Z_eff ≈ 17 − 10 − (small same-shell contribution) ≈ +7 — the outer electron is gripped tightly; Cl is a strong electron acceptor.

    2.4 Slater's rules (elementary estimate)

    Approximate σ for a valence electron:

    1. Same-shell (n) other electrons: contribute 0.35 each (0.30 for 1s).
    2. (n − 1) shell electrons: contribute 0.85 each.
    3. Deeper shells (n − 2 and in): contribute 1.00 each.

    Then Z_eff = Z − σ. (NEB needs only the concept and the simple Z − σ form; Slater's numbers are the bonus that shows how the estimate is made.)

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Worked estimates (the 2m template)

    Q: Estimate Z_eff for the valence electron of Li (Z = 3) and F (Z = 9).

    • Li: 1s² shields the 2s¹ electron → Z_eff = 3 − 2 = +1.
    • F: 1s² shields; the other six 2p electrons add small same-shell shielding → Z_eff = 9 − 2 − 6(0.35) ≈ +5.2.

    Result: F's valence electron is pulled ~5× harder than Li's — one line that explains F's tiny radius, huge ionisation energy and electronegativity, and Li's readiness to lose its electron.

    4.2 Z_eff across a period (left → right)

    Electrons enter the same shell; same-shell electrons shield each other poorly (0.35 each) while Z grows by +1 per step → Z_eff rises steadily (Na +1 → Cl +7 roughly). Consequences: radius shrinks, ionisation energy climbs, electronegativity climbs, metallic character falls.

    4.3 Z_eff down a group (top → bottom)

    Each step adds a whole new inner shell (σ jumps by ~8–10) while Z's increase is fully cancelled → Z_eff on the valence shell stays nearly constant. But n grows, so the valence electrons sit farther out: radius grows, ionisation energy falls, metallic character rises.

    4.4 Penetration and the s < p < d < f energy order

    Within the same shell, an s electron penetrates closest to the nucleus (feels the largest Z_eff), then p, then d, then f. That is why 4s fills before 3d (Aufbau) and why subshell energies split — Z_eff is the physical content of the (n + l) rule.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Z_eff is electron-specific: the inner 1s electrons of Na feel Z_eff ≈ +10.9, not +1 — always say which electron you are computing for.

  • Down a group Z grows but Z_eff does not: the new shell's shielding cancels the extra protons; students who quote "Z increases so pull increases down a group" get the trend backwards.
  • Shielding vs penetration: shielding is what other electrons do to you; penetration is how close you get despite them. s penetrates best and is least shielded.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): Why is the first ionisation energy of Na much lower than that of Mg? — Both valence electrons sit in n = 3, but Mg's extra proton raises Z_eff on 3s (Mg ≈ +2 vs Na ≈ +1) while shielding is nearly equal; the stronger net pull holds Mg's 3s² pair more tightly → higher IE.

  • NEB-style (2m): The atomic radius of Na is greater than that of Mg although Mg has more protons. Explain. — Across period 3, Z_eff rises (Na ≈ +1 → Mg ≈ +2); the same shell is pulled inward more strongly in Mg → smaller radius. (The other half of NEB 2022's irregularity pair — see topic 26.)
  • NEB-style (3m): Define effective nuclear charge and explain its variation across a period and down a group. — Z_eff = Z − σ, the net nuclear pull an electron feels after shielding by intervening electrons. Across a period: electrons enter the same shell, same-shell shielding is poor, so Z_eff rises → radius falls, IE rises. Down a group: each element adds an inner shell whose strong shielding cancels the added protons, so valence Z_eff stays nearly constant while n grows → radius grows, IE falls.

Confusion

  • Z vs Z_eff: Z is the total proton count and never changes with position; Z_eff is what a particular electron actually feels — quoting one where the other is asked loses the mark.
  • Same-shell shielding is weak (0.35): that is the entire reason Z_eff rises across a period; treating all shielding as equal makes the trend disappear.

Key Points

Summary

Every periodic trend is the contest between nuclear charge Z (the proton count, the total pull) and shielding σ (inner electrons cancelling part of that pull); the score is Z_eff = Z − σ, the net pull an electron actually feels. Across a period, electrons enter the same shell and shield each other poorly, so Z_eff climbs (Na ≈ +1 → Cl ≈ +7): radii shrink, ionisation energies and electronegativity rise, metallic character falls. Down a group, each new inner shell shields almost completely, so valence Z_eff stays nearly constant while n grows: radii and reactivity of metals rise, ionisation energy falls. Penetration (s > p > d > f) is the same physics inside a shell and explains 4s-before-3d. Compute one Z_eff and the trend table of topic 26 writes itself.
