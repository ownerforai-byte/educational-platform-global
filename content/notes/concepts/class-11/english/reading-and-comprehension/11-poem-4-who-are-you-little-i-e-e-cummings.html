Notes

  • The poem at a glance

    "who are you, little i" (1961) by E. E. Cummings is eight lines long and looks almost carelessly written — no capitals where they should be, line breaks in odd places — and is in fact built as carefully as a watch. The speaker, waking near the end of November, watches a small child ("little i") at a window, gazing out at frost and falling snow. The poem's whole meaning lives in its form: the lower-case "i" that is Cummings' signature turns a letter into a person, and the question of the title — who are you, little i? — is the poem asking who the self is: the adult remembering, or the child still watching?

  • Reading it closely, line by line

    The opening question names the mystery before explaining it: "who are you, little i". The letter i — the self, reduced to its smallest possible size — is addressed as you, as if the speaker meets himself and is not sure which of him is speaking. The ambiguity is deliberate and is the poem's engine: the "little i" may be an actual small child at the window, or the speaker's own childhood self, or the poem's own lower-case i looking out at the world. Good answers hold all three and refuse to murder two of them.

    "(five or six years old) scraping in the snow" — the parenthesis does what parentheses do: it whispers a fact while the main sentence watches. The child is at the window with "the blankets and other beautiful things" — the details are household, ordinary, concrete: Cummings finds wonder without leaving the bedroom.

    Then the turn, in the poem's longest word: "how should tasting touching hearing seeing breathing — any — human being be careless". The list is the poem's centre: five senses stacked, asyndeton (no "and"s) making the verbs arrive like footsteps, and the meaning arriving with them — to be alive is to be tasting, touching, hearing, seeing, breathing, and to be careless of "this moment" is the only real poverty. The final lines call the child's gaze "the gold flush of a flower" — the moment made metaphor: perception itself blooms.

    Note what the poem does with capitalisation — Cummings refuses it, including on "i". The effect is not sloppiness but democracy: no word is more important than another, and the self is small enough to wonder. In an exam answer, form is content here — the question "why does Cummings use small letters?" is really: what does the smallness say? The smallness says: wonder.

  • The devices, with evidence

    • Ambiguity — the master device: "little i" is child, past self, and letter, all at once; the poem lives on the overlap.
    • Personification of a letter — the i addressed as a person: typography turned into character.
    • Imagery (sensory list) — "tasting touching hearing seeing breathing": the whole body made an instrument of attention.
    • Asyndeton — the missing conjunctions make the senses arrive unbroken, the way experience arrives.
    • Simile/metaphor — the moment's "gold flush of a flower": attention described as bloom.
    • Form as device — lower case, odd spacing, parenthesis: the typography is the tone (intimate, humble, playful).

    The exam question "what is unusual about the form of the poem?" is answered by naming the lower-case "i" and the line breaks, then saying what they do: they make the self small, the voice intimate, and the reader slow down — which is the poem's subject, attention, performed in its very shape.

  • The themes worth an essay

    Wonder as a way of seeing — the child does not see more than the adult; the child notices more. The sacredness of the ordinary moment — frost and snow, the plainest winter props, treated as revelation. The inner child — the adult speaker recovering his own eyes through the child at the window. And mindfulness before the word existed — the poem's ethics: to be careless of any moment is to waste the only life you are given. The theme that ties them: attention is love; the child is good at it, and the adult is learning.

  • The title, defended

    "who are you, little i" is the poem's own first line, asked in the poem's own grammar — small letters included. The defence: the title is a question the poem never fully answers on purpose; it hands the reader the mystery in miniature (even the title's i is lower case) and lets the eight lines deepen it rather than close it. A title that asks is rarer and more honest than a title that tells.

  • How NEB asks it

    "Who is 'little i' in the poem?" (2–4 marks) — hold the ambiguity: the child at the window, and the speaker's own childhood self; the poem means both. "Why does Cummings write in lower case?" — intimacy and smallness: the self humbled, wonder enlarged; form performing meaning. "What does the poem say about being careless of a moment?" — the senses list is life itself; carelessness is the only real waste. "How does the poem make an ordinary moment extraordinary?" — the concrete details, the senses stacked, the flower metaphor: attention is the alchemy.

    And the transferable habit: when a poet breaks the rules of print (capitals, spacing, punctuation), the broken rule is a device. Ask what the break does — and the answer is usually the poem's whole tone in one stroke.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: The speaker watches a small child ('little i') watching the sunset — the poem is about wonder and the adult's lost ability to see freshly; state this reversal.
  • Trick 2: Cummings's typography: lowercase 'i', broken syntax, compressed lines — the form itself enacts childlike simplicity; name the style.
  • Trick 3: November sunset imagery: 'gold red' sky, 'the quivering leaf' — connect one image to the mood of quiet wonder.

Practice Questions

  • Q. Who is 'little i' and what is he doing? (2 marks) — a small child gazing at the November sunset from a window.
  • Q. Why does Cummings write 'i' in lowercase? (1 mark) — to humble the ego and mirror the child's smallness and wonder.

Key Points

  • The speaker addresses a small child (himself) — innocence vs adult compromise.
