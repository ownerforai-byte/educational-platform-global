Notes

  • The poem at a glance

    "All the World's a Stage" is Jaques' famous speech from Shakespeare's As You Like It (Act II, Scene vii) — technically drama, treated in the syllabus as a poem, and one of the most extended extended metaphors in English: the world is a stage, all people are actors, and a life is a play in seven acts. The seven "ages of man" — infant, schoolboy, lover, soldier, justice, pantaloon (the retired old man), and second childhood — walk across the stage in order, each with a costume and a line. The speech is melancholy in flavour (Jaques is the play's resident pessimist) and comic in observation: Shakespeare watches humanity the way a photographer watches a street.

  • Reading it closely, age by age

    The opening metaphor sets the rules: "All the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players." Merely is the knife in the sentence — not just players, but only players: our roles are assigned, our exits not ours to schedule. Once the metaphor is accepted, everything that follows must obey theatre logic, and it does: entrances and exits, acts and scenes, costumes and parts.

    The infant "mewls and pukes in the nurse's arms" — Shakespeare does not sentimentalise the beginning; the first act is noise and sickness. The schoolboy "creeps like snail unwillingly to school" — the simile every student in Nepal can feel, and the satchel and shining morning face are the costume and makeup of act two.

    The lover "sighs like furnace, with a woeful ballad made to his mistress' eyebrow" — the second simile, and Shakespeare's gentle mockery: love as heat, and poetry written to an eyebrow, which is exactly how ridiculous and how sincere young love is. The soldier is full of "strange oaths" and "bearded like the pard" — ambition as costume: quick to quarrel, "seeking the bubble reputation" — honour described as a bubble, the speech's sharpest word, an ambition that pops at a touch.

    The justice with round belly and wise saws is authority as performance: the words are quotations ("instances of modern instance"), wisdom recycled from other plays. The pantaloon — the lean, slippered old man of Italian comedy — shrinks into youth's clothes, a reversal: the man who once filled the stage now barely fills his stockings. And the seventh age is "second childishness and mere oblivion, sans teeth, sans eyes, sans taste, sans everything" — the final repetition hammering sans (without) like nails in a coffin lid: the play ends where it began, in helplessness, minus even the noise.

  • The devices, with evidence

    • Extended metaphor — the master device: world = stage, life = play, and every detail obedient to it (entrances, exits, parts, acts).
    • Simile — "creeps like snail", "sighs like furnace", "bearded like the pard": each age given an animal or an object that is its temperament.
    • Imagery — theatrical and bodily at once: satchels, beards, round bellies, slippers, hose.
    • Alliteration and repetition — "sans teeth, sans eyes, sans taste, sans everything": rhythm used as verdict.
    • Irony — the "bubble reputation": honour, the thing men die for, named as soap film. And the deepest irony: the speech about acting is itself an actor's speech, delivered by a character in a play.
    • Tone — wry, detached, melancholy: observation without consolation.

    The exam question "explain the comparison of life to a stage" is answered by holding the two halves: the metaphor flatters (life is performance, we all have parts) and disturbs (we do not write our parts, and the exits are scheduled).

  • The themes worth an essay

    The stages of life — the surface theme, and the exam's favourite: identify the seven ages and their traits. The predictability of human life — Jaques implies every life runs the same route; the comfort and the horror are the same fact. Role-playing and identity — we are our costumes as much as our selves; the lover is only a lover while the part lasts. Time as the only villain — no enemy appears in the speech except duration; the play runs and cannot be rerun. And the quiet question under all of it: if we are merely players, what is left that is ours? The speech does not answer — which is why four centuries of students keep asking it.

  • The title, defended

    The speech is known by its first line because the first line is the poem: "All the world's a stage" is not a title hung on an argument but the argument itself, stated as fact. A candidate defending it should say exactly that: the title performs the metaphor's confidence — no "perhaps", no "like" — and the certainty is what makes the seven ages follow so obediently.

  • How NEB asks it

    "List the seven ages of man with one trait each." (7 marks) — infant (helpless), schoolboy (unwilling), lover (sentimental), soldier (ambitious), justice (pompous), pantaloon (shrinking), second childhood (dependent). Learn them as a walk, not a list. "Explain: 'seeking the bubble reputation'." (2 marks) — the soldier risks death for honour, which is fragile as a bubble: fame pops at a touch. "Why does the poet compare old age to second childhood?" — the reversal: dependence returns, the circle closes, and the speech's symmetry (helpless to helpless) is its structure. "What is the tone — optimistic or pessimistic? Justify." — detached and melancholy; merely and sans carry the verdict.

    And the habit this speech teaches for all drama-in-the-syllabus: remember a speech has a speaker with a position. Jaques is not Shakespeare; he is a professional pessimist in a comedy, and the play is gently laughing at him even as the audience memorises him. Answers that know this write about the speech; answers that do not, write inside it.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: The extended metaphor: life = drama, people = actors, God = playwright — state the controlling metaphor before anything else.
  • Trick 2: The seven ages in order: infant, schoolboy, lover, soldier, justice, pantaloon (old man), second childhood — memorise the list; sequencing questions are guaranteed.
  • Trick 3: The last age is the harshest: sans teeth, sans eyes, sans taste, sans everything — the repetition 'sans' drives the message of decline; quote it.

Practice Questions

  • Q. List the seven ages of man. (3 marks) — infant, schoolboy, lover, soldier, justice, pantaloon, second childhood.
  • Q. What is the message of the poem? (2 marks) — life is a predictable performance from entry to exit; humans merely play their parts.

Key Points

  • Extended metaphor: life as theatre; the seven ages are seven acts.
  • Know the stages in order — infant, schoolboy, lover, soldier, justice, pantaloon, second childhood.
