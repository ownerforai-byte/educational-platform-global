Notes

  • The poem at a glance

    "The Gift in Wartime" by Tran Mong Tu is a Vietnamese war poem built as a one-sided conversation with the dead. The speaker brings offerings to a grave — flowers, a wedding gown, letters — and the dead soldier answers, item by item, with what war gave him instead: the smell of gunpowder, a medal, his youth. The device that carries everything is irony of structure: the word gift in the title belongs to the living, and every "gift" the poem actually records is something no one would choose. It is an anti-war poem that never raises its voice, which is why it lands so hard.

  • Reading it closely, exchange by exchange

    The poem is built as call and response — the most human of structures: an offering, an answer; an offering, an answer. The speaker brings "the fragrance of flowers" — and the answer is "the smell of gunpowder": the two smells are the poem's first juxtaposition, garden against battlefield, and the war wins every exchange by rules the reader did not agree to.

    She brings the wedding gown — the poem's most private, most hopeful object — and the answer is "the Medal and the tattered flag": the state's honours where a marriage should have been. The irony here is institutional as well as personal: the country gave him a medal; she had promised him a life. Which gift was worth more is not a question the poem needs to ask aloud.

    She brings letters — words, the living's only export to the dead — and the answer is "my youth". Not a thing but a duration: what war took was not an object but the years themselves. The exchange completes the poem's terrible arithmetic: everything she gives is replaceable; everything he gives back is not.

    And the close turns the conversation inward: she asks what she can bring now, and the answer is herself — "come to me, close my eyes" — the bride finally joining the groom in the only place war left for them. Whether read as reunion or as the speaker's own death-wish, the ending makes the cost of war familiar: it is not statistics but a wedding interrupted.

  • The devices, with evidence

    • Structural irony — the title's "gift" promised and the poem's gifts delivered are opposites; the gap between the word and the world is the poem.
    • Apostrophe — the entire poem addresses one who cannot answer; the dead soldier's replies are imagined, which makes the one-sidedness of war literal.
    • Juxtaposition — flowers/gunpowder, wedding gown/medal, letters/youth: hope set beside what war returns.
    • Symbolism — the wedding gown (the future that will not happen), the medal (the state's substitution for a life), the tattered flag (the nation stitched from sacrifice).
    • Tone — quiet, ceremonial, almost tender: grief without shouting, which is why it indicts so effectively.
    • Reversal — the final offering reverses the direction of the poem: she who brought gifts becomes the gift.

    The exam question "why is the poem titled 'The Gift in Wartime'?" is answered by holding title and text together: the gifts of love are met by the gifts of war, and the second set ruins the first — the title promises a birthday and delivers an epitaph.

  • The themes worth an essay

    The cost of war measured in the personal — not territory or treaties but a wedding, letters, youth. The hollowness of medals — the state's honours as poor currency for a life; the poem's quietest accusation. Love that outlasts death — the speaker keeps visiting, keeps offering: fidelity as the poem's engine. And war's theft of the future — every gift in the poem belongs to a future that will not arrive; the poem is grief for what never happened, which is war's signature crime.

  • The title, defended

    "The Gift in Wartime" — the title's irony is the poem's architecture. The defence a candidate should write: in peacetime, gifts celebrate; in wartime, the poem shows, they compensate — flowers for gunpowder, a gown for a medal, letters for youth. The title is sincere on the surface and broken underneath, and the poem is the sound of that breaking.

  • How NEB asks it

    "What gifts does the speaker bring, and what does she receive?" (4 marks) — the three exchanges, named and paired; the structure is the answer. "What does the medal symbolise?" — the state's substitution: honour where a husband should have been. "How does the poem show the tragedy of war?" — not through battle but through the interrupted wedding: the personal ledger of war. "Why does the poem end with the speaker offering herself?" — love that persists past death; the last gift is the giver.

    And the habit this poem teaches for all war poetry: resist the temptation to argue about the war. The poem does not take sides on the fighting; it takes the side of the bride. Answers that stay inside the poem's ledger — what was given, what was taken — are the ones that read as understanding rather than opinion.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Structure: each stanza pairs a GIFT the speaker offers with the war's grim 'gift' returned — the ironic exchange is the poem's device; explain one pair.
  • Trick 2: Anti-war message through personal loss: flowers and youth exchanged for a grave and death — the contrast between tender gifts and war's returns is the theme.
  • Trick 3: The address to the dead beloved (apostrophe) makes grief intimate — name the device and its emotional effect.

Practice Questions

  • Q. What 'gifts' does war return to the speaker? (2 marks) — her beloved's grave, the smell of death, a lost youth — war's cruel exchanges.
  • Q. What is the tone of the poem? (1 mark) — mournful and bitterly ironic.
