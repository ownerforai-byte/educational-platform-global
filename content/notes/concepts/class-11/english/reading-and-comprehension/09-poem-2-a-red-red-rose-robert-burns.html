Notes

  • The poem at a glance

    "A Red, Red Rose" (1794) by Robert Burns is a Scots lyric — a song, really — in which a speaker tells his beloved how long and how deeply he loves her. It is the NEB syllabus's purest lesson in simile, hyperbole and refrain: nearly every line is built on one of the three, and the poem works because the comparisons are enormous and the music is simple. Burns wrote it to be sung (it is built on a folk tune), which is why the rhythms are so regular that the poem almost hums when read aloud.

  • Reading it closely, stanza by stanza

    First stanza — the declaration by comparison. The beloved is "like a red, red rose" — the poem's most famous simile — and "like a melody that's sweetly played in tune". Notice the strategy: she is not described; she is compared to the most beautiful things the speaker knows, a June flower and a piece of perfect music. The repetition ("red, red", "deep, deep") is not childish; it is song-craft, the doubling that a tune needs and a heart feels.

    Second stanza — love measured in geology. "Till a' the seas gang dry" — the speaker now declares duration: he will love her until the oceans empty. This is hyperbole so extreme it becomes tender: no honest lover believes it, and that is the point — the exaggeration is the sincerity. The rocks melting with the sun completes the picture: love outlasting the physics of the planet.

    Third stanza — the arithmetic of devotion. The seas dry, the rocks melt, and still he will love her — the speaker adds time to time, "the sands o' life" running, and love running faster. The device is the same hyperbole, now stacked: each impossible event is merely the next milestone of his affection.

    Fourth stanza — the farewell. The only stanza that touches reality: the speaker must leave, "for a while", and promises to return "tho' 'twere ten thousand mile". The distance is the poem's one concrete fact — and the hyperbole returns in the number, vast but countable, a soldier's or sailor's distance. The refrain "my luve" closes the circle: the poem began with love as a rose and ends with love as a promise.

  • The devices, with evidence

    • Simile — "my luve is like a red, red rose"; "like a melody that's sweetly played in tune": the beloved rendered as flower and music, beauty seen and beauty heard.
    • Hyperbole — seas going dry, rocks melting, ten thousand miles: love declared in units the planet cannot survive. The exam question "why does the poet use exaggeration?" wants this answer: to make the depth of feeling visible; ordinary words cannot carry it.
    • Repetition/refrain — "red, red rose", "deep, deep", "my luve": song-craft and emotional hammering at once.
    • Imagery — visual (the rose, the melting rocks) and auditory (the melody): a love addressed to the senses.
    • Dialect — "luve, gang, o', tho'": Burns writes in Scots, and the dialect is itself a device — intimacy, folk authenticity, the language of the heart rather than the court.

    The exam question "what figures of speech dominate the poem?" is answered in one sentence: simile opens it, hyperbole carries it, and the refrain binds it — a song about love that talks like a lover talks: in comparisons too big to be true and too sincere to doubt.

  • The themes worth an essay

    Love as intensity, not logic — the poem never argues; it declares. Love versus time — the true antagonist of the poem is not a rival but duration, and love is shown beating it. Parting and fidelity — the fourth stanza's real promise: distance is temporary, devotion is not. And the quiet democratic theme: Burns, the ploughman poet, writes love in the speech of ordinary people — the rose and the melody belong to everyone, which is why the poem has never gone out of fashion.

  • The title, defended

    "A Red, Red Rose" takes its title from the opening simile — the poem's first and most famous image. The defence a candidate should write: the title announces the poem's method (comparison), its tone (fresh, natural, alive) and its subject (love in full bloom) in three words; the doubled "red" is the poem's music beginning before the poem does.

  • How NEB asks it

    "What does the poet compare his love to?" (2 marks) — a red, red rose (sight) and a sweetly played melody (sound): fresh, beautiful, harmonious. "Why does the speaker use exaggeration?" — to make love's depth visible; hyperbole is sincerity at full volume. "What promise does the speaker make in the last stanza?" — to return however far he must come, however long it takes. "What is the significance of the dialect?" — intimacy and folk voice: love in the language of home.

    And a reading habit this poem teaches for all lyric poetry: read it aloud once before analysing. The rhymes (June/tune, dry/sky) and the beat are not decoration — they are the poem's argument that love is music. Analysis that ignores the music misses half the meaning.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: The four similes are the poem — rose (beauty), melody (sweetness), the seas drying (eternity), the flower of June (youth); each simile = one mark in analysis.
  • Trick 2: Hyperbole question: 'till a' the seas gang dry' and 'rocks melt wi' the sun' are deliberate exaggerations of eternal love — name the device and its effect.
  • Trick 3: Dialect note: 'bonnie', 'gang', 'wi'' — Scots dialect adds intimacy and folk-song quality; mention it for the style mark.

Practice Questions

  • Q. What two things does the speaker compare his love to? (2 marks) — a red rose and a sweet melody.
  • Q. Why does the speaker use exaggeration? (1 mark) — to express the boundless, eternal nature of his love.

Key Points

  • Similes drive the poem: love compared to a red rose and a sweet melody.
  • Theme: eternal love — 'till a' the seas gang dry' uses hyperbole for devotion.
