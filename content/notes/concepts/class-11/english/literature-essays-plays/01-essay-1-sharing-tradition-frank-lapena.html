Notes

  • The essay at a glance

    "Sharing Tradition" by Frank LaPena is a short essay with a long memory: it argues that a culture survives only when its traditions are passed on by voice — stories, songs, dances, and the language they live in — from elders to the young. LaPena, a Wintu artist and writer from Northern California, speaks from inside the tradition he defends, and the essay's quiet authority comes from that position: he is not describing oral tradition from a library; he is one of its carriers. The argument is simple and urgent: culture is not a museum object but a living chain, and every generation is a link that either holds or breaks.

  • The argument, step by step

    The essay opens on the mechanism: tradition is shared by word of mouth, and the sharing has rules — the young must sit, listen, and try to understand, and the elders must be given the respect that makes speaking possible. LaPena is precise about what travels in the voice: language first (a language carries a people's way of seeing), then the stories and songs that hold history, law and humour, then the practical knowledge — plants, medicine, land, weather — that kept communities alive for centuries.

    Then the pressures, named honestly. The modern world competes for the young's attention; written records, however useful, are secondary — a tape or a book freezes a living act; and mistakes happen in transmission, which LaPena accepts without panic: the elders correct, the chain self-repairs, but only if it keeps running. His attitude to technology is measured, not hostile: tools can help, but they cannot replace the relationship between speaker and listener, because what is transmitted is not just information but trust.

    The close lands on responsibility: the young must come back and listen before the voices are gone. The essay's logic is an inheritance argument — you did not earn the culture; you are holding it in trust — and its plea is for the next link in the chain.

  • The devices, with evidence

    • Ethos — the essay's foundation: LaPena writes as a participant, not a researcher; every claim carries the weight of someone whose culture is at stake.
    • Repetition — the ideas of listening, respect and passing on return in each paragraph like a refrain, building the essay's patience.
    • Contrast — the living voice against the frozen record; the elder's presence against the machine; the old obligation against the modern distraction.
    • Calm tone — no anger, no nostalgia-mongering: the essay argues as someone confident the tradition can survive if the young simply return to it.
    • Imperative mood — the close turns descriptive sentences into instructions: listen, respect, share.

    The exam question "what is the essay's main argument?" is answered in one sentence: oral tradition is the only full way to pass on a culture, and it survives only through the young's active listening and respect.

  • The themes worth an essay

    Oral tradition as cultural survival — the central theme: a people's memory, values and knowledge travel by voice, and the voice needs listeners. Respect for elders — not decorative politeness but the precondition of transmission; the elder speaks fully only to a listener who honours the gift. Language as identity — when a language thins, the thought inside it thins with it. Continuity and change — LaPena accepts that the world changes; the essay's demand is that change not sever the chain. An essay on any of these should use the essay's own image-system: voice, listening, the chain, the season of learning.

  • The title, defended

    "Sharing Tradition" names the essay's verb, not its object — and the choice is exact. Tradition is not kept (which hoards it) or preserved (which embalms it) but shared, which requires two living parties. The defence a candidate should write: the title makes the young generation a participant rather than an audience, and that participation is the essay's whole demand.

  • NEB short answers, solved

    Q. What is oral tradition? (2 marks) A. Oral tradition is the passing of a people's culture — stories, songs, dances, history, practical knowledge and values — from generation to generation by word of mouth rather than in writing. In the essay it is described as the primary way a community keeps its identity alive.

    Q. Why is listening to elders important? (2 marks) A. Because the elders are the tradition's living carriers: their stories and songs hold the community's history, law and knowledge. Listening respectfully keeps the chain of transmission unbroken; without listeners, the knowledge dies with its speakers.

    Q. What role does language play in the essay? (2 marks) A. Language is the vessel of the whole tradition: a people's way of thinking and seeing the world is embedded in its words. If the language is lost, the stories and songs that carry culture lose their home, so keeping the language is keeping the culture.

    Q. How does the writer view modern technology? (2 marks) A. Measuredly: technology can help record and spread material, but it cannot replace the human relationship between the speaker and the listener. A recording freezes a living act; the real transmission needs presence, respect and trust.

    Q. What happens when transmission is imperfect? (2 marks) A. Mistakes and omissions occur, as they always have, but the tradition corrects itself: elders verify and repair what is retold. The system survives small errors — what it cannot survive is silence, the absence of anyone left to tell it.

    Q. What does the essay ask of young people? (2 marks) A. To return and listen: to give the elders their attention and respect, to learn the language, and to carry the tradition forward themselves. The essay treats the young as trustees of an inheritance they did not earn and must not lose.

  • NEB long answers, solved

    Q. "Tradition survives only when it is shared." Discuss with reference to the essay. (8 marks) A model answer opens with the essay's central mechanism: LaPena presents culture as a chain that exists only in transmission — stories, songs and knowledge pass from elder to young by voice, and each link must actively hold. It then develops the two conditions the essay sets: the elder's willingness to speak, which depends on respect, and the young's willingness to listen, which depends on humility. The answer should use the essay's own contrasts — the living voice against the frozen record, presence against the machine — and close on the responsibility argument: the young did not earn the culture, they hold it in trust, and sharing is how the trust is kept. A strong answer quotes or paraphrases the listening scenes rather than generalising about "culture".

    Q. Can technology replace oral tradition? Argue for or against with evidence from the essay. (8 marks) A model answer concedes the genuine benefits first — recordings preserve rare songs, archives survive disasters, the internet carries a tradition across continents. It then presents LaPena's distinction: what is transmitted in oral tradition is not just information but relationship — tone, presence, correction, trust — and a recording is a snapshot of a living act. The strongest answers land on synthesis: technology as servant, not substitute — tools that help the chain without replacing its links — and a final sentence that returns to the essay's own image: the voice still needs a listener.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Central claim: tradition survives only when elders are listened to — LaPena blames the young's inattention, not modernity itself; quote the blame line.
  • Trick 2: The essay's structure is argument by examples (storytelling, art, nature) — answer 'how does the writer support his view?' by listing two examples.
  • Trick 3: Apply to Nepal: oral traditions, festivals and crafts face the same neglect — one Nepali parallel lifts the answer.

Practice Questions

  • Q. According to LaPena, why are traditions being lost? (2 marks) — the young do not listen to elders; modern distractions replace storytelling.
  • Q. How can young people keep traditions alive? (2 marks) — listen to and record elders' stories; practise and pass on customs.

Key Points

  • Tradition survives only when shared — storytelling is the method of preservation.
  • LaPena blends Native American worldview: art, ritual and nature are one.
