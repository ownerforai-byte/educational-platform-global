Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — What Biology Studies and How Far It Reaches

    Biology (Greek bios = life, logos = study) is the science of living organisms — their structure, function, origin, evolution and relations. Its scope runs from molecules to the biosphere, and its fields split along three axes: the kind of organism (botany, zoology, microbiology), the level of organisation (molecular to ecosystem), and the applied purpose (medicine, agriculture, environment).

    5.2 Model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Five branches of biology with a problem each. Answer: (1) Botany — studies plants; e.g. why sal forests fail to regenerate under heavy lopping. (2) Zoology — studies animals; e.g. how tiger density responds to prey availability. (3) Microbiology — studies microorganisms; e.g. how Lactobacillus sours milk into curd. (4) Ecology — studies organism–environment relations; e.g. why eutrophication kills lake fish. (5) Genetics — studies heredity and variation; e.g. how haemophilia passes from carrier mothers to sons.

    Q2 (2m) — Basic vs applied research, two examples each. Answer: Basic research seeks knowledge for its own sake — e.g. discovering how DNA replicates; working out how the lac operon is regulated. Applied research targets a practical problem — e.g. producing human insulin in E. coli; developing Bt insect-resistant cotton. Basic research builds the principles applied research exploits.

    Q3 (2m) — Any four branches and what each studies. Answer: (1) Cytology — the structure and function of cells; (2) Physiology — the life processes of organisms (e.g. photosynthesis, respiration); (3) Taxonomy — the classification and naming of organisms; (4) Ecology — the interactions of organisms with each other and their environment. (Any four valid pairs.)

    5.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Naming a branch without saying what it studies — each pair earns the mark.
    2. Confusing anatomy (internal structure) with morphology (external form).
    3. Giving an applied example as basic research — test: is the outcome knowledge or a product?
    4. Listing "biology, chemistry, physics" as branches — those are other sciences.

Original Content

Notes
  • Biology: the study of living organisms (Greek: bios = life, logos = study).
  • Scope: from molecules (DNA, proteins) to cells, tissues, organs, organisms, populations, communities, ecosystems, and the biosphere.
  • Branches: Botany (plants), Zoology (animals), Microbiology (microorganisms), Genetics (heredity), Ecology (organism-environment), Physiology (function), Anatomy (structure), Taxonomy (classification), Biotechnology (application).
  • Characteristics of life: cellular organisation, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, response to stimuli, evolution, and adaptation.
  • Scientific method: observation → question → hypothesis → experiment → data → conclusion → (publish/peer review).
  • Biology is an experimental science; it uses controlled experiments, measurement, and statistical analysis.
  • NEB focus: definition, scope, fields (branches), and characteristics of life.
Confusion
  • Biology is NOT the same as medicine or agriculture; it is the foundational science, and medicine/agriculture are applied fields.
  • Botany studies plants (including algae and fungi in some systems); Zoology studies animals.
  • Microbiology includes bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists; it overlaps with botany and zoology.
  • Physiology = function; Anatomy = structure; they are complementary, not the same.
  • A "characteristic of life" must be present in ALL living things (e.g., metabolism, reproduction); not all organisms reproduce sexually (some reproduce asexually).
  • The scientific method is iterative; a single experiment rarely proves a hypothesis.

Universal Facts
  • There are approximately 8.7 million species on Earth (only ~15% described).
  • Biology is the fastest-growing science; over 200,000 biology research papers are published annually.
  • DNA (the genetic material) is the basis of modern genetics.
  • The term "biology" was first used by Goethe (1801) and popularised by Tiedemann (1830).
Examples
  • Botany: Oryza sativa (rice) — a monocot, angiosperm, Poaceae.
  • Zoology: Rana tigrina (tiger frog) — an amphibian, Chordata, Anura.
  • Microbiology: Escherichia coli — a Gram-negative, prokaryotic bacterium.
  • Genetics: Mendel's pea experiments — the foundation of modern genetics.
  • Ecology: a pond ecosystem — producers, consumers, decomposers, abiotic factors.

Summary

Biology is the scientific study of living organisms, from molecules (DNA, proteins) to the entire biosphere. It encompasses branches such as Botany, Zoology, Microbiology, Genetics, Ecology, Physiology, Anatomy, Taxonomy, and Biotechnology. Living things share seven characteristics: cellular organisation, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, response to stimuli, and evolution. Biology uses the scientific method (observation, hypothesis, experiment, conclusion) to build testable knowledge about life.

  • Which branch would you choose to study Nepal's yarsagumba trade, and why?

    Answer. Ecology (supported by economic botany and conservation biology). Yarsagumba (Ophiocordyceps sinensis, a fungus parasitic on alpine caterpillars) is harvested from alpine meadows above ~3,500 m, so the question is really an organism–environment–human interaction problem: how harvest pressure affects wild populations, what sustainable yield is, and how livelihoods depend on it. Ecology supplies the population and habitat tools; conservation biology supplies the sustainability framework.

Etymology and definition

Points
  • From Greek bios (life) + logos (study): the study of living organisms and their interactions
  • A living thing is defined by a set of properties, not by one feature alone

Defining properties of life

Points
  • Growth, reproduction, metabolism, response to stimuli, homeostasis, cellular organisation
  • Viruses sit outside the definition: they reproduce only inside a host cell
  • Organisation is hierarchical: molecule -> organelle -> cell -> tissue -> organ -> organism -> population -> community -> ecosystem -> biosphere

Structural and functional branches

1.
Name

Scientific method as a loop

Points
  • Observation -> question -> hypothesis -> prediction -> controlled experiment -> analysis -> revision
  • A hypothesis must be falsifiable; a theory is a well-tested explanatory framework, not a guess
  • Controls and variables: independent (manipulated), dependent (measured), controlled (held constant)

Name

Modern scale of the field

Points
  • About 8.7 million eukaryotic species estimated; roughly 1.5-2 million described so far
  • Genomics, CRISPR and single-cell sequencing moved biology from description to engineering
  • Model organisms (E. coli, yeast, Drosophila, Arabidopsis, zebrafish, mouse) carry most mechanistic work

Common confusions
