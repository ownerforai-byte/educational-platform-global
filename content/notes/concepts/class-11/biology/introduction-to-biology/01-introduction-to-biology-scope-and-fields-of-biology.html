Enriched Content

Notes
  • Biology: the study of living organisms (Greek: bios = life, logos = study).
  • Scope: from molecules (DNA, proteins) to cells, tissues, organs, organisms, populations, communities, ecosystems, and the biosphere.
  • Branches: Botany (plants), Zoology (animals), Microbiology (microorganisms), Genetics (heredity), Ecology (organism-environment), Physiology (function), Anatomy (structure), Taxonomy (classification), Biotechnology (application).
  • Characteristics of life: cellular organisation, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, response to stimuli, evolution, and adaptation.
  • Scientific method: observation → question → hypothesis → experiment → data → conclusion → (publish/peer review).
  • Biology is an experimental science; it uses controlled experiments, measurement, and statistical analysis.
  • NEB focus: definition, scope, fields (branches), and characteristics of life.
Confusion
  • Biology is NOT the same as medicine or agriculture; it is the foundational science, and medicine/agriculture are applied fields.
  • Botany studies plants (including algae and fungi in some systems); Zoology studies animals.
  • Microbiology includes bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists; it overlaps with botany and zoology.
  • Physiology = function; Anatomy = structure; they are complementary, not the same.
  • A "characteristic of life" must be present in ALL living things (e.g., metabolism, reproduction); not all organisms reproduce sexually (some reproduce asexually).
  • The scientific method is iterative; a single experiment rarely proves a hypothesis.
Practice

Universal Facts
  • There are approximately 8.7 million species on Earth (only ~15% described).
  • Biology is the fastest-growing science; over 200,000 biology research papers are published annually.
  • DNA (the genetic material) is the basis of modern genetics.
  • The term "biology" was first used by Goethe (1801) and popularised by Tiedemann (1830).
Examples
  • Botany: Oryza sativa (rice) — a monocot, angiosperm, Poaceae.
  • Zoology: Rana tigrina (tiger frog) — an amphibian, Chordata, Anura.
  • Microbiology: Escherichia coli — a Gram-negative, prokaryotic bacterium.
  • Genetics: Mendel's pea experiments — the foundation of modern genetics.
  • Ecology: a pond ecosystem — producers, consumers, decomposers, abiotic factors.

It encompasses branches such as Botany, Zoology, Microbiology, Genetics, Ecology, Physiology, Anatomy, Taxonomy, and Biotechnology.   • Introduction to Biology: Scope and fields of biology

    1. Introduction

    Biology (Greek bios = life, logos = study) is the natural science of life — of every living thing, from a virus particle barely 20 nanometres across to a blue whale, and of every level at which life is organised, from molecules inside a cell to the whole biosphere. What makes biology one science rather than many is its single question asked at every level: how do living systems stay ordered, reproduce and adapt while obeying the laws of chemistry and physics?

    A Nepali student meets this question daily without naming it. The rice in a Jhapa paddy that tillers after transplanting, the gundruk fermenting in a clay pot through lactic-acid bacteria, the Rhododendron (lali gurans) blooming across a mid-hill forest in Chaitra, the child vaccinated at a health post against measles — each is a biological process a biologist can describe, explain and predict.

    Scope of this topic. You will learn (i) the definition and meaning of biology and why it is called the science of life; (ii) the scope of biology — what it studies and where its boundaries lie; (iii) the major fields (branches) of biology, pure and applied, each with its subject matter and examples; and (iv) how biology connects with everyday life in Nepal — agriculture, medicine, forestry and food. The next topic shows how biology borrows from and lends to physics, chemistry and the other sciences.

    Learning outcomes. After this topic you can: define biology and state its scope; list and distinguish at least ten fields of biology with one example each; classify any new branch (e.g., bioinformatics) as pure or applied; and explain two Nepal-context applications of biological knowledge.

  • 2. Hierarchy body — what biology is and what it covers

    What biology is

    1. Definition. Biology is the branch of natural science that studies living organisms — their structure, function, growth, origin, evolution and distribution — and their interactions with each other and the non-living environment.
    2. Why "science of life". Like physics studies matter-energy and chemistry studies substances, biology studies the one class of natural systems that shows all seven life processes together: organisation, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, response and evolution. A flame grows and moves but does not reproduce with heredity; a crystal is ordered but does not metabolise — neither is alive.
    3. Biology is experimental. Observation (a naturalist watching red panda behaviour in Langtang), comparison (healthy vs diseased paddy), and controlled experiment (vaccine trials) are its methods — the same scientific method as physics and chemistry, applied to living material.

    Scope of biology

    1. Breadth of objects. Biology ranges over microbes (bacteria, viruses, fungi), plants, animals and humans — including fossils and extinct forms, which is why palaeontology belongs inside biology.
    2. Breadth of levels. Molecules → cells → tissues → organs → organisms → populations → communities → ecosystems → biosphere. No level is "the" biology; genetics works at the molecular level while ecology works at the population level, and both are fully biology.
    3. Breadth of time. From the origin of life (~3.5–3.8 billion years ago, evidenced by stromatolite fossils) to present-day evolution (antibiotic resistance appearing within years) — biology covers deep time and real time together.
    4. Breadth of purpose. Pure inquiry (how does photosynthesis split water?) and applied use (how do we breed drought-tolerant wheat?) are both inside the scope; NEB examines both angles.

  • Major fields of biology — pure branches

    1. Botany — study of plants: e.g., the morphology and life cycle of Marchantia (a liverwort you will meet in Floral Diversity).
    2. Zoology — study of animals: e.g., the organ systems of earthworm and frog (Faunal Diversity).
    3. Microbiology — study of microbes: e.g., bacterial structure, viruses and bacteriophages.
    4. Genetics — heredity and variation: e.g., Mendel's laws, DNA replication (Class 12 Heredity & Evolution).
    5. Cell biology (cytology) — structure and function of cells: e.g., the fluid-mosaic membrane (next unit).
    6. Physiology — how bodies work: e.g., digestion in humans, transpiration in plants.
    7. Ecology — organisms and their environment: e.g., pond ecosystem, food chains (Ecology unit).
    8. Evolutionary biology — origin and diversification of life: e.g., Lamarckism vs Darwinism.
    9. Taxonomy and systematics — naming and classifying organisms: e.g., the five-kingdom system, binomial nomenclature.
    10. Molecular biology — life at the molecule level: e.g., transcription and translation of the genetic code.

    Applied (interdisciplinary) branches

    1. Agriculture — crop and livestock science: e.g., Khumal-4 rice and hybrid maize bred for Nepali hills.
    2. Medicine and health — disease, treatment, public health: e.g., the national immunisation programme, antibiotics.
    3. Forestry — forest management and conservation: e.g., community forestry, protected areas (Conservation Biology).
    4. Biotechnology — using living systems to make products: e.g., fermentation, recombinant insulin (Class 12).
    5. Fisheries and animal husbandry — e.g., trout farming in Himalayan streams, poultry breeds.
    6. Newer applied fields — bioinformatics (biology + computing), biomedical engineering, environmental biotechnology. Any new "-omics" or "bio-" field slots into this table by the same rule: name → what it studies → one example.

  • 5. Worked examples and applications

    1. Rice breeding (agriculture). Problem: hill farmers need short-duration rice. Biology used: genetics (selecting early-flowering variants) + physiology (matching growth to monsoon). Result: varieties such as Khumal-4 mature before the cold — pure genetics applied to food security.
    2. Gundruk fermentation (microbiology + food). Mustard leaves + salt + warm anaerobic pot → lactic-acid bacteria (Lactobacillus, Pediococcus) convert sugars to lactic acid, preserving the vegetable for months. Steps: wilt → shred → pack airtight → ferment 7–10 days → sun-dry.
    3. Antibiotics (microbiology + medicine). Penicillium mould makes penicillin, which blocks bacterial cell-wall synthesis. Human cells have no walls, so the drug kills bacteria, not us — structure explains selectivity.
    4. Rhizobium biofertilizer (microbiology + agriculture). Root nodules of bean/pea house Rhizobium, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia the plant can use — applied nitrogen cycle, cutting urea cost.
    5. Community forestry (ecology + forestry). Nepal's community-forest groups manage local forests for fuel, fodder and timber while protecting habitat — population and ecosystem principles running a national programme.
    6. Immunisation (physiology + public health). A vaccine trains adaptive immunity (antibodies + memory cells) without the disease — the applied end of the immune-system tree you will draw in Class 12.
    7. Disease diagnosis (cell biology + medicine). A blood smear under the microscope shows malarial parasites inside red cells — cell biology read directly off a slide in a health post.

  • 6. Key terms and statements

    • Biology — the natural science of living organisms: their structure, function, growth, origin, evolution and distribution.
    • Bios / logos — Greek roots: life / study or discourse.
    • Scope — the full range a science covers: objects, levels, time and purposes.
    • Botany / zoology — study of plants / study of animals; the two classical pure divisions.
    • Microbiology — study of microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa).
    • Genetics — study of heredity and variation.
    • Ecology — study of interactions between organisms and their environment.
    • Physiology — study of how living bodies function.
    • Taxonomy — science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
    • Biotechnology — technological use of living systems or their products.
    • Pure vs applied branches — pure seeks understanding (genetics, ecology); applied seeks use (medicine, agriculture, biotechnology).
    • Levels of organisation — molecule → cell → tissue → organ → organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere.

    8. Summary and self-check

    Biology is the science of life — its structure, function, growth, origin, evolution and distribution. Its scope spans all organisms, all levels from molecule to biosphere, deep time to real time, and pure understanding to applied use. Pure branches (botany, zoology, microbiology, genetics, cell biology, physiology, ecology, evolution, taxonomy, molecular biology) build the knowledge; applied branches (agriculture, medicine, forestry, biotechnology, fisheries) spend it — in Nepal, on rice, forests, fermentation and vaccines.

    Self-check. (1) Define biology and give its two Greek roots. (2) Name the nine levels of organisation with one example each. (3) Classify bioinformatics as pure or applied, with a reason. (4) Give two Nepal-context applications, each naming the pure branch behind it. (5) Why is a flame not alive although it grows and moves?

    Common confusions. Microbiology ≠ only disease (most microbes are harmless or useful) · biotechnology ≠ genetic engineering only (fermentation is biotechnology too) · scope ≠ branches (scope is the range; branches are the divisions) · ecology ≠ environment alone (it is the study of interactions).

Confusion

  • Biology is NOT the same as medicine or agriculture; it is the foundational science, and medicine/agriculture are applied fields.
  • Botany studies plants (including algae and fungi in some systems); Zoology studies animals.
  • Microbiology includes bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists; it overlaps with botany and zoology.
  • Physiology = function; Anatomy = structure; they are complementary, not the same.
  • A "characteristic of life" must be present in ALL living things (e.g., metabolism, reproduction); not all organisms reproduce sexually (some reproduce asexually).
  • The scientific method is iterative; a single experiment rarely proves a hypothesis.
  • Distinguish between key concepts in Scope and Fields of Biology.
  • Understand the differences between similar topics.
  • Practice identifying the correct application of principles.
  • Microbiology studies all microbes, not only pathogens — fermentation and nitrogen fixation are microbiology too.
  • Biotechnology is wider than genetic engineering: brewing, composting and selective breeding are older biotechnologies.
  • Ecology is the study of interactions between organisms and environment, not a synonym for 'environment'.

  • DNA (the genetic material) is the basis of modern genetics.

Summary

Biology (bios = life, logos = study) is the natural science of living organisms — their structure, function, growth, origin, evolution and distribution. Its scope covers all organisms, every level from molecule to biosphere, deep time to the present, and both pure inquiry (botany, zoology, microbiology, genetics, ecology, physiology, taxonomy) and applied use (agriculture, medicine, forestry, biotechnology). In Nepal this knowledge works daily: bred rice varieties, fermented foods, vaccines and community forests. Key ladder: molecule → cell → tissue → organ → organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere. Figures: fields mind-map, organisation ladder, three-domain tree of life, laboratory microscopy.
