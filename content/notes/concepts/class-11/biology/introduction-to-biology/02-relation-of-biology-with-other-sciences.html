Enriched Content

Notes
  • Biology is interconnected with other sciences: it relies on and contributes to Chemistry (biochemistry), Physics (biophysics), Mathematics (biostatistics), and Earth Science (geobiology).
  • Biochemistry: the chemistry of life — metabolism, enzymes, DNA/RNA/protein structures.
  • Biophysics: physics applied to biology — biophysics of the cell, nerve conduction, muscle mechanics, imaging (MRI, X-ray).
  • Biostatistics: mathematics used in biology — genetics (Punnett), population models, experimental design.
  • Geobiology: interaction of life with Earth — evolution (fossils, climate), ecology (biogeochemical cycles), palaeontology.
  • Interdisciplinary fields: medicine (biology + chemistry + physics), agriculture (biology + ecology), conservation biology (biology + policy), biotechnology (biology + engineering).
  • NEB focus: how biology connects with chemistry, physics, mathematics, and earth science.
  • Biology uses scientific tools from other disciplines: microscopes (physics), spectrometers (chemistry), statistical software (mathematics).
Confusion
  • Biochemistry is NOT the same as organic chemistry; it focuses on the chemistry of living systems (metabolism, enzymes, macromolecules).
  • Biophysics applies physics to biological problems; it is not "physics of life" in a vague sense.
  • Biostatistics is a branch of statistics, not a branch of biology; it is used BY biologists.
  • Geobiology (biology + earth science) is different from ecology (biology + environment).
  • Interdisciplinary fields combine multiple sciences; they are NOT a new "fourth" science.
  • Biology uses tools from other sciences (microscopes, spectrometers) but has its own methods (experiments, observations, classifications).
Practice
  • Explain how biology relates to chemistry (biochemistry).
  • Describe the role of physics in biology (biophysics).
  • Explain how mathematics is used in biology (biostatistics, genetics).
  • Describe the relationship between biology and earth science (geobiology).
  • List three interdisciplinary fields that combine biology with other sciences.
Universal Facts
  • The DNA double helix was discovered using X-ray crystallography (a physics technique) in 1953.
  • The electron microscope (physics) revealed cellular structures that light microscopes could not.
  • CRISPR-Cas9 (biology) is a gene-editing tool derived from a bacterial immune system.
  • Biology is the most data-rich science; a single human cell contains ~100,000 proteins.
Examples
  • Biochemistry: ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the energy currency of the cell — a molecule studied by chemistry.
  • Biophysics: nerve conduction involves electrical signals (physics) across the neuron membrane.
  • Biostatistics: Mendel's 3:1 ratio (Punnett squares) is a mathematical model of inheritance.
  • Geobiology: fossil records (earth science) provide evidence for evolution (biology).
  • Interdisciplinary: medicine combines biology (anatomy, physiology) with chemistry (pharmacology) and physics (imaging).

Biology is deeply interconnected with other sciences. Biochemistry studies the chemistry of life (metabolism, enzymes, macromolecules). Biophysics applies physics to biological problems (nerve conduction, muscle mechanics, imaging). Biostatistics uses mathematics in genetics, population models, and experimental design. Geobiology connects biology with earth science (fossils, climate, biogeochemical cycles). NEB focuses on these connections and the tools biology borrows from other disciplines.

  • Relation of biology with other sciences

    1. Introduction

    No science works alone, and biology least of all. A living body is simultaneously a physical system (it moves, conducts electricity along nerves, focuses light in the eye), a chemical system (it burns glucose, builds proteins, buffers its blood pH) and a measurable system (heartbeats per minute, cells per millilitre, quintals of paddy per hectare). So biology borrows the tools of other sciences to study life, and lends its own discoveries back to them. This two-way traffic is the whole topic.

    Nepal shows the traffic everywhere. A health-post microscope — ground-glass physics — reveals malarial parasites in blood. Urea fertilizer spread on a Terai wheat field is industrial chemistry feeding plant biochemistry. The Siwalik fossils dug from Nepal's own hills are read with geology's dating and biology's anatomy together. The national census tables that track population growth are statistics describing a biological population.

    What you will learn: (i) how biology relates to physics (biophysics, instruments, medical imaging); (ii) its relation to chemistry (biochemistry — photosynthesis, respiration, enzymes, buffers); (iii) its relation to mathematics and statistics (measurement, growth curves, genetics ratios); (iv) its relation to earth sciences and geography (fossils, dating, biogeography); (v) its newer relation to computer science (bioinformatics); and (vi) what biology gives back in return.

    Learning outcomes. You can: state one borrowed tool and one lent result for each of physics, chemistry, mathematics, earth science and computing; explain the microscope, photosynthesis and fossil-dating as relation-examples; and answer "two-way traffic" reasoning questions.

  • 2. Hierarchy body — the two-way traffic

    Relation with physics (biophysics)

    1. Instruments are applied physics. The compound microscope (lens optics), electron microscope (electron beams behave as waves), centrifuge (centripetal force separates cell fractions), autoclave (steam under pressure sterilises) and PCR thermocycler (precise heat control copies DNA) are all physics serving biology.
    2. Body processes explained physically. Vision = optics of the eye lens; hearing = sound-wave mechanics in the cochlea; nerve impulse = electrochemical wave (~120 m/s in myelinated fibres); blood pressure and ECG = fluid mechanics and bioelectricity.
    3. Medical imaging is physics reading the body. X-rays image bones, ultrasound images a foetus, MRI maps soft tissue with magnetism — every hospital department is a biophysics counter.
    4. Biology gives back: the eye inspired cameras and optical sensors; photosynthesis inspires solar-cell research; bird flight informs aerodynamics.

    Relation with chemistry (biochemistry)

    1. Life is controlled chemistry. Photosynthesis (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂) and aerobic respiration (the reverse) are chemical equations running inside cells; digestion, muscle contraction and nerve transmission are all enzyme-catalysed chemistry.
    2. Molecules explain heredity. X-ray crystallography (physics tool, chemical reasoning) revealed DNA's double helix; Chargaff's rules (A=T, G≡C) and the genetic code are chemistry underneath genetics.
    3. The internal sea is buffered. Blood pH is held near 7.4 by the bicarbonate buffer system — straight acid–base chemistry keeping a human alive; soil pH decides which crops a field can grow.
    4. Biology gives back: fermentation chemistry (alcohol, gundruk, curd), natural-product drugs (quinine, artemisinin against malaria), and fertiliser science.

    Relation with mathematics and statistics

    1. Measurement first. Cell sizes (µm), bacterial counts (CFU/mL), drug doses (mg/kg), crop yields (quintal/hectare) — biology without numbers is only stories.
    2. Patterns are mathematical. Population growth curves (J and S shapes), Mendel's 3:1 and 9:3:3:1 ratios, the Hardy–Weinberg equation (p² + 2pq + q² = 1) and doubling-time calculations are mathematics inside biology.
    3. Biostatistics decides truth. Vaccine trials, fertilizer field-trials and disease surveys all use sampling, means and significance tests — statistics is how biology proves a claim.
    4. Biology gives back: Fibonacci phyllotaxis (leaf spirals), population models used in demography, and epidemiological models (SIR) used by planners.

  • Relation with earth sciences and geography

    1. Fossils need geology. Nepal's Siwalik hills hold fossils of ancient elephants, giraffes and apes; radiometric dating of rock layers dates them, and the anatomy identifies them — palaeontology is biology × geology.
    2. Place shapes life. Altitude belts decide Nepal's vegetation (tropical sal → temperate oak → alpine juniper), monsoon decides cropping calendars, and island/terrain isolation drives speciation — biogeography is biology × geography.
    3. Biology gives back: indicator species read environmental health, and the fossil record calibrates geological time itself.

    Relation with computer science (bioinformatics)

    1. Data too big for paper. One human genome = ~3 billion letters; gene banks (GenBank), protein databases and sequence-alignment programs store and compare it — computing made modern genetics possible.
    2. Models predict epidemics and evolution. Phylogenetic software builds trees of life; epidemiological models forecast dengue or flu spread for health planning.
    3. Biology gives back: neural networks are brain-inspired computing, and DNA is studied as an information-storage molecule.

    The pattern to remember

    Every relation answers the same two questions — what does biology borrow? (tools, laws, instruments) and what does it lend? (discoveries, products, models). Any NEB "relation" question is answered in exactly these two halves.

  • 5. Worked examples and applications

    1. Blood-smear diagnosis (physics → biology). A health-post worker stains a drop of blood (chemistry), focuses the microscope (optics), and counts parasites per 200 white cells (mathematics) — one slide uses three sciences.
    2. Urea on wheat (chemistry → biology). A Terai farmer applies 60 kg urea per hectare; soil water hydrolyses it to ammonium, roots absorb it, and the biochemistry of amino-acid synthesis turns it into grain protein. Yield doubles — if soil pH is right (chemistry again).
    3. Siwalik fossil (geology + biology). A fossil jaw from the Siwaliks is dated by its rock layer (~8 million years) and identified by its teeth (a primitive horse). Age from geology, identity from biology.
    4. Dengue forecast (maths + computers + biology). Monsoon rainfall maps (geography) + mosquito breeding data (ecology) + case counts (statistics) feed a computer model that tells hospitals when dengue will peak.
    5. Insulin dose (biology → medicine). Recombinant human insulin (biotechnology) is prescribed in units per kg body weight (mathematics) to correct blood-glucose chemistry — a full circle of the traffic.
    6. Seed testing (statistics → agriculture). A seed lab germinates 4 × 100 seeds; 92% germination with a documented sampling method certifies the lot — statistics making biology tradable.
    7. Census population (biology → planning). Nepal's census counts a biological population; growth-rate mathematics from the same tables plans schools, vaccines and food supply.

  • 6. Key terms and statements

    • Biophysics — physics applied to living systems (instruments, imaging, biomechanics).
    • Biochemistry — chemistry of living systems (photosynthesis, respiration, enzymes, buffers).
    • Biostatistics — statistics applied to biological and medical data (trials, surveys, significance).
    • Palaeontology — study of fossils; biology × geology.
    • Biogeography — study of the distribution of organisms; biology × geography.
    • Bioinformatics — computing applied to biological data (genomes, phylogeny, modelling).
    • Two-way traffic — biology borrows tools from other sciences and lends results back; the frame for every "relation" answer.
    • Buffer (bicarbonate) — blood pH held near 7.4; the textbook example of chemistry keeping a body alive.
    • Model organism reasoning — drug/vaccine testing on animals before humans (medicine's debt to zoology).

    8. Summary and self-check

    Biology borrows from every science and pays every one back. From physics it takes optics, instruments and imaging (microscope, ECG, MRI) and returns biomimetic ideas. From chemistry it takes reactions, buffers and analysis (photosynthesis, respiration, blood pH 7.4) and returns fermentation products and drugs. Mathematics and statistics give it measurement, ratios and proof (Mendel's ratios, trial design); geology gives it dates and fossils (Siwaliks); geography gives it maps and climates; computers give it genome banks and epidemic models. The exam frame is always the same: what is borrowed, one worked example, what is lent.

    Self-check. (1) Name the parent science of: autoclave, buffer, fossil dating, genome bank. (2) Write the two equations (photosynthesis, respiration) that make the biology–chemistry link concrete. (3) Explain total microscope magnification as physics serving biology. (4) Give one example each of biology lending to medicine and to planning. (5) Why are vaccine trials a statistics question as well as a biology question?

    Common confusions. Biophysics ≠ medical instruments only (it includes biomechanics, bioelectricity) · biochemistry ≠ organic chemistry (it is chemistry in living systems) · bioinformatics ≠ just computers (it needs real biological data) · "relation" answers need both directions — one direction earns half marks.

Confusion

  • Biochemistry is NOT the same as organic chemistry; it focuses on the chemistry of living systems (metabolism, enzymes, macromolecules).
  • Biophysics applies physics to biological problems; it is not "physics of life" in a vague sense.
  • Biostatistics is a branch of statistics, not a branch of biology; it is used BY biologists.
  • Geobiology (biology + earth science) is different from ecology (biology + environment).
  • Interdisciplinary fields combine multiple sciences; they are NOT a new "fourth" science.
  • Biology uses tools from other sciences (microscopes, spectrometers) but has its own methods (experiments, observations, classifications).
  • Distinguish between key concepts in Relation with Other Sciences.
  • Understand the differences between similar topics.
  • Practice identifying the correct application of principles.
  • Biochemistry is chemistry in living systems — wider than enzymes alone (buffers, blood gases, soil pH all count).
  • Bioinformatics needs biological data first; software without sequences answers nothing.
  • Every 'relation' answer needs both directions (borrow + lend) for full marks.

Universal Facts

  • The DNA double helix was discovered using X-ray crystallography (a physics technique) in 1953.
  • The electron microscope (physics) revealed cellular structures that light microscopes could not.
  • CRISPR-Cas9 (biology) is a gene-editing tool derived from a bacterial immune system.
  • Biology is the most data-rich science; a single human cell contains ~100,000 proteins.

Examples

  • Biophysics: nerve conduction involves electrical signals (physics) across the neuron membrane.
  • Biostatistics: Mendel's 3:1 ratio (Punnett squares) is a mathematical model of inheritance.
  • Geobiology: fossil records (earth science) provide evidence for evolution (biology).
  • Interdisciplinary: medicine combines biology (anatomy, physiology) with chemistry (pharmacology) and physics (imaging).

  • Biology is interconnected with Chemistry, Physics, Mathematics, and Earth Science.
  • Biochemistry: the chemistry of life (metabolism, enzymes, macromolecules).
  • Biophysics: physics applied to biology (nerve conduction, muscle, imaging).
  • Biostatistics: mathematics used in biology (genetics, populations, experiments).
  • Geobiology: interaction of life with Earth (fossils, climate, cycles).
  • Interdisciplinary fields: medicine, agriculture, conservation, biotechnology.
  • Biology uses tools from other sciences (microscopes, spectrometers).

Summary

Biology borrows from every science and pays each back. Physics gives optics, instruments and imaging (microscope, ECG, MRI); chemistry gives reactions, buffers and analysis (photosynthesis 6CO₂+6H₂O→C₆H₁₂O₆+6O₂, blood pH 7.4, enzymes); mathematics gives measurement, ratios and proof (Mendel 3:1, growth curves, trial statistics); geology gives fossil dating (Siwaliks); geography gives climate and vegetation maps; computers give genome banks and epidemic models. In return biology lends drugs, crops, vaccines and planning data. Exam frame: what is borrowed + one worked example + what is lent.
