Notes

  • Vegetation is the plant cover of a place, set by temperature, rain, soil and height. Nepal's cover changes quickly because the land rises from the Terai to the high Himalaya.
  • Below about 1000 m the belt is tropical. Sal, Shorea robusta, dominates, with sisau along rivers. This is the Terai and the inner Terai.
  • From about 1000 to 2000 m the belt is subtropical. Chir pine, Pinus roxburghii, and Schima–Castanopsis forest are the markers.
  • From about 2000 to 3000 m the belt is temperate. Oak and Rhododendron arboreum, the national flower, are the markers. Rhododendron here is a flowering plant.
  • Toward 4000 m, fir, birch and juniper thin out. The timberline is the last trees. Above it, alpine herbs and grasses continue until the snowline, which is higher.
  • Forest and other wooded land cover about 45 percent of Nepal. The east is wetter than the west at the same height, so the species lists differ. Community forestry is a management success in the middle hills, not itself a belt.

Practice

  • Define + example each sub-idea: vegetation, introduction, types of vegetation in Nepal.

    Answer. Vegetation — the total plant cover of an area, described by its dominant species and growth form; e.g. the sal forests of the Terai. Introduction (context) — vegetation is controlled above all by climate, and climate in Nepal is controlled by altitude; e.g. the same mountain shows tropical base and alpine top. Types of vegetation in Nepal — the altitudinal belts: tropical sal, subtropical chir pine, temperate oak–rhododendron, subalpine conifer to the timberline near 4,000 m, then alpine meadow; e.g. rhododendron (Laliguras) dominating the temperate belt.

  • One worked example for "Vegetation: Introduction, types of vegetation in Nepal" — check units / edge cases.

    Answer. Worked example: a trekker climbs from Pokhara (~800 m) to Annapurna base camp (~4,130 m). Predict what she walks through.

    • 800–1,000 m: subtropical lower slopes — sal gives way to chir pine.
    • 1,000–2,000 m: chir pine belt.
    • 2,000–3,000 m: oak–rhododendron temperate forest (spring rhododendron bloom).
    • 3,000–4,000 m: birch–fir to the timberline.
    • Above ~4,000 m: alpine shrub and meadow.

    Edge cases: south-facing slopes run warmer and drier, so belts sit higher there; rain-shadow valleys (Mustang) skip the lush belts entirely and step straight to trans-Himalayan arid scrub — altitude alone does not decide, rainfall co-decides.

  • Recall card: definition → rule → limit → one error to avoid.

    Answer. Definition: vegetation = the plant cover of a region, arranged in belts. Rule: altitude replaces latitude — ~6 °C cooling per 1,000 m shifts the whole flora upslope. Limit: the timberline near 4,000 m caps tree growth; only alpine meadow lies above. Error to avoid: do not list the belts in the wrong order or place chir pine (subtropical) above oak–rhododendron (temperate) — chir pine is always the lower belt.

Practice Questions

  • Q. State and explain the main idea of "Vegetation: Introduction, types of vegetation in Nepal" with one example.

    Answer. Main idea: Nepal packs a full latitudinal range of vegetation into one country because altitude replaces latitude — every ~1,000 m of climb is worth several degrees of latitude and roughly 6 °C of cooling, so vegetation belts stack up the slopes.

    The belts, low to high:

    1. Tropical — sal (Shorea robusta) forest, Terai and Bhabar up to ~1,000 m.
    2. Subtropical — chir pine (Pinus roxburghii), ~1,000–2,000 m.
    3. Temperate — oak and rhododendron forest, ~2,000–3,000 m.
    4. Subalpine/upper montane — conifers (fir, birch) up to the timberline near 4,000 m.
    5. Alpine meadow and nival zone — grasses, shrubs and bare rock above.

    Example: sal (Shorea robusta) dominates the hot, monsoonal Terai but cannot survive even 1,000 m of altitude — its replacement by chir pine upslope shows the climate control directly.

  • Q. Compare "Vegetation" with the related idea from a neighbouring unit.

    Answer. Vegetation (this unit) is the floristic idea — which plant species and forest types cover an area, organised by altitude and climate. The neighbouring unit's ecosystem concept (Unit 4) is the functional idea — a biotic community plus its abiotic environment running energy flow and nutrient cycling.

    The link: each vegetation belt is the producer base of the ecosystem built on it — a sal forest, a chir pine stand and an alpine meadow each feed a different food web, support different animals and cycle nutrients at different rates. Vegetation answers "what grows there"; ecosystem answers "how it works".

  • Q. Solve / apply for "Vegetation: Introduction, types of vegetation in Nepal" and verify the answer is plausible.

    Answer. Task: predict the vegetation belts along a slope climbing from 800 m to 4,500 m in central Nepal.

    Solution:

    1. 800–1,000 m — tropical sal forest.
    2. 1,000–2,000 m — subtropical chir pine.
    3. 2,000–3,000 m — temperate oak–rhododendron.
    4. 3,000–4,000 m — upper montane conifer/birch to the timberline.
    5. Above 4,000 m — alpine meadow, then nival bare zone.

    Verification: the prediction follows the altitude-replaces-latitude rule (~6 °C per 1,000 m), matches the trekking experience on the Annapurna route, and reproduces the same sequence found on every central-Nepal slope — plausible.
