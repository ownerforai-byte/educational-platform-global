Enriched Content

Notes
  • Vegetation: the natural plant cover of a region, determined by climate (temperature, rainfall), altitude, soil, and human activities.
  • Nepal vegetation zones (by altitude):
  • Terai/subtropical (60–1000 m): sal (Shorea robusta), banyan, peepal, grasslands (Chitwan, Bardia); warm, low humidity.
  • Sub-montane/moist broadleaf (500–1500 m): mixed deciduous — rhododendron, oak, laurel, alder; moderate rainfall.
  • Temperate (1000–2000 m): oak (Quercus), rhododendron (Rhododendron arboreum — national flower), chestnut; cool, wet.
  • Sub-alpine (2000–3000 m): coniferous — pine (Pinus), fir (Abies), juniper; cold, dry winter.
  • Alpine (>3000 m): dwarf trees, shrubs, meadows, lichens, mosses; very cold, short growing season.
  • Nival (>4500 m): bare rock, snow, a few lichens and mosses; extreme cold.
  • Forest types by moisture: evergreen, semi-evergreen, moist deciduous (broadleaf), dry deciduous, coniferous, alpine/meadow.
  • Human impact: deforestation (firewood, shifting agriculture/burning, grazing, over-exploitation) has degraded vegetation; regrowth occurs when protected.
  • NEB focus: types of vegetation in Nepal (altitude zones), dominant species, and human impact.
  • Key species: sal (Terai), rhododendron (temperate hills), pine/fir (sub-alpine), oak (broadleaf); Chitwan sal forest supports tiger and rhino.
Confusion
  • Vegetation zones are defined by ALTITUDE + climate (rainfall, temperature), not by political boundaries.
  • Sal (Shorea robusta) is the dominant Terai/subtropical tree; rhododendron dominates the temperate hills.
  • Burning/shifting agriculture (jhum) degrades vegetation; natural regrowth is slow on poor soils.
  • Temperate forests (oak, rhododendron, chestnut) differ from subtropical (sal, peepal) in species and altitude.
  • Alpine zone has meadows and dwarf shrubs, NOT tall trees; the "tree line" ends around 3000–4000 m.
  • Vegetation is the NATURAL plant cover; "plantations" and "cultivated crops" are human-made, not natural vegetation.
Practice
  • List Nepal's vegetation zones by altitude with dominant species.
  • Explain the vegetation of the Terai (sal forest, grasslands).
  • Describe the temperate vegetation (rhododendron, oak, chestnut).
  • Explain the sub-alpine and alpine vegetation.
  • Discuss the impact of human activities on Nepali vegetation.
  • Differentiate temperate from subtropical vegetation.
Universal Facts
  • About 64% of Nepal's area is under forest; ~35% is evergreen.
  • Rhododendron arboreum, the national flower, dominates the temperate hills (1000–3000 m).
  • The Chitwan Terai sal forest supports the Bengal tiger and one-horned rhinoceros.
  • The "tree line" in the Himalaya ends at ~3000–4500 m; above it only dwarf shrubs, grasses, and lichens grow.
Examples
  • Terai: sal forest (Shorea robusta), banyan, peepal; Chitwan grasslands.
  • Sub-montane: mixed deciduous — rhododendron, oak, laurel, alder.
  • Temperate: oak (Quercus), rhododendron (Rhododendron arboreum), chestnut.
  • Sub-alpine: pine (Pinus wallichiana), fir (Abies), juniper.
  • Alpine: meadows, dwarf shrubs, lichens, mosses (e.g., Everest region).
  • Nival: bare rock, snow, sparse lichens and mosses (>4500 m).
Practice Questions

  • Describe the temperate vegetation of the hills.
  • Explain the sub-alpine and alpine vegetation.
  • Discuss the impact of human activities on Nepali vegetation.
Formulas
  • Vegetation zones (altitude): Terai (60–1000 m) → Sub-montane (500–1500 m) → Temperate (1000–2000 m) → Sub-alpine (2000–3000 m) → Alpine (>3000 m) → Nival (>4500 m)
  • Dominant species: sal (Terai), rhododendron + oak (temperate), pine + fir (sub-alpine), meadows + lichens (alpine)
Key Points
  • Vegetation is set by altitude + climate (temperature, rainfall) + soil + human activity.
  • Nepal zones: Terai → Sub-montane → Temperate → Sub-alpine → Alpine → Nival.
  • Terai: sal forest + grasslands (warm, low rainfall).
  • Temperate: oak, rhododendron (national flower), chestnut.
  • Sub-alpine: pine, fir, juniper; Alpine: meadows, dwarf shrubs, lichens.
  • Human impact: deforestation (firewood, jhum, grazing, over-exploitation).
  • Chitwan sal forest supports tiger and rhino; ~64% of Nepal is forested.
Summary

Nepal's vegetation is strongly zoned by altitude and climate. The Terai supports subtropical sal forests and grasslands; the sub-montane and temperate hills have mixed deciduous and broadleaf forests dominated by rhododendron, oak, and chestnut; the sub-alpine zone is coniferous (pine, fir, juniper); the alpine zone has meadows, dwarf shrubs, and lichens; and the nival zone is bare rock and snow. Human activities — deforestation for firewood, shifting agriculture, grazing, and over-exploitation — have degraded much of the vegetation. NEB focuses on the altitude-based vegetation zones, their dominant species, and the impact of human activities.

Special Notes
  • NEB expects the altitude-based vegetation zones with dominant species for each.
  • Sal (Terai) and rhododendron (temperate) are the key species to know.
  • The tree line ends at ~3000–4500 m; above it only dwarf shrubs and meadows grow.
  • Human impact: deforestation, jhum (shifting agriculture), grazing, over-exploitation.
  • Give specific Nepali examples (Chitwan sal, Everest alpine meadows).
Important Statements
  • Nepal's vegetation is zoned by altitude and climate.

  • The tree line ends at ~3000–4500 m; alpine vegetation is meadows and dwarf shrubs.

  • Tree line = ~3000–4500 m; above = no tall trees.
  • Human impact = jhum (shifting agriculture) + grazing + firewood + over-exploitation.

1.

A hiker ascending a Himalayan hill sees sal forest, then oak-rhododendron, then pine-fir, then meadows, then bare rock. Explain this sequence.

ID

1

Steps
  • Rising altitude = falling temperature + changing rainfall
  • Sal (Terai/subtropical, warm) → Oak-rhododendron (temperate) → Pine-fir (sub-alpine, cold) → Meadows (alpine) → Bare rock (nival)
Answer

The sequence reflects altitudinal vegetation zonation: as temperature falls and conditions change, dominant species shift from sal to temperate to coniferous to alpine meadows to bare rock

A hiker ascending a Himalayan hill sees sal forest, then oak-rhododendron, then pine-fir, then meadows, then bare rock. Explain this sequence.

ID

1

Steps
  • Rising altitude = falling temperature + changing rainfall
  • Sal (Terai/subtropical, warm) → Oak-rhododendron (temperate) → Pine-fir (sub-alpine, cold) → Meadows (alpine) → Bare rock (nival)
Answer

The sequence reflects altitudinal vegetation zonation: as temperature falls and conditions change, dominant species shift from sal to temperate to coniferous to alpine meadows to bare rock
