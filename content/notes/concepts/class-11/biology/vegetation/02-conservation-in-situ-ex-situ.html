Enriched Content

Notes
  • Vegetation conservation: protecting and managing natural plant cover; includes in-situ and ex-situ strategies.
  • In-situ conservation: protecting vegetation in its natural location — protected areas (national parks, conservation areas, wildlife reserves), community forests, sacred groves.
  • Ex-situ conservation: conserving plant material outside the natural habitat — botanical gardens, seed banks, tissue culture, herbaria.
  • Nepal in-situ: Chitwan, Sagarmatha, Annapurna, Kanchenjunga, etc. national parks; community forests (22,000+ groups manage ~36% of forest land); sacred groves (deval-ghari).
  • Ex-situ: Nepal National Herbarium (Tripureshwor), seed banks, botanical gardens, plant tissue-culture labs.
  • Threats to vegetation: deforestation (firewood, jhum/shifting agriculture, grazing, over-exploitation, illegal logging), habitat loss, climate change, invasive species, pollution.
  • Human activities: jhum (slash-and-burn) agriculture on hillsides causes severe erosion and forest loss; over-grazing degrades alpine meadows; firewood collection degrades riparian forests.
  • Conservation strategies: protected areas, community forestry, anti-logging laws (Forest Act), reforestation, awareness, international agreements (CITES, Paris).
  • Natural environment-vegetation: the interdependence of climate, soil, water, and plants; deforestation disrupts the water cycle and causes erosion, landslides, and climate change.
  • NEB focus: in-situ and ex-situ conservation, natural environment-vegetation, and human activities affecting vegetation.
  • Importance of vegetation: oxygen, climate regulation, soil conservation, water, biodiversity, food, medicine, culture, and livelihood.
Confusion
  • In-situ conservation protects vegetation IN its natural habitat; ex-situ conserves plant material OUTSIDE (botanical gardens, seed banks).
  • Community forests (in-situ) give local communities rights and responsibility; they differ from government-managed national parks.
  • Jhum (shifting/slash-and-burn) agriculture is a major cause of hill deforestation; it is different from permanent agriculture.
  • A sacred grove (deval-ghari) is a traditionally protected forest patch; it is a form of in-situ conservation.
  • Deforestation disrupts the water cycle and causes erosion, landslides, and climate change — not just loss of trees.
  • Ex-situ (seed banks, tissue culture) is a back-up; it cannot fully replace in-situ conservation of whole ecosystems.

Summary

Vegetation conservation protects natural plant cover through in-situ strategies (protected areas, community forests, sacred groves) and ex-situ strategies (botanical gardens, seed banks, tissue culture, herbaria). Nepal's community-forestry program (22,000+ groups managing ~36% of forest land) is one of the world's most successful. Threats to vegetation include jhum (shifting) agriculture, over-grazing, firewood collection, illegal logging, climate change, and pollution. Vegetation is vital to the natural environment — it regulates the water cycle, prevents erosion, stabilises the climate, and supports biodiversity, food, medicine, and culture. NEB focuses on in-situ vs ex-situ conservation, the natural environment-vegetation relationship, and human activities affecting vegetation.

Exercises
1.

A hillside village practises jhum agriculture on the same slope year after year. What are the consequences for the vegetation and the environment?

ID

1

Steps
  • Repeated slash-and-burn removes vegetation cover
  • Soil erosion increases (no roots to hold soil)
  • Landslide risk rises
  • Water cycle disrupted (less infiltration, more runoff)
  • Biodiversity loss (habitat destroyed)
Answer

Deforestation → soil erosion + landslide risk + disrupted water cycle + biodiversity loss
