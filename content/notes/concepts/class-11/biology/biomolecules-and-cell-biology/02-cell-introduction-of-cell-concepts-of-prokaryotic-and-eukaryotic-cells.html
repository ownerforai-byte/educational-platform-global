  • Cell: Introduction of cell, concepts of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

    1. Introduction

    The cell is the structural and functional unit of life: the smallest thing that shows all seven life processes together. A bacterium, an onion peel cell, your cheek cell scraped with a toothpick — each is a membrane-bound compartment where biomolecules meet organisation, and organisation becomes life. Everything in this syllabus — tissues, organs, genetics, disease — is cells behaving singly or together.

    The discovery story is short and examinable. Robert Hooke (1665) saw dead cork "cells"; Leeuwenhoek's lenses revealed living animalcules; Schleiden (plants, 1838) and Schwann (animals, 1839) declared all organisms cellular; Virchow (1855) added omnis cellula e cellula — every cell from a pre-existing cell. Then Chatton (1925) coined prokaryote/eukaryote, Dougherty (1957) proposed the two organisations as a deep divide, and Stanier and van Niel (1962) popularised the dichotomy that still frames this topic.

    What you will learn: (i) the cell concept and cell theory; (ii) cell size range and why cells stay small; (iii) the prokaryotic organisation (correct, modern version — mesosomes retired); (iv) the eukaryotic organisation; (v) the full comparison; (vi) exceptions that prove the rule (viruses, RBCs).

    Learning outcomes. You can: state cell theory with authors; compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells on eight features; explain the surface-area-to-volume reason for smallness; and correct the mesosome and "largest/smallest cell" statements.

  • 2. Hierarchy body — the cell idea, then the two organisations

    What a cell is, and cell theory

    1. Definition. A cell is the membrane-enclosed unit of structure and function of living organisms, containing hereditary material and the machinery to use it.
    2. Cell theory (exam core). Schleiden and Schwann: all living things are made of cells and cell products; Virchow: all cells arise from pre-existing cells. Modern addition: the cell is the unit of heredity and the site of energy flow — genetics and metabolism live inside cell theory now.
    3. How big are cells? Most are microscopic (bacteria ~1 µm, human cheek cell ~60 µm, human ovum ~0.12 mm — just visible). The largest known cell is the unfertilised ostrich ovum; mycoplasmas are among the smallest known free-living cells (~0.2–0.4 µm). A human body holds on the order of 3×10¹³ cells (published estimate; counts vary with body size).
    4. Why so small? Surface area grows as the square, volume as the cube: a big cell starves at its centre because supply crosses the surface. Smallness is a transport law, not an accident — the surface-area-to-volume ratio is the standard 2-mark explanation.
    5. Not everything cellular is a cell. Viruses are acellular (nucleic acid + protein coat, no metabolism outside a host); mature mammalian red blood cells and sieve-tube elements lose their nuclei to do their jobs better.

    The prokaryotic cell (modern account)

    1. Definition. Organisation without a membrane-bound nucleus or organelles: DNA lies in a nucleoid, ribosomes are 70S, cell division is by binary fission.
    2. Envelope. Capsule/slime layer (some) → cell wall (peptidoglycan in most bacteria; absent in mycoplasmas) → plasma membrane, across which respiratory electron transport occurs in aerobes.
    3. Validity note — mesosomes retired. Older books (and older notes) said membrane folds called mesosomes are the sites of respiration. Mesosomes are now known to be fixation artifacts of electron microscopy — the correction tested in this unit. Write: respiration enzymes sit in the plasma membrane itself.
    4. Cytoplasmic kit. 70S ribosomes, storage granules, plasmids (small extra DNA rings carrying antibiotic resistance), and in photosynthetic forms chromatophores/thylakoid membranes; flagella (simple filament) and pili/fimbriae in some.
    5. Examples. Escherichia coli (gut), cyanobacteria (photosynthetic, e.g., Nostoc), mycoplasmas (wall-less, among the smallest free-living cells).

  • The eukaryotic cell

    1. Definition. Organisation with a true membrane-bound nucleus (nuclear envelope + nucleolus + chromatin) and membrane-bound organelles — mitochondria, plastids (plants), ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles.
    2. Ribosomes and division. 80S ribosomes in the cytosol (70S inside mitochondria and chloroplasts — the endosymbiotic clue). Division is by mitosis/meiosis with a spindle, not simple fission.
    3. Two great lineages. Plant cells add wall, large central vacuole and plastids; animal cells add centrioles and lysosomes and lack the wall — the next topic draws every organelle.
    4. Where the two meet. Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own small DNA and 70S ribosomes: former free-living prokaryotes now living inside eukaryotes (endosymbiotic theory) — the bridge between this topic and the next.

    The comparison (learn as pairs, not lists)

    Prokaryote ↔ eukaryote on eight fixed features: genetic material (naked nucleoid ↔ chromatin in nucleus); nuclear envelope (absent ↔ present); membrane organelles (absent ↔ present); ribosomes (70S ↔ 80S cytosolic); wall chemistry (peptidoglycan, or absent ↔ cellulose/chitin, or absent in animals); division (binary fission ↔ mitosis/meiosis); size (mostly 0.2–10 µm ↔ mostly 10–100 µm); examples (E. coli, cyanobacteria ↔ onion cell, cheek cell).

  • 5. Worked examples and applications

    1. Cheek-cell mount. Scrape inside of cheek, smear, methylene-blue stain, focus 10× then 40×: flat irregular cells, dark central nuclei — your own eukaryotes in ten minutes.
    2. Onion peel. Peel epidermis, stain with safranin: brick-wall rectangular cells with walls and big vacuoles — the plant version in the same period.
    3. Curd under the lens. A drop of curd shows Lactobacillus rods (~2 µm): prokaryotes you ate for breakfast, no nucleus in sight.
    4. Why no giant bacteria? A 1 mm bacterium would have 1/1000th the surface per volume of a 1 µm one — diffusion could not feed it. Size answers structure.
    5. Antibiotic logic. Penicillin blocks peptidoglycan wall-building (prokaryote-only structure); streptomycin hits 70S ribosomes (human cytosol uses 80S) — the comparison table is pharmacology.
    6. RBC design. Mammalian red cells eject the nucleus to carry more haemoglobin — losing the defining eukaryotic feature for function; exceptions prove the rule.
    7. Yoghurt vs antibiotic. Antibiotics kill gut bacteria (prokaryotes) while sparing gut lining (eukaryote 80S) — selective toxicity read straight off Table 1.

  • 6. Key terms and statements

    • Cell — membrane-bound structural and functional unit of life.
    • Cell theory — all organisms are cellular; every cell comes from a pre-existing cell (Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow).
    • Prokaryote — cell without membrane-bound nucleus or organelles; 70S ribosomes; binary fission.
    • Eukaryote — cell with true nucleus and membrane organelles; 80S cytosolic ribosomes; mitosis/meiosis.
    • Nucleoid — naked circular DNA region of prokaryotes (no envelope).
    • Mesosome note — membrane folds once called respiratory sites are fixation artifacts; respiration enzymes sit in the plasma membrane itself.
    • 70S vs 80S — prokaryotic (and mitochondrial/chloroplast) vs eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes — the antibiotic target difference.
    • Binary fission — direct prokaryotic division: replicate DNA, split in two.
    • Omnis cellula e cellula — Virchow's dictum: every cell from a cell.
    • Endosymbiotic clue — organelle 70S ribosomes + own DNA point to prokaryote ancestors.

    8. Summary and self-check

    The cell is life's unit; cell theory (Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow) plus modern heredity/energy additions frames it. Prokaryotes pack naked DNA (nucleoid), 70S ribosomes and a wall into ~1 µm and divide by fission — with respiration across the plasma membrane (mesosomes retired as artifacts). Eukaryotes add a true nucleus, membrane organelles and 80S ribosomes at ~10–100 µm and divide by mitosis/meiosis. Size spans mycoplasma (~0.3 µm) to ostrich egg, ruled by surface-to-volume law. Organelle DNA/70S whispers the endosymbiotic past. Figures: comparison schematic, size scale, NCBI comparison plate, labelled animal cell.

    Self-check. (1) State cell theory with one author per clause. (2) Reproduce the eight-feature comparison from memory. (3) A student writes "mesosomes respire" — correct her in two sentences. (4) Why can you see an ovum but not a mycoplasma? (5) How does the 70S/80S split explain antibiotic safety?

    Common confusions. Prokaryote ≠ only bacteria (includes cyanobacteria; mycoplasmas lack walls) · nucleoid ≠ nucleus (no envelope) · 70S also inside mitochondria/chloroplasts · viruses are acellular, not prokaryotic · largest cell = ostrich ovum (unfertilised), not whale cells.

Summary

The cell is life's structural and functional unit. Cell theory: Schleiden and Schwann (all organisms cellular) + Virchow (omnis cellula e cellula). Prokaryotes carry naked nucleoid DNA, 70S ribosomes and (usually) peptidoglycan walls at ~1 µm, dividing by binary fission, with respiratory enzymes in the plasma membrane — mesosomes retired as fixation artifacts. Eukaryotes add a true nucleus, membrane organelles and 80S ribosomes at ~10–100 µm, dividing by mitosis/meiosis. Sizes run from mycoplasma (~0.2–0.4 µm) to the ostrich ovum, ruled by the surface-area-to-volume law; the body holds ~3×10¹³ cells (estimate). Exceptions: viruses (acellular), RBCs (no nucleus). Organelle DNA + 70S point to endosymbiosis. Figures: comparison schematic, size scale, NCBI comparison plate, labelled animal cell.
