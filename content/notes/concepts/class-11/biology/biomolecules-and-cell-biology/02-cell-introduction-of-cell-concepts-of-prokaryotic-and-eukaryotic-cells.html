  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Start Biology With the Cell?

    Everything you study after this topic — tissues, organs, digestion, heredity, evolution — is built on one organising idea: the cell is the smallest unit of life. Nothing smaller than a cell is alive; everything alive is made of one or more cells.

    • Why it matters: every biomolecule you studied in Topic 1 exists inside cells, organised into compartments (organelles) that separate reactions and make complex life possible.
    • Where it leads: Topic 3 dissects the eukaryotic cell organelle-by-organelle; Topic 4 explains how cells reproduce. Prokaryote vs eukaryote is the single most-asked comparison in this unit's NEB papers.
    • Historical thread: cells were invisible until instruments made them visible — the story of cell discovery is the story of the microscope.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding — What a Cell Actually Is

    2.1 Definition and the essential idea

    A cell is the smallest, basic structural, functional and biological unit of all living organisms — a mass of cytoplasm bound by a membrane, containing genetic material and capable of independent existence and reproduction. Two words carry the whole concept:

    • Structural unit — every organism is built of cells (bricks of a building).
    • Functional unit — every property of life (metabolism, growth, response, reproduction) happens inside cells, not between them.

    An organism can be unicellular (the single cell does every job — Amoeba, Chlamydomonas, bacteria) or multicellular (cells specialise and divide the labour — muscle cell, nerve cell, guard cell). Specialisation is possible only because all cells carry the same complete genome.

    2.2 The history of cell discovery — who saw what, and when

    YearScientistDiscoverySignificance

    1831Robert Brown (Scotland)Discovered the nucleus in orchid cellsIdentified the control centre

    Conceptual point: Hooke saw cell walls of dead cork; Leeuwenhoek saw living contents. The cell theory came two centuries later because theory needed better microscopes and comparative study.

    2.3 Cell theory — statement and exceptions

    Cell theory (Schleiden 1838, Schwann 1839, Virchow 1855):

    1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
    2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
    3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells by division.

    Modern additions: hereditary information passes from cell to cell (DNA); all cells are fundamentally alike in chemistry; energy flow occurs within cells.

    Exceptions / limitations (asked in NEB 2024):

    ExceptionWhy it is an exception
    VirusesAcellular — no cell membrane, no cytoplasm, no metabolism of their own; alive only inside a host
    Viroids and prionsEven simpler — naked RNA (viroids) or only protein (prions)
    Coenocytes / syncytiaSome "cells" are multinucleate masses (fungal hyphae, muscle fibres) — one body, many nuclei
    Mitochondria and chloroplastsContain their own DNA and ribosomes and arise only by division of pre-existing organelles (endosymbiotic origin)

    2.4 Cell size, shape and number — why cells are small

    Cells vary enormously in size and shape, always matched to function:

    CellSizeWhy this size/shape
    Mycoplasma (PPLO)~0.1–0.3 µmSmallest known cell
    Human red blood cell~7–8 µmSmall + biconcave = maximum O₂ diffusion surface
    Amoeba~1 mmIrregular — pseudopodia for movement
    Ostrich egg~15–18 cmLargest single cell (yolk = stored food)
    Nerve cellup to ~1 m longLong processes carry impulses across the body

    Why are cells so small? (surface-area-to-volume reasoning — a favourite conceptual question): as a cell grows, its volume grows faster than its surface area (volume ∝ length³, surface ∝ length²). A large cell could not absorb nutrients or expel wastes fast enough through its membrane for its interior. Small size keeps every part of the cytoplasm close to the surface and keeps diffusion fast. Large organisms solve the problem by having many small cells, not few big ones.

    2.5 Basic components every cell shares

    Whatever its type, every living cell has:

    1. Cell membrane — the selective boundary (plasma lemma).
    2. Cytoplasm — aqueous colloidal medium (cytosol + organelles) where metabolism happens.
    3. Genetic material — DNA organised as nucleoid (prokaryote) or nucleus (eukaryote).
    4. Ribosomes — protein synthesis machinery (universal: 70S in prokaryotes, 80S in eukaryote cytoplasm).

    Everything else — cell wall, plastids, mitochondria, ER, Golgi — differs between groups, and those differences are exactly what the next sections classify.

  • 3. Prokaryotic Cells — Life Before Organelles

    3.1 Definition and who the prokaryotes are

    Prokaryotes (Greek pro = primitive, karyon = nucleus) are cells that lack a true nucleus and any membrane-bound organelles. Their genetic material lies naked in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Domain Bacteria (and Archaea) — bacteria, cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), mycoplasma — are all prokaryotes. They appeared ~3.5 billion years ago, the earliest forms of life.

    3.2 Structure of a prokaryotic (bacterial) cell — component by component

    StructureNatureFunction
    Cell wallRigid, of peptidoglycan (murin) — NOT celluloseShape, protection, prevents bursting in hypotonic media
    Plasma membraneLipoprotein bilayer with mesosomes (infoldings)Permeability; mesosomes aid respiration and cell division
    NucleoidSingle circular DNA, naked (no histones, no nuclear envelope)Heredity; replication by binary fission
    PlasmidsSmall circular extra DNAExtra genes (antibiotic resistance); used in genetic engineering
    70S ribosomesFree in cytoplasm (70S = 50S + 30S subunits)Protein synthesis
    Gas vacuoles / inclusion bodiesStorage granules (glycogen, PHB), gas vesiclesStorage; buoyancy in aquatic bacteria
    FlagellaProtein (flagellin) filaments, no 9+2 arrangementLocomotion
    Pili and fimbriaeSurface protein filamentsAttachment; conjugation (gene transfer)
    Photosynthetic membranes (cyanobacteria)Thylakoid-like lamellae, no chloroplastPhotosynthesis with chlorophyll a

    Key conceptual idea: prokaryotes achieve compartmentalisation by invagination and inclusion, not by membrane-bound organelles. Respiration happens on mesosomes/plasma membrane; photosynthesis on internal lamellae — the same chemistry as eukaryotes, at the membrane surface instead of inside organelles.

    3.3 Prokaryotic special features (exam list)

    • No true nucleus — no nuclear envelope, no nucleolus.
    • No membrane-bound organelles — no mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, plastids.
    • 70S ribosomes (eukaryote cytoplasm has 80S) — this difference is why many antibiotics (streptomycin, tetracycline) attack bacterial ribosomes but not ours.
    • Circular naked DNA + plasmids.
    • Peptidoglycan cell wall (mycoplasma lacks even the wall — smallest cells).
    • Binary fission — no mitosis/meiosis, no spindle.
    • Coupled transcription–translation: mRNA is translated while still being transcribed — possible only because there is no nuclear envelope separating the two.

  • 4. Eukaryotic Cells — The Compartmentalised Cell

    4.1 Definition and who the eukaryotes are

    Eukaryotes (Greek eu = true) have a true nucleus — DNA enclosed in a double nuclear envelope with a nucleolus — plus membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, plastids). Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes; they appeared ~1.5–2 billion years ago.

    4.2 The compartmentalisation principle (the deep idea of this topic)

    Eukaryotic life is organised around division of labour inside the cell:

    • Membrane-bound compartments allow incompatible chemistries to coexist — lysosomes digest at pH ~5 while the cytosol works at pH ~7.2.
    • The nuclear envelope separates transcription from translation, enabling RNA processing and complex gene regulation.
    • Endomembrane system (ER → Golgi → vesicles → membrane) manufactures and ships proteins.
    • Endosymbiotic theory: mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes engulfed by a host cell — evidence: their own circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, double membranes and fission-style division. This theory is the conceptual bridge between the two cell types.

    5.1 Similarities (never forget the shared core)

    Both have: plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes (protein synthesis), DNA as genetic material, and the same genetic code — which is why genetic engineering can make bacteria express human genes.

  • 6. Conceptual Relationships

    • Topic 1 → here: all the biomolecules are now seen in place — phospholipids build the membrane, DNA sits in the nucleoid/nucleus, enzymes float in the cytosol.
    • Here → Topic 3: the eukaryotic organelles mentioned by name here (mitochondria, plastids, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, ribosomes, nucleus) are dissected in detail next.
    • Here → Topic 4: prokaryotes divide by binary fission; eukaryotes by the cell cycle, mitosis and meiosis.
    • Endosymbiosis → evolution (Unit 7): the prokaryote→eukaryote transition is the deepest example of cooperation in evolution.
    • Antibiotics: the 70S vs 80S ribosome difference is the molecular reason selective toxicity is possible — a direct health application.
    • Viruses (Unit 3): viruses are not cells at all — they expose the boundary of cell theory.
  • Deep dive — why compartmentalisation changed everything. A eukaryote can run incompatible chemistries at once: lysosomes digest at pH ~5 while the cytosol stays near pH 7; the mitochondrion keeps its own proton gradient. A prokaryote must keep all reactions in one compartment, which caps its complexity. That single architectural difference — membranes inside membranes — is why eukaryotes could grow large, specialise into tissues, and ultimately build multicellular life.

    (Any three clear bases with both sides stated = full marks.)

    Q2 (3m) — Cell theory, proposers, exceptions. Answer: Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839) proposed that all organisms are made of cells, the cell being the structural and functional unit of life; Virchow (1855) added that all cells arise from pre-existing cells (omnis cellula e cellula). Exceptions: viruses (acellular, no metabolism), viroids/prions, multinucleate coenocytes/syncytia, and mitochondria/chloroplasts (own DNA, arise by fission).

    Q3 (2m) — Why is the mitochondrion the powerhouse of the cell? Answer: Because it is the site of aerobic respiration — the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation on its cristae generate most of the cell's ATP (the energy currency), which powers all cellular work. Cells with high energy demand (muscle, liver) have many mitochondria.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying prokaryotes have "no nucleus" without adding no nuclear envelope / nucleoid — the precise statement scores.
    2. Confusing 70S/80S ribosomes or writing "prokaryotes have no ribosomes" (they do — 70S).
    3. Calling the bacterial wall cellulose — it is peptidoglycan.
    4. Listing viruses as prokaryotes — viruses are acellular, outside the cell concept entirely.
    5. Forgetting similarities (membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, DNA, genetic code) when a question asks "compare".

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Define a cell.

    Answer. The cell is the smallest basic structural, functional and biological unit of all living organisms — a mass of cytoplasm surrounded by a membrane, containing genetic material, capable of independent existence and reproduction. Unicellular organisms perform every life function with a single cell.

  • Short answer (2m): Name the scientists associated with the cell theory and their contributions.

    Answer. - Robert Hooke (1665) — first saw and named “cells” in dead cork (though the cell theory came later).

    • Schleiden (1838) — all plants are made of cells.
    • Schwann (1839) — all animals are made of cells.
    • Virchow (1855) — all cells arise from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula e cellula).
  • Short answer (2m): What are plasmids and why are they important?

    Answer. Small, self-replicating circular DNA molecules in prokaryotes, separate from the main chromosome. They carry accessory genes — antibiotic resistance, fertility factors — and, because they can be cut and re-ligated, they are the standard vectors of genetic engineering (e.g. insulin production in E. coli).

  • Short answer (2m): What is a nucleoid?

    Answer. The irregular, non-membrane-bound region of a prokaryotic cytoplasm where the single circular, naked DNA chromosome lies — no nuclear envelope separates it from the cytoplasm, which is why transcription and translation can be coupled.

  • Long answer (5m): Differentiate between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.

    Answer. | Basis | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |

    |---|---|---|

    | Nucleus | No nuclear envelope; nucleoid with naked circular DNA | True nucleus; double envelope, nucleolus, pores |

    | Chromosomes | Single circular DNA + plasmids, no histones | Multiple linear chromosomes with histones |

    | Membrane-bound organelles | Absent (no mitochondria, ER, Golgi, plastids) | Present |

    | Ribosomes | 70S (50S + 30S) | 80S (60S + 40S); 70S inside organelles |

    | Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (bacteria) | Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), or none (animals) |

    | Cell division | Binary fission (~20 min) | Mitosis / meiosis |

    | Transcription–translation | Coupled — both in cytoplasm simultaneously | Separated — transcription in nucleus, translation in cytoplasm |

    | Size | 0.5–5 µm | 10–100 µm |

    | Respiration site | Plasma membrane (mesosomes) | Mitochondria |

    | Examples | Bacteria, cyanobacteria | Protists, fungi, plants, animals |

    Similarities (the point of continuity): plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, DNA as genetic material, and the same genetic code — all life shares one chemistry.

  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure of a bacterial cell with a labelled diagram.

    Answer. From outside in:

    1. Cell wall — peptidoglycan (murein): rigid, gives shape and osmotic protection; attacked by lysozyme and penicillin. (A slime capsule may lie outside it.)
    2. Plasma membrane: typical phospholipid bilayer; carries respiratory enzymes (no mitochondria); may fold in as mesosomes.
    3. Nucleoid: one circular naked DNA chromosome, no nuclear envelope; plus small plasmids (resistance, fertility genes).
    4. Ribosomes — 70S (50S + 30S), thousands, free in cytoplasm; protein synthesis (and antibiotic target).
    5. Inclusion bodies / gas vacuoles: stored glycogen, lipid, phosphate granules; buoyancy in aquatic forms.
    6. Flagellum — simple rotary organ of locomotion made of flagellin (no 9+2 arrangement).
    7. Pili and fimbriae — protein threads: pili for conjugation (gene transfer), fimbriae for attachment.

    Labelled diagram: capsule (if drawn), wall, membrane with mesosome, nucleoid loop + plasmid ring, ribosome dots, flagellum and pili.

  • Conceptual (3m): "The eukaryotic cell is a compartmentalised cell." Explain.

    Answer. Membrane-bound organelles wall off separate chemical environments inside one cell:

    • Lysosomes hold acid hydrolases at pH ~5 while the cytosol runs at pH ~7.2 — the same cell hosts two incompatible chemistries side by side.
    • The nuclear envelope separates transcription (nucleus) from translation (cytoplasm), allowing RNA processing bacteria cannot do.
    • Mitochondria concentrate respiration enzymes and proton gradients; the ER/Golgi run a dedicated export assembly line.

    Compartmentalisation prevents interference between processes, raises local enzyme and substrate concentrations, and permits larger cells and specialisation — the structural basis of eukaryotic complexity.

  • Conceptual (3m): Why do cells remain small even in large organisms?

    Answer. Geometry and diffusion. When a cell's linear size doubles, its volume grows 8× (cube) but its surface only 4× (square) — the surface-area-to-volume ratio falls. Every nutrient and waste molecule must cross the membrane, and inside a large cell diffusion is far too slow over long distances. A big cell would starve in the middle and poison itself. Large organisms therefore multiply small, specialised cells instead of enlarging individual ones — size comes from cell number, not cell size.

  • Application (3m): A new antibiotic inhibits 70S ribosomes. Why is it likely safe for humans, and what limitation must it have?

    Answer. Why safe: human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S (60S + 40S), so a drug that binds only 70S ribosomes selectively blocks bacterial protein synthesis while leaving the patient's own cytoplasmic translation untouched — selective toxicity on a real structural difference.

    Limitation: human mitochondria carry 70S ribosomes (an endosymbiotic relic), so at high dose or long duration the drug can also hit mitochondrial translation — harming mitochondria-rich tissues (liver, kidney, bone marrow, inner ear). That mitochondrial overlap is the basis of several antibiotic side effects.

Practice

  • Why can mRNA be translated while it is still being transcribed in bacteria but not in humans?

    Answer. In bacteria there is no nuclear envelope: transcription and translation happen in the same compartment, so ribosomes can attach to the growing mRNA and start making protein before RNA polymerase has finished — a genuine speed advantage for a 20-minute life cycle. In humans the nuclear envelope separates the two processes: mRNA must first be transcribed, capped, spliced (introns out) and polyadenylated inside the nucleus, then exported through pores before cytoplasmic ribosomes can read it. Coupling is impossible — and the processing step is precisely why it must be.

  • Why do antibiotics like streptomycin not stop human protein synthesis?

    Answer. Ribosome architecture. Streptomycin binds specific sites on the 70S bacterial ribosome (30S subunit) that the 80S human cytoplasmic ribosome does not possess — different proteins, different shapes, different drug-binding pockets. The antibiotic therefore freezes bacterial initiation and misreads bacterial mRNA while our cytoplasmic translation runs on. The one human structure that is 70S — the mitochondrion — explains the side effects at high doses, but ordinary doses spare human protein synthesis: selective toxicity from a structural difference.

  • A cell grows to twice its linear size. What happens to its surface-area-to-volume ratio and why does this limit cell size?

    Answer. Surface area scales with length², volume with length³. Doubling the linear size:

    • surface area × 4
    • volume × 8
    • SA:V ratio falls to half (from, say, 6:1 to 3:1).

    The membrane is the cell's only exchange surface, so a doubled cell has half the import/export capacity per unit of living matter. Diffusion across the interior also slows proportionally more than demand grows — the centre starves and drowns in its own waste before the membrane can service it. This is the mathematical ceiling on cell size, and why large organisms add cells instead of enlarging them.

  • Why are mitochondria and chloroplasts considered evidence for endosymbiosis?

    Answer. Both organelles behave like captured bacteria:

    1. Own circular DNA, naked and bacteria-like — separate from nuclear chromosomes.
    2. 70S ribosomes (not the eukaryotic 80S) — and sensitive to the same antibiotics that hit bacteria.
    3. Double membrane — the outer from the host's engulfing vacuole, the inner the original bacterial membrane.
    4. Binary fission — they divide independently, like bacteria, never built de novo.
    5. Genetic similarity — their rRNA sequences match Rickettsia-like bacteria (mitochondria) and cyanobacteria (chloroplasts).

    An ancestral eukaryote engulfed an aerobic bacterium (→ mitochondrion) and, later, a photosynthetic cyanobacterium (→ chloroplast); instead of digesting them, it kept them — and the guests kept their bacterial machinery as the receipt.

  • Plasma membrane
  • Ribosomes
Answer

B

Explanation

Wall, plastids and central vacuole are plant-specific; membrane, mitochondria and ribosomes are shared.

Confusion

  • "Prokaryotes have no nucleus" vs "no DNA": prokaryotes have DNA — just no nuclear envelope around it. The nucleoid is DNA + cytoplasm, not a membrane compartment.
  • Viruses are NOT prokaryotes: viruses are acellular (no membrane, no cytoplasm, no ribosomes); prokaryotes are complete living cells. This is also why viruses are exceptions to cell theory.
  • 70S vs 80S: the S (Svedberg) unit measures sedimentation rate, not size — 70S ribosomes are not "smaller than 80S by 10"; they differ in subunit composition (50S+30S vs 60S+40S).
  • Cell wall vs cell membrane: every cell has a membrane; only some have a wall. The wall is outside the membrane, fully permeable; the membrane is the selective barrier.
  • Binary fission vs mitosis: binary fission has no spindle, no chromosome condensation, no nuclear envelope breakdown — it is simpler DNA replication + splitting.

Key Points

  • Cell = smallest structural + functional unit of life; unicellular organisms do everything with one cell.
  • Discovery chain: Hooke (1665, dead cork, named "cell") → Leeuwenhoek (1674, living cells) → Brown (1831, nucleus) → Schleiden & Schwann (1838–39, theory) → Virchow (1855, cells from cells).
  • Cell theory: (1) organisms made of cells, (2) cell = structural + functional unit, (3) cells arise from pre-existing cells. Exceptions: viruses, viroids/prions, coenocytes, mitochondria/chloroplasts.
  • Cells are small because volume grows faster than surface area — diffusion would fail in a big cell.
  • Every cell has: membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, DNA, same genetic code.
  • Prokaryote: no nuclear envelope, no membrane-bound organelles, 70S ribosomes, naked circular DNA + plasmids, peptidoglycan wall, binary fission.
  • Eukaryote: true nucleus + nucleolus, membrane-bound organelles, 80S ribosomes, histone-bound linear chromosomes, mitosis/meiosis.
  • Endosymbiotic theory explains mitochondria/chloroplasts: own circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, double membrane.
  • Plant vs animal cells: wall, plastids, central vacuole, centrioles are the four standard differences.

Examples

  • Mycoplasma — smallest known cell (~0.1 µm), no cell wall, prokaryote.
  • Ostrich egg — largest single cell (~15–18 cm).
  • Human RBC — ~7–8 µm, biconcave, loses nucleus at maturity to maximise O₂ carriage.
  • E. coli — rod-shaped bacterium; nucleoid + plasmids; model prokaryote of genetics.
  • Anabaena — filamentous cyanobacterium; photosynthetic lamellae, heterocysts fix nitrogen.

Summary

The cell is the smallest structural and functional unit of life — discovered by Hooke (1665), theorised by Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow, and limited in size by the surface-area-to-volume ratio. Every cell shares a membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA, but two great designs exist: prokaryotic cells (bacteria, cyanobacteria) keep their naked circular DNA in a nucleoid, have no membrane-bound organelles, use 70S ribosomes and divide by binary fission; eukaryotic cells enclose their histone-bound chromosomes in a true nucleus, run their chemistry inside membrane-bound organelles (endosymbiotic mitochondria and chloroplasts), use 80S ribosomes and divide by mitosis and meiosis. Plant and animal eukaryotic cells differ mainly in cell wall, plastids, central vacuole and centrioles. These differences — especially 70S vs 80S ribosomes — are the molecular basis of selective antibiotic action.

Universal Facts

  • Robert Hooke coined the word "cell" in 1665 (Micrographia).
  • Prokaryotes appeared ~3.5 billion years ago; eukaryotes ~1.5–2 billion years ago.
  • 70S = 50S + 30S subunits; 80S = 60S + 40S subunits.
  • Human body ≈ 30 trillion cells (adult estimate).
