  • 1. Topic Introduction — Inside the Eukaryotic Cell

    Topic 2 established that eukaryotic cells have membrane-bound organelles; this topic opens every compartment and explains how each one is built and what it does. The syllabus names the full inventory: cell wall, cell membrane, mitochondria, plastids, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi bodies, lysosomes, ribosomes, nucleus, chromosomes, cilia, flagella and cell inclusions.

    • Why it matters: every disease, drug, hormone and metabolic process acts at some organelle. Antibiotics hit ribosomes; cyanide hits mitochondria; cholesterol drugs hit the ER.
    • Study method: for each organelle learn the same five questions — structure (membranes?), location, chemistry, function, and the evidence/interesting fact. NEB questions are almost always built from these five.
    • Connection: the phospholipid bilayer from Topic 1 is the universal building unit of every structure below.

  • 2. Cell Wall — The Outer Armor of Plant Cells

    Definition: the rigid, non-living outer layer outside the plasma membrane, found in plants, fungi, bacteria and algae (absent in animals).

    FeatureDetail
    ChemistryPlants: cellulose (microfibrils in a matrix of hemicellulose and pectin). Fungi: chitin. Bacteria: peptidoglycan
    LayersMiddle lamella (pectin, cements adjacent cells) → primary wall (thin, extensible, growing cells) → secondary wall (thick, rigid, lignified, mature cells)
    FunctionsShape and rigidity; protection against mechanical injury and pathogens; prevents bursting in hypotonic water (turgor pressure); plasmodesmata allow transport between cells

    Key concept: the wall is fully permeable — it is not the selective barrier; the membrane is. Water moves freely through the wall; osmosis is decided by the membrane.

  • 3. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane) — The Selective Barrier

    3.1 Structure — the fluid-mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972)

    • A phospholipid bilayer ~7–8 nm thick: hydrophilic heads face the aqueous inside and outside; hydrophobic tails form the interior.
    • Proteins float like icebergs in the lipid sea: integral (intrinsic) proteins span the membrane (channels, carriers, pumps); peripheral (extrinsic) proteins sit on the surfaces (enzymes, anchors).
    • Cholesterol in animal membranes stabilises fluidity across temperatures.
    • Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the outer face form the glycocalyx — cell recognition.
    • The membrane is fluid (lipids and proteins move laterally) and a mosaic (patchwork of proteins) — hence the name.

    3.2 Functions

    1. Selective permeability — decides what enters and leaves (small non-polar molecules diffuse freely; ions need channels/pumps).
    2. Transport — passive (diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion) and active (ATP-driven pumps, e.g. Na⁺/K⁺ pump).
    3. Endocytosis and exocytosis — bulk transport in vesicles (phagocytosis, pinocytosis).
    4. Recognition and signalling — receptors for hormones; glycoprotein identity markers.
    5. Compartmentalisation — the same bilayer principle builds every organelle.

    Historical models (short-answer favourite): Robertson (unit membrane, 1950s) → Singer & Nicolson (fluid mosaic, 1972). Also recall: Overton (lipid nature), Gorter & Grendel (bilayer from RBC lipids, 1925), Davson–Danielli (protein sandwich).

  • 4. Mitochondria — The Powerhouse

    4.1 Structure

    • Double-membraned organelle, 0.5–1.0 µm wide, rod or sausage shaped ("chondriosome" — granules seen by Kölliker, 1880s; named by Benda, 1898).
    • Outer membrane: smooth, permeable to small molecules (porins).
    • Inner membrane: folded into cristae — folds that hugely increase surface area; studded with oxysomes (F₁ particles) where ATP is made. Cristae density reflects the cell's energy demand (heart muscle: very dense).
    • Matrix: contains enzymes of the Krebs cycle, circular mitochondrial DNA, 70S ribosomes, tRNA and enzymes of fat/protein oxidation.
    • Semi-autonomous: mitochondria divide by fission like bacteria (endosymbiotic evidence).

    4.2 Function

    • Aerobic respiration: pyruvate → Krebs cycle (matrix) → electron transport chain + oxidative phosphorylation (inner membrane) → ~36 ATP per glucose.
    • Stores and releases Ca²⁺; synthesises some lipids and amino acids; generates heat (brown fat).

    NEB 2022 asked it directly: the mitochondrion is the "powerhouse of the cell" because it carries out aerobic respiration and produces most of the cell's ATP.

    Plastids are double-membraned, DNA-bearing organelles found only in plants and algae, classified by pigment:

    TypePigmentColourFunctionExample

    ChromoplastCarotenoids (carotene, xanthophyll)Yellow/orange/redPigmentation, attract pollinators and seed dispersersTomato fruit, carrot root, petals

    5.1 Chloroplast structure (draw this in exams)

    • Oval, 4–10 µm; double membrane envelope.
    • Inside: colourless stroma (enzymes of the Calvin cycle, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes) and grana — stacks of disc-like thylakoids holding chlorophyll in their membranes (light reaction site). Grana are connected by stroma lamellae.
    • Semi-autonomous like mitochondria — own DNA and ribosomes (endosymbiosis).

    Functions: photosynthesis (light reaction on thylakoids, carbon fixation in stroma); stores starch temporarily.

  • 6. Endoplasmic Reticulum — The Intracellular Highway

    Definition: a network (reticulum) of membrane-bound tubules, vesicles and flattened sacs (cisternae) running from the nuclear envelope outward. Discovered by Porter, Claude and Fullam (1945) in cultured cells.

    TypeStructureRibosomes?Main roleWhere abundant
    Rough ER (RER)Flattened cisternae, studded with ribosomesYes (80S)Synthesis and folding of proteins destined for membranes, lysosomes or secretionProtein-exporting cells: pancreatic acinar cells, plasma cells
    Smooth ER (SER)Tubular network, no ribosomesNoLipid and steroid synthesis; detoxification of drugs; Ca²⁺ storageSteroid-secreting cells, liver, muscle (as sarcoplasmic reticulum)

    Functions: synthesis and transport of materials; the ER lumen connects to the nuclear envelope and Golgi — part of the endomembrane system; provides surface for enzyme attachment; in muscle, the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ for contraction.

  • 7. Golgi Apparatus — The Packaging and Dispatch Department

    • Discovered by Camillo Golgi (1898) in nerve cells (silver stain).
    • Structure: stacks of 4–8 curved, flattened membrane sacs (cisternae) with a convex forming (cis) face receiving vesicles from the ER and a concave maturing (trans) face shipping vesicles outward.
    • Functions:
    1. Modification of proteins and lipids arriving from the ER (glycosylation — adding sugars).
    2. Packaging into secretory vesicles, lysosomes and membrane-bound granules.
    3. Secretion — dispatch to the cell surface (exocytosis).
    4. Source of lysosomes and of the cell plate during plant cytokinesis.
    5. Complex sugar synthesis (mucopolysaccharides, plant cell-wall matrix).

    Conceptual flow to memorise: RER (make protein) → vesicle → Golgi cis face (modify) → trans face (package) → vesicle → destination (membrane / lysosome / secretion).

  • 8. Lysosomes — The Suicide Bags

    • Discovered by Christian de Duve (1949/1955, Nobel 1974): single-membraned sacs (~0.2–0.8 µm) packed with ~50 acid hydrolases (digestive enzymes active at acidic pH ~5).
    • Why they don't digest the cell: the inner membrane's H⁺-pump keeps the lumen acidic while the cytosol stays near pH 7.2, where the enzymes are inactive — compartmentalisation in action.
    • Forms: primary lysosome (fresh enzymes) → secondary lysosome (fusing with food vacuole = digestive vacuole) → residual body (undigested waste, expelled by exocytosis).
    • Functions: intracellular and extracellular digestion; digestion of worn-out organelles (autophagy); destruction of engulfed pathogens (in white blood cells); autolysis — rupture releases enzymes and digests the cell itself, hence "suicide bags" (e.g. tadpole tail reabsorption during metamorphosis, uterine wall breakdown at menstruation).

  • 9. Ribosomes — The Protein Factories

    • Discovered by Palade (1953, Nobel 1974) — dense granules ~15–20 nm, made of rRNA + proteins, no membrane.
    • Two subunits: eukaryotic 80S = 60S + 40S; prokaryotic/organelle 70S = 50S + 30S.
    • Occur free in the cytosol (making proteins used inside the cell) or attached to RER (making secretory/membrane proteins). Several ribosomes on one mRNA form a polyribosome (polysome).
    • Function: site of protein synthesis (translation) — they read mRNA codons and link amino acids by peptide bonds. Because of the 70S/80S difference, antibiotics (streptomycin, tetracycline) can block bacterial ribosomes without stopping ours.

  • 10. Nucleus — The Control Centre

    10.1 Structure

    • Discovered by Robert Brown (1831). Usually one per cell (multinucleate cells exist — coenocytes); absent in mature RBCs and sieve tubes.
    • Nuclear envelope: double membrane with perinuclear space; perforated by nuclear pores (complex of proteins) that regulate traffic of mRNA out and proteins in.
    • Nucleoplasm: nucleic acids + proteins + enzymes.
    • Nucleolus: dense, rounded, membraneless region of rRNA genes — the ribosome factory.
    • Chromatin: DNA + histones in two forms — euchromatin (loose, active transcription) and heterochromatin (condensed, inactive).

    10.2 Chromosomes — the packaged genome

    • Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes during division. Each chromosome has two chromatids joined at the centromere (primary constriction); some carry a secondary constriction with a satellite.
    • Nucleosome organisation: DNA wound around histone octamers ("beads on a string").
    • Chromosome types by centromere position: metacentric (middle), submetacentric (off-middle), acrocentric (near end), telocentric (at end).
    • Karyotype = the ordered photograph of all chromosomes of a species.
    • Functions of the nucleus: controls all cell activities through gene expression; stores hereditary information; ribosome production; cell division distributes its DNA.
  • Deep dive — the endosymbiont clue. Mitochondria and chloroplasts each carry their own circular DNA, their own 70S ribosomes and a double membrane — the signature of bacteria that moved inside larger cells and stayed. The inner membrane corresponds to the original bacterial membrane; the outer one is the host's engulfing vacuole. NEB loves this as a link between cell structure and evolution: organelle anatomy is fossil evidence you can see in every cell.

    15.2 Common examination mistakes

    1. Drawing chloroplast grana without stroma lamellae connecting them, or omitting stroma.
    2. Writing "lysosomes digest the cell" without the suicide-bag mechanism (autolysis after rupture).
    3. Confusing cristae (mitochondria) with thylakoids/grana (chloroplasts).
    4. Saying ribosomes have a membrane — they are non-membranous.
    5. Forgetting that the cell wall is fully permeable and the membrane is the selective one.
    6. Mixing up cis (forming, receives from ER) and trans (maturing, ships out) faces of the Golgi.

Confusion

  • Cell wall vs cell membrane: the wall is outside, rigid, fully permeable, non-living; the membrane is living, selective, and the true gatekeeper of the cell.
  • Cristae vs thylakoids: cristae are folds of the mitochondrial inner membrane; thylakoids are separate flattened discs inside the chloroplast stroma, stacked into grana.
  • Lysosome vs vacuole: lysosomes are single-membraned enzyme sacs for digestion; the plant central vacuole is mainly storage/turgor, though it also contains hydrolases.
  • RER vs SER: ribosomes on the surface define RER and protein synthesis; smooth tubules without ribosomes make lipids and detoxify.
  • Cilia vs flagella: same 9+2 axoneme, but cilia are short, numerous and beat like oars; flagella are long, few and undulate.
  • Nucleolus vs nucleus: the nucleolus is a membraneless sub-region of the nucleus that makes rRNA/ribosomes — not a separate organelle.
  • Inclusions vs organelles: inclusions (starch, fat, crystals) are non-living, non-membrane-bound deposits; organelles are living, organised functional structures.

Practice

  • Why does a liver cell contain more SER than a muscle cell, but a pancreatic cell more RER?

    Answer. Structure follows chemistry. The liver is the body's detox and lipid-chemistry plant: it modifies drugs and poisons, synthesises lipoproteins and steroid precursors — all SER jobs, so hepatocytes are packed with smooth tubules. The pancreatic acinar cell is an export factory for digestive enzymes — proteins destined for secretion, which must be synthesised on RER ribosomes, threaded into the cisternae, glycosylated in the Golgi and packaged into zymogen granules — so it is stuffed with RER. A muscle cell needs little of either: its business is contraction, so its ER specialisation is the Ca²⁺-storing sarcoplasmic reticulum instead.

  • Why don't lysosomal enzymes digest the lysosome's own membrane first?

    Answer. Three safeguards:

    1. Membrane composition — the lysosomal membrane is unusually rich in heavily glycosylated (sugar-armoured) proteins (e.g. LAMP proteins) that present no protein backbone to the proteases and resist digestion.
    2. pH separation — the enzymes are acid hydrolases, active only at pH ~5 maintained by a proton pump; the cytosol is pH ~7.2, where they are nearly inert. If a little leaks out, cytosolic enzymes neutralise it.
    3. Compartmentalisation — substrates are delivered into the lysosome (by phagocytosis, autophagosome fusion), so the enzymes meet their targets inside, not outside.

    Only when the membrane is ruptured — damage or programmed death — does the system fail, and that failure is precisely the 'suicide bag' function.

  • A student draws a chloroplast with grana but no stroma DNA. Which features did they miss and why do they matter?

    Answer. Missed features: the circular DNA (nucleoid region), the 70S ribosomes and the starch grains in the stroma — and often the stroma lamellae connecting the grana.

    Why they matter:

    • The DNA + 70S ribosomes are the evidence that the chloroplast is semi-autonomous — an endosymbiotic cyanobacterium that still makes some of its own proteins and divides by fission.
    • Starch grains show the stroma is where the Calvin cycle stores the fixed carbon.
    • Stroma lamellae are structurally real: grana are not free-floating stacks but connected units of one continuous thylakoid system.

    A chloroplast without stroma DNA is drawn as a bag of membranes — missing the organelle's evolutionary story and its biochemistry.

  • Why are mitochondria and chloroplasts called semi-autonomous organelles?

    Answer. Because they are partly independent, partly dependent:

    Autonomous side:

    • their own circular DNA;
    • their own 70S ribosomes and tRNAs — they synthesise some of their own proteins;
    • they divide by binary fission, never assembled from scratch.

    Dependent side:

    • most of their proteins are encoded in the nucleus, synthesised on cytoplasmic 80S ribosomes and imported;
    • they cannot survive or replicate outside the host cell.

    So they run their own housekeeping but depend on the nucleus for most of their parts — exactly the state predicted for former endosymbiotic bacteria that handed most of their genes to the host genome.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Why is the mitochondrion called the powerhouse of the cell?

    Answer. Because it runs aerobic respiration: the Krebs cycle in the matrix oxidises food derivatives, and the electron transport chain + ATP synthase on the cristae convert that energy into most of the cell's ATP — the universal energy currency. Muscle, liver and nerve cells, the biggest energy spenders, are packed with mitochondria.

  • Short answer (2m): Distinguish RER and SER (two points).

    Answer. 1. Ribosomes: RER bears ribosomes on flattened cisternae; SER lacks ribosomes and forms tubules.

    1. Function: RER synthesises proteins for secretion and membranes; SER synthesises lipids and steroids, detoxifies drugs and stores Ca²⁺ (sarcoplasmic reticulum in muscle).
  • Short answer (2m): What are cell inclusions? Give two examples.

    Answer. Non-living, non-membrane-bound cytoplasmic deposits of stored food or waste material — not organelles, just stored material.

    Examples: starch grains (potato tuber), fat droplets (adipose tissue); also glycogen granules, protein crystals and pigment granules.

  • Short answer (2m): State the fluid-mosaic model in one sentence and name its proposer.

    Answer. The membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer carrying a mosaic of embedded integral and peripheral proteins (plus cholesterol and surface glycocalyx) that can move laterally — proposed by Singer and Nicolson (1972).

  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure of a chloroplast with a labelled diagram.

    Answer. Structure:

    1. Double envelope — outer permeable membrane + inner selective membrane, with a narrow intermembrane space.
    2. Stroma — the protein-rich fluid matrix holding Calvin-cycle (CO₂-fixation) enzymes, plus circular DNA, 70S ribosomes and starch grains (semi-autonomous).
    3. Grana — stacks of disc-shaped thylakoids; the thylakoid membranes carry chlorophyll a, b and accessory pigments with the light-reaction complexes (photosystems, ETC, ATP synthase).
    4. Stroma lamellae — flattened membranes connecting the grana.

    Functions:

    • Light reaction on the thylakoid membranes: light energy → ATP + NADPH, O₂ released from water splitting.
    • Dark reaction (Calvin cycle) in the stroma: CO₂ fixed into sugar using that ATP and NADPH.
    • Temporary starch storage as grains in the stroma.

    Labelled diagram: envelope (double), stroma, one granum of stacked thylakoids, stroma lamella, starch grain, DNA region.

  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure and role of the Golgi apparatus.

    Answer. Structure: stacks of curved, flattened membrane cisternae (3–8 per stack) with vesicles budding off at the edges. The stack is polarised:

    • cis (forming) face — receives transport vesicles from the RER;
    • trans (maturing) face — dispatches finished products.

    Roles:

    1. Modification — glycosylation of proteins and lipids (making glycoproteins/glycolipids).
    2. Packaging and sorting — routing materials to their destinations in vesicles.
    3. Secretion — packaging export proteins (enzymes, hormones, mucus) for release.
    4. Lysosome formation — budding off the enzyme-filled lysosomes.
    5. Cell-plate formation — in plant cells the Golgi supplies the material that becomes the middle lamella during cytokinesis.
    6. Complex polysaccharide synthesis (pectin, hemicellulose).

    Flow diagram: RER → (vesicle) → cis-Golgi → modified through cisternae → trans-Golgi → vesicles → secretion / lysosome / membrane.

  • Conceptual (3m): Why is the cell membrane called selectively permeable while the cell wall is not?

    Answer. The membrane is a lipid bilayer with specific proteins — channels, carriers and pumps — so it discriminates: small non-polar molecules (O₂, CO₂) diffuse through freely, water crosses via aquaporins, but ions and sugars pass only through controlled protein routes. It decides what enters and leaves.

    The cellulose wall is an open mesh: its microfibril pores are large, so water and dissolved solutes pass freely — it is fully permeable. Its job is support and shape, not selection. Selection is the membrane's job.

  • Conceptual (3m): "Lysosomes are suicide bags." Justify.

    Answer. Lysosomes contain acid hydrolases — enzymes able to digest every class of biomolecule (proteases, lipases, nucleases, glycosidases). In life, the membrane isolates them and the cytosol's pH ~7.2 keeps the enzymes (which need pH ~5) nearly inactive. But when a cell is badly damaged or programmed to die, the lysosomal membrane ruptures, the enzymes flood the cytoplasm, drop the local pH and digest the cell from within — autolysis.

    Useful examples: resorption of the tadpole's tail at metamorphosis, breakdown of the menstrual endometrium, dissolution of webbing between digits in development, and self-destruction of injured cells. The cell carries its own digestion kit in a bag — hence the name.

  • Application (3m): A patient's ciliated airway epithelium is destroyed by years of smoking. What functional loss follows?

    Answer. The mucus escalator is lost. Beating cilia normally sweep the mucus blanket — loaded with trapped dust, pollen and microbes — upward to the pharynx to be swallowed or spat. With the cilia destroyed, mucus stagnates: pathogens and particles accumulate, causing chronic irritation, repeated chest infections and the persistent smoker's cough — the body compensating with a cough reflex for the transport the cilia no longer perform. Prolonged stasis also exposes the epithelium to carcinogens longer, raising cancer risk.

Key Points

  • Cell wall: cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), peptidoglycan (bacteria); middle lamella → primary → secondary; fully permeable.
  • Membrane: fluid-mosaic model (Singer & Nicolson 1972) — phospholipid bilayer + integral/peripheral proteins + cholesterol + glycocalyx; selective permeability, transport, signalling.
  • Mitochondria: double membrane, cristae with oxysomes, matrix with Krebs enzymes + circular DNA + 70S ribosomes; aerobic respiration → ATP ("powerhouse").
  • Plastids: chloroplast (photosynthesis), chromoplast (colour), leucoplast (storage: amylo/elai/aleuroplast); grana = thylakoid stacks in stroma.
  • RER: ribosome-studded cisternae — protein synthesis for export; SER: tubules — lipids, steroids, detox, Ca²⁺ (sarcoplasmic reticulum).
  • Golgi (Golgi 1898): cis face receives from ER, modifies (glycosylation), trans face ships — vesicles, lysosomes, cell plate.
  • Lysosomes (de Duve): acid hydrolases at pH ~5; digestion, autophagy, autolysis — "suicide bags".
  • Ribosomes (Palade 1953): rRNA + protein, no membrane; 80S = 60S+40S (eukaryote), 70S = 50S+30S (prokaryote/organelles); polysomes; translation.
  • Nucleus (Brown 1831): double envelope + pores, nucleolus (rRNA factory), chromatin (eu- vs heterochromatin) → chromosomes with centromere; metacentric/submetacentric/acrocentric/telocentric.
  • Cilia/flagella: 9+2 microtubule axoneme from basal bodies; cilia short/numerous/oar-like, flagella long/few/undulating.
  • Inclusions: starch, glycogen, fat droplets, protein crystals, pigments, crystals — non-living stores; plant central vacuole maintains turgor.

Summary

The eukaryotic cell is a city of membrane-bounded departments. The cellulose cell wall (with middle lamella, primary and secondary layers) gives shape but is fully permeable; the fluid-mosaic plasma membrane (Singer & Nicolson, 1972) is the selective gatekeeper. Mitochondria (cristae, matrix, own DNA, 70S ribosomes) run aerobic respiration and supply ATP; plastids — chloroplasts with grana-bearing thylakoids in stroma, chromoplasts and leucoplasts — are the plant's photosynthetic and storage organs. The endoplasmic reticulum (rough = protein synthesis, smooth = lipid/detox), the Golgi apparatus (cis receives, trans dispatches) and lysosomes (acid hydrolases, autolysis) form the endomembrane production line, while non-membranous ribosomes (80S) build proteins. The nucleus — envelope with pores, nucleolus, chromatin condensing into centromere-joined chromosomes — stores and expresses heredity. Cilia and flagella share the 9+2 axoneme for movement, and non-living inclusions (starch, glycogen, fat, crystals, vacuolar sap) fill the cytoplasm's storage needs.

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Inside the Eukaryotic Cell

    Topic 2 established that eukaryotic cells have membrane-bound organelles; this topic opens every compartment and explains how each one is built and what it does. The syllabus names the full inventory: cell wall, cell membrane, mitochondria, plastids, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi bodies, lysosomes, ribosomes, nucleus, chromosomes, cilia, flagella and cell inclusions.

    • Why it matters: every disease, drug, hormone and metabolic process acts at some organelle. Antibiotics hit ribosomes; cyanide hits mitochondria; cholesterol drugs hit the ER.
    • Study method: for each organelle learn the same five questions — structure (membranes?), location, chemistry, function, and the evidence/interesting fact. NEB questions are almost always built from these five.
    • Connection: the phospholipid bilayer from Topic 1 is the universal building unit of every structure below.

  • 2. Cell Wall — The Outer Armor of Plant Cells

    Definition: the rigid, non-living outer layer outside the plasma membrane, found in plants, fungi, bacteria and algae (absent in animals).

    FeatureDetail
    ChemistryPlants: cellulose (microfibrils in a matrix of hemicellulose and pectin). Fungi: chitin. Bacteria: peptidoglycan
    LayersMiddle lamella (pectin, cements adjacent cells) → primary wall (thin, extensible, growing cells) → secondary wall (thick, rigid, lignified, mature cells)
    FunctionsShape and rigidity; protection against mechanical injury and pathogens; prevents bursting in hypotonic water (turgor pressure); plasmodesmata allow transport between cells

    Key concept: the wall is fully permeable — it is not the selective barrier; the membrane is. Water moves freely through the wall; osmosis is decided by the membrane.

  • 3. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane) — The Selective Barrier

    3.1 Structure — the fluid-mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972)

    • A phospholipid bilayer ~7–8 nm thick: hydrophilic heads face the aqueous inside and outside; hydrophobic tails form the interior.
    • Proteins float like icebergs in the lipid sea: integral (intrinsic) proteins span the membrane (channels, carriers, pumps); peripheral (extrinsic) proteins sit on the surfaces (enzymes, anchors).
    • Cholesterol in animal membranes stabilises fluidity across temperatures.
    • Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the outer face form the glycocalyx — cell recognition.
    • The membrane is fluid (lipids and proteins move laterally) and a mosaic (patchwork of proteins) — hence the name.

    3.2 Functions

    1. Selective permeability — decides what enters and leaves (small non-polar molecules diffuse freely; ions need channels/pumps).
    2. Transport — passive (diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion) and active (ATP-driven pumps, e.g. Na⁺/K⁺ pump).
    3. Endocytosis and exocytosis — bulk transport in vesicles (phagocytosis, pinocytosis).
    4. Recognition and signalling — receptors for hormones; glycoprotein identity markers.
    5. Compartmentalisation — the same bilayer principle builds every organelle.

    Historical models (short-answer favourite): Robertson (unit membrane, 1950s) → Singer & Nicolson (fluid mosaic, 1972). Also recall: Overton (lipid nature), Gorter & Grendel (bilayer from RBC lipids, 1925), Davson–Danielli (protein sandwich).

  • 4. Mitochondria — The Powerhouse

    4.1 Structure

    • Double-membraned organelle, 0.5–1.0 µm wide, rod or sausage shaped ("chondriosome" — granules seen by Kölliker, 1880s; named by Benda, 1898).
    • Outer membrane: smooth, permeable to small molecules (porins).
    • Inner membrane: folded into cristae — folds that hugely increase surface area; studded with oxysomes (F₁ particles) where ATP is made. Cristae density reflects the cell's energy demand (heart muscle: very dense).
    • Matrix: contains enzymes of the Krebs cycle, circular mitochondrial DNA, 70S ribosomes, tRNA and enzymes of fat/protein oxidation.
    • Semi-autonomous: mitochondria divide by fission like bacteria (endosymbiotic evidence).

    4.2 Function

    • Aerobic respiration: pyruvate → Krebs cycle (matrix) → electron transport chain + oxidative phosphorylation (inner membrane) → ~36 ATP per glucose.
    • Stores and releases Ca²⁺; synthesises some lipids and amino acids; generates heat (brown fat).

    NEB 2022 asked it directly: the mitochondrion is the "powerhouse of the cell" because it carries out aerobic respiration and produces most of the cell's ATP.

  • 5. Plastids — The Plant-Only Organelles

    Plastids are double-membraned, DNA-bearing organelles found only in plants and algae, classified by pigment:

    TypePigmentColourFunctionExample
    ChloroplastChlorophyll a, b + carotenoidsGreenPhotosynthesisMesophyll cells
    ChromoplastCarotenoids (carotene, xanthophyll)Yellow/orange/redPigmentation, attract pollinators and seed dispersersTomato fruit, carrot root, petals

    5.1 Chloroplast structure (draw this in exams)

    • Oval, 4–10 µm; double membrane envelope.
    • Inside: colourless stroma (enzymes of the Calvin cycle, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes) and grana — stacks of disc-like thylakoids holding chlorophyll in their membranes (light reaction site). Grana are connected by stroma lamellae.
    • Semi-autonomous like mitochondria — own DNA and ribosomes (endosymbiosis).

    Functions: photosynthesis (light reaction on thylakoids, carbon fixation in stroma); stores starch temporarily.

  • 6. Endoplasmic Reticulum — The Intracellular Highway

    Definition: a network (reticulum) of membrane-bound tubules, vesicles and flattened sacs (cisternae) running from the nuclear envelope outward. Discovered by Porter, Claude and Fullam (1945) in cultured cells.

    TypeStructureRibosomes?Main roleWhere abundant
    Rough ER (RER)Flattened cisternae, studded with ribosomesYes (80S)Synthesis and folding of proteins destined for membranes, lysosomes or secretionProtein-exporting cells: pancreatic acinar cells, plasma cells
    Smooth ER (SER)Tubular network, no ribosomesNoLipid and steroid synthesis; detoxification of drugs; Ca²⁺ storageSteroid-secreting cells, liver, muscle (as sarcoplasmic reticulum)

    Functions: synthesis and transport of materials; the ER lumen connects to the nuclear envelope and Golgi — part of the endomembrane system; provides surface for enzyme attachment; in muscle, the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ for contraction.

  • 7. Golgi Apparatus — The Packaging and Dispatch Department

    • Discovered by Camillo Golgi (1898) in nerve cells (silver stain).
    • Structure: stacks of 4–8 curved, flattened membrane sacs (cisternae) with a convex forming (cis) face receiving vesicles from the ER and a concave maturing (trans) face shipping vesicles outward.
    • Functions:
    1. Modification of proteins and lipids arriving from the ER (glycosylation — adding sugars).
    2. Packaging into secretory vesicles, lysosomes and membrane-bound granules.
    3. Secretion — dispatch to the cell surface (exocytosis).
    4. Source of lysosomes and of the cell plate during plant cytokinesis.
    5. Complex sugar synthesis (mucopolysaccharides, plant cell-wall matrix).

    Conceptual flow to memorise: RER (make protein) → vesicle → Golgi cis face (modify) → trans face (package) → vesicle → destination (membrane / lysosome / secretion).

  • 8. Lysosomes — The Suicide Bags

    • Discovered by Christian de Duve (1949/1955, Nobel 1974): single-membraned sacs (~0.2–0.8 µm) packed with ~50 acid hydrolases (digestive enzymes active at acidic pH ~5).
    • Why they don't digest the cell: the inner membrane's H⁺-pump keeps the lumen acidic while the cytosol stays near pH 7.2, where the enzymes are inactive — compartmentalisation in action.
    • Forms: primary lysosome (fresh enzymes) → secondary lysosome (fusing with food vacuole = digestive vacuole) → residual body (undigested waste, expelled by exocytosis).
    • Functions: intracellular and extracellular digestion; digestion of worn-out organelles (autophagy); destruction of engulfed pathogens (in white blood cells); autolysis — rupture releases enzymes and digests the cell itself, hence "suicide bags" (e.g. tadpole tail reabsorption during metamorphosis, uterine wall breakdown at menstruation).

  • 9. Ribosomes — The Protein Factories

    • Discovered by Palade (1953, Nobel 1974) — dense granules ~15–20 nm, made of rRNA + proteins, no membrane.
    • Two subunits: eukaryotic 80S = 60S + 40S; prokaryotic/organelle 70S = 50S + 30S.
    • Occur free in the cytosol (making proteins used inside the cell) or attached to RER (making secretory/membrane proteins). Several ribosomes on one mRNA form a polyribosome (polysome).
    • Function: site of protein synthesis (translation) — they read mRNA codons and link amino acids by peptide bonds. Because of the 70S/80S difference, antibiotics (streptomycin, tetracycline) can block bacterial ribosomes without stopping ours.

  • 10. Nucleus — The Control Centre

    10.1 Structure

    • Discovered by Robert Brown (1831). Usually one per cell (multinucleate cells exist — coenocytes); absent in mature RBCs and sieve tubes.
    • Nuclear envelope: double membrane with perinuclear space; perforated by nuclear pores (complex of proteins) that regulate traffic of mRNA out and proteins in.
    • Nucleoplasm: nucleic acids + proteins + enzymes.
    • Nucleolus: dense, rounded, membraneless region of rRNA genes — the ribosome factory.
    • Chromatin: DNA + histones in two forms — euchromatin (loose, active transcription) and heterochromatin (condensed, inactive).

    10.2 Chromosomes — the packaged genome

    • Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes during division. Each chromosome has two chromatids joined at the centromere (primary constriction); some carry a secondary constriction with a satellite.
    • Nucleosome organisation: DNA wound around histone octamers ("beads on a string").
    • Chromosome types by centromere position: metacentric (middle), submetacentric (off-middle), acrocentric (near end), telocentric (at end).
    • Karyotype = the ordered photograph of all chromosomes of a species.
    • Functions of the nucleus: controls all cell activities through gene expression; stores hereditary information; ribosome production; cell division distributes its DNA.
  • Deep dive — the endosymbiont clue. Mitochondria and chloroplasts each carry their own circular DNA, their own 70S ribosomes and a double membrane — the signature of bacteria that moved inside larger cells and stayed. The inner membrane corresponds to the original bacterial membrane; the outer one is the host's engulfing vacuole. NEB loves this as a link between cell structure and evolution: organelle anatomy is fossil evidence you can see in every cell.

    3. Confusing cristae (mitochondria) with thylakoids/grana (chloroplasts).

  • Extra depth — mitochondrion and chloroplast as semi-autonomous organelles

    Both carry their own circular DNA, 70S ribosomes and double membranes, and both divide by fission — supporting the endosymbiotic theory: ancestral eukaryotes engulfed aerobic bacteria (→ mitochondria) and cyanobacteria (→ chloroplasts). The inner mitochondrial membrane folds into cristae carrying the electron-transport chain; the chloroplast stacks thylakoids into grana with the stroma around them. Mitochondria are in every aerobic eukaryote; chloroplasts only in plants and algae — the cleanest single distinction between the two cell types.

  • Extra depth — the endomembrane team

    The ER, Golgi, lysosomes and vesicles form one functional assembly line: rough ER (studded with ribosomes) synthesises proteins → transport vesicles → Golgi modifies, sorts and packages (glycosylation, phosphate addition) → secretory vesicles ship to the membrane, while lysosomes bud off carrying hydrolytic enzymes at pH ~4.8. Smooth ER makes lipids and detoxifies drugs (abundant in liver cells). Defects show as disease: Zellweger syndrome (peroxisome failure), I-cell disease (Golgi tagging failure) — examiners love one named example.

  • Extra depth — cytoskeleton and cell inclusions

    Beyond the membrane-bound organelles, the cell has a protein scaffold: microtubules (tubulin; spindle fibres, cilia/flagella 9+2 array), microfilaments (actin; streaming, cleavage furrow) and intermediate filaments (keratin; mechanical strength). Cilia and flagella share the 9+2 microtubule core with dynein arms that slide tubules to bend them. Cell inclusions — starch grains, glycogen, lipid droplets, crystals — are non-living stored material, not organelles.

Confusion
  • Cell wall vs cell membrane: the wall is outside, rigid, fully permeable, non-living; the membrane is living, selective, and the true gatekeeper of the cell.
  • Cristae vs thylakoids: cristae are folds of the mitochondrial inner membrane; thylakoids are separate flattened discs inside the chloroplast stroma, stacked into grana.
  • Lysosome vs vacuole: lysosomes are single-membraned enzyme sacs for digestion; the plant central vacuole is mainly storage/turgor, though it also contains hydrolases.
  • RER vs SER: ribosomes on the surface define RER and protein synthesis; smooth tubules without ribosomes make lipids and detoxify.
  • Cilia vs flagella: same 9+2 axoneme, but cilia are short, numerous and beat like oars; flagella are long, few and undulate.
  • Nucleolus vs nucleus: the nucleolus is a membraneless sub-region of the nucleus that makes rRNA/ribosomes — not a separate organelle.
  • Inclusions vs organelles: inclusions (starch, fat, crystals) are non-living, non-membrane-bound deposits; organelles are living, organised functional structures.

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): Why is the mitochondrion called the powerhouse of the cell? — Because it performs aerobic respiration: the Krebs cycle in the matrix and the electron transport chain on the cristae convert food energy into most of the cell's ATP, the universal energy currency.
  • Short answer (2m): Distinguish RER and SER (two points). — RER bears ribosomes on flattened cisternae and synthesises proteins for secretion/membranes; SER lacks ribosomes, is tubular, and synthesises lipids/steroids, detoxifies drugs and stores Ca²⁺.
  • Short answer (2m): What are cell inclusions? Give two examples. — Non-living, non-membrane-bound cytoplasmic deposits of food or waste. Examples: starch grains (potato), fat droplets (adipose tissue); also glycogen, protein crystals, pigment granules.
  • Short answer (2m): State the fluid-mosaic model in one sentence and name its proposer. — The membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer with a mosaic of embedded integral and peripheral proteins that can move laterally — proposed by Singer and Nicolson (1972).
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure of a chloroplast with a labelled diagram. — Double envelope → stroma (Calvin enzymes, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes) → grana of stacked thylakoids carrying chlorophyll (light reaction) → stroma lamellae connecting grana. Functions: light reaction on thylakoids, carbon fixation in stroma, temporary starch storage.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure and role of the Golgi apparatus. — Stacks of curved cisternae; cis (forming) face receives ER vesicles, trans (maturing) face dispatches. Roles: modification (glycosylation), packaging, secretion, lysosome formation, plant cell-plate formation, complex polysaccharide synthesis. Include the RER→Golgi→destination flow diagram.
  • Conceptual (3m): Why is the cell membrane called selectively permeable while the cell wall is not? — The membrane is a lipid bilayer with specific channels, carriers and pumps: small non-polar molecules pass by diffusion, ions and sugars only through controlled proteins — it discriminates. The cellulose wall is an open mesh with large pores: water and solutes pass freely; it provides support, not selection.
  • Conceptual (3m): "Lysosomes are suicide bags." Justify. — Lysosomes hold acid hydrolases that can digest every class of biomolecule. Normally the membrane isolates them and the cytosolic pH keeps the enzymes inactive. When a cell is damaged or programmed to die, lysosomes rupture, the enzymes flood the cytoplasm and digest the cell (autolysis) — e.g. tadpole tail resorption, menstrual endometrium breakdown.
  • Application (3m): A patient's ciliated airway epithelium is destroyed by years of smoking. What functional loss follows? — Cilia normally beat mucus loaded with dust and microbes upward to the pharynx. Without cilia, mucus stagnates, pathogens and particles accumulate, causing chronic cough and repeated chest infections — smokers' "smoker's cough" is the compensatory cough reflex.

  • Mitochondrial respiration: glycolysis → Krebs → ETS → ~34 ATP

Key Points
  • Cell wall: cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), peptidoglycan (bacteria); middle lamella → primary → secondary; fully permeable.
  • Membrane: fluid-mosaic model (Singer & Nicolson 1972) — phospholipid bilayer + integral/peripheral proteins + cholesterol + glycocalyx; selective permeability, transport, signalling.
  • Mitochondria: double membrane, cristae with oxysomes, matrix with Krebs enzymes + circular DNA + 70S ribosomes; aerobic respiration → ATP ("powerhouse").
  • Plastids: chloroplast (photosynthesis), chromoplast (colour), leucoplast (storage: amylo/elai/aleuroplast); grana = thylakoid stacks in stroma.
  • RER: ribosome-studded cisternae — protein synthesis for export; SER: tubules — lipids, steroids, detox, Ca²⁺ (sarcoplasmic reticulum).
  • Golgi (Golgi 1898): cis face receives from ER, modifies (glycosylation), trans face ships — vesicles, lysosomes, cell plate.
  • Lysosomes (de Duve): acid hydrolases at pH ~5; digestion, autophagy, autolysis — "suicide bags".
  • Ribosomes (Palade 1953): rRNA + protein, no membrane; 80S = 60S+40S (eukaryote), 70S = 50S+30S (prokaryote/organelles); polysomes; translation.
  • Nucleus (Brown 1831): double envelope + pores, nucleolus (rRNA factory), chromatin (eu- vs heterochromatin) → chromosomes with centromere; metacentric/submetacentric/acrocentric/telocentric.
  • Cilia/flagella: 9+2 microtubule axoneme from basal bodies; cilia short/numerous/oar-like, flagella long/few/undulating.
  • Inclusions: starch, glycogen, fat droplets, protein crystals, pigments, crystals — non-living stores; plant central vacuole maintains turgor.
Summary

The eukaryotic cell is a city of membrane-bounded departments. The cellulose cell wall (with middle lamella, primary and secondary layers) gives shape but is fully permeable; the fluid-mosaic plasma membrane (Singer & Nicolson, 1972) is the selective gatekeeper. Mitochondria (cristae, matrix, own DNA, 70S ribosomes) run aerobic respiration and supply ATP; plastids — chloroplasts with grana-bearing thylakoids in stroma, chromoplasts and leucoplasts — are the plant's photosynthetic and storage organs. The endoplasmic reticulum (rough = protein synthesis, smooth = lipid/detox), the Golgi apparatus (cis receives, trans dispatches) and lysosomes (acid hydrolases, autolysis) form the endomembrane production line, while non-membranous ribosomes (80S) build proteins. The nucleus — envelope with pores, nucleolus, chromatin condensing into centromere-joined chromosomes — stores and expresses heredity. Cilia and flagella share the 9+2 axoneme for movement, and non-living inclusions (starch, glycogen, fat, crystals, vacuolar sap) fill the cytoplasm's storage needs.

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Inside the Eukaryotic Cell

    Topic 2 established that eukaryotic cells have membrane-bound organelles; this topic opens every compartment and explains how each one is built and what it does. The syllabus names the full inventory: cell wall, cell membrane, mitochondria, plastids, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi bodies, lysosomes, ribosomes, nucleus, chromosomes, cilia, flagella and cell inclusions.

    • Why it matters: every disease, drug, hormone and metabolic process acts at some organelle. Antibiotics hit ribosomes; cyanide hits mitochondria; cholesterol drugs hit the ER.
    • Study method: for each organelle learn the same five questions — structure (membranes?), location, chemistry, function, and the evidence/interesting fact. NEB questions are almost always built from these five.
    • Connection: the phospholipid bilayer from Topic 1 is the universal building unit of every structure below.

  • 2. Cell Wall — The Outer Armor of Plant Cells

    Definition: the rigid, non-living outer layer outside the plasma membrane, found in plants, fungi, bacteria and algae (absent in animals).

    FeatureDetail
    ChemistryPlants: cellulose (microfibrils in a matrix of hemicellulose and pectin). Fungi: chitin. Bacteria: peptidoglycan
    LayersMiddle lamella (pectin, cements adjacent cells) → primary wall (thin, extensible, growing cells) → secondary wall (thick, rigid, lignified, mature cells)
    FunctionsShape and rigidity; protection against mechanical injury and pathogens; prevents bursting in hypotonic water (turgor pressure); plasmodesmata allow transport between cells

    Key concept: the wall is fully permeable — it is not the selective barrier; the membrane is. Water moves freely through the wall; osmosis is decided by the membrane.

  • 3. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane) — The Selective Barrier

    3.1 Structure — the fluid-mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972)

    • A phospholipid bilayer ~7–8 nm thick: hydrophilic heads face the aqueous inside and outside; hydrophobic tails form the interior.
    • Proteins float like icebergs in the lipid sea: integral (intrinsic) proteins span the membrane (channels, carriers, pumps); peripheral (extrinsic) proteins sit on the surfaces (enzymes, anchors).
    • Cholesterol in animal membranes stabilises fluidity across temperatures.
    • Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the outer face form the glycocalyx — cell recognition.
    • The membrane is fluid (lipids and proteins move laterally) and a mosaic (patchwork of proteins) — hence the name.

    3.2 Functions

    1. Selective permeability — decides what enters and leaves (small non-polar molecules diffuse freely; ions need channels/pumps).
    2. Transport — passive (diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion) and active (ATP-driven pumps, e.g. Na⁺/K⁺ pump).
    3. Endocytosis and exocytosis — bulk transport in vesicles (phagocytosis, pinocytosis).
    4. Recognition and signalling — receptors for hormones; glycoprotein identity markers.
    5. Compartmentalisation — the same bilayer principle builds every organelle.

    Historical models (short-answer favourite): Robertson (unit membrane, 1950s) → Singer & Nicolson (fluid mosaic, 1972). Also recall: Overton (lipid nature), Gorter & Grendel (bilayer from RBC lipids, 1925), Davson–Danielli (protein sandwich).

  • 4. Mitochondria — The Powerhouse

    4.1 Structure

    • Double-membraned organelle, 0.5–1.0 µm wide, rod or sausage shaped ("chondriosome" — granules seen by Kölliker, 1880s; named by Benda, 1898).
    • Outer membrane: smooth, permeable to small molecules (porins).
    • Inner membrane: folded into cristae — folds that hugely increase surface area; studded with oxysomes (F₁ particles) where ATP is made. Cristae density reflects the cell's energy demand (heart muscle: very dense).
    • Matrix: contains enzymes of the Krebs cycle, circular mitochondrial DNA, 70S ribosomes, tRNA and enzymes of fat/protein oxidation.
    • Semi-autonomous: mitochondria divide by fission like bacteria (endosymbiotic evidence).

    4.2 Function

    • Aerobic respiration: pyruvate → Krebs cycle (matrix) → electron transport chain + oxidative phosphorylation (inner membrane) → ~36 ATP per glucose.
    • Stores and releases Ca²⁺; synthesises some lipids and amino acids; generates heat (brown fat).

    NEB 2022 asked it directly: the mitochondrion is the "powerhouse of the cell" because it carries out aerobic respiration and produces most of the cell's ATP.

  • 5. Plastids — The Plant-Only Organelles

    Plastids are double-membraned, DNA-bearing organelles found only in plants and algae, classified by pigment:

    TypePigmentColourFunctionExample
    ChloroplastChlorophyll a, b + carotenoidsGreenPhotosynthesisMesophyll cells
    ChromoplastCarotenoids (carotene, xanthophyll)Yellow/orange/redPigmentation, attract pollinators and seed dispersersTomato fruit, carrot root, petals
    LeucoplastNo pigmentColourlessStorage: amyloplast (starch), elaioplast (oil), aleuroplast (protein)Potato tuber, castor seed

    5.1 Chloroplast structure (draw this in exams)

    • Oval, 4–10 µm; double membrane envelope.
    • Inside: colourless stroma (enzymes of the Calvin cycle, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes) and grana — stacks of disc-like thylakoids holding chlorophyll in their membranes (light reaction site). Grana are connected by stroma lamellae.
    • Semi-autonomous like mitochondria — own DNA and ribosomes (endosymbiosis).

    Functions: photosynthesis (light reaction on thylakoids, carbon fixation in stroma); stores starch temporarily.

  • 6. Endoplasmic Reticulum — The Intracellular Highway

    Definition: a network (reticulum) of membrane-bound tubules, vesicles and flattened sacs (cisternae) running from the nuclear envelope outward. Discovered by Porter, Claude and Fullam (1945) in cultured cells.

    TypeStructureRibosomes?Main roleWhere abundant
    Rough ER (RER)Flattened cisternae, studded with ribosomesYes (80S)Synthesis and folding of proteins destined for membranes, lysosomes or secretionProtein-exporting cells: pancreatic acinar cells, plasma cells
    Smooth ER (SER)Tubular network, no ribosomesNoLipid and steroid synthesis; detoxification of drugs; Ca²⁺ storageSteroid-secreting cells, liver, muscle (as sarcoplasmic reticulum)

    Functions: synthesis and transport of materials; the ER lumen connects to the nuclear envelope and Golgi — part of the endomembrane system; provides surface for enzyme attachment; in muscle, the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ for contraction.

  • 7. Golgi Apparatus — The Packaging and Dispatch Department

    • Discovered by Camillo Golgi (1898) in nerve cells (silver stain).
    • Structure: stacks of 4–8 curved, flattened membrane sacs (cisternae) with a convex forming (cis) face receiving vesicles from the ER and a concave maturing (trans) face shipping vesicles outward.
    • Functions:
    1. Modification of proteins and lipids arriving from the ER (glycosylation — adding sugars).
    2. Packaging into secretory vesicles, lysosomes and membrane-bound granules.
    3. Secretion — dispatch to the cell surface (exocytosis).
    4. Source of lysosomes and of the cell plate during plant cytokinesis.
    5. Complex sugar synthesis (mucopolysaccharides, plant cell-wall matrix).

    Conceptual flow to memorise: RER (make protein) → vesicle → Golgi cis face (modify) → trans face (package) → vesicle → destination (membrane / lysosome / secretion).

  • 8. Lysosomes — The Suicide Bags

    • Discovered by Christian de Duve (1949/1955, Nobel 1974): single-membraned sacs (~0.2–0.8 µm) packed with ~50 acid hydrolases (digestive enzymes active at acidic pH ~5).
    • Why they don't digest the cell: the inner membrane's H⁺-pump keeps the lumen acidic while the cytosol stays near pH 7.2, where the enzymes are inactive — compartmentalisation in action.
    • Forms: primary lysosome (fresh enzymes) → secondary lysosome (fusing with food vacuole = digestive vacuole) → residual body (undigested waste, expelled by exocytosis).
    • Functions: intracellular and extracellular digestion; digestion of worn-out organelles (autophagy); destruction of engulfed pathogens (in white blood cells); autolysis — rupture releases enzymes and digests the cell itself, hence "suicide bags" (e.g. tadpole tail reabsorption during metamorphosis, uterine wall breakdown at menstruation).

  • 9. Ribosomes — The Protein Factories

    • Discovered by Palade (1953, Nobel 1974) — dense granules ~15–20 nm, made of rRNA + proteins, no membrane.
    • Two subunits: eukaryotic 80S = 60S + 40S; prokaryotic/organelle 70S = 50S + 30S.
    • Occur free in the cytosol (making proteins used inside the cell) or attached to RER (making secretory/membrane proteins). Several ribosomes on one mRNA form a polyribosome (polysome).
    • Function: site of protein synthesis (translation) — they read mRNA codons and link amino acids by peptide bonds. Because of the 70S/80S difference, antibiotics (streptomycin, tetracycline) can block bacterial ribosomes without stopping ours.

  • 10. Nucleus — The Control Centre

    10.1 Structure

    • Discovered by Robert Brown (1831). Usually one per cell (multinucleate cells exist — coenocytes); absent in mature RBCs and sieve tubes.
    • Nuclear envelope: double membrane with perinuclear space; perforated by nuclear pores (complex of proteins) that regulate traffic of mRNA out and proteins in.
    • Nucleoplasm: nucleic acids + proteins + enzymes.
    • Nucleolus: dense, rounded, membraneless region of rRNA genes — the ribosome factory.
    • Chromatin: DNA + histones in two forms — euchromatin (loose, active transcription) and heterochromatin (condensed, inactive).

    10.2 Chromosomes — the packaged genome

    • Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes during division. Each chromosome has two chromatids joined at the centromere (primary constriction); some carry a secondary constriction with a satellite.
    • Nucleosome organisation: DNA wound around histone octamers ("beads on a string").
    • Chromosome types by centromere position: metacentric (middle), submetacentric (off-middle), acrocentric (near end), telocentric (at end).
    • Karyotype = the ordered photograph of all chromosomes of a species.
    • Functions of the nucleus: controls all cell activities through gene expression; stores hereditary information; ribosome production; cell division distributes its DNA.

Confusion
  • Cell wall vs cell membrane: the wall is outside, rigid, fully permeable, non-living; the membrane is living, selective, and the true gatekeeper of the cell.
  • Cristae vs thylakoids: cristae are folds of the mitochondrial inner membrane; thylakoids are separate flattened discs inside the chloroplast stroma, stacked into grana.
  • Lysosome vs vacuole: lysosomes are single-membraned enzyme sacs for digestion; the plant central vacuole is mainly storage/turgor, though it also contains hydrolases.
  • RER vs SER: ribosomes on the surface define RER and protein synthesis; smooth tubules without ribosomes make lipids and detoxify.
  • Cilia vs flagella: same 9+2 axoneme, but cilia are short, numerous and beat like oars; flagella are long, few and undulate.
  • Nucleolus vs nucleus: the nucleolus is a membraneless sub-region of the nucleus that makes rRNA/ribosomes — not a separate organelle.
  • Inclusions vs organelles: inclusions (starch, fat, crystals) are non-living, non-membrane-bound deposits; organelles are living, organised functional structures.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Distinguish RER and SER (two points). — RER bears ribosomes on flattened cisternae and synthesises proteins for secretion/membranes; SER lacks ribosomes, is tubular, and synthesises lipids/steroids, detoxifies drugs and stores Ca²⁺.
  • Short answer (2m): What are cell inclusions? Give two examples. — Non-living, non-membrane-bound cytoplasmic deposits of food or waste. Examples: starch grains (potato), fat droplets (adipose tissue); also glycogen, protein crystals, pigment granules.
  • Short answer (2m): State the fluid-mosaic model in one sentence and name its proposer. — The membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer with a mosaic of embedded integral and peripheral proteins that can move laterally — proposed by Singer and Nicolson (1972).
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure of a chloroplast with a labelled diagram. — Double envelope → stroma (Calvin enzymes, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes) → grana of stacked thylakoids carrying chlorophyll (light reaction) → stroma lamellae connecting grana.   • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure and role of the Golgi apparatus. — Stacks of curved cisternae; cis (forming) face receives ER vesicles, trans (maturing) face dispatches. The cellulose wall is an open mesh with large pores: water and solutes pass freely; it provides support, not selection.

  • Cell wall: cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), peptidoglycan (bacteria); middle lamella → primary → secondary; fully permeable.
  • Membrane: fluid-mosaic model (Singer & Nicolson 1972) — phospholipid bilayer + integral/peripheral proteins + cholesterol + glycocalyx; selective permeability, transport, signalling.
  • Mitochondria: double membrane, cristae with oxysomes, matrix with Krebs enzymes + circular DNA + 70S ribosomes; aerobic respiration → ATP ("powerhouse").
  • Plastids: chloroplast (photosynthesis), chromoplast (colour), leucoplast (storage: amylo/elai/aleuroplast); grana = thylakoid stacks in stroma.
  • RER: ribosome-studded cisternae — protein synthesis for export; SER: tubules — lipids, steroids, detox, Ca²⁺ (sarcoplasmic reticulum).
  • Golgi (Golgi 1898): cis face receives from ER, modifies (glycosylation), trans face ships — vesicles, lysosomes, cell plate.
  • Lysosomes (de Duve): acid hydrolases at pH ~5; digestion, autophagy, autolysis — "suicide bags".
  • Ribosomes (Palade 1953): rRNA + protein, no membrane; 80S = 60S+40S (eukaryote), 70S = 50S+30S (prokaryote/organelles); polysomes; translation.
  • Nucleus (Brown 1831): double envelope + pores, nucleolus (rRNA factory), chromatin (eu- vs heterochromatin) → chromosomes with centromere; metacentric/submetacentric/acrocentric/telocentric.
  • Cilia/flagella: 9+2 microtubule axoneme from basal bodies; cilia short/numerous/oar-like, flagella long/few/undulating.
  • Inclusions: starch, glycogen, fat droplets, protein crystals, pigments, crystals — non-living stores; plant central vacuole maintains turgor.
Summary

The eukaryotic cell is a city of membrane-bounded departments. The cellulose cell wall (with middle lamella, primary and secondary layers) gives shape but is fully permeable; the fluid-mosaic plasma membrane (Singer & Nicolson, 1972) is the selective gatekeeper. Mitochondria (cristae, matrix, own DNA, 70S ribosomes) run aerobic respiration and supply ATP; plastids — chloroplasts with grana-bearing thylakoids in stroma, chromoplasts and leucoplasts — are the plant's photosynthetic and storage organs. The endoplasmic reticulum (rough = protein synthesis, smooth = lipid/detox), the Golgi apparatus (cis receives, trans dispatches) and lysosomes (acid hydrolases, autolysis) form the endomembrane production line, while non-membranous ribosomes (80S) build proteins. The nucleus — envelope with pores, nucleolus, chromatin condensing into centromere-joined chromosomes — stores and expresses heredity. Cilia and flagella share the 9+2 axoneme for movement, and non-living inclusions (starch, glycogen, fat, crystals, vacuolar sap) fill the cytoplasm's storage needs.

Mitochondrial inner membrane folds into cristae carrying the electron-transport chain; chloroplasts are double-membraned but their internal folds are thylakoids/grana.

Options
  • Robertson
  • Singer and Nicolson
  • Davson and Danielli
  • Gorter and Grendel
Answer

B

Explanation

Singer and Nicolson (1972) described the membrane as a fluid lipid bilayer with a mosaic of proteins; Robertson proposed the earlier rigid unit-membrane.

3.

Which organelle is called the 'suicide bag' of the cell?

Options
  • Ribosome
  • Lysosome
  • Peroxisome
  • Vacuole
Answer

B

Explanation

Lysosomes pack acid hydrolases; on rupture (autolysis) they digest the cell itself — hence de Duve's 'suicide bags'.

4.

Ribosomes are produced in the:

Options
  • Nucleolus
  • Nuclear pore
  • Rough ER
  • Golgi body
Answer

A

Explanation

The nucleolus transcribes rRNA and assembles ribosomal subunits; they then exit through nuclear pores to the cytoplasm.

5.

Which combination is correct for an 80S ribosome?

Options
  • 50S + 30S
  • 60S + 40S
  • 70S + 10S
  • 40S + 30S
Answer

B

Explanation

Eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S = 60S + 40S; 70S = 50S + 30S belongs to prokaryotes and organelles.

Which plastid stores oil?

Options
  • Amyloplast
  • Elaioplast
  • Aleuroplast
  • Chromoplast
Answer

B

Explanation

Leucoplasts specialise: amyloplast stores starch, elaioplast stores oil, aleuroplast stores protein.

7.

The 9+2 microtubule arrangement is found in:

Options
  • Bacterial flagella
  • Centrioles only
  • Cilia and flagella
  • Cell walls
Answer

C

Explanation

Motile eukaryotic cilia and flagella share the 9+2 axoneme; bacterial flagella are protein filaments without microtubules.

8.

Which face of the Golgi apparatus receives vesicles from the ER?

Options
  • Trans face
  • Cis face
  • Lateral face
  • Maturing face
Answer

B

Explanation

The convex cis (forming) face receives ER vesicles; the concave trans (maturing) face ships modified products out.

9.

Which cell would have abundant smooth ER?

Options
  • Pancreatic enzyme-secreting cell
  • Steroid-hormone-secreting cell
  • Plasma antibody cell
  • Nerve cell body
Answer

B

Explanation

SER makes lipids and steroids and detoxifies drugs — abundant in steroid-secreting (and liver) cells. Protein-exporting cells are RER-rich.

Which structure is fully permeable?

Options
  • Plasma membrane
  • Mitochondrial inner membrane
  • Cell wall
  • Tonoplast
Answer

C

Explanation

The cellulose wall is an open mesh — water and solutes pass freely; all membranes listed are selective barriers.

Important Concepts
  • Endomembrane system flow: nucleus → RER → Golgi (cis→trans) → vesicles → membrane/lysosome/secretion — the cell's production line.

  • Semi-autonomous organelles: mitochondria and chloroplasts carry circular DNA + 70S ribosomes and divide by fission — the endosymbiotic signature.
