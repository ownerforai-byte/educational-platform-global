Enriched Content

Notes
  • Biomolecules are organic molecules essential to life: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, plus minerals, enzymes and water.
  • Carbohydrates (CH2O)n: monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, ribose), disaccharides (sucrose=glucose+fructose, lactose, maltose), polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose). Provide energy (4 kcal/g) and structural support.
  • Proteins: polymers of 20 standard amino acids (L-forms; glycine is achiral) joined by peptide (-CO-NH-) bonds. Four structural levels (primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary). Functions: enzymes, transport (haemoglobin), contraction, defence, structural.
  • Lipids: hydrophobic; triglycerides (fat storage, 9 kcal/g), phospholipids (membrane bilayer), steroids (cholesterol, hormones). Not true polymers.
  • Nucleic acids: DNA (double helix, deoxyribose, A=T 2 H-bonds, G=C 3 H-bonds) stores heredity; RNA (single strand, ribose, A=U) in protein synthesis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA).
  • Enzymes: biological catalysts (mostly proteins); work by the lock-and-key / induced-fit model; denatured by high temperature or extreme pH; show high substrate specificity.
  • Water (major solvent, ~70% of cell) and minerals (Ca, P, K, Na, Fe, I) act as structural, catalytic and osmotic regulators; deficiency causes deficiency diseases.
Confusion
  • Cellulose is a structural polymer in plant cell walls, not an energy reserve; glycogen (animal) and starch (plant) are the storage forms.
  • Starch is unbranched amylose + branched amylopectin; both yield maltose on hydrolysis.
  • Ribose and deoxyribose differ only by one oxygen atom on C-2; hence RNA vs DNA sugars.
  • Denaturation unfolds protein (loss of secondary/tertiary structure); it is often reversible at low temperature.
  • Lipids are not polymers; they do not have repeating monomers like the other three macromolecule classes.
  • A peptide bond forms between the -COOH of one amino acid and the -NH2 of the next, releasing one water (condensation).
Practice
  • Match each biomolecule to a function: insulin (hormone), haemoglobin (transport), cellulose (structure), ATP (energy currency).
  • Draw a dipeptide from glycine + alanine and circle the peptide bond.
  • Differentiate DNA and RNA by sugar, bases, strands and function.
  • Explain why an enzyme is still present after catalysing a reaction.
  • Relate an enzyme deficiency (e.g. phenylalanine) to a disease (PKU).
Universal Facts
  • Carbon makes up roughly a fifth of human body mass and forms the covalent backbone of every biomolecule.
  • Hemoglobin binds about 1.34 mL of oxygen per gram; fully saturated blood carries roughly 20 mL of oxygen per 100 mL.
  • Water has an anomalously high specific heat and acts as a biological thermostat.
  • Human insulin (51 amino acids) was produced by recombinant-DNA technology in 1978 and approved in 1982 — the first approved recombinant-DNA pharmaceutical.
Examples
  • Starch in rice/potato is a storage polysaccharide; glycogen in liver/muscle is the animal reserve.
  • Cellulose (beta-glucose) forms the cell wall of green plants.
  • DNA replication and mRNA synthesis link nucleic acids to protein production.
  • Iodine deficiency enlarges the thyroid; iron deficiency causes anaemia.
Practice Questions
  • How is a triglyceride different from a phospholipid in structure and function?
  • List the four structural levels of a protein with one example of a quaternary protein.
  • Describe the induced-fit model of enzyme action and its significance.
  • Why are essential amino acids called "essential"?
  • Differentiate amylose and amylopectin and state the test for starch.
Formulas
  • General carbohydrate ratio: C : H : O = 1 : 2 : 1, i.e. (CH2O)n
  • Endergonic polymerisation (condensation): monomer + monomer → dimer + H2O
  • Peptide unit: -C(=O)-NH-
  • DNA base pairing: A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds)
Key Points
  • Functions: C=energy+structure, P=enzymes/transport/structure, L=storage+membrane, N=heredity+protein synthesis.
  • A hydrolysis breaks a bond + water; a condensation joins molecules - water.
  • Enzymes are specific, reversible regulators, not consumed in the reaction.
  • DNA is the gene; RNA is the messenger/scaffold in protein synthesis.
  • Essential amino acids (8 in adults, 9 in children) must come from food.
  • Deficiency of minerals leads to named diseases (Fe→anaemia, I→goitre, Ca→rickets).
Summary

Biomolecules are the chemical basis of life. Carbohydrates supply and store energy and form structural fibres; proteins (from 20 amino acids) act as enzymes, transporters, contractile and defence molecules in four structural levels; lipids store energy and build membranes; nucleic acids (DNA/RNA) store and express genetic information; enzymes, water and minerals complete the set. Mastery of structure-function links and enzyme kinetics is a frequent NEB examination focus.

Special Notes
  • In NEB written answers, pair every macromolecule with BOTH its monomer and its key function to earn full marks.
  • Protein denaturation is a favourite "application" question (cooking an egg, or fever denaturing enzymes).
  • Stress that enzymes lower activation energy; they do NOT supply energy.
Important Statements
  • All cells share the same basic biochemical machinery of DNA→RNA→protein (central dogma).
  • The 20 naturally occurring L-amino acids differ only in their R-group (side chain).
  • Phospholipid bilayers form spontaneously because the head is hydrophilic and the tail is hydrophobic.
  • Enzyme specificity is explained by the induced-fit model.
  • Water is the universal biological solvent and a participant in hydrolysis and condensation.
Important Notes
  • Memorise monomer → polymer: glucose→starch, amino acid→protein, fatty acid+glycerol→triglyceride, nucleotide→nucleic acid.
  • Learn base-pairing and hydrogen-bond counts (A=T=2, G=C=3).
  • Do 2–3 diagram questions: phospholipid bilayer, peptide bond, a DNA nucleotide ladder.
  • Link enzyme inhibitors (competitive vs non-competitive) to drug design.
Exam Short Tricks
  • Remember C-H-O-N: C=energy, P=work, L=lipid, N=genetic.
  • A-T two H-bonds (think "A" has 2 strokes), G-C three.
  • Enzymes are proteins that END in "-ase".
  • Starch test: iodine → blue-black; reducing sugar: Benedict's → brick-red.
Exam Notes
  • High frequency: monomer/polymer matching tables, enzyme action and factors affecting enzymes.
  • Diagram-based marks: label a phospholipid bilayer and a DNA nucleotide.
  • Application: explain a disease caused by a biomolecule deficiency.
  • Do not confuse structural (cellulose) with storage (starch/glycogen) polysaccharides.

Exercises
1.

A polypeptide contains 200 amino acids. How many peptide bonds and how many water molecules are involved?

ID

1

Steps
  • n residues have (n-1) peptide bonds
  • 200 - 1 = 199 bonds
  • Each peptide bond releases 1 water
  • Hence 199 water molecules released
Answer

199 peptide bonds and 199 H2O

2.

In a DNA sample, adenine is 20%. What percentage is guanine?

ID

2

Steps
  • Chargaff: A = T → T = 20%
  • A + T + G + C = 100%
  • G + C = 60%; G = C → G = 30%
Answer

Guanine = 30%
