  • 1. Topic Introduction — How Life Copies Itself at the Cellular Level

    A cell's life is a cycle of growth, DNA copying and division. Every scar that heals, every root that grows, every sperm and egg that carries your genes to the next generation is the result of cell division.

    • Why it matters: growth, repair, reproduction and heredity all pass through this topic; it also explains cancer (uncontrolled division) and genetic variation (crossing over).
    • The one-sentence core: mitosis copies a cell exactly; meiosis halves the chromosome number and shuffles genes.
    • Connections: chromosomes from Topic 3 are the actors here; meiosis connects directly to heredity and evolution (variation), and mitosis to growth and healing.

  • 2. The Cell Cycle — The Cell's Life Story

    2.1 Definition and the grand division

    The cell cycle (Howard and Pelc, 1953) is the sequence of events from the formation of a cell to its own division into two daughter cells. It has two great phases:

    Cell cycle=Interphase (I-phase)I1​+S+I2​​​+DivisionM phase​​+C(cytokinesis)

    PhaseNameDuration (typical 24 h human cell)What happens
    G₁First gap (post-mitotic)~11 hGrowth; RNA and proteins synthesised; organelles multiply; cell decides to divide or exit (G₀)
    SSynthesis~8 hDNA replication — each chromosome is duplicated into two sister chromatids; histones synthesised
    G₂Second gap (pre-mitotic)~4 hFurther growth; spindle proteins, microtubules and RNA made; cell checks DNA for errors
    MMitotic phase~1 hKaryokinesis (nuclear division): prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase, then cytokinesis

    Interphase misconception: interphase is NOT a resting phase — it is the most metabolically active period (DNA replication and growth happen here); only division is absent.

    2.2 Checkpoints and G₀

    • Checkpoints (G₁/S, G2/M, spindle checkpoint) verify that DNA is intact and complete before the next step — the cell's quality control.
    • Cells that permanently leave the cycle enter G₀ (quiescent phase): mature neurons and muscle cells stay in G₀; liver cells can re-enter the cycle on injury.
    • Cancer = checkpoint failure: cells escape control and divide endlessly. This single sentence is the conceptual bridge between cell division and oncology.

  • 3. Amitosis — Direct Division

    Definition: direct, simple splitting of the nucleus (and then cytoplasm) without spindle formation, without chromosome condensation — described by Remak (1841).

    FeatureDetail
    WhereProkaryotes, some yeast, degenerating/old cells, foetal membranes, cartilage cells
    MechanismNucleus elongates, constricts in the middle; cytoplasm divides; two nuclei form
    ResultTwo cells with roughly equal but imprecisely distributed nuclear material
    SignificanceFast, low-cost division for simple/fast-growing or dying tissues
    DrawbackUnequal distribution of chromatin; no genetic precision

  • 4. Mitosis — Equational Division

    4.1 Definition

    Mitosis (Flemming, 1882; Greek mitos = thread) is the division of a somatic cell into two daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent (2n → 2n). It has karyokinesis (nuclear division) followed by cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division).

    4.2 The four stages — what happens and why

    StageKey eventsStructures to name
    ProphaseChromatin condenses into visible chromosomes (each = 2 sister chromatids joined at the centromere); nucleolus disappears; nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle begins; centrioles move to poles (animals)Sister chromatids, centromere, spindle
    MetaphaseChromosomes line up on the equatorial (metaphase) plate; spindle fibres from both poles attach to kinetochores; the best stage to count and study chromosomes (karyotyping)Equatorial plate, kinetochore fibres, polar fibres
    AnaphaseCentromeres split; sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles (appears V-, J- or L-shaped by centromere position); poles move apartDaughter chromosomes, spindle elongation
    TelophaseChromosomes decondense; nuclear envelopes reform around each set; nucleoli reappear; spindle dissolvesTwo daughter nuclei

    4.3 Cytokinesis — two different solutions

    BasisPlant cytokinesisAnimal cytokinesis
    MechanismCell plate forms from Golgi vesicles at the equator, growing outward until it fuses with the wallCleavage furrow: actin–myosin ring contracts, pinching the membrane inward
    DirectionCentripetal (inside → outside)Centripetal (outside → inside)
    Why the differenceRigid wall cannot be pinchedNo wall, flexible membrane

    4.4 Significance of mitosis (exam list — give the reason with each)

    1. Growth of multicellular organisms (zygote → body by repeated mitosis).
    2. Repair and healing — replacement of worn-out and wounded cells.
    3. Genetic stability — exact copies: daughter cells keep the same chromosome number and gene set.
    4. Asexual reproduction — binary fission, budding, vegetative propagation are mitotic.
    5. Regeneration — replacement of lost parts (lizard tail, planaria).
    6. Maintains the surface-area-to-volume ratio of tissues by renewing cells.

  • 5. Meiosis — Reductional Division

    5.1 Definition

    Meiosis (Farmer and Moore, 1905; Greek meion = to lessen) is a single DNA replication followed by two divisions, producing four haploid (n) cells from one diploid (2n) cell. It occurs only in germ (reproductive) cells — sporocytes in plants, gamete-forming cells in animals. It consists of meiosis I (reductional) and meiosis II (equational), with a brief interkinesis between them (no DNA replication in interkinesis — key point).

    5.2 Meiosis I — the reduction

    StageKey events
    Prophase I (longest, most complex — 5 substages)See below
    Metaphase IHomologous pairs (bivalents/tetrads) align on the equatorial plate in two rows; spindle attaches to kinetochores of homologs (not sister chromatids)
    Anaphase IWhole homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles — centromeres do NOT split; sister chromatids stay together
    Telophase IHaploid set (each chromosome still double-stranded) reaches each pole; nuclear envelope may re-form; cytokinesis follows

    Prophase I substages (the most examined sequence in this topic):

    SubstageEvents
    LeptoteneChromosomes condense and become visible
    ZygoteneSynapsis — homologous chromosomes pair; a synaptonemal complex forms
    PachyteneCrossing over — non-sister chromatids of the bivalent exchange segments at recombination nodules; visible chiasmata appear; recombinase (Reca) enzymes act
    DiploteneSynaptonemal complex dissolves; chiasmata clearly visible; homologs begin to separate (oocytes of some vertebrates stay here for years — dictyotene)
    DiakinesisTerminalisation of chiasmata; chromosomes fully condensed; nucleolus and envelope disappear; spindle forms

    5.3 Meiosis II — the equational division

    After interkinesis (no DNA replication), each haploid cell divides like a mitosis: prophase II → metaphase II (chromosomes in single row) → anaphase II (centromeres split, sister chromatids separate) → telophase II. Result: four haploid cells.

    5.4 Significance of meiosis (exam list)

    1. Maintains the chromosome number constant across generations — 2n gamete-forming cells → n gametes; fertilisation restores 2n.
    2. Creates genetic variation by crossing over (pachytene) and independent assortment (metaphase I orientation) — the raw material of evolution.
    3. Forms gametes/spores for sexual reproduction.
    4. Repairs defects and aids adaptation through recombination.
    5. Aids speciation — variation on which natural selection acts.

  • 6. Conceptual Relationships

    • Topic 3 → here: the spindle is built from microtubules; the Golgi makes the plant cell plate; chromosomes come from nuclear chromatin.
    • Here → heredity (Class 12): segregation of homologs (meiosis I) and independent assortment are the physical basis of Mendel's laws.
    • Here → evolution: crossing over and independent assortment generate the variation natural selection acts on.
    • Here → health: loss of mitotic control = cancer; failure of meiotic segregation (nondisjunction) = chromosomal disorders (Down syndrome, trisomy 21).
    • Here → reproduction: gametogenesis in animals and sporogenesis in plants are meiotic; fertilisation reverses the reduction.

    7.1 Previous year questions

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Define the cell cycle. Describe the events of interphase. Answer: The cell cycle is the sequence of changes a cell undergoes from its formation to its own division. Interphase (the metabolically active "resting" phase, ~23 h of a 24 h cycle) has three sub-phases: G₁ — growth, RNA/protein synthesis, organelle multiplication; S — DNA replication, producing two sister chromatids per chromosome; G₂ — spindle-protein synthesis and final preparation for mitosis.

    Q2 (5m) — Describe the stages of mitosis with diagrams. Answer: Draw the four stages. Prophase: condensation into two-chromatid chromosomes, nuclear envelope and nucleolus disappear, spindle forms. Metaphase: chromosomes align on the equatorial plate, kinetochore fibres from both poles attach. Anaphase: centromeres split, sister chromatids move to opposite poles. Telophase: envelopes and nucleoli reform, chromosomes decondense. End with cytokinesis: cell plate (plants) vs cleavage furrow (animals).

    Q3 (5m) — Describe prophase I of meiosis and state its significance. Answer: Five substages — leptotene (chromosomes visible), zygotene (synapsis of homologs, synaptonemal complex), pachytene (crossing over at recombination nodules, chiasmata appear), diplotene (complex dissolves, chiasmata visible), diakinesis (terminalisation, spindle forms). Significance: crossing over produces new gene combinations — the source of genetic variation.

    Q4 (3m) — Give four differences between mitosis and meiosis. Answer: Any four table rows — occurrence (somatic vs germ cells), divisions (one vs two), products (2 identical 2n vs 4 varied n cells), crossing over (absent vs present), metaphase arrangement (one row vs pairs in two rows).

    Q5 (3m) — Why is meiosis significant for a species? Answer: (1) It halves the chromosome number so fertilisation can restore it — chromosome constancy across generations; (2) crossing over and independent assortment create genetic variation; (3) variation gives natural selection material to act on, enabling adaptation and evolution.

    Q6 (2m) — Why is interphase not a "resting phase"? Answer: Because DNA replication (S phase), massive protein/RNA synthesis and growth (G₁, G₂) all occur then — the cell is metabolically at its busiest; only nuclear division is absent.

    Q7 (2m) — How does cytokinesis differ in plant and animal cells? Answer: Plants: a cell plate of Golgi vesicles forms at the equator and grows outward (centripetal) to become the middle lamella + wall. Animals: an actin–myosin contractile ring pinches the membrane inward as a cleavage furrow.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying DNA replicates "before meiosis II" — replication happens only before meiosis I; interkinesis has none.
    2. Splitting centromeres in anaphase I — homologs separate whole; centromeres split only in anaphase II (and mitosis).
    3. Drawing metaphase I chromosomes in a single row — they align as pairs in two rows.
    4. Confusing chiasmata (visible X-shaped contact points) with crossing over (the exchange event at pachytene).
    5. Writing "meiosis produces gametes" for plants without mentioning spores (meiosis makes spores in plants; gametes form by mitosis in the gametophyte).
    6. Calling amitosis the main division of prokaryotes without noting its imprecision (unequal chromatin distribution).
Confusion
  • Chromatid vs chromosome: after S phase, each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at one centromere — it is still ONE chromosome. The count changes only when centromeres split (anaphase).
  • Mitosis vs meiosis II: both split centromeres and separate sister chromatids, but meiosis II starts from haploid, recombined cells and follows meiosis I without new DNA replication.
  • Crossing over vs independent assortment: crossing over = physical exchange between non-sister chromatids (pachytene); independent assortment = random orientation of bivalents at metaphase I. Both create variation, by different mechanisms.
  • Interkinesis vs interphase: interkinesis between meiosis I and II has NO DNA replication; interphase of the cell cycle always includes S phase.
  • Amitosis vs binary fission: related but not identical — amitosis is the imprecise direct nuclear division seen in some eukaryotic cells; bacterial binary fission is precise DNA replication followed by septation.

Universal Facts
  • Typical human cell cycle ≈ 24 h: G₁ 11 h, S 8 h, G₂ 4 h, M 1 h.
  • Flemming observed and named mitosis (1882); term "meiosis" — Farmer and Moore (1905).
  • Crossing over occurs at pachytene; visible chiasmata from diplotene.
Examples
  • Bone marrow — mitotic factory replacing blood cells (~200 billion RBCs/day).
  • Healing skin wound — mitosis fills the gap from the wound edge.
  • Human spermatogenesis — meiosis I + II convert one spermatogonium into 4 haploid spermatids.
  • Lily anther sporocytes — classic meiosis-I slide material; pollen grains are the haploid products.
Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): Define cell cycle. — The sequence of events by which a cell grows, replicates its DNA and divides into two daughter cells; it comprises interphase (G₁, S, G₂) and the M phase with cytokinesis.
  • Short answer (2m): What is crossing over? — The exchange of corresponding segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during pachytene of prophase I, producing recombined chromosomes and genetic variation.
  • Short answer (2m): What is a bivalent? — The pair of homologous chromosomes (four chromatids, hence "tetrad") formed by synapsis during zygotene of prophase I.
  • Short answer (2m): What is the G₀ phase? — A quiescent state outside the active cycle into which cells withdraw after mitosis (e.g. mature neurons); they remain metabolically active but do not divide, though some can re-enter the cycle on stimulus.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the stages of mitosis with labelled diagrams and state its significance. — Prophase (condensation, spindle, envelope loss) → metaphase (equatorial plate, spindle attachment) → anaphase (centromere split, chromatids to poles) → telophase (re-formation of nuclei) → cytokinesis (cell plate vs furrow). Significance: growth, repair, genetic stability, asexual reproduction, regeneration.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe meiosis I with diagrams and state how it reduces the chromosome number. — Prophase I with five substages (synapsis, crossing over), metaphase I (bivalents in two rows), anaphase I (whole homologs separate — centromere intact), telophase I (haploid nuclei). Reduction happens because each pole receives one chromosome of every homologous pair, while each chromosome still has two chromatids.
  • Conceptual (3m): Why does meiosis create variation while mitosis does not? — Mitosis copies an existing genome exactly (no pairing, no exchange). Meiosis adds two sources of novelty: crossing over recombines maternal and paternal alleles within chromosomes (pachytene), and independent assortment randomises which homolog of each pair reaches which pole (metaphase I). Each gamete therefore carries a unique combination.
  • Conceptual (3m): "Cancer is a disease of the cell cycle." Explain. — Normal division is stopped at checkpoints by control genes (proto-oncogenes, tumour suppressors such as p53). When mutations disable these brakes, cells skip G₁/S and spindle checkpoints and divide without restraint, invading tissues — uncontrolled mitosis is the mechanism of tumour growth.
  • Application (2m): Why do antibiotics/chemotherapy drugs that block spindle formation stop tumour growth? — Drugs like colchicine and vinblastine prevent microtubule assembly, so the mitotic spindle cannot form; cells arrest in metaphase and cannot complete division. Rapidly dividing tumour cells are hit hardest.
Formulas
  • Mitosis: 2n → 2 daughter cells of 2n
  • Meiosis: 2n → 4 gametes of n
  • Crossing over (Prophase I) + independent assortment (Metaphase I) → variation
  • Chromosome count: after S each chromosome = 2 chromatids
Key Points
  • Cell cycle = interphase (G₁ growth, S DNA synthesis, G₂ preparation) + M phase (mitosis) + cytokinesis; checkpoints guard the transitions; G₀ = quiescent exit.
  • Interphase is the most active phase — DNA replication and growth happen there.
  • Amitosis: direct splitting, no spindle, imprecise (Remak, 1841).
  • Mitosis (Flemming, 1882): prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase; centromeres split in anaphase; 2 identical 2n daughters; growth, repair, asexual reproduction.
  • Cytokinesis: cell plate (plants, centripetal from Golgi vesicles) vs cleavage furrow (animals, actin–myosin).
  • Meiosis (Farmer & Moore, 1905): one replication + two divisions → 4 haploid varied cells; only in germ cells.
  • Prophase I: leptotene → zygotene (synapsis) → pachytene (crossing over, chiasmata) → diplotene → diakinesis.
  • Anaphase I separates whole homologs (centromere intact); anaphase II splits centromeres.
  • Meiosis significance: chromosome-number constancy + genetic variation + gamete/spore formation.
Summary

The cell cycle runs through interphase — G₁ growth, S DNA replication, G₂ preparation — into the M phase. Amitosis is a rare, imprecise direct splitting. Mitosis (prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase, then cytokinesis by cell plate in plants or cleavage furrow in animals) produces two genetically identical diploid cells for growth, repair and asexual reproduction. Meiosis — one replication followed by two divisions — produces four genetically unique haploid cells: prophase I (leptotene, zygotene synapsis, pachytene crossing over, diplotene, diakinesis), metaphase I with bivalents in two rows, anaphase I separating whole homologs, then the equational meiosis II. Meiosis keeps chromosome numbers constant across generations and generates the variation on which evolution acts; its failure (nondisjunction) and mitosis's failure (checkpoint loss → cancer) show why precise division matters.

    2.2 Checkpoints and G₀

    • Checkpoints (G₁/S, G2/M, spindle checkpoint) verify that DNA is intact and complete before the next step — the cell's quality control.
    • Cells that permanently leave the cycle enter G₀ (quiescent phase): mature neurons and muscle cells stay in G₀; liver cells can re-enter the cycle on injury.
    • Cancer = checkpoint failure: cells escape control and divide endlessly. This single sentence is the conceptual bridge between cell division and oncology.

  • 3. Amitosis — Direct Division

    Definition: direct, simple splitting of the nucleus (and then cytoplasm) without spindle formation, without chromosome condensation — described by Remak (1841).

  • 4. Mitosis — Equational Division

    4.1 Definition

    Mitosis (Flemming, 1882; Greek mitos = thread) is the division of a somatic cell into two daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent (2n → 2n). It has karyokinesis (nuclear division) followed by cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division).

    4.4 Significance of mitosis (exam list — give the reason with each)

    1. Growth of multicellular organisms (zygote → body by repeated mitosis).
    2. Repair and healing — replacement of worn-out and wounded cells.
    3. Genetic stability — exact copies: daughter cells keep the same chromosome number and gene set.
    4. Asexual reproduction — binary fission, budding, vegetative propagation are mitotic.
    5. Regeneration — replacement of lost parts (lizard tail, planaria).
    6. Maintains the surface-area-to-volume ratio of tissues by renewing cells.

  • 5. Meiosis — Reductional Division

    5.1 Definition

    Meiosis (Farmer and Moore, 1905; Greek meion = to lessen) is a single DNA replication followed by two divisions, producing four haploid (n) cells from one diploid (2n) cell. It occurs only in germ (reproductive) cells — sporocytes in plants, gamete-forming cells in animals. It consists of meiosis I (reductional) and meiosis II (equational), with a brief interkinesis between them (no DNA replication in interkinesis — key point).

    After interkinesis (no DNA replication), each haploid cell divides like a mitosis: prophase II → metaphase II (chromosomes in single row) → anaphase II (centromeres split, sister chromatids separate) → telophase II. Result: four haploid cells.

    5.4 Significance of meiosis (exam list)

    1. Maintains the chromosome number constant across generations — 2n gamete-forming cells → n gametes; fertilisation restores 2n.
    2. Creates genetic variation by crossing over (pachytene) and independent assortment (metaphase I orientation) — the raw material of evolution.
    3. Forms gametes/spores for sexual reproduction.
    4. Repairs defects and aids adaptation through recombination.
    5. Aids speciation — variation on which natural selection acts.

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Define the cell cycle. Describe the events of interphase. Answer: The cell cycle is the sequence of changes a cell undergoes from its formation to its own division. Interphase (the metabolically active "resting" phase, ~23 h of a 24 h cycle) has three sub-phases: G₁ — growth, RNA/protein synthesis, organelle multiplication; S — DNA replication, producing two sister chromatids per chromosome; G₂ — spindle-protein synthesis and final preparation for mitosis.

    Q2 (5m) — Describe the stages of mitosis with diagrams. Answer: Draw the four stages. Prophase: condensation into two-chromatid chromosomes, nuclear envelope and nucleolus disappear, spindle forms. Metaphase: chromosomes align on the equatorial plate, kinetochore fibres from both poles attach. Anaphase: centromeres split, sister chromatids move to opposite poles. Telophase: envelopes and nucleoli reform, chromosomes decondense. End with cytokinesis: cell plate (plants) vs cleavage furrow (animals).

    Q3 (5m) — Describe prophase I of meiosis and state its significance. Answer: Five substages — leptotene (chromosomes visible), zygotene (synapsis of homologs, synaptonemal complex), pachytene (crossing over at recombination nodules, chiasmata appear), diplotene (complex dissolves, chiasmata visible), diakinesis (terminalisation, spindle forms). Significance: crossing over produces new gene combinations — the source of genetic variation.

    Q4 (3m) — Give four differences between mitosis and meiosis. Answer: Any four table rows — occurrence (somatic vs germ cells), divisions (one vs two), products (2 identical 2n vs 4 varied n cells), crossing over (absent vs present), metaphase arrangement (one row vs pairs in two rows).

    Q5 (3m) — Why is meiosis significant for a species? Answer: (1) It halves the chromosome number so fertilisation can restore it — chromosome constancy across generations; (2) crossing over and independent assortment create genetic variation; (3) variation gives natural selection material to act on, enabling adaptation and evolution.

    Q6 (2m) — Why is interphase not a "resting phase"? Answer: Because DNA replication (S phase), massive protein/RNA synthesis and growth (G₁, G₂) all occur then — the cell is metabolically at its busiest; only nuclear division is absent.

    Q7 (2m) — How does cytokinesis differ in plant and animal cells? Answer: Plants: a cell plate of Golgi vesicles forms at the equator and grows outward (centripetal) to become the middle lamella + wall. Animals: an actin–myosin contractile ring pinches the membrane inward as a cleavage furrow.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying DNA replicates "before meiosis II" — replication happens only before meiosis I; interkinesis has none.
    2. Splitting centromeres in anaphase I — homologs separate whole; centromeres split only in anaphase II (and mitosis).
    3. Drawing metaphase I chromosomes in a single row — they align as pairs in two rows.
    4. Confusing chiasmata (visible X-shaped contact points) with crossing over (the exchange event at pachytene).
    5. Writing "meiosis produces gametes" for plants without mentioning spores (meiosis makes spores in plants; gametes form by mitosis in the gametophyte).
    6. Calling amitosis the main division of prokaryotes without noting its imprecision (unequal chromatin distribution).
Confusion
  • Chromatid vs chromosome: after S phase, each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at one centromere — it is still ONE chromosome. The count changes only when centromeres split (anaphase).
  • Mitosis vs meiosis II: both split centromeres and separate sister chromatids, but meiosis II starts from haploid, recombined cells and follows meiosis I without new DNA replication.
  • Crossing over vs independent assortment: crossing over = physical exchange between non-sister chromatids (pachytene); independent assortment = random orientation of bivalents at metaphase I. Both create variation, by different mechanisms.
  • Interkinesis vs interphase: interkinesis between meiosis I and II has NO DNA replication; interphase of the cell cycle always includes S phase.
  • Amitosis vs binary fission: related but not identical — amitosis is the imprecise direct nuclear division seen in some eukaryotic cells; bacterial binary fission is precise DNA replication followed by septation.

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): Define cell cycle. — The sequence of events by which a cell grows, replicates its DNA and divides into two daughter cells; it comprises interphase (G₁, S, G₂) and the M phase with cytokinesis.
  • Short answer (2m): What is crossing over? — The exchange of corresponding segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during pachytene of prophase I, producing recombined chromosomes and genetic variation.
  • Short answer (2m): What is a bivalent? — The pair of homologous chromosomes (four chromatids, hence "tetrad") formed by synapsis during zygotene of prophase I.
  • Short answer (2m): What is the G₀ phase? — A quiescent state outside the active cycle into which cells withdraw after mitosis (e.g. mature neurons); they remain metabolically active but do not divide, though some can re-enter the cycle on stimulus.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the stages of mitosis with labelled diagrams and state its significance. — Prophase (condensation, spindle, envelope loss) → metaphase (equatorial plate, spindle attachment) → anaphase (centromere split, chromatids to poles) → telophase (re-formation of nuclei) → cytokinesis (cell plate vs furrow). Significance: growth, repair, genetic stability, asexual reproduction, regeneration.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe meiosis I with diagrams and state how it reduces the chromosome number. — Prophase I with five substages (synapsis, crossing over), metaphase I (bivalents in two rows), anaphase I (whole homologs separate — centromere intact), telophase I (haploid nuclei). Reduction happens because each pole receives one chromosome of every homologous pair, while each chromosome still has two chromatids.
  • Conceptual (3m): Why does meiosis create variation while mitosis does not? — Mitosis copies an existing genome exactly (no pairing, no exchange). Meiosis adds two sources of novelty: crossing over recombines maternal and paternal alleles within chromosomes (pachytene), and independent assortment randomises which homolog of each pair reaches which pole (metaphase I). Each gamete therefore carries a unique combination.
  • Conceptual (3m): "Cancer is a disease of the cell cycle." Explain. — Normal division is stopped at checkpoints by control genes (proto-oncogenes, tumour suppressors such as p53). When mutations disable these brakes, cells skip G₁/S and spindle checkpoints and divide without restraint, invading tissues — uncontrolled mitosis is the mechanism of tumour growth.
  • Application (2m): Why do antibiotics/chemotherapy drugs that block spindle formation stop tumour growth? — Drugs like colchicine and vinblastine prevent microtubule assembly, so the mitotic spindle cannot form; cells arrest in metaphase and cannot complete division. Rapidly dividing tumour cells are hit hardest.
Formulas
  • Mitosis: 2n → 2 daughter cells of 2n
  • Meiosis: 2n → 4 gametes of n
  • Crossing over (Prophase I) + independent assortment (Metaphase I) → variation
  • Chromosome count: after S each chromosome = 2 chromatids
Key Points
  • Cell cycle = interphase (G₁ growth, S DNA synthesis, G₂ preparation) + M phase (mitosis) + cytokinesis; checkpoints guard the transitions; G₀ = quiescent exit.
  • Interphase is the most active phase — DNA replication and growth happen there.
  • Amitosis: direct splitting, no spindle, imprecise (Remak, 1841).
  • Mitosis (Flemming, 1882): prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase; centromeres split in anaphase; 2 identical 2n daughters; growth, repair, asexual reproduction.
  • Cytokinesis: cell plate (plants, centripetal from Golgi vesicles) vs cleavage furrow (animals, actin–myosin).
  • Meiosis (Farmer & Moore, 1905): one replication + two divisions → 4 haploid varied cells; only in germ cells.
  • Prophase I: leptotene → zygotene (synapsis) → pachytene (crossing over, chiasmata) → diplotene → diakinesis.
  • Anaphase I separates whole homologs (centromere intact); anaphase II splits centromeres.
  • Meiosis significance: chromosome-number constancy + genetic variation + gamete/spore formation.
Summary

The cell cycle runs through interphase — G₁ growth, S DNA replication, G₂ preparation — into the M phase. Amitosis is a rare, imprecise direct splitting. Mitosis (prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase, then cytokinesis by cell plate in plants or cleavage furrow in animals) produces two genetically identical diploid cells for growth, repair and asexual reproduction. Meiosis — one replication followed by two divisions — produces four genetically unique haploid cells: prophase I (leptotene, zygotene synapsis, pachytene crossing over, diplotene, diakinesis), metaphase I with bivalents in two rows, anaphase I separating whole homologs, then the equational meiosis II. Meiosis keeps chromosome numbers constant across generations and generates the variation on which evolution acts; its failure (nondisjunction) and mitosis's failure (checkpoint loss → cancer) show why precise division matters.

    DiakinesisTerminalisation of chiasmata; chromosomes fully condensed; nucleolus and envelope disappear; spindle forms

    5.3 Meiosis II — the equational division

    After interkinesis (no DNA replication), each haploid cell divides like a mitosis: prophase II → metaphase II (chromosomes in single row) → anaphase II (centromeres split, sister chromatids separate) → telophase II. Result: four haploid cells.

    5.4 Significance of meiosis (exam list)

    1. Maintains the chromosome number constant across generations — 2n gamete-forming cells → n gametes; fertilisation restores 2n.
    2. Creates genetic variation by crossing over (pachytene) and independent assortment (metaphase I orientation) — the raw material of evolution.
    3. Forms gametes/spores for sexual reproduction.
    4. Repairs defects and aids adaptation through recombination.
    5. Aids speciation — variation on which natural selection acts.

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Define the cell cycle. Describe the events of interphase. Answer: The cell cycle is the sequence of changes a cell undergoes from its formation to its own division. Interphase (the metabolically active "resting" phase, ~23 h of a 24 h cycle) has three sub-phases: G₁ — growth, RNA/protein synthesis, organelle multiplication; S — DNA replication, producing two sister chromatids per chromosome; G₂ — spindle-protein synthesis and final preparation for mitosis.

    Q2 (5m) — Describe the stages of mitosis with diagrams. Answer: Draw the four stages. Prophase: condensation into two-chromatid chromosomes, nuclear envelope and nucleolus disappear, spindle forms. Metaphase: chromosomes align on the equatorial plate, kinetochore fibres from both poles attach. Anaphase: centromeres split, sister chromatids move to opposite poles. Telophase: envelopes and nucleoli reform, chromosomes decondense. End with cytokinesis: cell plate (plants) vs cleavage furrow (animals).

    Q3 (5m) — Describe prophase I of meiosis and state its significance. Answer: Five substages — leptotene (chromosomes visible), zygotene (synapsis of homologs, synaptonemal complex), pachytene (crossing over at recombination nodules, chiasmata appear), diplotene (complex dissolves, chiasmata visible), diakinesis (terminalisation, spindle forms). Significance: crossing over produces new gene combinations — the source of genetic variation.

    Q4 (3m) — Give four differences between mitosis and meiosis. Answer: Any four table rows — occurrence (somatic vs germ cells), divisions (one vs two), products (2 identical 2n vs 4 varied n cells), crossing over (absent vs present), metaphase arrangement (one row vs pairs in two rows).

    Q5 (3m) — Why is meiosis significant for a species? Answer: (1) It halves the chromosome number so fertilisation can restore it — chromosome constancy across generations; (2) crossing over and independent assortment create genetic variation; (3) variation gives natural selection material to act on, enabling adaptation and evolution.

    Q6 (2m) — Why is interphase not a "resting phase"? Answer: Because DNA replication (S phase), massive protein/RNA synthesis and growth (G₁, G₂) all occur then — the cell is metabolically at its busiest; only nuclear division is absent.

    Q7 (2m) — How does cytokinesis differ in plant and animal cells? Answer: Plants: a cell plate of Golgi vesicles forms at the equator and grows outward (centripetal) to become the middle lamella + wall. Animals: an actin–myosin contractile ring pinches the membrane inward as a cleavage furrow.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying DNA replicates "before meiosis II" — replication happens only before meiosis I; interkinesis has none.
    2. Splitting centromeres in anaphase I — homologs separate whole; centromeres split only in anaphase II (and mitosis).
    3. Drawing metaphase I chromosomes in a single row — they align as pairs in two rows.
    4. Confusing chiasmata (visible X-shaped contact points) with crossing over (the exchange event at pachytene).
    5. Writing "meiosis produces gametes" for plants without mentioning spores (meiosis makes spores in plants; gametes form by mitosis in the gametophyte).
    6. Calling amitosis the main division of prokaryotes without noting its imprecision (unequal chromatin distribution).

Confusion

  • Chromatid vs chromosome: after S phase, each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at one centromere — it is still ONE chromosome. The count changes only when centromeres split (anaphase).
  • Mitosis vs meiosis II: both split centromeres and separate sister chromatids, but meiosis II starts from haploid, recombined cells and follows meiosis I without new DNA replication.
  • Crossing over vs independent assortment: crossing over = physical exchange between non-sister chromatids (pachytene); independent assortment = random orientation of bivalents at metaphase I. Both create variation, by different mechanisms.
  • Interkinesis vs interphase: interkinesis between meiosis I and II has NO DNA replication; interphase of the cell cycle always includes S phase.
  • Amitosis vs binary fission: related but not identical — amitosis is the imprecise direct nuclear division seen in some eukaryotic cells; bacterial binary fission is precise DNA replication followed by septation.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Define cell cycle. — The sequence of events by which a cell grows, replicates its DNA and divides into two daughter cells; it comprises interphase (G₁, S, G₂) and the M phase with cytokinesis.
  • Short answer (2m): What is crossing over? — The exchange of corresponding segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during pachytene of prophase I, producing recombined chromosomes and genetic variation.
  • Short answer (2m): What is a bivalent? — The pair of homologous chromosomes (four chromatids, hence "tetrad") formed by synapsis during zygotene of prophase I.
  • Short answer (2m): What is the G₀ phase? — A quiescent state outside the active cycle into which cells withdraw after mitosis (e.g. mature neurons); they remain metabolically active but do not divide, though some can re-enter the cycle on stimulus.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the stages of mitosis with labelled diagrams and state its significance. — Prophase (condensation, spindle, envelope loss) → metaphase (equatorial plate, spindle attachment) → anaphase (centromere split, chromatids to poles) → telophase (re-formation of nuclei) → cytokinesis (cell plate vs furrow). Significance: growth, repair, genetic stability, asexual reproduction, regeneration.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe meiosis I with diagrams and state how it reduces the chromosome number. — Prophase I with five substages (synapsis, crossing over), metaphase I (bivalents in two rows), anaphase I (whole homologs separate — centromere intact), telophase I (haploid nuclei). Reduction happens because each pole receives one chromosome of every homologous pair, while each chromosome still has two chromatids.
  • Conceptual (3m): Why does meiosis create variation while mitosis does not? — Mitosis copies an existing genome exactly (no pairing, no exchange). Meiosis adds two sources of novelty: crossing over recombines maternal and paternal alleles within chromosomes (pachytene), and independent assortment randomises which homolog of each pair reaches which pole (metaphase I). Each gamete therefore carries a unique combination.
  • Conceptual (3m): "Cancer is a disease of the cell cycle." Explain. — Normal division is stopped at checkpoints by control genes (proto-oncogenes, tumour suppressors such as p53). When mutations disable these brakes, cells skip G₁/S and spindle checkpoints and divide without restraint, invading tissues — uncontrolled mitosis is the mechanism of tumour growth.
  • Application (2m): Why do antibiotics/chemotherapy drugs that block spindle formation stop tumour growth? — Drugs like colchicine and vinblastine prevent microtubule assembly, so the mitotic spindle cannot form; cells arrest in metaphase and cannot complete division. Rapidly dividing tumour cells are hit hardest.

Formulas

  • Mitosis: 2n → 2 daughter cells of 2n
  • Meiosis: 2n → 4 gametes of n
  • Crossing over (Prophase I) + independent assortment (Metaphase I) → variation
  • Chromosome count: after S each chromosome = 2 chromatids

Key Points

  • Cell cycle = interphase (G₁ growth, S DNA synthesis, G₂ preparation) + M phase (mitosis) + cytokinesis; checkpoints guard the transitions; G₀ = quiescent exit.
  • Interphase is the most active phase — DNA replication and growth happen there.
  • Amitosis: direct splitting, no spindle, imprecise (Remak, 1841).
  • Mitosis (Flemming, 1882): prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase; centromeres split in anaphase; 2 identical 2n daughters; growth, repair, asexual reproduction.
  • Cytokinesis: cell plate (plants, centripetal from Golgi vesicles) vs cleavage furrow (animals, actin–myosin).
  • Meiosis (Farmer & Moore, 1905): one replication + two divisions → 4 haploid varied cells; only in germ cells.
  • Prophase I: leptotene → zygotene (synapsis) → pachytene (crossing over, chiasmata) → diplotene → diakinesis.
  • Anaphase I separates whole homologs (centromere intact); anaphase II splits centromeres.
  • Meiosis significance: chromosome-number constancy + genetic variation + gamete/spore formation.

Summary

The cell cycle runs through interphase — G₁ growth, S DNA replication, G₂ preparation — into the M phase. Amitosis is a rare, imprecise direct splitting. Mitosis (prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase, then cytokinesis by cell plate in plants or cleavage furrow in animals) produces two genetically identical diploid cells for growth, repair and asexual reproduction. Meiosis — one replication followed by two divisions — produces four genetically unique haploid cells: prophase I (leptotene, zygotene synapsis, pachytene crossing over, diplotene, diakinesis), metaphase I with bivalents in two rows, anaphase I separating whole homologs, then the equational meiosis II. Meiosis keeps chromosome numbers constant across generations and generates the variation on which evolution acts; its failure (nondisjunction) and mitosis's failure (checkpoint loss → cancer) show why precise division matters.
