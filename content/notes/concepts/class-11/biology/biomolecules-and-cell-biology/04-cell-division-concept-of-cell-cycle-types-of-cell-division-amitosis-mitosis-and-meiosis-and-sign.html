    1. Introduction

    Every cell in your body — all ~3×10¹³ of them — descends from one fertilised egg by repeated division, and every grain of rice starts as a zygote that divided the same way. Cell division is how life grows, repairs and reproduces; the cell cycle is the ordered programme (grow → copy DNA → split) that keeps it faithful. Get it right and a cut heals, a seedling rises, a species continues. Get it wrong and the failures have names: cancer (checkpoints broken), Down syndrome (chromosomes mis-segregated).

  • 2. Hierarchy body — cycle first, then the three divisions

    Concept of cell cycle

    1. Two eras. Interphase (the working life: G1 growth, S DNA replication, G2 preparation — roughly 90% of the cycle) and M phase (mitosis + cytokinesis). A cell spends most of its life not dividing.
    2. Checkpoints guard the order. G1/S (is DNA fit to copy?), G2/M (is copying complete?), spindle assembly (are all chromosomes attached?). The p53 protein can halt or sentence a damaged cell — broken checkpoints are the road to cancer.
    3. Regulators. Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) drive the cycle forward; growth factors permit it. Onion root-tip cells cycle continuously; your neurons exited to G0 permanently.
    4. Chromosome arithmetic. Human somatic cell: 2n = 46. S phase doubles DNA (still 46 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids); division distributes them.

    Amitosis (direct division)

    1. Mechanism. Nucleus elongates and pinches in two, then cytoplasm splits — no spindle, no chromosome condensation visible.
    2. Where. Described in some protozoans, yeasts/some fungi, and certain pathological or regenerating cells (validity-kept: binucleate liver cells more often arise by cytokinesis failure, so liver is no longer cited as the classic example).
    3. Exam note. One line suffices: define + contrast with mitosis (no phases, no spindle).

    Mitosis (equational division)

    1. Prophase. Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes; nucleolus vanishes; spindle forms (centrioles in animals, spindle-pole bodies in plants).
    2. Metaphase. Chromosomes line the equatorial plate, centromeres on the line — the best stage to count chromosomes and see karyotypes.
    3. Anaphase. Sister chromatids separate (now daughter chromosomes) and move to opposite poles — the defining motion of mitosis.
    4. Telophase + cytokinesis. Nuclear envelopes reform; cytoplasm splits by furrow (animals) or cell plate (plants, built by Golgi vesicles).
    5. Result. One 2n cell → two genetically identical 2n cells. Growth, repair, asexual reproduction.

  • Meiosis (reductional division)

    1. Why two divisions. One DNA replication (S phase) followed by meiosis I (homologues separate: 2n → n, reductional) and meiosis II (sisters separate: equational, like mitosis). No S phase between the two — the single most-tested fact of the topic.
    2. Prophase I (the long one). Leptotene → zygotene (synapsis: homologues pair) → pachytene (crossing over between non-sister chromatids exchanges segments) → diplotene → diakinesis. Crossing over is variation's first engine.
    3. Metaphase I vs metaphase (mitosis). Homologous pairs line up (not single chromosomes); orientation of each pair is random — independent assortment, variation's second engine.
    4. Anaphase I / telophase I. Homologues separate (sisters stay together) → two haploid (n) cells, each chromosome still two-chromatid.
    5. Meiosis II. Runs like mitosis in both n cells: sisters separate → four haploid (n) cells, each chromosome one chromatid.
    6. Gamete maths (validity-kept). Spermatogenesis: 1 primary spermatocyte → 4 sperms. Oogenesis: 1 primary oocyte → 1 functional ovum (n) + usually 2–3 polar bodies — and meiosis II in the oocyte completes only if fertilisation occurs.
    7. Errors matter. Non-disjunction (homologues failing to separate) yields aneuploid gametes: fertilisation can give trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) — usually full trisomy (~95% of cases; translocation and mosaic forms also occur).

    Cytokinesis and significances

    1. Furrow vs cell plate. Animal cells pinch via an actin-myosin furrow; plant cells build a Golgi-vesicle cell plate from the centre out (the rigid wall cannot pinch).
    2. Mitosis significance. Growth, repair/healing, replacement (skin, gut lining, blood), asexual reproduction, and genetic stability (identical copies).
    3. Meiosis significance. Halves the number (2n → n) so fertilisation restores it; crossing over + assortment generate variation; the raw material of evolution and breeding.
    4. Amitosis significance. Quick, cheap division where fidelity matters less — microbial growth spurts, some regenerating tissues.

  • 5. Worked examples and applications

    1. Root-tip squash. Onion root tip + acetocarmine stain + warm + squash: prophase threads, metaphase plates, anaphase Vs — all of Table 2 in one slide, because the meristem cycles continuously.
    2. Counting the 90%. In 100 root-tip cells, ~90 sit in interphase. The wheel (Fig. 6.1) predicts the slide — phase duration reads as cell frequency.
    3. Karyotype reading. A metaphase spread with three copies of chromosome 21 = trisomy 21 — diagnosis read straight off a metaphase plate.
    4. Hybrid maize. Breeders cross inbred lines; meiosis shuffles parental genomes (assortment + crossing over), and the F1 out-yields both — variation monetised.
    5. Cutting propagation. A rose stem cutting roots because mitosis rebuilds meristems genetically identical to the parent — cloning by PMAT.
    6. Cancer in one line. A p53 mutation breaks the G1/S checkpoint: damaged DNA copies anyway — uncontrolled mitosis is the disease.
    7. Age and trisomy. Non-disjunction risk rises with maternal age because oocytes pause in prophase I from foetal life until ovulation — decades of waiting, one segregation error.
  • Deep dive — why meiosis matters more than mitosis. Mitosis copies; meiosis shuffles. Crossing over in Prophase I and independent assortment in Metaphase I mean no two gametes carry the same gene combination — a human can make over 8 million genetically different gametes before fertilisation multiplies the variety again. Without that reshuffling, selection would have nothing new to select; meiosis is the engine that keeps evolution running.

  • 6. Key terms and statements

    • Cell cycle — G1 → S → G2 → M: grow, replicate, prepare, divide; interphase ≈ 90%.
    • Checkpoint — G1/S, G2/M, spindle-assembly controls; p53 can halt a damaged cell.
    • Amitosis — direct nuclear + cytoplasmic pinching without spindle or visible phases.
    • Mitosis (PMAT) — prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase + cytokinesis; 2n → 2×2n identical.
    • Meiosis I / II — reductional (homologues split, 2n→n) / equational (sisters split); no S phase between.
    • Synapsis / crossing over — homologue pairing / non-sister segment exchange in prophase I.
    • Independent assortment — random orientation of homologue pairs at metaphase I.
    • Cytokinesis — furrow (animals) vs cell plate (plants).
    • Non-disjunction — homologue segregation failure → aneuploid gametes (e.g., trisomy 21).
    • Polar bodies — oogenesis discards 2–3 tiny cells per one ovum; meiosis II completes only on fertilisation.

    8. Summary and self-check

    The cycle runs G1–S–G2–M (≈90% interphase) under three checkpoints (p53-guarded). Amitosis pinches directly without a spindle. Mitosis walks PMAT and splits cytoplasm by furrow (animals) or cell plate (plants): 2n → two identical 2n — growth, repair, cloning. Meiosis runs one replication then two divisions with no S between: prophase-I crossing over and metaphase-I assortment create variation; meiosis I reduces (2n→n), meiosis II equates sisters; spermatogenesis yields 4 sperms, oogenesis 1 ovum + 2–3 polar bodies (II completes only on fertilisation). Non-disjunction gives trisomy 21 (usually full, ~95%). Figures: cycle wheel, PMAT/meiosis flows, three reference plates.

    Common confusions. Chromatids ≠ chromosomes after anaphase (each chromatid becomes a chromosome) · 2n→n happens in meiosis I, not II · crossing over exchanges non-sister segments of homologues, not sisters · "reductional" describes number, not size · amitosis is rare and specific — never cite liver cells as the example.

Confusion

  • Mitosis maintains the chromosome number (2n → 2n); meiosis halves it (2n → n).
  • Crossing over and independent assortment occur in MEIOSIS only, not mitosis.
  • In Anaphase I of meiosis, HOMOLOGOUS chromosomes separate; in Anaphase of mitosis and Anaphase II of meiosis, SISTER CHROMATIDs separate.
  • DNA replication happens ONLY in the S phase; meiosis has one S phase then two divisions.
  • G2 is not a "resting" phase; DNA has already doubled in S and the cell is preparing for M.
  • Non-disjunction = a homologous pair (or chromatid pair) fails to separate → one cell gets both, the other gets none.
  • Sister chromatids separate in Anaphase of mitosis and in Anaphase II of meiosis; homologous chromosomes separate in Anaphase I.
  • Meiosis produces 4 haploid cells; mitosis produces 2 diploid cells.
  • G2 is not a resting phase; DNA has already doubled in S, cells grow in G1.
  • Non-disjunction means a pair fails to separate; it can happen in meiosis I or II.

    Answer. Worked example: a human germ cell (2n = 46) enters meiosis.

    • After S: 46 chromosomes, 92 chromatids.
    • Anaphase I: 23 homologous pairs separate → two cells of 23 chromosomes (n), 46 chromatids.
    • Anaphase II: chromatids split → four gametes of 23 single-stranded chromosomes.

    Edge cases:

    1. Non-disjunction at Anaphase I or II produces gametes with 24 or 22 chromosomes — fertilisation then gives trisomy 21 (Down syndrome, ~95% full trisomy).

  • Explain the role of each checkpoint in preventing cancer.

    Answer. 1. G1/S checkpoint — the gatekeeper: checks cell size, nutrients, growth signals and DNA integrity before replication is allowed. Damage here triggers p53-mediated arrest, repair or apoptosis — a cell with broken DNA never copies it, so mutations are not propagated.

    1. G2/M checkpoint — verifies replication completed without error and the cell is large enough; blocks mitosis with unfinished or damaged copies.
    2. Spindle (M) checkpoint — every chromosome must be attached to spindle fibres from both poles before anaphase; prevents mis-segregation and the aneuploidy that destabilises genomes.

    Cancer link: tumours arise when these brakes fail — inactivated tumour suppressors (p53 mutated in most human cancers) or overactive proto-oncogenes let damaged cells keep cycling. Each bypassed checkpoint lets mutations accumulate: checkpoint integrity is cancer prevention at the cellular level.

  • Explain why meiosis is important for sexual reproduction.

    Answer. 1. It halves the chromosome number (2n → n), so fertilisation of two gametes restores the diploid number — without meiosis, every generation would double its chromosomes and the genome would explode.

    1. It creates variation — crossing over and independent assortment make every gamete genetically unique, and random fertilisation multiplies this; variation is the raw material on which natural selection and adaptation work.
    2. It maintains the species' chromosome number across generations while still refreshing gene combinations each generation.

    In short: meiosis is the mechanism that lets two parents combine without doubling the genome — the necessary arithmetic and the evolutionary engine of sexual reproduction.

  • Describe the cell cycle and the role of the G1/S checkpoints.

    Answer. The cycle: G1 (growth; proteins and organelles built) → S (DNA replication — each chromosome doubled into sister chromatids) → G2 (error repair, spindle preparation) → M (mitosis + cytokinesis) → back to G1 (or into G0, the resting state, for cells like neurons).

    Role of the G1/S checkpoint (restriction point): it is the commitment gate — the cell must pass size, nutrient, growth-signal and DNA-integrity checks before it may copy its genome. If DNA is damaged, the p53 pathway halts the cycle here, directs repair, or pushes the cell into apoptosis; if signals are absent, the cell rests in G0. Passing the gate commits the cell to division, so it is the single most important anti-cancer control point in the cycle.

Practice Questions

  • Q. State and explain the main idea of "Cell division: Concept of cell cycle, types of cell division (amitosis, mitosis and meiosis) and significances" with one example.

    Answer. Main idea: the cell cycle (G1 → S → G2 → M) is a controlled production line; division comes in three types that serve different jobs — amitosis (direct cleavage, no spindle — e.g. old or degenerating cells), mitosis (one division → two identical diploid cells for growth and repair) and meiosis (two divisions, one DNA replication → four different haploid cells for gametes and variation).

    Example: a human skin cell repairing a cut performs mitosis — 46 chromosomes copy in S phase, PMAT separates them, and two identical 46-chromosome cells result; a germ cell performs meiosis, halving to 23 and shuffling genes so no two sperm are alike.

  • Q. Compare "Cell division" with the related idea from a neighbouring unit.

    Answer. Cell division (this topic) is the mechanics — how the cycle's checkpoints copy and separate chromosomes, and how mitosis and meiosis differ in divisions, outcomes and sources of variation.

    The neighbouring unit's related idea is genetics/heredity (Mendel's laws): meiosis is the physical basis of those laws — homologous pairs separate at Anaphase I (segregation), and independent assortment at Metaphase I produces the 9:3:3:1 ratios Mendel measured.

    So cell division supplies the cellular machinery; genetics describes the statistical consequence in offspring. Crossing over in Prophase I is the bridge — a division event that becomes an inheritance pattern.

  • Q. How is Anaphase I of meiosis different from Anaphase of mitosis?

    Answer. Anaphase I (meiosis): homologous chromosomes separate — whole chromosomes (each still of two sister chromatids) move to opposite poles; the centromeres do not split; chromosome number halves (2n → n).

    Anaphase (mitosis): sister chromatids separate — centromeres split and each chromatid becomes an independent chromosome; the chromosome number stays 2n in both daughter cells.

    One separates partners, the other separates copies.

  • Q. Explain why meiosis produces genetic variation (two mechanisms).

    Answer. 1. Crossing over (Prophase I): non-sister chromatids of a bivalent exchange segments at chiasmata, recombining maternal and paternal alleles onto single chromosomes — new gene combinations never present in either parent.

    1. Independent assortment (Metaphase I): each homologous pair lines up at the equator independently of every other pair, so maternal and paternal chromosomes are dealt to the poles at random — with n pairs, 2ⁿ possible gamete chromosome combinations (2²³ ≈ 8.4 million in humans, before crossing over).

    (Random fertilisation of two such gametes adds a further layer on top.)

  • Q. What would happen if the S phase did not occur?

    Answer. No DNA replication — each chromosome would remain a single unreplicated chromatid entering M phase. Mitosis would then separate chromosomes that were never copied: daughter cells would receive incomplete genetic material (roughly half the required DNA per chromosome set) and most would die or malfunction. The cell cycle's checkpoints would normally block entry into M phase until replication is complete — S phase is what guarantees each daughter cell a full, identical genome.

  • Q. Describe the G1/S, G2/M, and M checkpoints and their role in preventing cancer.

    Answer. 1. G1/S checkpoint (restriction point): verifies cell size, nutrients, DNA integrity and growth signals before committing to DNA replication; damaged DNA here halts the cycle (p53 pathway) or triggers repair/apoptosis.

    1. G2/M checkpoint: confirms replication is complete and error-free and the cell is big enough before mitosis begins.
    2. M (spindle assembly) checkpoint: every kinetochore must be attached to spindle fibres from both poles before anaphase — preventing mis-segregation (aneuploidy).

    Role in cancer: cancer is uncontrolled division caused by checkpoint failure — loss-of-function mutations in tumour-suppressor genes (e.g. p53, the guardian that blocks G1/S on DNA damage) or gain-of-function in proto-oncogenes remove the brakes, so damaged cells keep cycling and accumulate further mutations. Intact checkpoints either repair or kill the cell; broken checkpoints breed tumours.

  • Q. How is the anaphase of meiosis I different from that of mitosis?

    Answer. Meiosis I anaphase: homologous chromosomes separate — whole chromosomes (each still of two sister chromatids) move to opposite poles; centromeres do not split; the chromosome number halves (2n → n).

    Mitosis anaphase: sister chromatids separate — centromeres split and each chromatid becomes an independent chromosome; the chromosome number stays 2n in both daughter cells.

  • Q. Explain why meiosis produces genetic variation.

    Answer. Two mechanisms:

    1. Crossing over (Prophase I): non-sister chromatids of paired homologues exchange segments at chiasmata — maternal and paternal alleles are recombined onto single chromosomes.
    2. Independent assortment (Metaphase I): each bivalent orients independently at the equator, so maternal and paternal chromosomes are distributed to the poles at random — 2ⁿ combinations (≈8.4 million in humans).

    Random fertilisation of two such varied gametes multiplies the possibilities again.

  • Q. Differentiate amitosis, mitosis and meiosis (3 points each).

    Answer. Amitosis:

    1. Direct cleavage of nucleus and cytoplasm, no spindle, no chromosome condensation;
    2. occurs in degenerating/old cells, some protozoa;
    3. unequal, imprecise distribution — least common.

    Mitosis:

    1. One division, chromosomes condense and a spindle forms;
    2. two daughter cells identical to the parent (2n → 2n);
    3. serves growth, repair and asexual reproduction.

    Meiosis:

    1. Two successive divisions after one S phase;
    2. four haploid, genetically different cells (2n → n);
    3. produces gametes and generates variation (crossing over + independent assortment).
  • Q. Draw the cell-cycle wheel and name one event per phase.

    Answer. G1 — growth; proteins and organelles made; cell commits at the G1/S restriction point. S — DNA replication: every chromosome copied into two sister chromatids. G2 — preparation for division; repair of replication errors; spindle proteins made (G2/M checkpoint). M — mitosis + cytokinesis: PMAT separates the copied chromosomes into two identical cells.

    (Then back to G1 — or to G0, the resting state, for cells like neurons.)

  • Q. Why is there no S phase between meiosis I and II?

    Answer. Because replication already happened once — before meiosis I. Each chromosome entering meiosis II is still the pair of sister chromatids created in the pre-meiotic S phase; meiosis II exists precisely to separate those chromatids (like a mitosis without prior copying). A second S phase would re-double the DNA and destroy the halving that meiosis achieved — gametes would end up diploid again.

  • Q. Describe mitosis with labelled diagrams and state its significance.

    Answer. Stages (PMAT):

    1. Prophase — chromosomes condense and become visible (each = 2 sister chromatids); nuclear envelope and nucleolus break down; spindle fibres form from the poles.
    2. Metaphase — chromosomes line up single file on the equator, each kinetochore attached to spindle fibres from opposite poles (metaphase plate).
    3. Anaphase — centromeres split; sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles as independent chromosomes.
    4. Telophase — nuclear envelopes re-form around each pole set; chromosomes decondense; nucleoli reappear.
    5. Cytokinesis — animal cells: cleavage furrow pinches inward; plant cells: a cell plate builds the new wall.

    Diagrams: one panel per stage, spindle poles, equator, separated chromatids labelled.

    Significance:

    1. Growth — a zygote becomes a multicellular body by mitotic multiplication.
    2. Repair and replacement — wounds heal, blood and skin cells are renewed.
    3. Asexual reproduction — clones in yeast, hydra budding, vegetative propagation.
    4. Genetic constancy — each daughter cell receives an identical chromosome set, preserving the body's information.
  • Q. Describe meiosis I and II and explain how meiosis creates variation.

    Answer. Meiosis I (reductional):

    1. Prophase I — homologues synapse into bivalents; crossing over exchanges segments at chiasmata (sub-stages: leptonema → zygonema → pachynema → diplonema → diakinesis).
    2. Metaphase I — bivalents line up as pairs on the equator, independent assortment deciding each orientation.
    3. Anaphase I — homologues separate to the poles (centromeres intact).
    4. Telophase I — two haploid cells, chromosomes still double-stranded.

    Meiosis II (equational — like mitosis):

    1. Prophase II/Metaphase II — chromosomes line up singly.
    2. Anaphase II — sister chromatids separate.
    3. Telophase II + cytokinesis — four haploid cells, each different.

    Sources of variation:

    • Crossing over recombines maternal and paternal alleles;
    • independent assortment deals whole chromosomes at random (2ⁿ combinations);
    • random fertilisation then combines two such gametes.

    Significance: halves the chromosome number so fertilisation restores diploidy, and manufactures the variation on which natural selection acts.

Notes
  • DETAILED CELL DIVISION: the full cell cycle and every mitotic/meiotic phase.
  • Interphase (90% of the cycle): G1 (growth, organelle duplication, protein synthesis), S (DNA replication — each chromosome now has 2 sister chromatids), G2 (preparation for mitosis, checkpoint).
  • Checkpoints: G1/S (DNA damage, nutrients), G2/M (replication complete), M (spindle attachment) — failures cause cancer.
  • Mitosis phases (PMAT): Prophase (chromatin → chromosomes, spindle forms, nuclear envelope breaks), Metaphase (chromosomes align at the equatorial plate, spindle fibres attached to centromeres), Anaphase (centromeres split, sister chromatids pulled to opposite poles), Telophase (chromosomes decondense, nuclear envelopes reform, cytokinesis).
  • Cytokinesis: animal cells — cleavage furrow (actin); plant cells — cell plate → new wall.
  • Meiosis: two divisions. Meiosis I (reductional): Prophase I (chromosomes pair = synapsis; crossing over between non-sister chromatids), Metaphase I (homologous pairs at the equator; independent assortment), Anaphase I (homologs separate), Telophase I. Meiosis II (equational): like mitosis but no DNA replication; sister chromatids separate.
  • Outcome: meiosis → 4 haploid (n) genetically distinct cells; mitosis → 2 diploid (2n) identical cells.
  • Crossing over (Prophase I) + independent assortment (Metaphase I) = the two sources of meiotic variation.
  • Amitosis (direct division): simple karyokinesis; rare — described in some protozoans, yeasts/some fungi and certain pathological or regenerating cells.
  • Errors: mitotic errors → uncontrolled division = cancer; meiotic non-disjunction → aneuploidy (e.g., Down syndrome = usually trisomy 21).
  • Significance: mitosis = growth, repair, asexual reproduction; meiosis = gamete formation + genetic diversity.
  • NEB focus: the full cell cycle, all mitotic and meiotic phases, variation, and errors.
Confusion
  • Mitosis maintains the chromosome number (2n → 2n); meiosis halves it (2n → n).
  • Crossing over and independent assortment occur in MEIOSIS only, not mitosis.
  • In Anaphase I of meiosis, HOMOLOGOUS chromosomes separate; in Anaphase of mitosis and Anaphase II of meiosis, SISTER CHROMATIDs separate.
  • DNA replication happens ONLY in the S phase; meiosis has one S phase then two divisions.
  • G2 is not a "resting" phase; DNA has already doubled in S and the cell is preparing for M.
  • Non-disjunction = a homologous pair (or chromatid pair) fails to separate → one cell gets both, the other gets none.

  • Interphase occupies ~90% of the cell cycle; mitosis is brief.
  • A human cell with 46 chromosomes yields two 46-chromosome cells after mitosis and four 23-chromosome cells after meiosis.
  • Crossing over can occur at multiple points per chromosome pair in Prophase I.
  • Cancer is fundamentally uncontrolled mitosis due to failed checkpoints (e.g., p53 mutations).
Examples

  • Spermatogenesis: 1 primary (2n) → 2 secondary (n) → 4 spermatids (n) → 4 sperm.
  • Oogenesis: 1 primary (2n) → 1 secondary (n) + 1 polar body → 1 functional ovum (n) + usually 2–3 polar bodies (meiosis II completes only if fertilization occurs).

  • Amitosis: direct division without a spindle; described in some protozoans and yeasts.

Summary

The cell cycle (G1, S, G2, M) is controlled by checkpoints. Mitosis (PMAT) produces two genetically identical diploid cells for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction; cytokinesis differs between animal (cleavage furrow) and plant (cell plate) cells. Meiosis is a two-division process that halves the chromosome number and, through crossing over and independent assortment, generates the genetic diversity needed for sexual reproduction. Amitosis is a rare direct division. Mitotic errors lead to cancer; meiotic non-disjunction causes aneuploidies such as Down syndrome. NEB focuses on the full cell cycle, all mitotic and meiotic phases, the sources of variation, and the consequences of errors.
