  • 1. Topic Introduction — What Are Biomolecules and Why Do They Matter?

    Biomolecules are the carbon-based chemical compounds that make up every living cell and carry out every process we call "life". A cell is not a bag of random chemicals — it is a highly organised factory built from a small, special set of molecules: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, minerals, enzymes and water.

    Why this topic is the foundation of all of Biology:

    • Structure: every cell wall, membrane, cytoskeleton and chromosome is built from biomolecules.
    • Energy: every beat of your heart, every nerve impulse and every cell division is powered by the chemical energy stored in carbohydrates and lipids.
    • Information: nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) store and translate the hereditary instructions that make a mango tree a mango tree and you, you.
    • Regulation: enzymes (proteins) control the speed of every chemical reaction; minerals and water create the environment in which those reactions can happen.
    • Connection to the syllabus: this topic directly feeds into cell structure (membranes are phospholipids + proteins), cell division (chromosomes are DNA + proteins), enzymes (all of metabolism), and later units like ecology (energy flow is chemical energy flowing through biomolecules).

    Where biomolecules come from: almost all biomolecules are ultimately built by photosynthesis, which converts CO₂ and H₂O into glucose. Animals cannot make all of their own biomolecules from scratch — they must eat plants or other animals. This is why biomolecules are also the chemical basis of food chains.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding — The Chemical Basis of Life

    2.1 The elements of life

    Over 99% of living matter is made of just six elements: C, H, O, N, P, S (remember: CHNOPS), plus minerals in smaller amounts.

    ElementRole in biomolecules
    Carbon (C)Backbone of all organic molecules — forms 4 stable covalent bonds
    Hydrogen (H)Part of every organic molecule; electron carrier in respiration
    Oxygen (O)Oxidation, water, carbohydrates, energy release
    Nitrogen (N)Amino acids, proteins, nucleic acid bases
    Phosphorus (P)Nucleic acids, ATP, phospholipids, bone
    Sulphur (S)Some amino acids (cysteine, methionine), protein shape

    Why carbon? Carbon has exactly 4 valence electrons, so it can form four strong covalent bonds, bond to itself in chains and rings, and build enormous, stable yet versatile molecules. Silicon, the closest alternative, forms weaker bonds and cannot build the diversity of molecules life needs. This is the deepest reason life is carbon-based.

    2.2 The monomer → polymer principle

    Almost every large biomolecule (macromolecule) is a polymer — a chain of repeated small units called monomers, joined by a specific covalent bond.

    Biomolecule classMonomer (building block)Polymer / large formBond joining monomers
    CarbohydratesMonosaccharides (glucose, fructose)Disaccharides, polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose)Glycosidic bond
    ProteinsAmino acids (20 standard types)Polypeptides → proteinsPeptide bond (−CO−NH−)
    LipidsGlycerol + fatty acidsTriglycerides, phospholipidsEster bond (−COO−)
    Nucleic acidsNucleotidesDNA, RNAPhosphodiester bond

    Key conceptual point: lipids (triglycerides) are often listed with the macromolecules but are not true polymers — fatty acids are not repeated identical monomers joined head-to-tail the way glucose or amino acids are. This is a favourite NEB trick question.

    2.3 How polymers are built and broken: condensation and hydrolysis

    • Condensation (dehydration synthesis): two monomers are joined and one molecule of water is removed. Building reactions — e.g. glucose + glucose → maltose + H₂O.
    • Hydrolysis: water is added to split the polymer back into monomers. Breaking reactions — digestion is entirely hydrolysis.

    Cause and effect: condensation requires energy (anabolic, enzyme-driven), hydrolysis releases it (catabolic). A polypeptide of n amino acids contains (n − 1) peptide bonds and loses (n − 1) water molecules during synthesis — a standard numerical asked in NEB exams.

  • 3. Carbohydrates — The Primary Energy and Structural Molecules

    3.1 Definition and general formula

    Carbohydrates are organic compounds of C, H and O in which hydrogen and oxygen occur in the same 2:1 ratio as in water. General formula: Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ — e.g. glucose C₆H₁₂O₆, sucrose C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. They are also called saccharides (Greek sakcharon = sugar). They are the first products of photosynthesis and the main immediate fuel of respiration.

    3.3 The glycosidic bond and how disaccharides form

    A glycosidic bond is the C–O–C covalent link formed between two monosaccharides by condensation, catalysed by enzymes. Glucose + glucose → maltose + H₂O; glucose + fructose → sucrose; glucose + galactose → lactose.

    3.6 Functions of carbohydrates

    1. Energy source: 4 kcal/g; glucose is the universal fuel of respiration.
    2. Energy storage: starch (plants), glycogen (animals).
    3. Structural material: cellulose in cell walls, chitin in fungi and arthropods.
    4. Precursors: carbon skeletons for amino acids, fatty acids, nucleotides.
    5. Transport: sucrose moves food through phloem.
    6. Cell recognition: oligosaccharides on cell surfaces (blood groups, immunity).
    7. Component of nucleic acids: ribose and deoxyribose.

  • 4. Proteins — The Working Molecules of the Cell

    4.1 Definition and building blocks

    Proteins are large polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. The word comes from Greek proteios = "of first importance" (coined by Berzelius). Proteins make up ~50% of a cell's dry weight and perform more different jobs than any other biomolecule class.

    Amino acid structure: every standard amino acid has a central (α-) carbon bonded to four groups:

    1. An amino group (−NH₂) — basic
    2. A carboxyl group (−COOH) — acidic
    3. A hydrogen atom
    4. A variable R-group (side chain) — this alone defines which amino acid it is

    Because of the amino and carboxyl groups, amino acids are amphoteric — they act as both acid and base, existing as zwitterions in solution. There are 20 standard amino acids; humans cannot synthesise about 9–10 of them (essential amino acids — must come from diet, e.g. lysine, tryptophan, methionine).

    4.2 The peptide bond — how proteins form

    The −COOH of one amino acid condenses with the −NH₂ of the next, releasing water and forming the peptide bond (−CO−NH−):

    H2​N–CH(R)–COOH+H2​N–CH(R′)–COOH→H2​N–CH(R)–CO–NH–CH(R′)–COOH+H2​O

    Two amino acids → dipeptide; many → polypeptide; one or more folded polypeptides → protein. A chain of n amino acids has (n − 1) peptide bonds.

    4.6 Denaturation

    Denaturation is the loss of a protein's natural 3-D structure (secondary → quaternary) by heat, pH extremes, or chemicals — peptide bonds are NOT broken, so the primary sequence survives, but the protein loses its biological activity (boiled egg; curdled milk). Renaturation is possible in some proteins if the denaturing agent is removed.

    Tests: Biuret test (violet colour — peptide bonds), Xanthoproteic test (yellow with HNO₃ — aromatic amino acids), Million's test (for tyrosine).

  • 5. Lipids — Concentrated Energy and Membrane Bricks

    5.1 Definition

    Lipids are a heterogeneous group of organic compounds that are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents (ether, chloroform, benzene). They contain C, H, O — but with far less oxygen than carbohydrates, which is why they yield more than twice the energy (9 kcal/g vs 4 kcal/g) on oxidation.

    5.4 Triglycerides — the storage fat

    One glycerol (a 3-carbon alcohol with 3 −OH groups) + 3 fatty acids → triglyceride + 3 H₂O, via three ester bonds (−COO−). Stored in adipose tissue; the body's most concentrated energy reserve.

    5.5 Phospholipids — why membranes form (conceptual core)

    A phospholipid = glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate group + nitrogen base. It is amphipathic: a hydrophilic (water-loving) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (water-fearing) fatty-acid tails. In water, heads face the water and tails hide from it — so phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer, the universal framework of every biological membrane. This directly explains why the fluid-mosaic membrane exists (connect to Topic 3).

    5.6 Functions of lipids

    1. Concentrated energy storage — 9 kcal/g; 1 g fat ≈ energy of ~2.25 g carbohydrate.
    2. Membrane structure — phospholipid bilayer.
    3. Insulation and padding — subcutaneous fat conserves heat and cushions organs.
    4. Hormones and vitamins — steroid hormones (oestrogen, testosterone, cortisol); fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K.
    5. Waterproofing — waxes on leaf cuticles, skin oils.
    6. Electrical insulation — myelin sheath (lipid) around neurons.
    7. Metabolic water — oxidised fat yields metabolic water (desert animals, camel hump — the hump stores fat, not water).

    Tests: Sudan III / IV stain (red), translucent-spot test on paper, saponification (soap formation with NaOH).

  • 6. Nucleic Acids — The Information Molecules

    6.1 Definition and the nucleotide

    Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides — the molecules that store, transmit and express hereditary information. First isolated by Friedrich Miescher (1868–69) from pus cells as "nuclein".

    Every nucleotide has three parts:

    1. A pentose sugar — ribose (RNA) or deoxyribose (DNA, missing one O on C-2′)
    2. A nitrogenous base — purines (Adenine, Guanine — double ring) or pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil — single ring)
    3. A phosphate group — gives the molecule its acidic character

    Nucleoside = sugar + base. Nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate. Nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one and the phosphate of the next, forming a sugar–phosphate backbone with bases sticking inward.

    ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is itself a nucleotide — proof that nucleotides are also the energy currency of the cell, not just genetic material.

    6.3 Watson–Crick double-helix model (1953) — salient features

    1. DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands (one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′).
    2. The sugar–phosphate backbones face outside; the bases pair inside the helix.
    3. Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds), G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds) — a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, keeping the helix a uniform 2 nm wide.
    4. The two strands are complementary — knowing one sequence fixes the other; this is the physical basis of accurate replication.
    5. One complete turn occurs every 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs per turn.
    6. Chargaff's rule: in any DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C (follows directly from pairing).

    6.5 Functions of nucleic acids

    1. Storage of genetic information (DNA).
    2. Transmission of heredity from parents to offspring and cell to daughter cell.
    3. Expression of information — DNA → RNA → protein (the central dogma).
    4. Mutation and evolution — changes in DNA sequence create variation.
    5. Energy transfer — ATP, NAD, FAD are nucleotide derivatives.

    Test: diphenylamine reagent turns blue with DNA (Dische test).

  • 8. Enzymes — Biological Catalysts

    8.1 Definition and nature

    Enzymes are biological catalysts — almost always globular proteins (a few RNAs, "ribozymes", also catalyse) — that accelerate biochemical reactions thousands to millions of times without being consumed themselves. The term was coined by Kühne (1878); Sumner (1926) crystallised the first enzyme, urease, and proved enzymes are protein.

    8.2 Properties of enzymes (exam favourite — "any four")

    1. Proteinaceous (except ribozymes); denatured by heat and extreme pH.
    2. Catalytic — huge turnover numbers; not consumed; needed in tiny amounts.
    3. Specific — usually one enzyme for one substrate or one reaction type.
    4. Work in mild conditions — aqueous, near-neutral pH, moderate temperature.
    5. Reversible — the same enzyme catalyses forward and reverse reactions.
    6. pH- and temperature-sensitive — each enzyme has an optimum (human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; pepsin ≈ pH 2, trypsin ≈ pH 8).
    7. Can be inhibited — by competitive and non-competitive inhibitors.
    8. Often require cofactors.

    8.3 How enzymes work — lowering the activation energy

    Every reaction needs an energy barrier to be crossed — the activation energy. Enzymes provide an alternative reaction path with a much lower activation energy: substrate binds in the active site → strained enzyme–substrate (ES) complex forms → product is released and the enzyme is free again. Enzymes never change the equilibrium or the energy released — they only change the speed.

  • 9. Water — The Medium of Life

    Water makes up 70–90% of most cells (a jellyfish ~95%; a human ~65%). Life almost certainly evolved in water, and every biological reaction happens in it.

  • Deep dive — the monomer-to-polymer principle. Every biomolecule follows the same construction logic: small monomers join by removing water (condensation) into polymers, and polymers split by adding water (hydrolysis). Glucose → glycogen/starch/cellulose; amino acids → proteins; nucleotides → DNA/RNA. Even triglycerides follow it (glycerol + fatty acids). Learn the four monomers and you can reconstruct the whole chapter — the exam rewards exactly this structural thinking.

  • 11. Conceptual Relationships — How This Topic Connects to Everything Else

    • Carbohydrates ↔ photosynthesis and respiration: glucose is made in photosynthesis and broken in respiration; the pentose sugar ribose reappears in RNA.
    • Proteins ↔ enzymes and genetics: enzymes are proteins; proteins are made according to the code in nucleic acids — so DNA ultimately controls every biomolecule (the central dogma).
    • Lipids ↔ cell membrane: the phospholipid bilayer is the structural theme of Topic 3 (eukaryotic cell structure); membranes only work because of amphipathic lipid behaviour.
    • Nucleic acids ↔ cell division: chromosomes = DNA + histone proteins; mitosis and meiosis (Topic 4) distribute DNA.
    • Minerals and water ↔ every physiological process: nerve impulses (Na⁺/K⁺), oxygen transport (Fe in haemoglobin), photosynthesis (Mg in chlorophyll), and the fact that all reactions need an aqueous medium.
    • Ecology connection: the 4 and 9 kcal/g energy values are exactly why energy pyramids shrink so fast — energy is lost at every trophic level as heat.
    • Health connection: balanced diet = correct proportions of all biomolecule classes; deficiencies (anaemia, goitre, kwashiorkor, rickets) are mineral/protein deficiencies.

    12.2 Model answers (exam-style, marks-proportioned)

    Q1 (3m) — Main classes of biomolecules with one function each. Answer: Biomolecules are the organic and inorganic compounds that make up living cells. (i) Carbohydrates — main energy source and structural material (cellulose). (ii) Proteins — growth, repair, catalysis (enzymes) and defence (antibodies). (iii) Lipids — concentrated energy storage (9 kcal/g) and membrane structure. (iv) Nucleic acids — storage and transmission of hereditary information (DNA, RNA). (v) Enzymes — protein catalysts that speed up metabolism. (vi) Minerals and water — regulate osmotic balance, act as cofactors and provide the reaction medium. (Write definition + 4–6 classes + one clear function each; ½–½ marks per class with function.)

    Q2 (3m) — Structure of a nucleotide and its role in DNA. Answer: A nucleotide has three components: (1) a pentose sugar — deoxyribose in DNA; (2) a nitrogenous base — purine (adenine/guanine) or pyrimidine (cytosine/thymine); (3) a phosphate group. Sugar + base = nucleoside; adding phosphate makes a nucleotide. In DNA, nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds into long polynucleotide chains; the sequence of bases stores genetic information, and complementary base pairing (A=T, G≡C) allows exact replication and transmission of heredity.

    Q3 (4m) — Salient features of the Watson–Crick model. Answer: (1) DNA consists of two polynucleotide chains wound into a right-handed double helix; (2) the chains run antiparallel (5′→3′ and 3′→5′); (3) sugar–phosphate backbones lie outside, bases inside; (4) bases pair specifically — A with T (2 H-bonds), G with C (3 H-bonds) — a purine with a pyrimidine; (5) the strands are complementary, permitting semiconservative replication; (6) one turn = 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs; helix diameter 2 nm. (Keywords: double helix, antiparallel, complementary, hydrogen bonds, 3.4 nm.)

    Q4 (3m) — Enzymes: chemical nature and biocatalysts. Answer: Enzymes are chemically proteins (globular proteins; a few are catalytic RNAs). They are called biocatalysts because they are produced by living cells and accelerate biochemical reactions enormously — without being consumed or permanently changed — by lowering the activation energy, under mild conditions of temperature and pH.

    Q5 (3m) — Temperature and pH effects + immobilised enzymes. Answer: Temperature: enzyme activity rises with temperature up to an optimum (~37 °C in humans) because molecules collide more often; above the optimum, heat denatures the protein's tertiary structure and activity collapses. pH: each enzyme has a narrow optimum (pepsin ~2, trypsin ~8); at extreme pH the ionic charges of the active site change, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured. Advantage of immobilised enzymes: they are fixed to an inert support so they can be reused many times, recovered easily from products, and used continuously in industrial bioreactors — cheaper and cleaner.

    Q6 (2m) — Classify carbohydrates with one example each. Answer: (1) Monosaccharides — single sugar unit — glucose; (2) Disaccharides — two units — sucrose; (3) Oligosaccharides — 3–10 units — raffinose; (4) Polysaccharides — many units — starch/cellulose.

    Q7 (3m) — Saturated vs unsaturated fatty acids; why bilayers form. Answer: Saturated fatty acids have only C–C single bonds, carry maximum hydrogen, are straight, pack tightly and are solid at room temperature (ghee, palmitic acid). Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more C=C double bonds, are bent (kinked), pack loosely and are liquid oils at room temperature (mustard oil, oleic acid). Phospholipid bilayers: phospholipids are amphipathic — hydrophilic phosphate heads and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails. In water the heads face outward toward water while the tails hide from it, so two layers arrange tail-to-tail, forming the stable bilayer that is the basis of all cell membranes.

    Q8 (2m) — DNA vs RNA. Answer: DNA has deoxyribose, is double-stranded, uses thymine, is very stable, and stores heredity; RNA has ribose, is usually single-stranded, uses uracil, is less stable, and helps in protein synthesis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA).

    12.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling lipids "true polymers" — they are not; fatty acids are not repeated monomers.
    2. Writing "RNA has thymine" — RNA uses uracil.
    3. Confusing nucleoside (sugar + base) with nucleotide (sugar + base + phosphate).
    4. Saying enzymes "provide energy" — they only lower activation energy; they never supply energy or change equilibrium.
    5. Forgetting that denaturation does not break peptide bonds.
    6. Reversing A=T (2 bonds) and G≡C (3 bonds) hydrogen-bond counts.
    7. Saying the camel's hump stores water — it stores fat (which yields metabolic water when oxidised).

Confusion

  • Lipid vs carbohydrate as "polymer": carbohydrates, proteins and nucleic acids are true polymers of repeated monomers; triglycerides are NOT — glycerol and fatty acids are different molecules, not repeated units. NEB loves this trap.
  • Nucleoside vs nucleotide: nucleoside = sugar + base only; nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate. Only the nucleotide is the true monomer of nucleic acids.
  • Denaturation vs digestion: denaturation (heat, pH) destroys 3-D shape but leaves peptide bonds intact; hydrolysis (digestion) actually breaks peptide bonds into amino acids.
  • Starch vs glycogen vs cellulose: all are glucose polymers, but starch (plants, α-1,4/1,6), glycogen (animals, more highly branched), cellulose (β-1,4, indigestible). Same monomer, different bonds = different biology.
  • Competitive vs non-competitive inhibition: competitive inhibitors bind the active site and can be overcome by more substrate; non-competitive inhibitors bind an allosteric site and cannot.
  • Enzyme vs hormone: enzymes are catalysts (not consumed, act locally on substrates); hormones are chemical messengers (consumed after action, transported by blood to distant targets).
  • DNA "contains protein": DNA is pure nucleic acid; chromosomes contain DNA + histone protein. DNA itself has no protein.
  • Saturated fat vs cholesterol: saturation describes fatty-acid bonds; cholesterol is a steroid (derived lipid), a separate thing — both raise heart-disease risk but are chemically different.

Practice

  • Why is cellulose indigestible to humans while starch is digestible, though both are glucose polymers?

    Answer. The difference is the glycosidic bond geometry:

    • Starch links glucose units with α-1,4 bonds; human amylase is shaped exactly to hydrolyse α-bonds, so starch is food.
    • Cellulose links them with β-1,4 bonds; every alternate glucose is rotated 180°, producing straight, H-bonded microfibrils that only cellulase can attack — and humans lack cellulase.

    So cellulose passes through us as fibre (which still helps the gut), while ruminants manage it only because gut microbes supply the cellulase. Same monomer, different bond geometry, opposite digestive fates.

  • A polypeptide has 200 amino acids — how many peptide bonds and how many water molecules were released during its synthesis?

    Answer. 199 peptide bonds and 199 water molecules.

    Each condensation between two amino acids forms one peptide bond and releases one H₂O; linking n amino acids needs n − 1 bonds. (The two terminal groups remain free — one −NH₂ at the N-terminus, one −COOH at the C-terminus.)

  • Why does sucrose give a negative Benedict's test, but a positive one after boiling with dilute HCl?

    Answer. Benedict's reagent detects free (reducing) aldehyde/ketone groups. In intact sucrose both reactive groups are locked inside the glycosidic bond — sucrose is a non-reducing sugar, so no Cu²⁺ is reduced and the test stays blue (negative).

    Boiling with dilute HCl hydrolyses the bond, splitting sucrose into glucose + fructose; both monosaccharides now carry free reducing groups, so on re-testing with Benedict's the mixture turns orange-red (positive). This is the classic two-step proof that a sugar is non-reducing but hydrolysable.

  • Why do phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer while triglycerides do not?

    Answer. Phospholipids are amphipathic: one end is a hydrophilic, charged phosphate head, the other two hydrophobic fatty-acid tails. Dropped in water, the tails flee water while the heads seek it — the only stable arrangement is two sheets, tails inward, heads outward: a bilayer, the basis of every membrane.

    Triglycerides are entirely hydrophobic — three fatty acids on glycerol, no polar head at all. Water neither attracts nor repels a particular face, so there is no force to line them into a sheet; they simply coalesce into fat droplets (oil in water). No head, no bilayer.

  • Predict enzyme activity when an enzyme with optimum 37 °C is kept at 70 °C for 10 minutes, and explain why cooling afterwards does not restore it.

    Answer. Prediction: activity is destroyed — at 70 °C the enzyme is rapidly and irreversibly denatured.

    Why cooling does not restore it: heat at 70 °C breaks the weak bonds (hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions) that hold the protein's tertiary structure, so the polypeptide unfolds and the active site loses its precise 3-D shape. This is a change of primary-structure-level order into a random coil — an irreversible physical change, not a reversible chemical one. Cooling lets the chain stay tangled but cannot re-fold it into the exact original conformation; the active site no longer fits the substrate. (Contrast: activity lost at a moderately high temperature for a short time may be partly reversible — denaturation beyond a threshold is not.)

Universal Facts

  • Water is 70–90% of a living cell; protein is ~50% of dry weight.
  • Energy values: carbohydrate 4 kcal/g, protein 4 kcal/g, fat 9 kcal/g.
  • 20 standard amino acids; ~9 are essential for humans.
  • DNA: 10 base pairs per turn, 3.4 nm per turn, 2 nm diameter.
  • Human enzymes: optimum ~37 °C; pepsin optimum pH ~2, trypsin ~8.
  • First crystallised enzyme: urease — Sumner, 1926 (Nobel 1946).
  • Term "enzyme" coined by Kühne (1878); "protein" by Berzelius; nucleic acid "nuclein" discovered by Miescher (1868–69).

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Define biomolecules. Give two examples.

    Answer. Biomolecules are the organic and inorganic compounds that constitute living cells and take part in every life process — built mainly from C, H, O, N, P and S.

    Examples: glucose (a carbohydrate — the universal fuel), haemoglobin (a conjugated protein — oxygen transport). Also: lipids, nucleic acids, enzymes, water and minerals.

  • Short answer (2m): What is a peptide bond?

    Answer. The covalent bond (−CO−NH−) formed between the carboxyl group (−COOH) of one amino acid and the amino group (−NH₂) of the next by condensation, releasing one water molecule. Chained peptide bonds link amino acids into polypeptides — the primary structure of proteins.

  • Short answer (2m): Why is water called the universal solvent?

    Answer. Because water molecules are polar (δ⁺ H, δ⁻ O) and form hydrogen bonds and hydration shells around ions and polar molecules, pulling them into solution — water dissolves more substances than any other liquid. That property makes blood, cytoplasm and plant sap possible: salts, sugars, amino acids and gases all travel dissolved in it.

  • Short answer (2m): What are coenzymes? Give one example.

    Answer. Small organic, non-protein cofactors — often vitamin derivatives — that some apoenzymes require for activity; they frequently act as transient carriers of atoms or electrons in the reaction.

    Examples: NAD⁺ (from niacin — hydrogen carrier), FAD, coenzyme A.

  • Long answer (5m): Describe the classification of carbohydrates with examples and state their functions.

    Answer. Definition: carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones with the general formula Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ — literally “hydrated carbon”.

    Classification:

    ClassUnitsExamplesSignificance
    Monosaccharides1 sugar unit; cannot be hydrolysedGlucose, fructose, riboseUniversal respiratory fuel; RNA/DNA sugars
    Disaccharides2 unitsSucrose (glucose+fructose), lactose (glucose+galactose), maltoseTransport sugar of plants (sucrose); milk sugar
    Oligosaccharides3–10 unitsRaffinose; blood-group antigensCell-recognition markers on membranes
    PolysaccharidesMany unitsStarch (plants), glycogen (animals), cellulose, chitinStorage or structural
    • Storage polysaccharides: starch and glycogen — α-glycosidic bonds, digestible.
    • Structural polysaccharides: cellulose (plant walls) and chitin (fungi, arthropods) — β-glycosidic bonds, indigestible to humans.

    Functions:

    1. Energy — ~4 kcal/g; glucose is the universal fuel.
    2. Storage — starch in plants, glycogen in liver and muscle.
    3. Structure — cellulose in cell walls, chitin in exoskeletons.
    4. Transport — sucrose carries sugar in phloem.
    5. Cell recognition — oligosaccharide chains of glycoproteins.
    6. Genetic chemistry — ribose and deoxyribose form the backbone of RNA and DNA.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure of DNA according to the Watson–Crick model.

    Answer. Watson–Crick model (1953):

    1. Double helix — two polynucleotide chains wound around a common axis, antiparallel (one 5'→3', the other 3'→5').
    2. Backbone outside: alternating deoxyribose sugar and phosphate groups joined by 3'–5' phosphodiester bonds form the rails.
    3. Bases inside: flat nitrogenous bases face the axis, one from each strand, paired by hydrogen bonds:
    • A = T (2 hydrogen bonds),
    • G ≡ C (3 hydrogen bonds).
    1. Purine–pyrimidine pairing (A, G are purines; T, C pyrimidines) keeps the helix a uniform 2 nm diameter.
    2. 10 base pairs per complete turn, 3.4 nm per turn, 0.34 nm between bases.
    3. Complementarity: each strand carries the information for the other — the basis of semiconservative replication and of Chargaff's rule (%A = %T, %G = %C).

    Labelled diagram: double helix with sugar–phosphate rails, base pairs as rungs, H-bond counts marked (2 for A–T, 3 for G–C).

  • Long answer (5m): What are enzymes? Explain the factors affecting enzyme activity.

    Answer. Enzymes are protein catalysts that accelerate reactions by lowering the activation energy. The substrate binds a specific active site (lock-and-key / induced fit) to form an enzyme–substrate complex, which converts to product; the enzyme is released unchanged.

    Factors affecting activity:

    1. Temperature — rate rises with temperature to an optimum (human enzymes ~37 °C); beyond it heat denatures the protein and activity collapses.
    2. pH — each enzyme has a narrow optimum pH (pepsin ~2, trypsin ~8); on either side ionic groups change charge and the active site distorts.
    3. Substrate concentration — rate rises with [S] until all active sites are occupied, then plateaus (saturation).
    4. Enzyme concentration — with excess substrate, rate is proportional to enzyme amount.
    5. Inhibitors — competitive (resemble the substrate, block the active site; effect reversed by more substrate) vs non-competitive (bind elsewhere, distort the enzyme; not reversed by substrate).

    Curves to draw: rate vs temperature (peak at optimum, sharp fall) and rate vs pH (bell curve).

  • Conceptual (3m): "Structure determines function." Justify this statement with two biomolecule examples.

    Answer. 1. Starch vs cellulose — both are glucose polymers, but the glycosidic bond geometry differs: starch uses α-bonds that human amylase can hydrolyse (digestible food), cellulose uses β-bonds that only cellulase can break (indigestible fibre, structural wall). Same monomer, different bond geometry, opposite functions.

    2. Haemoglobin — a single amino-acid substitution in the β-chain (glutamic acid → valine) reshapes the quaternary structure: in low oxygen the molecules crystallise, red cells sickle, and O₂-carrying efficiency collapses (sickle-cell anaemia). One changed residue out of 146 alters the whole function.

    In both cases the three-dimensional arrangement, not the chemical composition alone, decides what the molecule does.

  • Conceptual (3m): Why are enzymes called biocatalysts but not "sources of energy"?

    Answer. Because an enzyme only lowers the activation energy so the reaction reaches equilibrium faster — it is not consumed in the reaction and it supplies no energy. The energy released or absorbed comes from the substrate's own chemical bonds; the enzyme changes the rate, never the ΔG or the final equilibrium position. A catalyst is a shortcut, not a fuel.

  • Application (3m): A patient's blood test shows low haemoglobin and an enlarged thyroid. Which minerals are deficient, and why?

    Answer. Iron deficiency → low haemoglobin. Fe²⁺ is the central atom of the haem prosthetic group — the actual O₂-binding site of haemoglobin; without iron, haem cannot be built and anaemia follows.

    Iodine deficiency → enlarged thyroid (goitre). Iodine is required to synthesise thyroxine; when iodine is short, thyroxine falls, the pituitary releases more TSH, and the over-stimulated thyroid hypertrophies in a futile attempt to produce more hormone.

  • Application (2m): Why is sucrose, not glucose, the sugar transported in plant phloem?

    Answer. Sucrose is a non-reducing, chemically inert sugar: its glycosidic link ties up both reactive groups, so it neither reacts with nor is metabolised by the cells it passes through during its long journey from leaf to sink. Glucose, a reducing sugar, would be oxidised, consumed or converted en route by the transport tissues themselves — wasting the cargo. Sucrose is also highly soluble and moves easily in the sap; it is the parcel that survives the journey unopened.

Key Points

  • Biomolecules = carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids + minerals, enzymes, water; built from C, H, O, N, P, S.
  • Monomer → polymer by condensation (water out); polymer → monomer by hydrolysis (water in).
  • Carbohydrates: Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ; mono/di/oligo/poly; glucose = universal fuel; starch/glycogen store, cellulose/chitin structure.
  • α-bonds (starch) digestible; β-bonds (cellulose) not — humans lack cellulase.
  • Proteins: 20 amino acids, peptide bond −CO−NH−, 4 structural levels, structure determines function.
  • Conjugated protein = protein + prosthetic group (haemoglobin = haem + globin).
  • Lipids: water-insoluble, organic-solvent-soluble; 9 kcal/g; phospholipids are amphipathic → bilayer membranes.
  • Saturated fats: single bonds, solid, animal; unsaturated: C=C, liquid oils, plant.
  • Nucleotide = pentose + base + phosphate; DNA: deoxyribose + A,T,G,C, double helix; RNA: ribose + A,U,G,C, single strand.
  • A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds); Chargaff: %A=%T, %G=%C.
  • Enzymes: protein catalysts; lower activation energy; apoenzyme + cofactor = holoenzyme; six IUB classes.
  • Water: 70–90% of cell; polar, high specific heat, max density at 4 °C, universal solvent, reactant in hydrolysis/photosynthesis.

Summary

Biomolecules — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, plus minerals, enzymes and water — are the chemical basis of life. Monomers join into polymers by condensation (water out) and are split by hydrolysis (water in). Carbohydrates (Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ) supply quick energy and structure; proteins (amino acids + peptide bonds, four structural levels) do the cell's work; lipids (water-insoluble, 9 kcal/g) store concentrated energy and build the amphipathic phospholipid bilayer of all membranes; nucleic acids (nucleotides, A=T/G≡C pairing) store and express heredity. Minerals act as structural materials, cofactors and electrolytes, while water — polar, hydrogen-bonded, 70–90% of the cell — is the solvent, reactant and temperature buffer in which all life chemistry happens. Enzymes, protein catalysts that lower activation energy with high specificity, control the speed of every one of these reactions.

  • 1. Topic Introduction — What Are Biomolecules and Why Do They Matter?

    Biomolecules are the carbon-based chemical compounds that make up every living cell and carry out every process we call "life". A cell is not a bag of random chemicals — it is a highly organised factory built from a small, special set of molecules: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, minerals, enzymes and water.

    Why this topic is the foundation of all of Biology:

    • Structure: every cell wall, membrane, cytoskeleton and chromosome is built from biomolecules.
    • Energy: every beat of your heart, every nerve impulse and every cell division is powered by the chemical energy stored in carbohydrates and lipids.
    • Information: nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) store and translate the hereditary instructions that make a mango tree a mango tree and you, you.
    • Regulation: enzymes (proteins) control the speed of every chemical reaction; minerals and water create the environment in which those reactions can happen.
    • Connection to the syllabus: this topic directly feeds into cell structure (membranes are phospholipids + proteins), cell division (chromosomes are DNA + proteins), enzymes (all of metabolism), and later units like ecology (energy flow is chemical energy flowing through biomolecules).

    Where biomolecules come from: almost all biomolecules are ultimately built by photosynthesis, which converts CO₂ and H₂O into glucose. Animals cannot make all of their own biomolecules from scratch — they must eat plants or other animals. This is why biomolecules are also the chemical basis of food chains.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding — The Chemical Basis of Life

    2.1 The elements of life

    Over 99% of living matter is made of just six elements: C, H, O, N, P, S (remember: CHNOPS), plus minerals in smaller amounts.

    ElementRole in biomolecules
    Carbon (C)Backbone of all organic molecules — forms 4 stable covalent bonds
    Hydrogen (H)Part of every organic molecule; electron carrier in respiration
    Oxygen (O)Oxidation, water, carbohydrates, energy release
    Nitrogen (N)Amino acids, proteins, nucleic acid bases
    Phosphorus (P)Nucleic acids, ATP, phospholipids, bone

    Why carbon? Carbon has exactly 4 valence electrons, so it can form four strong covalent bonds, bond to itself in chains and rings, and build enormous, stable yet versatile molecules.   • 3. Carbohydrates — The Primary Energy and Structural Molecules

    3.1 Definition and general formula

  • 4. Proteins — The Working Molecules of the Cell

    4.1 Definition and building blocks

    Proteins are large polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. The word comes from Greek proteios = "of first importance" (coined by Berzelius). Proteins make up ~50% of a cell's dry weight and perform more different jobs than any other biomolecule class.

    Amino acid structure: every standard amino acid has a central (α-) carbon bonded to four groups:

    1. An amino group (−NH₂) — basic
    2. A carboxyl group (−COOH) — acidic
    3. A hydrogen atom
    4. A variable R-group (side chain) — this alone defines which amino acid it is

    Because of the amino and carboxyl groups, amino acids are amphoteric — they act as both acid and base, existing as zwitterions in solution. There are 20 standard amino acids; humans cannot synthesise about 9–10 of them (essential amino acids — must come from diet, e.g. lysine, tryptophan, methionine).

    4.2 The peptide bond — how proteins form

    The −COOH of one amino acid condenses with the −NH₂ of the next, releasing water and forming the peptide bond (−CO−NH−):

    H2​N–CH(R)–COOH+H2​N–CH(R′)–COOH→H2​N–CH(R)–CO–NH–CH(R′)–COOH+H2​O

    Two amino acids → dipeptide; many → polypeptide; one or more folded polypeptides → protein. A chain of n amino acids has (n − 1) peptide bonds.

  • 5. Lipids — Concentrated Energy and Membrane Bricks

    5.1 Definition

    Lipids are a heterogeneous group of organic compounds that are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents (ether, chloroform, benzene). They contain C, H, O — but with far less oxygen than carbohydrates, which is why they yield more than twice the energy (9 kcal/g vs 4 kcal/g) on oxidation.

    5.4 Triglycerides — the storage fat

    One glycerol (a 3-carbon alcohol with 3 −OH groups) + 3 fatty acids → triglyceride + 3 H₂O, via three ester bonds (−COO−). Stored in adipose tissue; the body's most concentrated energy reserve.

    5.5 Phospholipids — why membranes form (conceptual core)

    A phospholipid = glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate group + nitrogen base. It is amphipathic: a hydrophilic (water-loving) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (water-fearing) fatty-acid tails. In water, heads face the water and tails hide from it — so phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer, the universal framework of every biological membrane. This directly explains why the fluid-mosaic membrane exists (connect to Topic 3).

    5.6 Functions of lipids

    1. Concentrated energy storage — 9 kcal/g; 1 g fat ≈ energy of ~2.25 g carbohydrate.
    2. Membrane structure — phospholipid bilayer.
    3. Insulation and padding — subcutaneous fat conserves heat and cushions organs.
    4. Hormones and vitamins — steroid hormones (oestrogen, testosterone, cortisol); fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K.
    5. Waterproofing — waxes on leaf cuticles, skin oils.
    6. Electrical insulation — myelin sheath (lipid) around neurons.
    7. Metabolic water — oxidised fat yields metabolic water (desert animals, camel hump — the hump stores fat, not water).

    Tests: Sudan III / IV stain (red), translucent-spot test on paper, saponification (soap formation with NaOH).

  • 6. Nucleic Acids — The Information Molecules

    6.1 Definition and the nucleotide

    Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides — the molecules that store, transmit and express hereditary information. First isolated by Friedrich Miescher (1868–69) from pus cells as "nuclein".

    Every nucleotide has three parts:

    1. A pentose sugar — ribose (RNA) or deoxyribose (DNA, missing one O on C-2′)
    2. A nitrogenous base — purines (Adenine, Guanine — double ring) or pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil — single ring)
    3. A phosphate group — gives the molecule its acidic character

    Nucleoside = sugar + base. Nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate. Nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one and the phosphate of the next, forming a sugar–phosphate backbone with bases sticking inward.

    ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is itself a nucleotide — proof that nucleotides are also the energy currency of the cell, not just genetic material.

    6.3 Watson–Crick double-helix model (1953) — salient features

    1. DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands (one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′).
    2. The sugar–phosphate backbones face outside; the bases pair inside the helix.
    3. Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds), G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds) — a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, keeping the helix a uniform 2 nm wide.
    4. The two strands are complementary — knowing one sequence fixes the other; this is the physical basis of accurate replication.
    5. One complete turn occurs every 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs per turn.
    6. Chargaff's rule: in any DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C (follows directly from pairing).

  • 8. Enzymes — Biological Catalysts

    8.1 Definition and nature

    Enzymes are biological catalysts — almost always globular proteins (a few RNAs, "ribozymes", also catalyse) — that accelerate biochemical reactions thousands to millions of times without being consumed themselves. The term was coined by Kühne (1878); Sumner (1926) crystallised the first enzyme, urease, and proved enzymes are protein.

    8.2 Properties of enzymes (exam favourite — "any four")

    1. Proteinaceous (except ribozymes); denatured by heat and extreme pH.
    2. Catalytic — huge turnover numbers; not consumed; needed in tiny amounts.
    3. Specific — usually one enzyme for one substrate or one reaction type.
    4. Work in mild conditions — aqueous, near-neutral pH, moderate temperature.
    5. Reversible — the same enzyme catalyses forward and reverse reactions.
    6. pH- and temperature-sensitive — each enzyme has an optimum (human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; pepsin ≈ pH 2, trypsin ≈ pH 8).
    7. Can be inhibited — by competitive and non-competitive inhibitors.
    8. Often require cofactors.

    8.3 How enzymes work — lowering the activation energy

    Every reaction needs an energy barrier to be crossed — the activation energy. Enzymes provide an alternative reaction path with a much lower activation energy: substrate binds in the active site → strained enzyme–substrate (ES) complex forms → product is released and the enzyme is free again. Enzymes never change the equilibrium or the energy released — they only change the speed.

    12.2 Model answers (exam-style, marks-proportioned)

    Q1 (3m) — Main classes of biomolecules with one function each. Answer: Biomolecules are the organic and inorganic compounds that make up living cells. (i) Carbohydrates — main energy source and structural material (cellulose). (ii) Proteins — growth, repair, catalysis (enzymes) and defence (antibodies). (iii) Lipids — concentrated energy storage (9 kcal/g) and membrane structure. (iv) Nucleic acids — storage and transmission of hereditary information (DNA, RNA). (v) Enzymes — protein catalysts that speed up metabolism. (vi) Minerals and water — regulate osmotic balance, act as cofactors and provide the reaction medium. (Write definition + 4–6 classes + one clear function each; ½–½ marks per class with function.)

    Q2 (3m) — Structure of a nucleotide and its role in DNA. Answer: A nucleotide has three components: (1) a pentose sugar — deoxyribose in DNA; (2) a nitrogenous base — purine (adenine/guanine) or pyrimidine (cytosine/thymine); (3) a phosphate group. Sugar + base = nucleoside; adding phosphate makes a nucleotide. In DNA, nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds into long polynucleotide chains; the sequence of bases stores genetic information, and complementary base pairing (A=T, G≡C) allows exact replication and transmission of heredity.

    Q3 (4m) — Salient features of the Watson–Crick model. Answer: (1) DNA consists of two polynucleotide chains wound into a right-handed double helix; (2) the chains run antiparallel (5′→3′ and 3′→5′); (3) sugar–phosphate backbones lie outside, bases inside; (4) bases pair specifically — A with T (2 H-bonds), G with C (3 H-bonds) — a purine with a pyrimidine; (5) the strands are complementary, permitting semiconservative replication; (6) one turn = 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs; helix diameter 2 nm. (Keywords: double helix, antiparallel, complementary, hydrogen bonds, 3.4 nm.)

    Q4 (3m) — Enzymes: chemical nature and biocatalysts. Answer: Enzymes are chemically proteins (globular proteins; a few are catalytic RNAs). They are called biocatalysts because they are produced by living cells and accelerate biochemical reactions enormously — without being consumed or permanently changed — by lowering the activation energy, under mild conditions of temperature and pH.

    Q5 (3m) — Temperature and pH effects + immobilised enzymes. Answer: Temperature: enzyme activity rises with temperature up to an optimum (~37 °C in humans) because molecules collide more often; above the optimum, heat denatures the protein's tertiary structure and activity collapses. pH: each enzyme has a narrow optimum (pepsin ~2, trypsin ~8); at extreme pH the ionic charges of the active site change, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured. Advantage of immobilised enzymes: they are fixed to an inert support so they can be reused many times, recovered easily from products, and used continuously in industrial bioreactors — cheaper and cleaner.

    Q6 (2m) — Classify carbohydrates with one example each. Answer: (1) Monosaccharides — single sugar unit — glucose; (2) Disaccharides — two units — sucrose; (3) Oligosaccharides — 3–10 units — raffinose; (4) Polysaccharides — many units — starch/cellulose.

    Q7 (3m) — Saturated vs unsaturated fatty acids; why bilayers form. Answer: Saturated fatty acids have only C–C single bonds, carry maximum hydrogen, are straight, pack tightly and are solid at room temperature (ghee, palmitic acid). Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more C=C double bonds, are bent (kinked), pack loosely and are liquid oils at room temperature (mustard oil, oleic acid). Phospholipid bilayers: phospholipids are amphipathic — hydrophilic phosphate heads and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails. In water the heads face outward toward water while the tails hide from it, so two layers arrange tail-to-tail, forming the stable bilayer that is the basis of all cell membranes.

    Q8 (2m) — DNA vs RNA. Answer: DNA has deoxyribose, is double-stranded, uses thymine, is very stable, and stores heredity; RNA has ribose, is usually single-stranded, uses uracil, is less stable, and helps in protein synthesis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA).

    12.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling lipids "true polymers" — they are not; fatty acids are not repeated monomers.
    2. Writing "RNA has thymine" — RNA uses uracil.
    3. Confusing nucleoside (sugar + base) with nucleotide (sugar + base + phosphate).
    4. Saying enzymes "provide energy" — they only lower activation energy; they never supply energy or change equilibrium.
    5. Forgetting that denaturation does not break peptide bonds.
    6. Reversing A=T (2 bonds) and G≡C (3 bonds) hydrogen-bond counts.
    7. Saying the camel's hump stores water — it stores fat (which yields metabolic water when oxidised).

  • Extra depth — why these four classes matter together

    The four classes interlock: nucleic acids (DNA) store the recipe, proteins (enzymes) execute it, carbohydrates supply the instant fuel the enzymes burn, and lipids warehouse the long-term reserve. A deficiency in any one breaks the chain — no vitamin-derived cofactor (minerals) means an enzyme cannot bind its substrate; no membrane lipids means the cell has no compartment to keep reactions ordered. Water deserves special mention: it is the medium in which all four dissolve, react and transport — 70–90% of a cell's mass. Minerals (Na, K, Ca, Fe, Mg) are needed in milligrams yet without them haemoglobin, chlorophyll, nerve impulses and bone simply do not exist.

  • Extra depth — tests that identify each class

    Carbohydrates: Benedict's test (reducing sugars give brick-red Cu₂O on heating), iodine test (starch → blue-black). Proteins: Biuret test (violet with dilute CuSO₄ + NaOH), Xanthoproteic (yellow with conc. HNO₃). Lipids: Sudan III stains red; the translucent-spot test on paper. Nucleic acids: diphenylamine (DNA → blue), orcinol (RNA → green). These colour tests are standard 2–3 mark NEB questions — memorise reagent and colour together.

Confusion
  • Lipid vs carbohydrate as "polymer": carbohydrates, proteins and nucleic acids are true polymers of repeated monomers; triglycerides are NOT — glycerol and fatty acids are different molecules, not repeated units. NEB loves this trap.
  • Nucleoside vs nucleotide: nucleoside = sugar + base only; nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate. Only the nucleotide is the true monomer of nucleic acids.
  • Denaturation vs digestion: denaturation (heat, pH) destroys 3-D shape but leaves peptide bonds intact; hydrolysis (digestion) actually breaks peptide bonds into amino acids.
  • Starch vs glycogen vs cellulose: all are glucose polymers, but starch (plants, α-1,4/1,6), glycogen (animals, more highly branched), cellulose (β-1,4, indigestible). Same monomer, different bonds = different biology.
  • Competitive vs non-competitive inhibition: competitive inhibitors bind the active site and can be overcome by more substrate; non-competitive inhibitors bind an allosteric site and cannot.
  • Enzyme vs hormone: enzymes are catalysts (not consumed, act locally on substrates); hormones are chemical messengers (consumed after action, transported by blood to distant targets).
  • DNA "contains protein": DNA is pure nucleic acid; chromosomes contain DNA + histone protein. DNA itself has no protein.
  • Saturated fat vs cholesterol: saturation describes fatty-acid bonds; cholesterol is a steroid (derived lipid), a separate thing — both raise heart-disease risk but are chemically different.

  • Conceptual (3m): "Structure determines function." Justify this statement with two biomolecule examples. — (1) Starch vs cellulose: identical glucose monomers, but α vs β glycosidic bonds make one digestible and the other structural fibre. (2) Haemoglobin: one amino-acid substitution in the β-chain (sickle-cell anaemia) changes the quaternary shape and destroys O₂-carrying efficiency.   • Conceptual (3m): Why are enzymes called biocatalysts but not "sources of energy"? — Enzymes only lower the activation energy so reactions reach equilibrium faster; they are not consumed and they supply no energy themselves — the energy comes from the substrate's chemical bonds.   • Application (2m): Why is sucrose, not glucose, the sugar transported in plant phloem? — Sucrose is non-reducing and chemically inert, so it neither reacts with, nor is consumed by, the cells it passes through during long-distance transport; glucose would be metabolised or react en route.

Summary

Biomolecules — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, plus minerals, enzymes and water — are the chemical basis of life. Monomers join into polymers by condensation (water out) and are split by hydrolysis (water in). Carbohydrates (Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ) supply quick energy and structure; proteins (amino acids + peptide bonds, four structural levels) do the cell's work; lipids (water-insoluble, 9 kcal/g) store concentrated energy and build the amphipathic phospholipid bilayer of all membranes; nucleic acids (nucleotides, A=T/G≡C pairing) store and express heredity. Minerals act as structural materials, cofactors and electrolytes, while water — polar, hydrogen-bonded, 70–90% of the cell — is the solvent, reactant and temperature buffer in which all life chemistry happens. Enzymes, protein catalysts that lower activation energy with high specificity, control the speed of every one of these reactions.

  • 1. Topic Introduction — What Are Biomolecules and Why Do They Matter?

    Biomolecules are the carbon-based chemical compounds that make up every living cell and carry out every process we call "life". A cell is not a bag of random chemicals — it is a highly organised factory built from a small, special set of molecules: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, minerals, enzymes and water.

    Why this topic is the foundation of all of Biology:

    • Structure: every cell wall, membrane, cytoskeleton and chromosome is built from biomolecules.
    • Energy: every beat of your heart, every nerve impulse and every cell division is powered by the chemical energy stored in carbohydrates and lipids.
    • Information: nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) store and translate the hereditary instructions that make a mango tree a mango tree and you, you.
    • Regulation: enzymes (proteins) control the speed of every chemical reaction; minerals and water create the environment in which those reactions can happen.
    • Connection to the syllabus: this topic directly feeds into cell structure (membranes are phospholipids + proteins), cell division (chromosomes are DNA + proteins), enzymes (all of metabolism), and later units like ecology (energy flow is chemical energy flowing through biomolecules).

    Where biomolecules come from: almost all biomolecules are ultimately built by photosynthesis, which converts CO₂ and H₂O into glucose. Animals cannot make all of their own biomolecules from scratch — they must eat plants or other animals. This is why biomolecules are also the chemical basis of food chains.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding — The Chemical Basis of Life

    Over 99% of living matter is made of just six elements: C, H, O, N, P, S (remember: CHNOPS), plus minerals in smaller amounts.

    2.3 How polymers are built and broken: condensation and hydrolysis

    • Condensation (dehydration synthesis): two monomers are joined and one molecule of water is removed. Building reactions — e.g. glucose + glucose → maltose + H₂O.
    • Hydrolysis: water is added to split the polymer back into monomers. Breaking reactions — digestion is entirely hydrolysis.

  • 3. Carbohydrates — The Primary Energy and Structural Molecules

    3.1 Definition and general formula

    Carbohydrates are organic compounds of C, H and O in which hydrogen and oxygen occur in the same 2:1 ratio as in water. General formula: Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ — e.g. glucose C₆H₁₂O₆, sucrose C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. They are also called saccharides (Greek sakcharon = sugar). They are the first products of photosynthesis and the main immediate fuel of respiration.

  • 4. Proteins — The Working Molecules of the Cell

    4.1 Definition and building blocks

    Proteins are large polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. The word comes from Greek proteios = "of first importance" (coined by Berzelius). Proteins make up ~50% of a cell's dry weight and perform more different jobs than any other biomolecule class.

    Amino acid structure: every standard amino acid has a central (α-) carbon bonded to four groups:

    1. An amino group (−NH₂) — basic
    2. A carboxyl group (−COOH) — acidic
    3. A hydrogen atom
    4. A variable R-group (side chain) — this alone defines which amino acid it is

    Because of the amino and carboxyl groups, amino acids are amphoteric — they act as both acid and base, existing as zwitterions in solution. There are 20 standard amino acids; humans cannot synthesise about 9–10 of them (essential amino acids — must come from diet, e.g. lysine, tryptophan, methionine).

    4.2 The peptide bond — how proteins form

    The −COOH of one amino acid condenses with the −NH₂ of the next, releasing water and forming the peptide bond (−CO−NH−):

    H2​N–CH(R)–COOH+H2​N–CH(R′)–COOH→H2​N–CH(R)–CO–NH–CH(R′)–COOH+H2​O

    Two amino acids → dipeptide; many → polypeptide; one or more folded polypeptides → protein. A chain of n amino acids has (n − 1) peptide bonds.

    Key concept: structure determines function. Change the sequence (primary structure) — as in sickle-cell anaemia, where one wrong amino acid in haemoglobin's β-chain changes the whole shape — and the protein's function collapses.

    4.6 Denaturation

    Denaturation is the loss of a protein's natural 3-D structure (secondary → quaternary) by heat, pH extremes, or chemicals — peptide bonds are NOT broken, so the primary sequence survives, but the protein loses its biological activity (boiled egg; curdled milk). Renaturation is possible in some proteins if the denaturing agent is removed.

  • 5. Lipids — Concentrated Energy and Membrane Bricks

    5.1 Definition

    Lipids are a heterogeneous group of organic compounds that are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents (ether, chloroform, benzene). They contain C, H, O — but with far less oxygen than carbohydrates, which is why they yield more than twice the energy (9 kcal/g vs 4 kcal/g) on oxidation.

    5.4 Triglycerides — the storage fat

    One glycerol (a 3-carbon alcohol with 3 −OH groups) + 3 fatty acids → triglyceride + 3 H₂O, via three ester bonds (−COO−). Stored in adipose tissue; the body's most concentrated energy reserve.

    5.5 Phospholipids — why membranes form (conceptual core)

    A phospholipid = glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate group + nitrogen base. It is amphipathic: a hydrophilic (water-loving) phosphate head and two hydrophobic (water-fearing) fatty-acid tails. In water, heads face the water and tails hide from it — so phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer, the universal framework of every biological membrane. This directly explains why the fluid-mosaic membrane exists (connect to Topic 3).

    5.6 Functions of lipids

    1. Concentrated energy storage — 9 kcal/g; 1 g fat ≈ energy of ~2.25 g carbohydrate.
    2. Membrane structure — phospholipid bilayer.
    3. Insulation and padding — subcutaneous fat conserves heat and cushions organs.
    4. Hormones and vitamins — steroid hormones (oestrogen, testosterone, cortisol); fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K.
    5. Waterproofing — waxes on leaf cuticles, skin oils.
    6. Electrical insulation — myelin sheath (lipid) around neurons.
    7. Metabolic water — oxidised fat yields metabolic water (desert animals, camel hump — the hump stores fat, not water).

    Tests: Sudan III / IV stain (red), translucent-spot test on paper, saponification (soap formation with NaOH).

  • 6. Nucleic Acids — The Information Molecules

    6.1 Definition and the nucleotide

    Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides — the molecules that store, transmit and express hereditary information. First isolated by Friedrich Miescher (1868–69) from pus cells as "nuclein".

    Every nucleotide has three parts:

    1. A pentose sugar — ribose (RNA) or deoxyribose (DNA, missing one O on C-2′)
    2. A nitrogenous base — purines (Adenine, Guanine — double ring) or pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil — single ring)
    3. A phosphate group — gives the molecule its acidic character

    Nucleoside = sugar + base. Nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate. Nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one and the phosphate of the next, forming a sugar–phosphate backbone with bases sticking inward.

    ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is itself a nucleotide — proof that nucleotides are also the energy currency of the cell, not just genetic material.

    6.2 DNA vs RNA — the master comparison (asked almost every year)

    6.3 Watson–Crick double-helix model (1953) — salient features

    1. DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands (one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′).
    2. The sugar–phosphate backbones face outside; the bases pair inside the helix.
    3. Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds), G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds) — a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, keeping the helix a uniform 2 nm wide.
    4. The two strands are complementary — knowing one sequence fixes the other; this is the physical basis of accurate replication.
    5. One complete turn occurs every 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs per turn.
    6. Chargaff's rule: in any DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C (follows directly from pairing).

    6.5 Functions of nucleic acids

    1. Storage of genetic information (DNA).
    2. Transmission of heredity from parents to offspring and cell to daughter cell.
    3. Expression of information — DNA → RNA → protein (the central dogma).
    4. Mutation and evolution — changes in DNA sequence create variation.
    5. Energy transfer — ATP, NAD, FAD are nucleotide derivatives.

  • 8. Enzymes — Biological Catalysts

    8.1 Definition and nature

    Enzymes are biological catalysts — almost always globular proteins (a few RNAs, "ribozymes", also catalyse) — that accelerate biochemical reactions thousands to millions of times without being consumed themselves. The term was coined by Kühne (1878); Sumner (1926) crystallised the first enzyme, urease, and proved enzymes are protein.

    Terminology chain: some enzymes need a non-protein helper to work:

    8.2 Properties of enzymes (exam favourite — "any four")

    1. Proteinaceous (except ribozymes); denatured by heat and extreme pH.
    2. Catalytic — huge turnover numbers; not consumed; needed in tiny amounts.
    3. Specific — usually one enzyme for one substrate or one reaction type.
    4. Work in mild conditions — aqueous, near-neutral pH, moderate temperature.
    5. Reversible — the same enzyme catalyses forward and reverse reactions.
    6. pH- and temperature-sensitive — each enzyme has an optimum (human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; pepsin ≈ pH 2, trypsin ≈ pH 8).
    7. Can be inhibited — by competitive and non-competitive inhibitors.
    8. Often require cofactors.

    8.3 How enzymes work — lowering the activation energy

    Every reaction needs an energy barrier to be crossed — the activation energy. Enzymes provide an alternative reaction path with a much lower activation energy: substrate binds in the active site → strained enzyme–substrate (ES) complex forms → product is released and the enzyme is free again. Enzymes never change the equilibrium or the energy released — they only change the speed.

  • 11. Conceptual Relationships — How This Topic Connects to Everything Else

    • Carbohydrates ↔ photosynthesis and respiration: glucose is made in photosynthesis and broken in respiration; the pentose sugar ribose reappears in RNA.
    • Proteins ↔ enzymes and genetics: enzymes are proteins; proteins are made according to the code in nucleic acids — so DNA ultimately controls every biomolecule (the central dogma).
    • Lipids ↔ cell membrane: the phospholipid bilayer is the structural theme of Topic 3 (eukaryotic cell structure); membranes only work because of amphipathic lipid behaviour.
    • Nucleic acids ↔ cell division: chromosomes = DNA + histone proteins; mitosis and meiosis (Topic 4) distribute DNA.
    • Minerals and water ↔ every physiological process: nerve impulses (Na⁺/K⁺), oxygen transport (Fe in haemoglobin), photosynthesis (Mg in chlorophyll), and the fact that all reactions need an aqueous medium.
    • Ecology connection: the 4 and 9 kcal/g energy values are exactly why energy pyramids shrink so fast — energy is lost at every trophic level as heat.
    • Health connection: balanced diet = correct proportions of all biomolecule classes; deficiencies (anaemia, goitre, kwashiorkor, rickets) are mineral/protein deficiencies.

    12.2 Model answers (exam-style, marks-proportioned)

    Q1 (3m) — Main classes of biomolecules with one function each. Answer: Biomolecules are the organic and inorganic compounds that make up living cells. (i) Carbohydrates — main energy source and structural material (cellulose). (ii) Proteins — growth, repair, catalysis (enzymes) and defence (antibodies). (iii) Lipids — concentrated energy storage (9 kcal/g) and membrane structure. (iv) Nucleic acids — storage and transmission of hereditary information (DNA, RNA). (v) Enzymes — protein catalysts that speed up metabolism. (vi) Minerals and water — regulate osmotic balance, act as cofactors and provide the reaction medium. (Write definition + 4–6 classes + one clear function each; ½–½ marks per class with function.)

    Q2 (3m) — Structure of a nucleotide and its role in DNA. Answer: A nucleotide has three components: (1) a pentose sugar — deoxyribose in DNA; (2) a nitrogenous base — purine (adenine/guanine) or pyrimidine (cytosine/thymine); (3) a phosphate group. Sugar + base = nucleoside; adding phosphate makes a nucleotide. In DNA, nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds into long polynucleotide chains; the sequence of bases stores genetic information, and complementary base pairing (A=T, G≡C) allows exact replication and transmission of heredity.

    Q3 (4m) — Salient features of the Watson–Crick model. Answer: (1) DNA consists of two polynucleotide chains wound into a right-handed double helix; (2) the chains run antiparallel (5′→3′ and 3′→5′); (3) sugar–phosphate backbones lie outside, bases inside; (4) bases pair specifically — A with T (2 H-bonds), G with C (3 H-bonds) — a purine with a pyrimidine; (5) the strands are complementary, permitting semiconservative replication; (6) one turn = 3.4 nm with 10 base pairs; helix diameter 2 nm. (Keywords: double helix, antiparallel, complementary, hydrogen bonds, 3.4 nm.)

    Q4 (3m) — Enzymes: chemical nature and biocatalysts. Answer: Enzymes are chemically proteins (globular proteins; a few are catalytic RNAs). They are called biocatalysts because they are produced by living cells and accelerate biochemical reactions enormously — without being consumed or permanently changed — by lowering the activation energy, under mild conditions of temperature and pH.

    Q5 (3m) — Temperature and pH effects + immobilised enzymes. Answer: Temperature: enzyme activity rises with temperature up to an optimum (~37 °C in humans) because molecules collide more often; above the optimum, heat denatures the protein's tertiary structure and activity collapses. pH: each enzyme has a narrow optimum (pepsin ~2, trypsin ~8); at extreme pH the ionic charges of the active site change, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured. Advantage of immobilised enzymes: they are fixed to an inert support so they can be reused many times, recovered easily from products, and used continuously in industrial bioreactors — cheaper and cleaner.

    Q6 (2m) — Classify carbohydrates with one example each. Answer: (1) Monosaccharides — single sugar unit — glucose; (2) Disaccharides — two units — sucrose; (3) Oligosaccharides — 3–10 units — raffinose; (4) Polysaccharides — many units — starch/cellulose.

    Q7 (3m) — Saturated vs unsaturated fatty acids; why bilayers form. Answer: Saturated fatty acids have only C–C single bonds, carry maximum hydrogen, are straight, pack tightly and are solid at room temperature (ghee, palmitic acid). Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more C=C double bonds, are bent (kinked), pack loosely and are liquid oils at room temperature (mustard oil, oleic acid). Phospholipid bilayers: phospholipids are amphipathic — hydrophilic phosphate heads and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails. In water the heads face outward toward water while the tails hide from it, so two layers arrange tail-to-tail, forming the stable bilayer that is the basis of all cell membranes.

    Q8 (2m) — DNA vs RNA. Answer: DNA has deoxyribose, is double-stranded, uses thymine, is very stable, and stores heredity; RNA has ribose, is usually single-stranded, uses uracil, is less stable, and helps in protein synthesis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA).

    12.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling lipids "true polymers" — they are not; fatty acids are not repeated monomers.
    2. Writing "RNA has thymine" — RNA uses uracil.
    3. Confusing nucleoside (sugar + base) with nucleotide (sugar + base + phosphate).
    4. Saying enzymes "provide energy" — they only lower activation energy; they never supply energy or change equilibrium.
    5. Forgetting that denaturation does not break peptide bonds.
    6. Reversing A=T (2 bonds) and G≡C (3 bonds) hydrogen-bond counts.
    7. Saying the camel's hump stores water — it stores fat (which yields metabolic water when oxidised).
Confusion
  • Lipid vs carbohydrate as "polymer": carbohydrates, proteins and nucleic acids are true polymers of repeated monomers; triglycerides are NOT — glycerol and fatty acids are different molecules, not repeated units. NEB loves this trap.
  • Nucleoside vs nucleotide: nucleoside = sugar + base only; nucleotide = sugar + base + phosphate. Only the nucleotide is the true monomer of nucleic acids.
  • Denaturation vs digestion: denaturation (heat, pH) destroys 3-D shape but leaves peptide bonds intact; hydrolysis (digestion) actually breaks peptide bonds into amino acids.
  • Starch vs glycogen vs cellulose: all are glucose polymers, but starch (plants, α-1,4/1,6), glycogen (animals, more highly branched), cellulose (β-1,4, indigestible). Same monomer, different bonds = different biology.
  • Competitive vs non-competitive inhibition: competitive inhibitors bind the active site and can be overcome by more substrate; non-competitive inhibitors bind an allosteric site and cannot.
  • Enzyme vs hormone: enzymes are catalysts (not consumed, act locally on substrates); hormones are chemical messengers (consumed after action, transported by blood to distant targets).
  • DNA "contains protein": DNA is pure nucleic acid; chromosomes contain DNA + histone protein. DNA itself has no protein.
  • Saturated fat vs cholesterol: saturation describes fatty-acid bonds; cholesterol is a steroid (derived lipid), a separate thing — both raise heart-disease risk but are chemically different.

Examples
  • Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) — hexose monosaccharide; universal respiratory fuel; blood sugar.
  • Sucrose — non-reducing disaccharide (glucose + fructose); the transport sugar of plant phloem — non-reducing so it does not react en route.
  • Cellulose — β-1,4 glucose polymer; plant cell wall; human roughage (we lack cellulase; ruminants digest it via symbiotic microbes).
  • Chitin — N-acetylglucosamine polymer; fungal cell walls and arthropod exoskeletons.
  • Haemoglobin — conjugated chromoprotein (haem + globin) with Fe²⁺; transports O₂; 4 subunits = quaternary structure.
  • Collagen — most abundant animal protein; triple helix; skin, bone, connective tissue.
  • Keratin — α-helical structural protein of hair, nails, horn.
  • Phospholipid (lecithin) — amphipathic membrane lipid; basis of the fluid-mosaic bilayer.
  • Cholesterol — steroid; stabilises animal membranes; precursor of steroid hormones and vitamin D.
  • ATP — a nucleotide (adenine + ribose + 3 phosphates); energy currency of the cell.
  • Urease — first enzyme crystallised (Sumner, 1926), proving enzymes are protein.
  • Amylase — hydrolase; digests starch to maltose; optimum pH ~6.8 (salivary).
Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): Define biomolecules. Give two examples. — Biomolecules are the organic and inorganic compounds that constitute living cells and take part in all life processes. Examples: glucose (carbohydrate), haemoglobin (protein); also lipids, nucleic acids, water, minerals.
  • Short answer (2m): What is a peptide bond? — The covalent bond (−CO−NH−) formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next by condensation, releasing water; it links amino acids into polypeptides.
  • Short answer (2m): Why is water called the universal solvent? — Because its polar molecules form hydrogen bonds and hydration shells around ions and polar compounds, dissolving more substances than any other liquid; this makes blood, cytoplasm and sap possible.
  • Short answer (2m): What are coenzymes? Give one example. — Small organic, non-protein cofactors (often vitamin derivatives) required by some apoenzymes for activity. Example: NAD, FAD, coenzyme A.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the classification of carbohydrates with examples and state their functions. — Full answer: definition + general formula; four classes (mono-, di-, oligo-, polysaccharides) each with definition, examples and significance; storage vs structural polysaccharides (starch/glycogen vs cellulose/chitin); functions — energy (4 kcal/g), storage, structure, transport (sucrose), cell recognition, nucleic-acid sugars. Structure the answer as: definition → classification table → functions list.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the structure of DNA according to the Watson–Crick model. — Double helix of two antiparallel strands; sugar–phosphate backbone outside, bases inside; complementary pairing A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds); purine–pyrimidine pairing keeps 2 nm diameter; 10 bp/turn, 3.4 nm/turn; complementarity explains semiconservative replication and Chargaff's rule. Include a labelled diagram.
  • Long answer (5m): What are enzymes? Explain the factors affecting enzyme activity. — Definition + protein nature + active site + ES complex + activation-energy lowering. Factors: temperature (optimum then denaturation), pH (narrow optimum), substrate concentration (saturation plateau), enzyme concentration (proportional rise), inhibitors (competitive vs non-competitive). Draw rate-vs-temperature and rate-vs-pH curves.
  • Conceptual (3m): "Structure determines function." Justify this statement with two biomolecule examples. — (1) Starch vs cellulose: identical glucose monomers, but α vs β glycosidic bonds make one digestible and the other structural fibre. (2) Haemoglobin: one amino-acid substitution in the β-chain (sickle-cell anaemia) changes the quaternary shape and destroys O₂-carrying efficiency. In both cases the 3-D arrangement — not the composition alone — decides the biological role.
  • Conceptual (3m): Why are enzymes called biocatalysts but not "sources of energy"? — Enzymes only lower the activation energy so reactions reach equilibrium faster; they are not consumed and they supply no energy themselves — the energy comes from the substrate's chemical bonds. A catalyst changes rate, never the ΔG or the equilibrium position.
  • Application (3m): A patient's blood test shows low haemoglobin and an enlarged thyroid. Which minerals are deficient, and why? — Low haemoglobin → iron deficiency (Fe²⁺ is the central atom of haem, the O₂-binding prosthetic group of haemoglobin) → anaemia. Enlarged thyroid → iodine deficiency (iodine is required to synthesise thyroxine; without it TSH keeps stimulating the thyroid, causing goitre).
  • Application (2m): Why is sucrose, not glucose, the sugar transported in plant phloem? — Sucrose is non-reducing and chemically inert, so it neither reacts with, nor is consumed by, the cells it passes through during long-distance transport; glucose would be metabolised or react en route.

  • Biomolecules = carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids + minerals, enzymes, water; built from C, H, O, N, P, S.

Summary

Biomolecules — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, plus minerals, enzymes and water — are the chemical basis of life. Monomers join into polymers by condensation (water out) and are split by hydrolysis (water in). Carbohydrates (Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ) supply quick energy and structure; proteins (amino acids + peptide bonds, four structural levels) do the cell's work; lipids (water-insoluble, 9 kcal/g) store concentrated energy and build the amphipathic phospholipid bilayer of all membranes; nucleic acids (nucleotides, A=T/G≡C pairing) store and express heredity. Minerals act as structural materials, cofactors and electrolytes, while water — polar, hydrogen-bonded, 70–90% of the cell — is the solvent, reactant and temperature buffer in which all life chemistry happens. Enzymes, protein catalysts that lower activation energy with high specificity, control the speed of every one of these reactions.

1.

Which biomolecule is NOT a true polymer?

Options
  • Protein
  • Starch
  • Triglyceride (fat)
  • DNA
Answer

C

Explanation

Triglycerides are esters of one glycerol with three fatty acids — different molecules, not repeated identical monomers. Proteins, starch and DNA are true polymers of amino acids, glucose and nucleotides respectively.

2.

How many hydrogen bonds join guanine to cytosine in DNA?

Options
  • 1
  • 2
  • 3
  • 4
Answer

C

Explanation

G≡C pairing forms 3 hydrogen bonds; A=T forms only 2. More G≡C content makes DNA more heat-stable.

3.

The bond joining two amino acids is the:

Options
  • Glycosidic bond
  • Peptide bond
  • Ester bond
  • Phosphodiester bond
Answer

B

Explanation

Peptide bond (−CO−NH−), formed by condensation between −COOH of one amino acid and −NH₂ of the next. Glycosidic = sugars, ester = glycerol–fatty acid, phosphodiester = nucleotides.

4.

A polypeptide of 200 amino acids contains how many peptide bonds?

Options
  • 200
  • 199
  • 201
  • 100
Answer

B

Explanation

A chain of n amino acids has (n − 1) peptide bonds; 200 amino acids → 199 bonds and 199 water molecules released.

5.

Which sugar is transported through plant phloem and why?

Options
  • Glucose — it is a reducing sugar
  • Sucrose — it is non-reducing and chemically inert
  • Fructose — it is the sweetest sugar
  • Starch — it is insoluble
Answer

B

Explanation

Sucrose's glycosidic bond locks both anomeric carbons, so it does not react with (or get consumed by) tissues during transport.

6.

The induced-fit model of enzyme action was proposed by:

Options
  • Emil Fischer
  • Daniel Koshland
  • James Sumner
  • Michaelis and Menten
Answer

B

Explanation

Koshland (1959) proposed that the active site is flexible and moulds around the substrate; Fischer (1894) proposed the rigid lock-and-key model; Sumner crystallised urease.

7.

Which test distinguishes a reducing sugar from sucrose?

Options
  • Biuret test
  • Iodine test
  • Benedict's test
  • Sudan III test
Answer

C

Explanation

Benedict's/Fehling's gives a brick-red Cu₂O precipitate with reducing sugars (free aldehyde/ketone group) and is negative with sucrose.

8.

Phospholipids form bilayers in water because they are:

Options
  • Fully hydrophobic
  • Fully hydrophilic
  • Amphipathic
  • Charged polymers
Answer

C

Explanation

Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails; in water the heads face outward and tails hide inward, self-assembling a bilayer.

9.

Which of the following is a conjugated protein?

Options
  • Albumin
  • Keratin
  • Haemoglobin
  • Collagen
Answer

C

Explanation

Haemoglobin = globin (protein) + haem (iron-containing prosthetic group), so it is a chromoprotein — conjugated. Albumin, keratin and collagen are simple proteins.

10.

Ice floats on water because water has:

Options
  • High specific heat
  • Maximum density at 4 °C
  • High surface tension
  • High heat of vaporization
Answer

B

Explanation

Water reaches maximum density at 4 °C; below that, hydrogen bonding forms an open ice lattice that is less dense, so ice floats and insulates aquatic life.

Important Concepts
  • Condensation/hydrolysis cycle — the universal build-and-break chemistry of every biomolecule.
  • Structure determines function — protein shape (sickle-cell), α vs β glycosidic bonds (starch vs cellulose), amphipathic lipid shape (membrane bilayer).
  • Complementary base pairing — the physical basis of heredity, replication and Chargaff's rule.
  • Activation energy lowering — the single correct explanation of enzyme catalysis.
  • Energy density hierarchy — fat (9) > protein = carbohydrate (4) kcal/g, because lipids are more reduced (less oxygen per carbon).
