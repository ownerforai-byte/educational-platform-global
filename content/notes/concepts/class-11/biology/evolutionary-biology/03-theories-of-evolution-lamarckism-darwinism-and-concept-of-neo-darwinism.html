Enriched Content

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Two Theories and Their Modern Merger

    How did giraffes get long necks? Lamarck answered: use them and the stretch is inherited. Darwin answered: among ancestral necks of every length, the longest-reaching survived and bred — generation after generation. Modern genetics merged Darwin's selection with Mendel's inheritance into neo-Darwinism, today's consensus. NEB has asked Darwin's theory for 5 marks in both 2023 and 2024.

  • 2. Lamarckism — Inheritance of Acquired Characters (1809, Jean-Baptiste Lamarck)

    2.1 The four propositions

    1. Internal vital forces: living things have an innate tendency toward complexity.
    2. Environment and need: environmental changes create new needs; new needs generate new structures.
    3. Use and disuse: organs used more become stronger and larger; unused organs weaken and shrink.
    4. Inheritance of acquired characters: characters acquired in an organism's lifetime pass to offspring.

    2.2 Examples Lamarck used

    • Giraffe: ancestral short-necked browsers stretched to reach higher leaves; the stretched neck was inherited, lengthening over generations.
    • Snake: limbless by disuse of limbs in burrowing.
    • Swimmers (ducks, frogs): webbed feet by use of toes in swimming.
    • Cave animals: blind by disuse of eyes in darkness.

    2.3 Criticism (why Lamarckism failed)

    ObjectionDetail
    Acquired characters are not inheritedBody (somatic) changes do not reach the germ line — Weismann's germplasm theory (cutting mice's tails for 22 generations: tails still born)
    No known mechanismDNA/protein synthesis cannot "write back" use-stories into genes
    Falsified experimentallyTraining, mutilation and education effects are not transmitted
    Explains perfection poorlyComplicated organs (eye) need more than "use"

    Partial rehabilitation: epigenetics shows some environmentally induced marks (methylation) can persist a generation or two — but these are not the permanent inheritance Lamarck claimed.

  • 3. Darwinism — Natural Selection (1859, On the Origin of Species)

    Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace independently arrived at natural selection; Darwin published after Wallace's 1858 letter.

    3.1 Darwin's five observations and deductions (the NEB 5m skeleton)

    #Observation/DeductionContent
    1OverproductionOrganisms produce far more offspring than can survive (a cod: millions of eggs)
    2Struggle for existenceFood, space, mates, predators limited → competition (intraspecific, interspecific, environmental)
    3VariationIndividuals differ in every population (size, speed, colour, resistance) — heritable variation is the raw material
    4Survival of the fittestIndividuals whose variations fit the environment survive and reproduce ("fitness" = reproductive success, not strength)
    5Natural selection and inheritanceThe survivors' favourable variations pass to offspring; over generations the population changes — descent with modification; new species arise

    Darwin's example — industrial melanism (Kettlewell's confirmation): pre-industrial England's peppered moths were pale, camouflaged on lichen-covered trees; soot darkened trunks, and the rare dark mutant became camouflaged instead — predators (birds) ate the pale form, and dark moths rose to >90% in industrial regions. Pollution control reversed the trend — selection demonstrated in real time.

    Other classic examples: giraffe (long-necked variants out-browsed others in droughts); antibiotic-resistant bacteria (selection under drug pressure); Galápagos finch beaks shifting with seed hardness during droughts.

    3.2 Criticism of Darwinism in its original form

    1. No mechanism of inheritance/variation (Mendel's work was unknown to him).
    2. "Survival of the fittest" (Spencer's phrase) risks tautology.
    3. Small continuous variations may be swamped by blending inheritance (resolved by genetics).
    4. Cannot explain vestigial organs or the origin of very complex organs in early stages (partially addressed by exaptation).

  • 4. Lamarckism vs Darwinism (NEB 2024 comparison)

    BasisLamarckismDarwinism

  • Natural selection — the four-step logic

  • 5. Neo-Darwinism — The Modern Synthesis (Dobzhansky, Mayr, Huxley, Fisher)

    Darwin's selection + Mendelian genetics + population genetics:

    1. Genetic variation arises by mutation, recombination, gene flow — random with respect to need.
    2. Natural selection changes allele frequencies non-randomly — the directional filter.
    3. Genetic drift — random change, strongest in small populations.
    4. Reproductive isolation — geographic then genetic — completes speciation.
    5. Evolution = change in allele frequencies over generations (measured by Hardy–Weinberg, Topic 36).

    Also part of the modern picture: mutationism (De Vries — saltation via mutations in Oenothera), isolation theory (Mayr), and molecular clock dating.

    7.2 Model answers

    Q1 (5m, 2023 & 2024) — Darwin's theory of natural selection with an example. Answer: Darwin's theory rests on two observations and three deductions: (1) Overproduction — every species produces far more offspring than can possibly survive (a single cod releases millions of eggs); (2) Struggle for existence — since food, space and mates are limited, offspring compete with each other, other species and the environment; (3) Variation — individuals of every population differ in heritable traits such as speed, colour or resistance; (4) Survival of the fittest — individuals whose variations suit the prevailing environment survive and reproduce more successfully ("fitness" means reproductive success); (5) Natural selection and inheritance — the favourable variations of the survivors pass to the next generation, so the population's composition shifts generation by generation, producing new adaptations and, in time, new species — descent with modification. Example — industrial melanism: in pre-industrial England the pale peppered moth (Biston betularia) camouflaged on lichen-covered trunks; industrial soot blackened the trees, making pale moths conspicuous to birds while a rare dark mutant became camouflaged. Within decades dark moths rose above 90% in industrial areas; when pollution control restored the lichens, pale moths returned. Predatory birds acted as the selecting agent — natural selection demonstrated in real time. (Alternative examples: giraffe long-necked variants surviving droughts; antibiotic-resistant bacteria under drug pressure.)

    Q2 (3m, 2024) — Compare Lamarck's and Darwin's theories. Answer: (1) Mechanism: Lamarck held that organisms acquire characters through need and use/disuse and pass them to offspring; Darwin held that heritable variations arise randomly and the environment selects the fittest individuals. (2) Direction: Lamarck's evolution is need-driven and purposeful; Darwin's is undirected, working through differential survival. (3) Giraffe: Lamarck — ancestral giraffes stretched their necks reaching leaves and the stretch was inherited; Darwin — among ancestral giraffes, those born with longer necks reached more food in droughts and left more offspring, so neck length increased over generations. (4) Fate: inheritance of acquired characters was disproved (Weismann's germplasm theory; somatic changes do not enter the germ line), while Darwin's selection, updated by genetics, remains the core of modern evolutionary theory.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Writing "survival of the fittest" as "survival of the strongest" — fitness = reproductive success.
    2. Explaining the giraffe Lamarck-style in a Darwin answer — the most common conceptual error.
    3. Omitting the example — the 5m question explicitly demands one.
    4. Missing "overproduction" or "variation" — all five points carry marks.
    5. Saying Darwin knew about genes — he did not; genetics is the neo-Darwinian addition.
Confusion
  • Natural selection vs genetic drift: selection is non-random, direction-giving; drift is random change in allele frequencies, strongest in small populations.
  • Darwinism vs neo-Darwinism: Darwin lacked the inheritance mechanism; the modern synthesis adds Mendelian genetics, mutation, drift and isolation.
Practice

Universal Facts
  • Darwin and Wallace presented their idea jointly in 1858 — one of science's rare independent double discoveries.
Examples
  • Biston betularia — peppered moth; the textbook live demonstration of selection.
  • Weismann's mice — 22 generations of tail-cutting, all born tailed: acquired characters not inherited.
Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): What is industrial melanism? — The rise of dark (melanic) forms of the peppered moth in soot-blackened industrial England because bird predators could no longer see them on dark trunks — and their decline after pollution control — natural selection observed within decades.
Formulas
  • Hardy-Weinberg: p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (p = dominant allele freq, q = recessive)
  • Natural selection: variation → struggle → survival of fittest → adaptation
  • Evolution (modern): change in allele frequencies in a population over generations
Key Points
  • Lamarckism: vital forces, new needs, use/disuse, inheritance of acquired characters; giraffe by stretching; disproved by Weismann's germplasm theory (tail-cutting mice).
  • Darwinism: overproduction → struggle → variation → survival of the fittest → selection and inheritance → descent with modification; example: industrial melanism (Biston betularia), giraffe, antibiotic resistance.
  • Neo-Darwinism: mutation + recombination + gene flow + selection + drift + isolation → allele-frequency change → speciation.
Summary

Two theories explain life's change. Lamarck said organisms acquire characters through use and need and pass them on — the stretched giraffe neck — but Weismann's germplasm theory and genetics killed inheritance of acquired characters. Darwin said the opposite: variation arises randomly and heritably, the struggle for existence filters it, and the fittest — meaning the most reproductively successful — leave more offspring, shifting populations generation by generation until new species form. Industrial melanism proved it live: soot-darkened trunks flipped the peppered moth's selection pressure within decades. The modern synthesis (neo-Darwinism) added mutation, recombination, drift and isolation to Darwin's filter, turning evolution into measurable change in allele frequencies — the theory that every later topic in this unit assumes.

Original Content

Notes
  • Lamarckism (Lamarck, 1809): "use and disuse" of organs + "inheritance of acquired characteristics"; organisms change during life and pass on acquired traits. Mostly rejected (no mechanism for heredity).
  • Darwinism (Darwin, 1859): natural selection; variation exists in populations; overproduction leads to struggle for existence; organisms better adapted survive and reproduce (survival of the fittest).
  • Neo-Darwinism (synthetic theory, early 20th c.): integrates Mendelian genetics with natural selection; evolution = change in allele frequencies in a population; mechanisms: mutation, recombination, natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow.
  • Population genetics: Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p² + 2pq + q² = 1); evolution occurs when allele frequencies change.
  • Evidences supporting modern synthesis: observed selection (peppered moth, antibiotic resistance), genetics (mutations, recombination), and molecular data.
  • Key concept: evolution acts on populations, not individuals; individuals adapt within a generation, but evolution is change across generations.
Confusion
  • Lamarck's "inheritance of acquired characteristics" is largely rejected: a muscle built by exercise is NOT passed to offspring.
  • Darwin did NOT know about genes; his mechanism (natural selection) was later explained by genetics (Neo-Darwinism).
  • Natural selection acts on VARIATION in a population; it does not create new traits (mutations create variation).
  • Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequency (important in small populations); natural selection is NON-random (directed by fitness).
  • Neo-Darwinism = modern synthesis = genetics + natural selection + drift + gene flow + recombination.
  • Evolution is NOT goal-oriented; there is no "progress" toward a goal, only adaptation to current environments.

  • State the Hardy-Weinberg equation and its significance.
Universal Facts
  • Darwin's 1859 book "On the Origin of Species" laid out natural selection.
  • Lamarck published "Philosophie Zoologique" (1809), proposing inheritance of acquired traits.
  • The "modern synthesis" (Neo-Darwinism) was consolidated in the 1930s–40s.
  • Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is a direct, observable example of natural selection.
Examples
  • Lamarck: giraffes stretched necks → passed longer necks (rejected).
  • Darwin: peppered moth (Biston betularia) — industrial melanism (dark form favoured post-industrial).
  • Antibiotic resistance: a few resistant bacteria survive → become the population (selection).
  • Genetic drift: founder effect in island populations (random allele change).
  • Neo-Darwinism: changes in allele frequencies (e.g., sickle-cell trait in malaria regions).

Formulas
  • Hardy-Weinberg: p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (p = dominant allele freq, q = recessive)
  • Natural selection: variation → struggle → survival of fittest → adaptation
  • Evolution (modern): change in allele frequencies in a population over generations
Key Points
  • Lamarckism: use/disuse + inheritance of acquired traits (rejected).
  • Darwinism: natural selection; variation, overproduction, survival of fittest.
  • Neo-Darwinism: genetics + selection + drift + gene flow + recombination.
  • Evolution = change in allele frequencies in a population.
  • Natural selection is non-random; genetic drift is random.
  • Hardy-Weinberg: p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (equilibrium when no evolution).
Summary

Theories of evolution progressed from Lamarck's inheritance of acquired traits (rejected) to Darwin's natural selection (variation, struggle, survival of the fittest) to Neo-Darwinism (modern synthesis), which integrates Mendelian genetics with selection, genetic drift, gene flow, and recombination. In modern terms, evolution is the change in allele frequencies in a population, governed by the Hardy-Weinberg principle. Observable examples (peppered moth, antibiotic resistance) confirm these mechanisms.

    7.2 Model answers

    Q1 (5m, 2023 & 2024) — Darwin's theory of natural selection with an example. Answer: Darwin's theory rests on two observations and three deductions: (1) Overproduction — every species produces far more offspring than can possibly survive (a single cod releases millions of eggs); (2) Struggle for existence — since food, space and mates are limited, offspring compete with each other, other species and the environment; (3) Variation — individuals of every population differ in heritable traits such as speed, colour or resistance; (4) Survival of the fittest — individuals whose variations suit the prevailing environment survive and reproduce more successfully ("fitness" means reproductive success); (5) Natural selection and inheritance — the favourable variations of the survivors pass to the next generation, so the population's composition shifts generation by generation, producing new adaptations and, in time, new species — descent with modification. Example — industrial melanism: in pre-industrial England the pale peppered moth (Biston betularia) camouflaged on lichen-covered trunks; industrial soot blackened the trees, making pale moths conspicuous to birds while a rare dark mutant became camouflaged. Within decades dark moths rose above 90% in industrial areas; when pollution control restored the lichens, pale moths returned. Predatory birds acted as the selecting agent — natural selection demonstrated in real time. (Alternative examples: giraffe long-necked variants surviving droughts; antibiotic-resistant bacteria under drug pressure.)

    Q2 (3m, 2024) — Compare Lamarck's and Darwin's theories. Answer: (1) Mechanism: Lamarck held that organisms acquire characters through need and use/disuse and pass them to offspring; Darwin held that heritable variations arise randomly and the environment selects the fittest individuals. (2) Direction: Lamarck's evolution is need-driven and purposeful; Darwin's is undirected, working through differential survival. (3) Giraffe: Lamarck — ancestral giraffes stretched their necks reaching leaves and the stretch was inherited; Darwin — among ancestral giraffes, those born with longer necks reached more food in droughts and left more offspring, so neck length increased over generations. (4) Fate: inheritance of acquired characters was disproved (Weismann's germplasm theory; somatic changes do not enter the germ line), while Darwin's selection, updated by genetics, remains the core of modern evolutionary theory.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Writing "survival of the fittest" as "survival of the strongest" — fitness = reproductive success.
    2. Explaining the giraffe Lamarck-style in a Darwin answer — the most common conceptual error.
    3. Omitting the example — the 5m question explicitly demands one.
    4. Missing "overproduction" or "variation" — all five points carry marks.
    5. Saying Darwin knew about genes — he did not; genetics is the neo-Darwinian addition.

Confusion

  • Natural selection vs genetic drift: selection is non-random, direction-giving; drift is random change in allele frequencies, strongest in small populations.
  • Darwinism vs neo-Darwinism: Darwin lacked the inheritance mechanism; the modern synthesis adds Mendelian genetics, mutation, drift and isolation.

Practice

  • Why does antibiotic overuse create resistance so fast?

    Answer. Antibiotic overuse is a powerful selection pressure on an enormous, fast-reproducing population (billions of cells doubling every ~20 minutes):

    1. Rare resistant mutants (or cells carrying R-plasmids) survive the dose.
    2. With susceptible competitors killed, the resistant cells multiply without competition.
    3. Conjugation/plasmid transfer spreads resistance genes between species.

    Within days the population is dominated by resistant strains — Darwinian natural selection running in a petri dish. Overuse also selects resistance in harmless bacteria, which then pass the genes to pathogens.

Universal Facts

  • Darwin and Wallace presented their idea jointly in 1858 — one of science's rare independent double discoveries.

Examples

  • Biston betularia — peppered moth; the textbook live demonstration of selection.
  • Weismann's mice — 22 generations of tail-cutting, all born tailed: acquired characters not inherited.

Practice Questions

  • Q. What is industrial melanism?

    Answer. The rise of dark (melanic) forms of the peppered moth (Biston betularia) in soot-blackened industrial England: light lichened trunks turned dark, so birds could no longer spot the melanic moths, which survived and reproduced disproportionately. When pollution control cleaned the trunks, light forms returned — natural selection observed reversing within decades.

Formulas

  • Hardy-Weinberg: p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (p = dominant allele freq, q = recessive)
  • Natural selection: variation → struggle → survival of fittest → adaptation
  • Evolution (modern): change in allele frequencies in a population over generations

Key Points

  • Lamarckism: vital forces, new needs, use/disuse, inheritance of acquired characters; giraffe by stretching; disproved by Weismann's germplasm theory (tail-cutting mice).
  • Darwinism: overproduction → struggle → variation → survival of the fittest → selection and inheritance → descent with modification; example: industrial melanism (Biston betularia), giraffe, antibiotic resistance.
  • Neo-Darwinism: mutation + recombination + gene flow + selection + drift + isolation → allele-frequency change → speciation.

Summary

Two theories explain life's change. Lamarck said organisms acquire characters through use and need and pass them on — the stretched giraffe neck — but Weismann's germplasm theory and genetics killed inheritance of acquired characters. Darwin said the opposite: variation arises randomly and heritably, the struggle for existence filters it, and the fittest — meaning the most reproductively successful — leave more offspring, shifting populations generation by generation until new species form. Industrial melanism proved it live: soot-darkened trunks flipped the peppered moth's selection pressure within decades. The modern synthesis (neo-Darwinism) added mutation, recombination, drift and isolation to Darwin's filter, turning evolution into measurable change in allele frequencies — the theory that every later topic in this unit assumes.
