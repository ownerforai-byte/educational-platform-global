  • 1. Topic Introduction — Two Fungi, Two Strategies

    Mucor and yeast are the two fungi the syllabus dissects in detail — deliberately chosen because they represent the two structural and reproductive extremes of the kingdom: Mucor, a coenocytic phycomycete reproducing by sporangia and zygospores, and Saccharomyces (yeast), a unicellular ascomycete reproducing by budding and ascospores. Master these two and every other fungus becomes a variation on their themes.

  • 2. Mucor — The Pin Mould

    2.1 Occurrence and structure

    • Occurrence: saprophytic mould on moist bread, dung, decaying fruit and vegetables ("bread mould"; "pin mould" from the pin-like sporangiophores).
    • Mycelium: white, cottony, coenocytic (aseptate), multinucleate, branched hyphae with chitin walls.
    • Three kinds of hyphae:
    HyphaDirectionFunction
    SporangiophoresUpright, aerialBear sporangia at their tips (asexual)
    StolonsHorizontal along the surfaceSpread the colony
    RhizoidsDownward, branched root-likeAnchor and absorb; secrete enzymes

    2.2 Reproduction

    (A) Asexual — by sporangiospores (the usual route):

    1. Upright sporangiophore grows from a node behind a rhizoid cluster.
    2. Its tip swells into a globular sporangium; the cytoplasm divides into many multinucleate sporangiospores.
    3. A dome-shaped columella separates spores from the stalk.
    4. The sporangium wall bursts in dampness; thousands of dry spores disperse by wind.
    5. Each spore germinates on a suitable substrate into a new mycelium — genetically identical to the parent.

    (B) Sexual — by conjugation (zygospore formation):

    1. Occurs when opposite (+ and −) mating strains meet on the same substrate.
    2. Progametangia grow toward each other from opposing hyphae.
    3. Each develops a gametangium (multinucleate) + a suspensor cell.
    4. The two gametangia fuse (conjugation) — their nuclei pair and fuse, forming many diploid nuclei.
    5. The fused cell develops a thick, black, warty wall — the zygospore (resting spore).
    6. On germination, meiosis occurs and a sporangiophore bearing a small germ sporangium grows out; spores of both mating types are released.

    Conceptual point: the zygospore is a survival structure — thick-walled, dormant, enduring unfavourable conditions; asexual sporangiospores are for rapid spread, the zygospore for waiting out hard times.

  • 3. Yeast — The Unicellular Ascomycete

    3.1 Occurrence and structure

    • Occurrence: Saccharomyces cerevisiae — sugar-rich substrates: grape and cane juice, nectar, milk, soil near wineries ("sugar fungus").
    • Shape: oval/ellipsoid unicellular cells, 5–10 µm, visible only under the microscope.
    • Cell structure: chitin-rich wall → plasma membrane → cytoplasm with a large vacuole, glycogen/oil granules, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum and a true nucleus (eukaryote).
    • Cells may remain attached after division, forming short pseudomycelium chains.

    3.2 Reproduction

    (A) Asexual — budding (the dominant mode):

    1. A small outgrowth (bud) arises from the parent cell wall.
    2. The nucleus divides mitotically; one daughter nucleus migrates into the bud.
    3. The bud grows, constricts and separates — or remains attached forming chains.
    4. Under abundant food, budding is explosive — hence the name "sugar fungus".

    (B) Asexual — fission (in Schizosaccharomyces, the fission yeast): the cell divides transversely into two equal cells.

    (C) Sexual — ascospore formation:

    1. Under unfavourable conditions (food scarcity), two yeast cells (or two daughter nuclei within one cell) act as gametangia and fuse — conjugation.
    2. The diploid zygote nucleus undergoes meiosis, forming typically 4 (sometimes 8) haploid ascospores inside an ascus.
    3. The ascus germinates; ascospores are released and conjugate or bud again — yeast shows haplodiplobiontic life cycle (alternation of haploid and diploid phases).

    6.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Describe the asexual reproduction of Mucor. Answer: Upright sporangiophores arise from the coenocytic mycelium; each tip swells into a globular sporangium whose multinucleate cytoplasm cleaves into many sporangiospores, separated from the stalk by a dome-shaped columella. The sporangium wall ruptures in damp air, releasing thousands of wind-dispersed spores that germinate into new mycelia identical to the parent.

    Q2 (3m) — Describe sexual reproduction in Mucor with a diagram. Answer: When + and − mating strains meet, progametangia from opposing hyphae grow toward each other; each differentiates into a gametangium plus a suspensor. The gametangia fuse (conjugation); nuclei pair and fuse, and the fused cell develops a thick, dark, warty wall — the zygospore. On germination, meiosis precedes growth of a sporangiophore bearing a germ sporangium releasing spores of both strains.

    Q3 (3m) — Describe budding in yeast with a diagram. Answer: A small wall outgrowth (bud) forms on the parent cell; the nucleus divides mitotically and one daughter nucleus moves into the bud; the bud enlarges and either separates or stays attached, producing chains (pseudomycelium). Budding repeats rapidly in sugar solution.

    Q4 (3m) — Describe sexual reproduction in yeast. Answer: Under scarcity, two haploid cells fuse as gametangia (conjugation); the diploid zygote becomes an ascus in which meiosis yields typically 4 haploid ascospores. Ascospores germinate into haploid cells that bud again — a haplodiplobiontic cycle.

    Q5 (2m) — Why is yeast economically important? Answer: It ferments sugars with zymase to ethanol and CO₂ — the basis of bread (CO₂ leavens dough) and brewing (ethanol); it also supplies vitamin B and is the standard eukaryote of genetic research.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Drawing the Mucor sporangium without the columella.
    2. Writing that yeast ascospores form by mitosis — they follow meiosis after conjugation.
    3. Calling the zygospore a spore for dispersal — it is a resting/survival spore.
    4. Omitting the +/− mating strains in Mucor conjugation — the heterothallic condition is the point.
    5. Saying yeast reproduces "only by budding" — sexual ascospore formation also occurs.

Confusion

  • Sporangiospore vs ascospore: sporangiospores form asexually inside a sporangium (Mucor); ascospores form sexually after meiosis inside an ascus (yeast).
  • Zygospore vs ascus: the zygospore is a thick-walled diploid resting structure; the ascus is a sac in which meiosis produces haploid spores.
  • Budding vs binary fission: budding produces an unequal bud (yeast); fission divides the cell into two equal halves (Schizosaccharomyces, bacteria).
  • Rhizoid vs root: rhizoids are fungal anchoring filaments — no vascular tissue, no root cap, no organs.

Practice

  • Why does a Mucor colony spread so fast across a slice of bread?

    Answer. Three engineering choices make it fast:

    1. Coenocytic hyphae grow at the tip without internal walls, so cytoplasm, nuclei and nutrients stream continuously — nothing slows extension.
    2. Stolons arch across the surface and root down as rhizoids at each new point, so the colony leaps forward rather than inching.
    3. Sporangia burst in thousands and the air-borne sporangiospores land everywhere on the moist, starchy, slightly acidic surface — bread is close to a perfect medium (moisture + starch + room temperature).

    Tip growth × leapfrogging stolons × mass spore rain = a visible furry colony within a day or two.

Universal Facts

  • Buchner (1897) showed cell-free yeast extract ferments sugar — the enzyme zymase; birth of enzymology.

Examples

  • Mucor mucedo — the classic pin mould of bread and dung.
  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae — baker's and brewer's yeast.
  • Schizosaccharomyces octosporus — fission yeast, 8 ascospores.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is a columella?

    Answer. The dome-shaped sterile dome inside the Mucor sporangium — the swollen tip of the sporangiophore pushing up into the spore mass, separating it from the fertile sporangiospores and supporting them.

  • Short answer (2m): What is a zygospore?

    Answer. The thick-walled, dark, diploid resting spore formed when the gametangia of + and − mating strains of Mucor conjugate. It survives drought and unfavourable seasons, and on germination undergoes meiosis to start a new haploid mycelium.

Key Points

  • Mucor: coenocytic mycelium with sporangiophores, stolons, rhizoids; asexual sporangiospores with columella; sexual conjugation of +/− strains → zygospore (resting spore).
  • Yeast: unicellular ascomycete, vacuolate eukaryotic cell; asexual budding (and fission); sexual conjugation → ascus with 4–8 ascospores after meiosis; haplodiplobiontic cycle.
  • Yeast fermentation: zymase converts glucose → ethanol + CO₂ + energy; basis of bread and brewing.

Summary

Mucor, the pin mould, builds a coenocytic mycelium of sporangiophores, stolons and rhizoids; it spreads asexually by thousands of sporangiospores released from columella-bearing sporangia and survives hard times as a thick-walled zygospore made by conjugation of + and − strains. Yeast, the unicellular ascomycete, multiplies asexually by budding (or fission) and, when conditions turn poor, conjugates to form an ascus in which meiosis yields 4–8 haploid ascospores — a haplodiplobiontic life cycle. Its zymase fermentation (glucose → ethanol + CO₂) underlies bread, beer and industrial alcohol, making these two fungi the perfect pair: one shows the mould plan, the other the yeast plan, of fungal life.

Important Statements

  • "Every mould's life is a race between spreading fast (sporangiospores) and surviving long (zygospores)."

Formulas

  • The columella inside a Mucor sporangium is sterile. Spores are cut from the cytoplasm around it. Plus and minus strains are both required for the zygospore in the usual heterothallic account.
  • Yeast in the absence of oxygen turns glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide. That is fermentation. The sexual spores, usually four, sit in an ascus, which is the cell itself after fusion and meiosis.

Confusion
  • Sporangiospore vs ascospore: sporangiospores form asexually inside a sporangium (Mucor); ascospores form sexually after meiosis inside an ascus (yeast).
  • Zygospore vs ascus: the zygospore is a thick-walled diploid resting structure; the ascus is a sac in which meiosis produces haploid spores.
  • Budding vs binary fission: budding produces an unequal bud (yeast); fission divides the cell into two equal halves (Schizosaccharomyces, bacteria).
  • Rhizoid vs root: rhizoids are fungal anchoring filaments — no vascular tissue, no root cap, no organs.
Key Points
  • Mucor: coenocytic mycelium with sporangiophores, stolons, rhizoids; asexual sporangiospores with columella; sexual conjugation of +/− strains → zygospore (resting spore).
  • Yeast: unicellular ascomycete, vacuolate eukaryotic cell; asexual budding (and fission); sexual conjugation → ascus with 4–8 ascospores after meiosis; haplodiplobiontic cycle.
  • Yeast fermentation: zymase converts glucose → ethanol + CO₂ + energy; basis of bread and brewing.
Important Concepts
  • Two dispersal strategies: asexual spores for rapid identical spread; sexual spores (zygospore/ascospores) for survival and recombination.
Examples
  • Mucor mucedo — the classic pin mould of bread and dung.
  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae — baker's and brewer's yeast.
  • Schizosaccharomyces octosporus — fission yeast, 8 ascospores.
Universal Facts
  • Buchner (1897) showed cell-free yeast extract ferments sugar — the enzyme zymase; birth of enzymology.
Important Statements
  • "Every mould's life is a race between spreading fast (sporangiospores) and surviving long (zygospores)."

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): What is a columella? — The dome-shaped sterile dome inside the Mucor sporangium separating the sporangiophore from the sporangiospores.
  • Short answer (2m): What is a zygospore? — The thick-walled, dark, resting diploid spore formed by conjugation of + and − gametangia in Mucor; it survives unfavourable conditions and germinates after meiosis.
Summary

Mucor, the pin mould, builds a coenocytic mycelium of sporangiophores, stolons and rhizoids; it spreads asexually by thousands of sporangiospores released from columella-bearing sporangia and survives hard times as a thick-walled zygospore made by conjugation of + and − strains. Yeast, the unicellular ascomycete, multiplies asexually by budding (or fission) and, when conditions turn poor, conjugates to form an ascus in which meiosis yields 4–8 haploid ascospores — a haplodiplobiontic life cycle. Its zymase fermentation (glucose → ethanol + CO₂) underlies bread, beer and industrial alcohol, making these two fungi the perfect pair: one shows the mould plan, the other the yeast plan, of fungal life.
