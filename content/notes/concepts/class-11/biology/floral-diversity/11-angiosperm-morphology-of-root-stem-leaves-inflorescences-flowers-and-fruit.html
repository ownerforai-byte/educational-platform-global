  • 1. Topic Introduction — Reading the Body of a Flowering Plant

    Angiosperms dominate the land because of two inventions: the flower (protected ovary) and the fruit (seed packaging and dispersal). This topic is the vocabulary of the plant body — root systems, stem types, leaf forms, inflorescence patterns, flower parts and fruit types. Every later identification question (Topic 17's families) depends on this vocabulary.

  • 3. Stem — The Axis of Leaves and Conduction

    3.1 General features

    Bears nodes (leaf attachment) and internodes; carries buds (terminal, axillary); exogenous origin; usually positively phototropic.

    3.2 Stem modifications

    TypeModificationExample
    UndergroundTuber (potato — "eyes" are buds), rhizome (ginger), corm (colocasia), bulb (onion — fleshy scale leaves)Food storage + perennation

  • 4. Leaf — The Photosynthetic Machine

    4.1 Parts and venation

    A typical leaf = leaf base (sometimes with stipules) + petiole + lamina (blade).

    BasisReticulate venationParallel venation
    PatternBranched net-like veinsVeins run parallel

    4.2 Leaf types and shapes

    • Simple leaf: one lamina, one axillary bud (mango). Compound leaf: lamina divided into leaflets; buds only on the main axis — pinnately compound (neem, rose) or palmately compound (silk cotton).
    • Phyllotaxy (leaf arrangement on the stem): alternate, opposite, whorled.
    • Leaf modifications: tendril (pea leaflets), spine (cactus, Acacia — protection/water saving), scale leaf (onion storage), insectivorous leaves (Nepenthes pitcher — nitrogen trapping).

(3) Venation: reticulate vs parallel. (4) Floral parts: tetra/pentamerous vs trimerous. Examples: dicot — mango (Mangifera indica); monocot — maize (Zea mays). (Any two points + examples = full marks.)

    9.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling a compound leaf's leaflet axil a bud — buds occur only on the rachis axis, not in leaflet axils.
    2. Mixing racemose and cymose directions — acropetal vs basipetal.
    3. Calling wheat's fruit a seed — it is a caryopsis (pericarp fused to the seed coat).
    4. Saying all roots store food or all stems climb — always give the modification + example pair.
    5. Reversing placentation examples (marginal = pea, parietal = mustard).

Confusion

  • Leaflet axil vs stem axil: only the compound leaf's rachis has an axillary bud; individual leaflets never do — the surest simple-vs-compound test.
  • Rhizome vs tuber: rhizome is a horizontal stem with nodes/internodes (ginger); tuber is a swollen stem tip with "eyes" (potato).
  • Thorn vs prickle: thorn is a modified stem with vascular connection (deep-seated); prickles are superficial outgrowths (rose) removable without tearing.
  • Fruit vs seed: fruit = fertilised ovary (+ accessories); seed = fertilised ovule. Wheat grain = caryopsis fruit, not a bare seed.

Practice

  • Why does a potato "eye" prove the potato is a stem, not a root?

    Answer. The "eyes" are buds — axillary buds in the axil of a scale leaf, each able to sprout a new shoot. Buds and nodes are stem characters only: roots never bear buds or leaves, so a swollen root (like sweet potato or carrot) has no eyes and sprouts only from its crown. The potato tuber also bears the other stem signatures — it develops at the tip of an underground stem branch (stolon) from an axillary bud, and shows internode-like spacing between eyes. A bud-bearing swollen organ is therefore a modified underground stem, whatever its job.

Universal Facts

  • Caryopsis (grass fruit) fuses pericarp and seed coat — the world's staple "seeds" (wheat, rice, maize) are actually fruits.

Examples

  • Banyan prop roots — pillars from branches; support.
  • Avicennia pneumatophores — breathing roots of swampy Sundarbans.
  • Opuntia phylloclade — flattened green stem doing photosynthesis; leaves reduced to spines.
  • Sunflower head — racemose inflorescence compressed into a receptacle with ray and disc florets.
  • Mango drupe — fleshy mesocarp + stony endocarp around the seed.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Define inflorescence. Distinguish racemose from cymose.

    Answer. Inflorescence — the arrangement of flowers on a floral axis.

    BasisRacemoseCymose
    Axis growthIndefinite — apical meristem keeps growingDefinite — axis ends in a flower
    Flower orderAcropetal — older flowers below, younger at tipBasipetal — oldest flower at the tip, later ones lateral
    BranchingAxis may branch onceLateral branches repeat the pattern
    ExampleRaceme, spike, umbel, headUniparasitic/multiparasitic cyme (e.g. Solanaceae)
  • Short answer (2m): What is placentation? Name two types.

    Answer. Placentation — the arrangement of ovules within the ovary (where they attach to the placenta).

    Two types:

    1. Marginal — ovules along one ventral suture of a monocarpellary ovary (pea).
    2. Axile — ovules on a central axis in a multilocular ovary (tomato).

    (Also: parietal — mustard; free-central; basal — sunflower.)

  • Long answer (5m): Describe root modifications with examples.

    Answer. 1. Storage tap roots — the tap root swells with food:

    • Conical (carrot), fusiform (radish), napiform (turnip).

    2. Nodulated roots — pea and other legumes carry Rhizobium nodules that fix atmospheric nitrogen symbiotically.

    3. Prop roots — banyan: pillar-like adventitious roots drop from branches and support the spreading crown.

    4. Stilt roots — maize and sugarcane: short angled roots from lower nodes brace the weak stem.

    5. Climbing roots — money plant: clasping adventitious roots grip supports.

    6. Pneumatophores (breathing roots) — Avicennia in saline mangrove mud: negatively geotropic spikes with lenticels that aerate the buried root system.

    Each modification = a root doing a job other than anchorage-and-absorption: storage, nitrogen supply, support, climbing or breathing.

  • Long answer (5m): Classify fruits with two examples of each type.

    Answer. I. Simple fruits (from one flower, one ovary):

    Fleshy:

    1. Drupe — stone fruit from a superior ovary; mango, coconut.
    2. Berry — whole pericarp fleshy; tomato, banana.

    Dry dehiscent:

    1. Legume (pod) — one carpel splitting both sutures; pea, bean.
    2. Capsule — multicarpellary, splits in many ways; cotton, poppy.

    Dry indehiscent:

    1. Caryopsis — pericarp fused with the seed coat; wheat, maize.
    2. Achene — small one-seeded, loose pericarp; Mirabilis, sunflower (on the receptacle).
    3. Nut — hard, one-seeded; cashew, oak.

    II. Aggregate fruit — one flower, many free carpels on a receptacle: custard apple (Annona), raspberry.

    III. Multiple fruit — a whole inflorescence of many flowers fuses: pineapple, jackfruit, mulberry.

    Origin rule: simple and aggregate fruits come from one flower; a multiple fruit is many flowers grown together.

Key Points

  • Roots: tap (dicot) vs fibrous (monocot); modifications — storage, nodulated (Rhizobium), prop (banyan), stilt (maize), pneumatophores (Avicennia).
  • Stems: nodes/buds always present; underground (tuber, rhizome, corm, bulb), aerial (tendril, thorn, phylloclade), sub-aerial (runner, stolon, offset, sucker).
  • Leaves: base+petiole+lamina; reticulate (dicot) vs parallel (monocot) venation; simple vs compound (pinnate/palmate); phyllotaxy; modifications — tendril, spine, pitcher.
  • Inflorescence: racemose (indefinite, acropetal — raceme, spike, umbel, head) vs cymose (definite, basipetal).
  • Flower: calyx, corolla, androecium, gynoecium; actinomorphic vs zygomorphic; hypogynous/perigynous/epigynous; placentation types.
  • Fruits: simple fleshy (drupe — mango; berry — tomato), simple dry (legume — pea; caryopsis — wheat; achene, nut, capsule), aggregate (custard apple), multiple (pineapple).
  • Dicot vs monocot: two vs one cotyledon, tap vs fibrous root, reticulate vs parallel venation, tetra/pentamerous vs trimerous flowers.

Summary

The angiosperm body is a vocabulary: tap or fibrous roots with storage, nodulated, prop, stilt and breathing modifications; stems that always betray nodes and buds whether tuber, rhizome, tendril, thorn or phylloclade; leaves with reticulate (dicot) or parallel (monocot) venation, simple or compound, sometimes tendrils, spines or pitchers; inflorescences racemose (indefinite, acropetal) or cymose (definite, basipetal); flowers in four whorls — calyx, corolla, androecium, gynoecium — with defined symmetry, ovary position and placentation; and fruits from drupes and berries to legumes, caryopses, aggregates and multiples. Dicots and monocots split on cotyledons, roots, venation and floral merosity — the master table behind every family identification.

Important Statements

  • "Every modified organ confesses its origin by its buds, nodes or vascular traces."

Formulas

  • A potato is a stem tuber because it has buds. A sweet potato is a storage root. A thorn of a citrus-type stem is a modified stem, while a prickle may be superficial. The presence of nodes or buds decides a stem.
  • Racemose inflorescences open from below upward. Cymose ones open the top flower first. A flower is hypogynous when the ovary sits above the other parts, and the fruit is the ripened ovary, with a pericarp around the seeds.

    9.2 Model answer

    Q (2m) — Differentiate dicot and monocot angiosperms with one example each. Answer: (1) Seed: dicots have two cotyledons, monocots one. (2) Root: dicots tap root, monocots fibrous. (3) Venation: reticulate vs parallel. (4) Floral parts: tetra/pentamerous vs trimerous. Examples: dicot — mango (Mangifera indica); monocot — maize (Zea mays). (Any two points + examples = full marks.)

    9.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling a compound leaf's leaflet axil a bud — buds occur only on the rachis axis, not in leaflet axils.
    2. Mixing racemose and cymose directions — acropetal vs basipetal.
    3. Calling wheat's fruit a seed — it is a caryopsis (pericarp fused to the seed coat).
    4. Saying all roots store food or all stems climb — always give the modification + example pair.
    5. Reversing placentation examples (marginal = pea, parietal = mustard).
Confusion
  • Leaflet axil vs stem axil: only the compound leaf's rachis has an axillary bud; individual leaflets never do — the surest simple-vs-compound test.
  • Rhizome vs tuber: rhizome is a horizontal stem with nodes/internodes (ginger); tuber is a swollen stem tip with "eyes" (potato).
  • Thorn vs prickle: thorn is a modified stem with vascular connection (deep-seated); prickles are superficial outgrowths (rose) removable without tearing.
  • Fruit vs seed: fruit = fertilised ovary (+ accessories); seed = fertilised ovule. Wheat grain = caryopsis fruit, not a bare seed.
Key Points
  • Roots: tap (dicot) vs fibrous (monocot); modifications — storage, nodulated (Rhizobium), prop (banyan), stilt (maize), pneumatophores (Avicennia).
  • Stems: nodes/buds always present; underground (tuber, rhizome, corm, bulb), aerial (tendril, thorn, phylloclade), sub-aerial (runner, stolon, offset, sucker).
  • Leaves: base+petiole+lamina; reticulate (dicot) vs parallel (monocot) venation; simple vs compound (pinnate/palmate); phyllotaxy; modifications — tendril, spine, pitcher.
  • Inflorescence: racemose (indefinite, acropetal — raceme, spike, umbel, head) vs cymose (definite, basipetal).
  • Flower: calyx, corolla, androecium, gynoecium; actinomorphic vs zygomorphic; hypogynous/perigynous/epigynous; placentation types.
  • Fruits: simple fleshy (drupe — mango; berry — tomato), simple dry (legume — pea; caryopsis — wheat; achene, nut, capsule), aggregate (custard apple), multiple (pineapple).
  • Dicot vs monocot: two vs one cotyledon, tap vs fibrous root, reticulate vs parallel venation, tetra/pentamerous vs trimerous flowers.
Important Concepts
  • Bud test for stem nature: any flattened/spiny/root-like organ with nodes and buds is a modified stem.
Examples
  • Banyan prop roots — pillars from branches; support.
  • Avicennia pneumatophores — breathing roots of swampy Sundarbans.
  • Opuntia phylloclade — flattened green stem doing photosynthesis; leaves reduced to spines.
  • Sunflower head — racemose inflorescence compressed into a receptacle with ray and disc florets.
  • Mango drupe — fleshy mesocarp + stony endocarp around the seed.
Universal Facts
  • Caryopsis (grass fruit) fuses pericarp and seed coat — the world's staple "seeds" (wheat, rice, maize) are actually fruits.
Important Statements
  • "Every modified organ confesses its origin by its buds, nodes or vascular traces."
Important Notes

Practice
  • Why does a potato "eye" prove the potato is a stem, not a root?
Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): Define inflorescence. Distinguish racemose from cymose. — The arrangement of flowers on a floral axis. Racemose: axis indefinite, growing, flowers acropetal; cymose: axis definite, ending in a flower, further flowers basipetal from lateral branches.
  • Short answer (2m): What is placentation? Name two types. — The arrangement of ovules within the ovary. Types: marginal (pea — along the ventral suture), axile (tomato — central axis in a multilocular ovary), parietal (mustard), free-central, basal (sunflower).
  • Long answer (5m): Describe root modifications with examples. — Storage tap roots (carrot conical, radish fusiform, turnip napiform), nodulated roots (pea + Rhizobium), prop roots (banyan), stilt roots (maize), climbing roots (money plant), pneumatophores (Avicennia) — each with function.
  • Long answer (5m): Classify fruits with two examples of each type. — Simple fleshy (drupe: mango; berry: tomato), simple dry dehiscent (legume: pea; capsule: cotton), simple dry indehiscent (caryopsis: wheat; achene: Mirabilis; nut: cashew), aggregate (custard apple), multiple (pineapple) — with origin (ovary vs inflorescence).
Summary

The angiosperm body is a vocabulary: tap or fibrous roots with storage, nodulated, prop, stilt and breathing modifications; stems that always betray nodes and buds whether tuber, rhizome, tendril, thorn or phylloclade; leaves with reticulate (dicot) or parallel (monocot) venation, simple or compound, sometimes tendrils, spines or pitchers; inflorescences racemose (indefinite, acropetal) or cymose (definite, basipetal); flowers in four whorls — calyx, corolla, androecium, gynoecium — with defined symmetry, ovary position and placentation; and fruits from drupes and berries to legumes, caryopses, aggregates and multiples. Dicots and monocots split on cotyledons, roots, venation and floral merosity — the master table behind every family identification.
