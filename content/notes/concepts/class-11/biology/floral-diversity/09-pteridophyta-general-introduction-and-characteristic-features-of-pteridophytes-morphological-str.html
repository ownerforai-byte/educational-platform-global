Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The First Vascular Plants

    Pteridophytes — ferns and their allies — are the first plants with vascular tissue (xylem and phloem), the first with true roots, stems and leaves, and the first with a dominant sporophyte. They are the evolutionary bridge between bryophytes and seed plants: like bryophytes they still need water for fertilisation; like seed plants they hold the sporophyte generation high.

  • 3. Dryopteris — Structure and Reproduction (the syllabus specimen)

    3.1 Morphology (the sporophyte)

    • Rhizome: horizontal underground stem bearing adventitious roots below and fronds above; covered with brown ramenta (scales).
    • Fronds: large, pinnately compound megaphylls; a young coiled frond tip (fiddle head/circinate vernation) unrolls as it grows.
    • Sori: brown clusters on the lower surface of fertile pinnae, each covered by a kidney-shaped indusium; each sporangium has a row of thick-walled annulus cells whose drying tension snaps the sporangium open, flinging spores.

    3.2 Reproduction

    (A) Vegetative: fragmentation of the rhizome.

    (B) Sexual — alternation of generations:

    1. Spore formation: meiosis in sporangia of the sori produces haploid spores (homosporous).
    2. Prothallus: each spore germinates into a small, green, heart-shaped, monoecious prothallus (gametophyte) — independent, 1 cm, rhizoids below.
    3. Sex organs: antheridia (among rhizoids, producing multiflagellate antherozoids) and archegonia (near the notch, flask-shaped with an egg).
    4. Fertilisation: in a water film, antherozoids swim to the archegonium; one fuses with the egg → zygote (2n).
    5. Embryo and young sporophyte: the zygote develops into an embryo anchored to the prothallus by a foot; the young sporophyte grows roots and a first frond, then becomes independent — the prothallus withers.
    6. The mature sporophyte again bears sori — completing the cycle.

    Life cycle type: diplo-haplontic with sporophyte dominance and both generations free-living — the textbook contrast with bryophytes.

    6.2 Model answer

    Q (3m) — General characteristics of Pteridophyta with two examples. Answer: Pteridophytes are the first vascular land plants: (1) the sporophyte has true roots, stem and leaves with xylem and phloem; (2) spores are produced in sporangia grouped into sori (often with an indusium) on sporophylls; (3) the haploid spore grows into a small independent green prothallus bearing antheridia and archegonia; (4) fertilisation still needs water — multiflagellate antherozoids swim to the archegonium; (5) the life cycle is diplo-haplontic with a dominant, independent sporophyte. Examples: Dryopteris (male fern), Selaginella (or Equisetum, Pteris).

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying the prothallus is the fern "plant" — the fern plant is the sporophyte; the prothallus is a temporary gametophyte.
    2. Writing "seeds" anywhere in pteridophytes — they reproduce by spores; seeds begin with gymnosperms.
    3. Forgetting the indusium and annulus in Dryopteris descriptions.
    4. Calling fern leaves microphylls — fern fronds are megaphylls (microphylls belong to Lycopsida).
    5. Omitting water dependence — pteridophytes still need it for fertilisation.

Confusion

  • Sorus vs sporangium: sporangium = the spore case; sorus = a cluster of sporangia on the frond underside.
  • Prothallus vs protonema: prothallus is the fern gametophyte (heart-shaped); protonema is the moss gametophyte's first filamentous stage.
  • Homospory vs heterospory: one spore type (most ferns) vs micro- and megaspores (Selaginella) — heterospory is the precondition of seeds.
  • Circinate vernation: the coiled fiddle-head unrolling of young fern fronds — a signature fern character.

Practice

  • Why does the fern prothallus have to be small and flat?

    Answer. Because it is a gametophyte with no vascular tissue living an exposed life on damp soil:

    1. Every cell must touch a film of water — it absorbs directly through its surface, and only a thin, flat, one-cell-thick sheet can keep its whole body moist and fed by diffusion.
    2. Fertilisation needs a continuous water film connecting antheridia to archegonia — a tiny flat plate keeps the swimming distance millimetres short.
    3. Photosynthesis must sustain it alone — a small green sheet maximises surface per biomass.

    Being small is not a weakness but the design: anything taller or thicker would dry out and starve its inner cells, which is exactly why vascular sporophytes, not gametophytes, are the ones that grow big.

Universal Facts

  • Coal seams of the Carboniferous period (about 300 million years ago) are largely fossil pteridophyte forests.

Examples

  • Dryopteris filix-mas — male fern; anthelmintic rhizome.
  • Azolla pinnata — aquatic fern hosting nitrogen-fixing Anabaena in rice paddies.
  • Equisetum — horsetail with silica-roughened jointed stems.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is a sorium (sorus)?

    Answer. A cluster of sporangia borne on the underside of a fern sporophyll (frond), usually protected by a flap of tissue called the indusium; each sporangium carries an annulus that snaps to fling the spores.

  • Short answer (2m): What is circinate vernation?

    Answer. The coiled, fiddle-head posture of a young fern frond: the blade curls from the tip inward, protected by its own coiling, and unrolls as it matures — a signature fern feature.

  • Long answer (5m): Describe the life cycle of Dryopteris with diagrams.

    Answer. Dominant sporophyte (2n): a horizontal scaly rhizome bears pinnate fronds that unroll by circinate vernation; fertile fronds carry sori on the underside, each protected by a kidney-shaped indusium.

    1. In each sporangium, meiosis produces haploid spores; the annulus dries, snaps and flings them out.
    2. A spore germinates into the gametophyte (n) — a small, flat, green, heart-shaped prothallus living independently on moist soil.
    3. The prothallus bears antheridia (below, among rhizoids) and archegonia (near the notch).
    4. Swimming multiflagellate antherozoids need a film of water to reach the archegonium — fertilisation is water-dependent.
    5. The zygote develops into an embryo nourished first by the prothallus, then grows into a young independent sporophyte; the prothallus withers.
    6. The young fern matures, grows fronds and sori — the cycle repeats.

    Key statement: both generations are independent and photosynthetic, but the sporophyte dominates — the reverse of bryophytes.

    Diagram: fern with sori → zoom on sporangium + annulus → spores → heart-shaped prothallus with antheridia/archegonia → zygote → young sporophyte.

Key Points

  • Pteridophytes: first vascular plants (xylem + phloem), true root/stem/leaf, megaphylls, sori with indusium, annulus snaps to fling spores, independent prothallus, water-dependent fertilisation, dominant sporophyte.
  • Classes: Psilopsida (Psilotum), Lycopsida (Lycopodium, Selaginella — microphylls), Sphenopsida (Equisetum), Pteropsida (true ferns — Dryopteris).
  • Dryopteris: scaly rhizome, pinnate fronds with circinate vernation, sori with kidney indusium; spore → heart-shaped prothallus → antheridia/archegonia → swimming antherozoids → embryo → independent sporophyte.
  • Economic: ornamentals (Adiantum, Nephrolepis), Azolla biofertiliser, male-fern anthelmintic, coal from fossil pteridophyte forests.

Summary

Pteridophytes are the first vascular plants: a dominant sporophyte with true roots, stem and megaphylls run by xylem and phloem, bearing sori (with indusium and annulus) that fling haploid spores. Each spore grows into a small independent heart-shaped prothallus with antheridia and archegonia; fertilisation still needs a water film for the multiflagellate antherozoids, after which the embryo grows into a new fern. Dryopteris shows the full plan — scaly rhizome, circinate fronds, kidney-shaped indusia. Ferns decorate gardens (Adiantum), fix rice-paddy nitrogen (Azolla), once formed the coal forests, and stand one heterosporous step before the evolution of seeds.

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): What is a sorium (sorus)? — A cluster of sporangia on the underside of a fern sporophyll, often protected by an indusium.
  • Short answer (2m): What is circinate vernation? — The coiled, fiddle-head posture of young fern fronds that unrolls as the frond matures.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the life cycle of Dryopteris with diagrams. — Sporophyte (rhizome, fronds, sori with indusium and annulus) → meiosis → spores → heart-shaped prothallus → antheridia and archegonia → water-dependent fertilisation → zygote → embryo → independent young sporophyte → mature fern with sori. State that both generations are independent and the sporophyte dominates.
Summary

Pteridophytes are the first vascular plants: a dominant sporophyte with true roots, stem and megaphylls run by xylem and phloem, bearing sori (with indusium and annulus) that fling haploid spores. Each spore grows into a small independent heart-shaped prothallus with antheridia and archegonia; fertilisation still needs a water film for the multiflagellate antherozoids, after which the embryo grows into a new fern. Dryopteris shows the full plan — scaly rhizome, circinate fronds, kidney-shaped indusia. Ferns decorate gardens (Adiantum), fix rice-paddy nitrogen (Azolla), once formed the coal forests, and stand one heterosporous step before the evolution of seeds.

The heart-shaped, independent prothallus bears antheridia and archegonia; protonema belongs to mosses.

B

Drying annulus cells snap the sporangium open, flinging spores; elaters belong to liverworts, peristome to mosses.
