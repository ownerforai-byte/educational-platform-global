Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Green Engines of Water

    Algae are the simple, chlorophyll-bearing thalloid organisms of ponds, seas and damp walls — the producers that feed every aquatic food chain and supply roughly half of the oxygen we breathe. The syllabus asks for the three great colour classes (green, brown, red), the structure and reproduction of Spirogyra, and the economic importance of algae.

  • Deep dive — pigments classify the algae. Algal classes are named by colour because colour is the visible summary of the pigment set: chlorophylls plus accessory pigments. Chlorophyceae (green) carry chlorophyll a and b like land plants — which is exactly why green algae are considered the ancestors of the plant kingdom. Phaeophyceae (brown) add fucoxanthin; Rhodophyceae (red) add r-phycoerythrin, which absorbs the blue-green light that penetrates deepest water — the reason red algae can live deeper than any other alga.

  • 4. Spirogyra — Structure and Reproduction (the syllabus specimen)

    4.1 Occurrence and structure

    • Freshwater "pond silk" — free-floating green masses in slow water; slippery because of the mucilaginous sheath.
    • Filament: unbranched chain of cylindrical cells.
    • Each cell: cellulose wall + pectin sheath → cytoplasm in a thin peripheral layer → one large central vacuole → a single nucleus suspended by cytoplasmic strands → 1 to many ribbon-shaped, spiral chloroplasts with pyrenoids (starch-forming bodies) — the feature giving the genus its name.

    4.2 Reproduction

    (A) Vegetative — fragmentation: any piece of the filament grows into a full filament.

    (B) Sexual — conjugation (the classic process):

    1. Occurs in mature filaments, usually in calm, nutrient-rich water.
    2. Two filaments lie parallel inside a common mucilage; cells align.
    3. Scalariform (ladder-like) conjugation: opposite cells develop protrusions that meet; the walls dissolve, forming a conjugation tube.
    4. The whole protoplast of the male (donor) cell crawls through the tube into the female (recipient) cell.
    5. The two protoplasts fuse — zygote (zygospore) formed inside the female cell.
    6. The zygospore secretes a thick three-layered wall and sinks to the bottom — a resting spore.
    7. On germination, meiosis occurs, producing 4 haploid nuclei; three degenerate and one forms a new filament — hence Spirogyra is haplontic (only the zygote is diploid).

    (Lateral conjugation occurs between adjacent cells of the same filament in some species.)

    6.2 Model answer

    Q (3m) — Classify algae into three major classes on the basis of pigments, with one example of each. Answer: (1) Chlorophyceae (green algae) — chlorophyll a and b dominate; starch as reserve food; e.g. Spirogyra, Chlamydomonas. (2) Phaeophyceae (brown algae) — chlorophyll a, c plus fucoxanthin gives the brown colour; mannitol/laminarin reserve; e.g. Sargassum, Laminaria. (3) Rhodophyceae (red algae) — chlorophyll a, d plus r-phycoerythrin gives the red colour; floridean starch reserve; e.g. Polysiphonia, Gelidium.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Giving starch as the reserve food of brown algae — it is mannitol/laminarin; starch belongs to green algae.
    2. Saying red algae have flagella — vegetative cells and spores are non-motile.
    3. Forgetting pyrenoids in Spirogyra's chloroplasts.
    4. Writing that zygospore germination is by mitosis — it begins with meiosis.
    5. Mixing up which pigment colours which class (fucoxanthin = brown; phycoerythrin = red).

Confusion

  • Algae vs plants: algae have no roots, stems, leaves, vascular tissue or embryos — a thallus; "plants" in the strict sense begin with bryophytes.
  • Pyrenoid vs nucleus: pyrenoids are protein bodies inside chloroplasts around which starch forms; the nucleus is suspended in the cell centre of Spirogyra.
  • Zygospore vs zygote: the zygote is the immediate fused cell; the zygospore is its thick-walled resting stage.
  • Scalariform vs lateral conjugation: scalariform = between cells of two parallel filaments (ladder-like); lateral = between adjacent cells of one filament.

Practice

  • Why does Spirogyra float as "pond silk" on the water surface?

    Answer. Because the filament manufactures its own flotation device: photosynthesis packs the cells with oxygen and the abundant mucilage sheath secreted around the filament traps gas and never wets. The combined filament + gas + mucilage becomes less dense than water, so the unbranched silk-gleaming threads rise and spread into a floating mat at the sunlit surface — the best-lit, best-CO₂ position for photosynthesis. In moving or nutrient-poor water the mat sinks again, which is why the silk is seasonal.

Universal Facts

  • Marine algae/phytoplankton produce roughly half of the world's oxygen.

Examples

  • Spirogyra — pond silk; conjugation specimen.
  • Chlamydomonas — unicellular motile green alga with an eyespot.
  • Volvox — hollow colony of green cells; coordinated flagellar motion.
  • Sargassum — brown seaweed of the Sargasso Sea.
  • Gelidium — the agar weed.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What are pyrenoids?

    Answer. Proteinaceous bodies embedded in the chloroplast around which starch is synthesised and stored — the algal equivalent of a starch-forming centre. Characteristic of Spirogyra, whose ribbon chloroplasts carry a row of pyrenoids.

  • Short answer (2m): Why are red algae able to live at greater depths?

    Answer. Their pigment r-phycoerythrin absorbs the blue-green light that penetrates deepest into clear ocean water — exactly the wavelengths green chlorophyll cannot use efficiently. By harvesting those deep-penetrating wavelengths, red algae photosynthesise far below the reach of green and brown algae, which is why they dominate the deepest photic zone (and why they look red: the green light is absorbed, the red reflected).

  • Long answer (5m): Describe conjugation in Spirogyra with diagrams.

    Answer. Steps of conjugation (scalariform):

    1. Two parallel filaments lie side by side inside a common mucilage sheath.
    2. Protrusions (papillae) grow out from opposite cells, touching end to end.
    3. The touching walls dissolve, forming a continuous conjugation tube.
    4. The male (donor) protoplast crawls amoeba-like through the tube into the female cell.
    5. The protoplasts fuse — plasmogamy then karyogamy — forming a diploid zygote.
    6. The zygote secretes a three-layered wall and becomes a zygospore, sinking to the bottom to overwinter.
    7. On germination the zygospore undergoes meiosis, producing four haploid nuclei of which three degenerate; the surviving haploid nucleus germinates into a new filament.

    Life-cycle note: the only diploid stage is the zygospore — a haplontic life cycle with zygotic meiosis.

    Diagram: draw two parallel ribbed filaments, papillae meeting, conjugation tubes, one protoplast mid-transfer, and a dark zygospore in the female cell.

Key Points

  • Algae: thalloid, autotrophic, chlorophyll a universal; classified by accessory pigments.
  • Green (Chlorophyceae): chl a+b, starch, motile with equal flagella, freshwater — Spirogyra, Chlamydomonas.
  • Brown (Phaeophyceae): chl a+c + fucoxanthin, mannitol/laminarin, marine — Sargassum, Laminaria.
  • Red (Rhodophyceae): chl a+d + r-phycoerythrin, floridean starch, non-motile, deep marine — Polysiphonia, Gelidium.
  • Economic: agar (Gelidium), carrageenin (Chondrus), algin (Laminaria), iodine (kelp), food (Porphyra, Chlorella), fertiliser, oxygen/food-chain base.
  • Spirogyra: unbranched filament; spiral ribbon chloroplasts with pyrenoids; central vacuole with suspended nucleus; conjugation → zygospore → meiosis (haplontic).

Summary

Algae are thalloid, chlorophyll-bearing aquatic producers classified by accessory pigments into green (Chlorophyceae — chl a+b, starch, freshwater, Spirogyra), brown (Phaeophyceae — fucoxanthin, mannitol/laminarin, marine, Sargassum) and red (Rhodophyceae — r-phycoerythrin, floridean starch, deep marine, Gelidium) algae. They feed humanity through agar, carrageenin, algin, iodine and seaweed foods, and the planet through half its oxygen. Spirogyra, the pond-silk filament with spiral pyrenoid-bearing chloroplasts, reproduces by fragmentation and by scalariform conjugation: the male protoplast crawls through a conjugation tube, fuses into a thick-walled zygospore, and meiosis on germination restores the haploid filament — a haplontic life cycle.

Important Statements

  • "Accessory pigments are depth adapters — each colour class lives at the depth its pigment can light."

Formulas

  • Spirogyra has a spiral chloroplast with pyrenoids. Sexual reproduction is conjugation: a tube joins two filaments and a zygospore results. That is not a seed.
  • Gelidium and related red algae supply agar. Brown algae supply algin and iodine-rich kelp. Green algae share chlorophyll a and b and store starch, which is why they sit nearest the land plants.

  • Pigments decide depth: phycoerythrin harvests deep-penetrating blue-green light, letting red algae live deeper than green or brown.

  • Gelidium — the agar weed.

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): What are pyrenoids? — Proteinaceous bodies embedded in algal chloroplasts around which starch is synthesised and stored; characteristic of Spirogyra.
  • Short answer (2m): Why are red algae able to live at greater depths? — Their r-phycoerythrin absorbs the blue-green light that penetrates deepest water, allowing photosynthesis below the reach of chlorophyll-dominated algae.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe conjugation in Spirogyra with diagrams. — Parallel filaments in common mucilage → opposite cells form protrusions that meet → walls dissolve forming the conjugation tube → male protoplast crawls through → protoplasts fuse → zygospore with three-layered wall → meiosis on germination → one haploid nucleus builds a new filament (haplontic life cycle).
Summary

Algae are thalloid, chlorophyll-bearing aquatic producers classified by accessory pigments into green (Chlorophyceae — chl a+b, starch, freshwater, Spirogyra), brown (Phaeophyceae — fucoxanthin, mannitol/laminarin, marine, Sargassum) and red (Rhodophyceae — r-phycoerythrin, floridean starch, deep marine, Gelidium) algae. They feed humanity through agar, carrageenin, algin, iodine and seaweed foods, and the planet through half its oxygen. Spirogyra, the pond-silk filament with spiral pyrenoid-bearing chloroplasts, reproduces by fragmentation and by scalariform conjugation: the male protoplast crawls through a conjugation tube, fuses into a thick-walled zygospore, and meiosis on germination restores the haploid filament — a haplontic life cycle.
