  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Visible Face of Fungi

    The mushroom is the only fungus most people ever see — the temporary fruit of a vast hidden mycelium. This topic covers the mushroom's structure, how to tell edible from deadly, and the full ledger of what fungi give and take from human life.

  • 2. The Mushroom (Agaricus) — Structure

    2.1 What a mushroom actually is

    The mushroom is a basidiocarp — the fruiting body of a basidiomycete whose true body is the white mycelium ramifying through soil and litter. When food and moisture are right, the mycelium condenses into "buttons" that expand into the familiar structure.

    2.2 Parts of the basidiocarp

    PartDescriptionFunction
    Stipe (stalk)Central cylindrical stem, ringed by the annulusLifts the cap for spore dispersal
    Pileus (cap)Umbrella-like topProtects the spore-bearing surface
    Gills (lamellae)Radiating plates on the cap's undersideBear hymenium with basidia
    AnnulusMembranous ring on the stipe (remnant of the partial veil)Identification character
    VolvaCup at the stipe base (remnant of universal veil — prominent in Amanita)Identification character

    2.3 Spore production

    Each gill's hymenium carries parallel basidia; each basidium forms 4 basidiospores externally on sterigmata. A single mature cap releases billions of spores — most never find a suitable substrate, which is why such enormous numbers are produced. Spores germinate into primary mycelium; compatible strains fuse into the dikaryotic secondary mycelium that later fruits.

    Field caution (real-life rule): no single test is safe — identification needs volva, annulus, gill colour, spore print and expert confirmation. Never eat wild mushrooms on colour alone; folklore tests (silver spoon blackening, animal edibility) are unreliable.

  • Deep dive — the mycelium is the organism. A common exam trap: students call the mushroom 'the fungus'. In fact the mushroom is a temporary spore-producing structure; the permanent organism is the mycelium running through soil or wood. The basidiocarp's job is purely reproductive: pack basidiospores onto gills with maximum surface area and let wind or rain-dispersal do the rest. That is why a fairy ring of mushrooms marks the growing edge of one vast hidden mycelium.

  • 6. Exam Orientation

    6.1 Previous year questions

    No mushroom-specific question exists in the authored bank (fungi characteristics were asked in 2024 — see Topic 7). Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    6.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Describe the structure of a mushroom with a labelled diagram. Answer: The basidiocarp consists of a stipe bearing an annulus (ring) and expanding into the pileus (cap); the cap's underside carries radiating gills whose hymenium bears basidia producing 4 basidiospores each. The mycelium in the soil is the true vegetative body. Label all parts.

    Q2 (3m) — How would you distinguish an edible mushroom from a poisonous one? Answer: Compare volva (absent vs cup-like present), gill/spore colour (pink-brown-dark vs white), cap texture (uniform vs scaled/bright), spore print (dark vs white) and odour; final identification requires expert confirmation because no single character is reliable.

    Q3 (3m) — "Fungi are both friends and foes of man." Justify. Answer: Friends — yeast fermentation (bread, alcohol), penicillin, mushrooms as food, mycorrhizal aid to crops, industrial acids/enzymes. Foes — rusts and smuts destroy crops, Amanita poisons, aflatoxin contaminates grain, mycoses (ringworm, thrush) and wood decay cost money and health. The same absorptive nutrition produces both roles.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling the mushroom "the fungus" — it is only the fruiting body; the mycelium is the organism.
    2. Drawing gills without basidia/basidiospores on the hymenium.
    3. Trusting single-field characters (e.g. "bright mushrooms are poisonous") — Amanita can be plain white.
    4. Listing economic uses without named examples — every point needs an example for full marks.

Confusion

  • Mushroom vs mycelium: the mushroom is the temporary reproductive structure; the perennial mycelium in the substrate is the actual fungal body.
  • Annulus vs volva: annulus = ring on the stipe (from the partial veil); volva = cup at the base (from the universal veil) — the volva is the Amanita warning sign.
  • Poisonous ≠ brightly coloured: Amanita phalloides can be plain white; identification requires multiple characters.

Practice

  • Why does a mushroom cap stand on a stipe instead of lying on the soil?

    Answer. Spore dispersal. Basidiospores are shot horizontally from the gills and must then fall clear of the gill space by gravity — they are too heavy for wind to lift directly from the ground. Elevating the cap on a stipe lifts the gills into moving air: spores drop free, are caught by even slight air currents and carried away. A cap flat on the soil would trap its own spores between gill and ground, and damp soil would rot them. The stipe is a launch tower; the tall, thin design also sheds rain and costs little material.

Universal Facts

  • Mushroom farming converts agricultural waste (straw, manure) into protein.

Examples

  • Agaricus bisporus — the cultivated white mushroom.
  • Morchella — the morel, prized wild edible.
  • Amanita phalloides — death cap; amatoxins; most lethal mushroom.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is a basidiocarp?

    Answer. The fruiting body of a basidiomycete — the mushroom (or bracket, puffball) made of stipe, annulus and pileus — whose gills carry the hymenial surface lined with basidia, each basidium producing four exogenous basidiospores. It is the spore-dispersal structure, not the whole fungus: the mycelium lives hidden in the soil.

Key Points

  • Mushroom = basidiocarp: stipe + annulus + pileus with gills bearing basidia → 4 exogenous basidiospores each; billions of spores per cap.
  • Edible vs poisonous: volva, gill/spore colour (dark vs white), cap texture, spore print, odour — never one character alone.
  • Beneficial fungi: food (Agaricus, Morchella), yeast fermentation, penicillin, organic acids, enzymes, vitamins, mycorrhiza, research (Neurospora).
  • Harmful fungi: rusts/smuts/blights, aflatoxin (Aspergillus flavus), mycoses (Candida, ringworm), Amanita poisoning, wood rot.

Summary

The mushroom is the basidiocarp of a soil mycelium: a stipe with annulus lifting a pileus whose gills carry basidia releasing four exogenous basidiospores each — billions per cap. Edible (Agaricus) and deadly (Amanita) species are separated by volva, gill and spore colour, cap texture and spore print, but only expert identification is safe. Economically fungi are friends (food, fermentation, penicillin, acids, enzymes, mycorrhiza) and foes (crop rusts, smuts and blights, aflatoxin, mycoses, poisoning, wood rot) — one absorptive biology, two human stories.
