Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Two Organisms, One Body

    A lichen is not a single organism: it is a stable symbiotic association between a fungus (mycobiont) and an alga or cyanobacterium (phycobiont) living as one thallus. The fungus builds the body and absorbs water and minerals; the alga photosynthesises and feeds both. Together they colonise places neither could survive alone — bare rocks, tree bark, Arctic soil — making lichens the pioneers of the living world.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts — Structure and Partnership

    2.1 The partners

    PartnerRole
    Mycobiont (fungus — usually an ascomycete, sometimes basidiomycete)Builds the thallus; anchors; absorbs water and minerals; protects the alga from drying and intense light; gives the lichen its name and fruiting bodies
    Phycobiont (green alga — Trebouxia most common; or cyanobacterium — Nostoc)Photosynthesis supplies organic food; cyanobacterial partners also fix atmospheric nitrogen

    2.2 Types of lichen thalli (by growth form)

    TypeFormExample
    CrustoseFlat, crust-like, tightly fused to the substrateGraphis, Lecanora
    FolioseLeaf-like, lobed, loosely attachedParmelia, Peltigera
    FruticoseShrub-like, erect or hanging branchedUsnea (old man's beard), Cladonia (reindeer moss)

    2.3 Internal structure (foliose, e.g. Parmelia)

    Upper cortex (dense fungal hyphae) → algal layer (phycobiont cells in a zone) → medulla (loose hyphae) → lower cortex with rhizines attaching to the substrate.

    2.4 Reproduction

    • Vegetative: fragmentation; specialised soredia (algal cells + few hyphae, powdery) and isidia (outgrowths containing both partners) — both disperse the partnership intact.
    • Asexual: pycnidia produce conidia (fungus only).
    • Sexual: the fungal partner produces ascocarps (apothecia) with ascospores; on germination the new fungus must re-acquire an alga from the environment — showing how essential the partnership is.

    2.5 Why lichens are such extreme survivors

    1. The fungus shields the alga from desiccation — lichens survive near-total drying and resume life when wet.
    2. Lichen acids (e.g. lichenic/usnic acid) deter grazers and weather rock.
    3. Tolerance of temperature extremes — from arctic tundra to hot deserts.
    4. They need only light, air and minimal minerals — hence bare-rock colonisation.

  • Deep dive — mutualism or controlled parasitism? The textbook answer is mutualism, but modern work shows tension: the alga grows faster alone than inside the lichen, so the fungus is partly 'farming' it. For NEB, state mutualism with the division of labour (shelter + minerals vs photosynthate), and add the ecological kicker: because the alga is the photosynthesiser, lichens survive on nothing but light, air and rain — which is exactly why they are so sensitive to air pollution.

    5.1 Previous year questions

    No lichen-specific question exists in the authored bank. Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    5.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — What is lichen? Describe its components and their roles. Answer: Lichen is a symbiotic association of a mycobiont (fungus, usually ascomycete) and a phycobiont (green alga such as Trebouxia, or cyanobacterium such as Nostoc). The fungus builds the thallus, anchors, absorbs water/minerals and shields the alga from desiccation and light; the alga photosynthesises, feeding both partners, and cyanobacterial partners fix nitrogen. Neither partner alone survives the lichen's habitats.

    Q2 (2m) — Name the three growth forms of lichens with one example each. Answer: Crustose — crust-like, flat (Lecanora); foliose — leaf-like lobes (Parmelia); fruticose — shrub-like or hanging (Usnea).

    Q3 (2m) — Why are lichens called pollution indicators? Answer: They absorb air directly and are extremely sensitive to sulphur dioxide; in polluted cities lichens disappear, so their presence/absence and species richness measure air quality.

    Q4 (2m) — How do lichens reproduce vegetatively? Answer: By fragmentation and by specialised dispersal units — soredia (powdery algal cells wrapped in hyphae) and isidia (small outgrowths containing both partners) — which spread the intact symbiosis.

    5.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling lichen a single organism — it is a dual organism; state both partners.
    2. Reversing the roles — the alga feeds; the fungus builds, shelters and absorbs.
    3. Saying lichens are parasitic on trees — most are harmless epiphytes; the association is mutualistic.
    4. Forgetting soredia/isidia in vegetative reproduction answers.

Confusion

  • Symbiosis vs parasitism in lichens: the association is mutualistic (both benefit); the fungus may slightly control the alga, but the classic teaching is mutualism.
  • Soredia vs isidia: soredia are powdery, microscopic, loose; isidia are compact outgrowths of the thallus surface — both carry both partners.
  • Lichen vs moss: lichens are dual fungi-algae; mosses are true bryophyte plants.

Practice

  • Why does a city street have no lichens on its walls while a village wall has three species?

    Answer. Lichens are sulphur-dioxide bioindicators: the fungal partner absorbs SO₂ and dissolved sulphite straight from the air and has no way to shed it, so even modest urban SO₂ (plus vehicle NOₓ and dust) poisons the symbiosis. A village wall gets clean, humid air — the alga photosynthesises, the fungus shelters it, and crustose, foliose and fruticose species can colonise. City walls also add alkaline dust and heat that further favour none. The absence of lichens is therefore a reading of the air itself: clean air, lichens; SO₂, none.

Universal Facts

  • Litmus indicator dye comes from lichens — a laboratory staple since the 17th century.

Examples

  • Usnea longissima — old man's beard; fruticose; medicinal usnic acid.
  • Cladonia rangiferina — reindeer moss; arctic fodder.
  • Roccella montagnei — source of litmus.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What are soredia?

    Answer. Powder-like vegetative propagules of lichens: one or a few algal cells wrapped in fungal hyphae, escaping from a soralium on the thallus surface. Wind carries them off, and each soredium can grow into a new lichen — they disperse the intact symbiosis, both partners together.

Key Points

  • Lichen = mycobiont (fungus, usually ascomycete) + phycobiont (Trebouxia alga or Nostoc cyanobacterium).
  • Growth forms: crustose (Lecanora), foliose (Parmelia), fruticose (Usnea, Cladonia).
  • Structure (foliose): upper cortex — algal layer — medulla — lower cortex with rhizines.
  • Reproduction: fragmentation, soredia, isidia (vegetative); conidia; ascospores via apothecia (sexual, fungus only).
  • Importance: reindeer food, litmus dye (Roccella), usnic-acid medicine, perfumes, air-pollution bioindicators (SO₂ sensitive), pioneer rock weatherers, nitrogen fixation by cyanolichens.

Summary

A lichen is a dual organism: a fungus (usually an ascomycete) that builds the thallus, absorbs water and minerals and shields its partner, and an alga or cyanobacterium (Trebouxia, Nostoc) that photosynthesises — and fixes nitrogen in cyanolichens. Thalli are crustose, foliose or fruticose; reproduction runs from soredia and isidia to fungal ascospores that must re-capture an alga. This partnership survives desiccation and bare rock, weathers stone as the pioneer of succession, yields litmus and usnic acid, feeds reindeer — and dies in SO₂-polluted air, making lichens the classic living air-quality monitors.

Litmus is obtained from the lichen:

  • Parmelia
  • Roccella
  • Cladonia
  • Graphis
Answer

4.

Lichens are excellent air-pollution indicators because they are sensitive to:

Options
  • CO2
  • Sulphur dioxide
  • Oxygen
  • Nitrogen gas
Answer

B

Explanation

SO2 destroys lichen chlorophyll and physiology; species disappear as pollution rises.

The fungal partner of a lichen is usually a(n):

Options
  • Basidiomycete
  • Phycomycete
  • Ascomycete
  • Deuteromycete
Answer

C

Explanation

Most lichen mycobionts are ascomycetes — hence the apothecia with ascospores.

Important Statements

  • "A lichen is a fungus that discovered agriculture."

Formulas

  • Crustose lichens lie flat on rock, foliose ones are leafy, and fruticose ones hang or stand, as Usnea does on temperate branches in Nepal.
  • Lichens colonise bare rock, indicate polluted air because many of them disappear when the air is foul, and some supply dye or fodder. The fungus usually takes the name. The alga makes the sugar.
