Notes

  • Symbiotic association. A lichen is a partnership between a fungus (mycobiont) and a photosynthetic alga or cyanobacterium (phycobiont); the fungus supplies shelter and minerals while the partner supplies food.
  • Thallus types. Lichens are crustose (crust-like, tightly attached), foliose (leafy, loosely attached) and fruticose (shrubby or pendant).
  • Pioneer vegetation. Lichens colonise bare rock and gradually build soil, so they are pioneer species in primary succession.
  • Pollution indicator. Lichens are extremely sensitive to sulphur dioxide and other air pollutants; their abundance signals clean air and their disappearance signals polluted air.
  • Economic importance. Lichens yield litmus and dyes, provide fodder (reindeer moss), and produce antibiotic compounds such as usnic acid.
  • Crustose lichens lie flat on rock, foliose ones are leafy, and fruticose ones hang or stand, as Usnea does on temperate branches in Nepal.

Who makes the sugar in a lichen, and who usually provides the name?

Steps
  • The alga or cyanobacterium photosynthesises.
  • The fungus takes water and minerals and most of the body shape.
  • The scientific name follows the fungus.
  • The partnership is the lichen, not either partner alone.

The alga makes sugar. The fungus provides the name.
