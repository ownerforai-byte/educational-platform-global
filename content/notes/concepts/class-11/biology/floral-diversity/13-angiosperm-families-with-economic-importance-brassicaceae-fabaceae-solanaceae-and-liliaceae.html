  • 1. Topic Introduction — Four Families You Must Know by Heart

    Family identification is applied taxonomy: given a flower, name the family from its floral formula, diagram and diagnostic features. The syllabus names four families — Brassicaceae, Fabaceae, Solanaceae, Liliaceae — chosen because they cover both dicots and a monocot and feed Nepal daily. Learn each by: vegetative habit → leaf → inflorescence → flower (formula + diagram) → fruit → floral characters → economic plants.

    4.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Give the diagnostic characters of Fabaceae with its floral formula and two economic plants. Answer: Herbs/shrubs with pinnately compound stipulate leaves; roots with Rhizobium nodules; flowers zygomorphic, papilionaceous (banner, wings, keel); 10 diadelphous stamens (9+1); monocarpellary superior ovary with marginal placentation; fruit a legume. Floral formula: % ⚥ K(₅) C₁₊₂₊(₂) A(₉)₊₁ G₁. Economic plants: pea (Pisum sativum), gram (Cicer arietinum).

    Q2 (3m) — Write the floral formula of Brassicaceae and explain any three of its diagnostic characters. Answer: ⊕ ⚥ K₂₊₂ C₄ A₂₊₄ G(₂). Diagnostics: (1) cruciform corolla — four petals in a cross; (2) tetradynamous androecium — six stamens, two short outer, four long inner; (3) replum — a false septum in the bicarpellary ovary with parietal placentation, giving the siliqua fruit.

    Q3 (3m) — Why is Liliaceae placed among monocots? Give its floral formula. Answer: Trimerous flowers (parts in threes — 6 tepals as 3+3, 6 stamens as 3+3, tricarpellary gynoecium), parallel-veined linear leaves, and fibrous/underground-storage habit (bulb of onion) — all monocot characters. Floral formula: ⊕ ⚥ P₃₊₃ A₃₊₃ G(₃).

    Q4 (2m) — Which family characters make Solanaceae recognisable in the field? Answer: Persistent 5-lobed calyx on the fruit (tomato crown), 5 epipetalous stamens in a fused corolla, cymose inflorescence, obliquely septum'd bicarpellary ovary with axile placentation, and berry/capsule fruits.

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Writing Brassicaceae stamens as "6 polyadelphous" — tetradynamous (2+4).
    2. Giving Fabaceae axile placentation — it is marginal (monocarpellary).
    3. Calling Liliaceae perianth segments "petals" — they are tepals (undifferentiated).
    4. Forgetting Solanaceae's persistent calyx and epipetalous stamens.
    5. Writing floral formulae without the correct symbols (⊕ actinomorphic, % zygomorphic, ⚥ bisexual, brackets for fusion, G for superior ovary).

Confusion

  • Tepals vs petals: tepals = undifferentiated sepals+petals (Liliaceae, monocots); petals are distinct from sepals in the dicot families.
  • Epipetalous vs epiphyllous: stamens attached to the corolla (Solanaceae) vs attached to tepals (Liliaceae).
  • Diadelphous vs tetradynamous: diadelphous = stamens fused into two bundles (9+1, Fabaceae); tetradynamous = six stamens in two lengths (2+4, Brassicaceae).
  • Siliqua vs legume: siliqua = two-valved fruit with a false septum (replum) between parietal placentas (Brassicaceae); legume = one carpel dehiscing along both sutures (Fabaceae).

Practice

  • Why do legume fields need less nitrogen fertiliser?

    Answer. Because legumes make their own nitrogen. Nodules on their roots house Rhizobium bacteria, which possess nitrogenase — the only enzyme that converts atmospheric N₂ into ammonia the plant can use. The plant feeds the bacteria sugars; the bacteria feed the plant fixed nitrogen, and surplus nitrogen stays in the soil after harvest, enriching it for the next crop (the basis of crop rotation and green manuring). A well-nodulated pea or soybean field can therefore skip most of the synthetic N — the fertiliser factory is already underground.

Universal Facts

  • Mendel chose Pisum (Fabaceae) partly for its clearly distinguishable traits — a family character that shaped genetics.

Examples

  • Brassica campestris — mustard; Nepal's cooking-oil crop.
  • Pisum sativum — pea; Mendel's experimental plant (link to genetics).
  • Solanum tuberosum — potato; underground stem tuber (morphology link).
  • Allium cepa — onion; bulb; classic cytology material (root tips).

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is a floral formula?

    Answer. A symbolic shorthand that summarises a flower in one line: bracteation, symmetry (⊕ actinomorphic / % zygomorphic), sex (⚥ bisexual), then the numbers, fusion and arrangement of K calyx, C corolla, A androecium, G gynoecium — brackets for fusion, underline for superior ovary, overline for inferior.

    Example — Solanaceae: ⊕ ⚥ K₍₅₎ C₍₅₎ A₅ G₍₂₎

  • Long answer (5m): Describe Solanaceae with floral formula, diagram and economic importance.

    Answer. Habit and leaf: mostly herbs and shrubs; alternate, exstipulate, usually simple leaves.

    Inflorescence: cymose (axis ends in a flower, later flowers from lateral branches).

    Flower: actinomorphic, bisexual, hypogynous, pentamerous.

    • Calyx: 5, gamosepalous, persistent (enlarges over the fruit — the tomato's green star).
    • Corolla: 5, gamopetalous, rotate or funnel-form.
    • Androecium: 5 epipetalous stamens (inserted on the corolla tube), alternipetalous, didynamous-looking but equal — actually 5 fertile, included.
    • Gynoecium: bicarpellary, syncarpous, ovary superior, bilocular with an oblique septum, many ovules on swollen axile placentas, basal placentation style terminal.

    Floral formula: ⊕ ⚥ K₍₅₎ C₍₅₎ A₅ G₍₂₎

    Fruit: berry (tomato, brinjal) or capsule (datura, tobacco).

    Economic importance:

    • Food: tomato, potato, brinjal (eggplant), chilli (also spice).
    • Drug/narcotic: tobacco (nicotine).
    • Medicine: Atropa belladonna (atropine), Datura (alkaloids), Withania somnifera (ashwagandha).
    • Ornamental: Petunia, Cestrum.

    Diagram: flower section showing persistent calyx, epipetalous stamens on the corolla tube, and the obliquely septate bilocular ovary.

Key Points

  • Brassicaceae: cruciform 4 petals, tetradynamous 6 stamens, replum + siliqua, parietal placentation — mustard, cabbage, radish.
  • Fabaceae: papilionaceous zygomorphic flower, 10 diadelphous stamens, marginal placentation, legume, Rhizobium nodules — pea, gram, lentil, soybean.
  • Solanaceae: 5 epipetalous stamens, persistent calyx, cymose inflorescence, oblique septum, axile placentation, berry/capsule — tomato, potato, chili, tobacco.
  • Liliaceae: trimerous, 6 tepals, 6 epiphyllous stamens, tricarpellary axile, capsule, underground bulbs/rhizomes — onion, garlic, aloe, colchicum.

Summary

Four families carry the syllabus: Brassicaceae — cruciform petals, tetradynamous stamens (2+4), replum-bearing siliqua (mustard, cabbage); Fabaceae — zygomorphic papilionaceous flowers, diadelphous stamens (9+1), marginal placentation, legume and Rhizobium nodules (pea, gram, lentil); Solanaceae — five epipetalous stamens, persistent calyx, oblique bicarpellary ovary, berries and capsules (tomato, potato, chili); Liliaceae — the monocot with trimerous tepal flowers, epiphyllous stamens and bulbs (onion, garlic, aloe). Each is read through its floral formula and diagram, and each feeds, heals or clothes Nepal — applied taxonomy at its most useful.

Important Statements

  • "A family is a hypothesis of common ancestry expressed in a floral formula."

Formulas

  • Brassicaceae has a cruciform corolla and tetradynamous stamens, four long and two short, and a siliqua. Fabaceae has a papilionaceous flower, diadelphous stamens in a 9+1 bundle, a legume, and Rhizobium nodules.
  • Solanaceae has five stamens joined to the petals and a berry or a capsule, as in tomato and Datura. Liliaceae is the monocot of the four: six tepals, six stamens, and a superior ovary, as in onion. Parallel veins support that monocot placement.

Answer: Herbs/shrubs with pinnately compound stipulate leaves; roots with Rhizobium nodules; flowers zygomorphic, papilionaceous (banner, wings, keel); 10 diadelphous stamens (9+1); monocarpellary superior ovary with marginal placentation; fruit a legume. Floral formula: % ⚥ K(₅) C₁₊₂₊(₂) A(₉)₊₁ G₁. Economic plants: pea (Pisum sativum), gram (Cicer arietinum).

    Q2 (3m) — Write the floral formula of Brassicaceae and explain any three of its diagnostic characters. Answer: ⊕ ⚥ K₂₊₂ C₄ A₂₊₄ G(₂). Diagnostics: (1) cruciform corolla — four petals in a cross; (2) tetradynamous androecium — six stamens, two short outer, four long inner; (3) replum — a false septum in the bicarpellary ovary with parietal placentation, giving the siliqua fruit.

    Q3 (3m) — Why is Liliaceae placed among monocots? Give its floral formula. Answer: Trimerous flowers (parts in threes — 6 tepals as 3+3, 6 stamens as 3+3, tricarpellary gynoecium), parallel-veined linear leaves, and fibrous/underground-storage habit (bulb of onion) — all monocot characters. Floral formula: ⊕ ⚥ P₃₊₃ A₃₊₃ G(₃).

    Q4 (2m) — Which family characters make Solanaceae recognisable in the field? Answer: Persistent 5-lobed calyx on the fruit (tomato crown), 5 epipetalous stamens in a fused corolla, cymose inflorescence, obliquely septum'd bicarpellary ovary with axile placentation, and berry/capsule fruits.

    4.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Writing Brassicaceae stamens as "6 polyadelphous" — tetradynamous (2+4).
    2. Giving Fabaceae axile placentation — it is marginal (monocarpellary).
    3. Calling Liliaceae perianth segments "petals" — they are tepals (undifferentiated).
    4. Forgetting Solanaceae's persistent calyx and epipetalous stamens.
    5. Writing floral formulae without the correct symbols (⊕ actinomorphic, % zygomorphic, ⚥ bisexual, brackets for fusion, G for superior ovary).
Confusion
  • Tepals vs petals: tepals = undifferentiated sepals+petals (Liliaceae, monocots); petals are distinct from sepals in the dicot families.
  • Epipetalous vs epiphyllous: stamens attached to the corolla (Solanaceae) vs attached to tepals (Liliaceae).
  • Diadelphous vs tetradynamous: diadelphous = stamens fused into two bundles (9+1, Fabaceae); tetradynamous = six stamens in two lengths (2+4, Brassicaceae).
  • Siliqua vs legume: siliqua = two-valved fruit with a false septum (replum) between parietal placentas (Brassicaceae); legume = one carpel dehiscing along both sutures (Fabaceae).
Key Points
  • Brassicaceae: cruciform 4 petals, tetradynamous 6 stamens, replum + siliqua, parietal placentation — mustard, cabbage, radish.
  • Fabaceae: papilionaceous zygomorphic flower, 10 diadelphous stamens, marginal placentation, legume, Rhizobium nodules — pea, gram, lentil, soybean.
  • Solanaceae: 5 epipetalous stamens, persistent calyx, cymose inflorescence, oblique septum, axile placentation, berry/capsule — tomato, potato, chili, tobacco.
  • Liliaceae: trimerous, 6 tepals, 6 epiphyllous stamens, tricarpellary axile, capsule, underground bulbs/rhizomes — onion, garlic, aloe, colchicum.
Important Concepts
  • The formula as fingerprint: each family's floral formula encodes its diagnosis — read symmetry, merosity, fusion and carpel number and the family follows.
Examples
  • Brassica campestris — mustard; Nepal's cooking-oil crop.
  • Pisum sativum — pea; Mendel's experimental plant (link to genetics).
  • Solanum tuberosum — potato; underground stem tuber (morphology link).
  • Allium cepa — onion; bulb; classic cytology material (root tips).
Universal Facts
  • Mendel chose Pisum (Fabaceae) partly for its clearly distinguishable traits — a family character that shaped genetics.

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): What is a floral formula? — A symbolic shorthand summarising a flower's bracteation, symmetry, sex, and the number/fusion/arrangement of calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium (e.g. ⊕ ⚥ K(₅) C(₅) A₅ G(₂) for Solanaceae).
  • Long answer (5m): Describe Solanaceae with floral formula, diagram and economic importance. — Habit/leaf → cymose inflorescence → actinomorphic bisexual hypogynous pentamerous flower → persistent calyx (5), gamopetalous corolla (5), 5 epipetalous stamens, bicarpellary syncarpous oblique ovary with axile placentation → berry/capsule. Formula ⊕ ⚥ K(₅) C(₅) A₅ G(₂). Economic: tomato, potato, brinjal, chili, tobacco, datura.
Summary

Four families carry the syllabus: Brassicaceae — cruciform petals, tetradynamous stamens (2+4), replum-bearing siliqua (mustard, cabbage); Fabaceae — zygomorphic papilionaceous flowers, diadelphous stamens (9+1), marginal placentation, legume and Rhizobium nodules (pea, gram, lentil); Solanaceae — five epipetalous stamens, persistent calyx, oblique bicarpellary ovary, berries and capsules (tomato, potato, chili); Liliaceae — the monocot with trimerous tepal flowers, epiphyllous stamens and bulbs (onion, garlic, aloe). Each is read through its floral formula and diagram, and each feeds, heals or clothes Nepal — applied taxonomy at its most useful.
