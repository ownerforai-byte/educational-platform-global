Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Where Nepal's Plants Stand in the World

    This topic answers a simple question with a striking answer: how much of the world's plant life does Nepal hold, and why? Nepal occupies only 0.1% of Earth's land area yet carries a disproportionate share of global plant diversity — the result of its position at the collision of two biogeographic realms and its vertical compression of almost every climate zone on Earth.

    • Why it matters: conservation (Unit 10), vegetation types and every "economic importance" question in this unit depend on knowing what grows here and how it compares globally.
    • Key idea to carry: altitude is Nepal's "latitude" — climbing from the Terai to the summit crosses the equivalent of tropical to polar zones within ~200 km.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts — What "Flora" and "Representation" Mean

    Flora = the plant life of a given region or period. Floristic representation = how a region's plant diversity compares with the world's — measured by species counts, endemism and share of global totals.

    TermMeaning
    Species richnessNumber of species in an area
    Endemic speciesFound only in that region and nowhere else on Earth
    Biodiversity hotspotRegion with exceptional endemism under severe threat
    Phytogeographic realmA large region sharing a characteristic flora (e.g. Indo-Malayan, Palaearctic)
    Vegetation zoneBelt of characteristic plant life, here set mainly by altitude

    Gymnosperms~30+ speciesIncluding Tsuga, Abies, Picea in the high conifer forests

    Algae and fungiThousandsincompletely catalogued

    Endemism: a substantial share of Nepal's flowering plants (~300+ species) are endemic to the Himalaya — products of isolation on young mountains.

    3.1 Why Nepal is so rich — the four reasons (the conceptual core)

    1. Altitudinal range: the country rises from ~60 m (Terai, Kechana Kalan) to 8,848 m (Everest) — a vertical span that packs tropical, subtropical, temperate, subalpine, alpine and nival zones into ~200 km of horizontal distance.
    2. Meeting of two realms: Nepal sits at the junction of the Palaearctic (Holarctic) and Indo-Malayan biogeographic realms, so species of both mingle here.
    3. Monsoon climate: the summer monsoon delivers most of Nepal's precipitation, sustaining dense forests and epiphyte-rich vegetation.
    4. Complex topography and microclimates: deep valleys, rain shadows (Mustang, Manang) and south–north slope contrasts create countless isolated habitats — each a speciation workshop.

    1. Deforestation for agriculture, fuelwood and timber.
    2. Overgrazing and encroachment of grasslands and wetlands.
    3. Invasive alien species (e.g. Chromolaena odorata, Mikania micrantha — "banmara").

  • Deep dive — why representation matters. 'Representation' means Nepal's share of the world's species. Nepal covers about 0.1% of the world's land yet holds a disproportionately large slice of global plant diversity because it compresses tropical, subtropical, temperate, subalpine and alpine zones into 150 km of altitude. That compression is also the vulnerability: every zone is narrow, so a road, a landslide or a warming shift can eliminate an entire band's flora faster than in a continent-spanning country.

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Why is Nepal rich in flora despite its small area? Answer: (1) Extreme altitudinal range (60 m → 8,848 m) packs tropical-to-nival vegetation zones within ~200 km; (2) Nepal lies at the meeting of two biogeographic realms (Palaearctic and Indo-Malayan), so species of both occur here; (3) the monsoon climate sustains dense, moist forests; (4) complex topography creates many microclimates and isolated habitats that promote speciation and endemism.

    Q2 (2m) — What is an endemic species? Why does the Himalaya have many? Answer: An endemic species is one confined to a particular region and found nowhere else. The young Himalaya isolated populations on separate valleys and slopes; millions of years of isolation in distinct microclimates produced many species found only here.

    Q3 (2m) — List the major vegetation zones of Nepal in order with one characteristic plant each. Answer: Subtropical — Sal (Shorea robusta); lower temperate — chir pine (Pinus roxburghii); upper temperate — oak–rhododendron; subalpine — birch (Betula) and fir (Abies); alpine — grasses and sedges; nival — lichens and mosses beyond the snowline.

    Q4 (2m) — Name two invasive plants threatening Nepal's flora and one effect. Answer: Mikania micrantha (banmara) and Chromolaena odorata; they smother native vegetation and tree seedlings, reducing native species diversity in forests and protected areas.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Quoting invented precise species totals — give the accepted ranges ("about 6,000–7,000 flowering plants").
    2. Calling the whole Himalaya a "national park" — the hotspot designation is different from protected-area categories.
    3. Confusing endemic (found only here) with endangered (at risk of extinction) — a species can be one without the other.

Confusion

  • Endemic vs endangered: endemic = geographically restricted to one region; endangered = at high risk of extinction. A species can be endemic and common, or widespread and endangered.
  • Flora vs vegetation: flora is the list of plant species; vegetation is the actual plant cover communities (forest, grassland) on the ground.
  • Hotspot vs protected area: a hotspot is a global scientific designation of high endemism + threat; a protected area is a legal national designation (park, reserve).

Practice

  • Why do Mustang and Manang (rain-shadow districts) have dry vegetation despite being in the monsoon belt?

    Answer. Because the mountains intercept the monsoon before it arrives. Moist air from the south is forced up the Annapurna–Dhaulagiri massif, cools and dumps its rain on the southern slopes; the air descending into Mustang and Manang behind the wall is already dry, and it warms as it sinks, holding its moisture even tighter. The result is a rain shadow: tens of centimetres of rain a year instead of metres, so trans-Himalayan arid scrub and steppe — juniper, seabuckthorn, sparse grasses — replace the lush temperate forest found at the same altitude on the wet southern side. Altitude sets the temperature; the rain shadow sets the water.

Universal Facts

  • Rhododendron arboreum (lali gurans) is Nepal's national flower; rhododendron forests peak between 1,800 and 3,600 m.

Examples

  • Shorea robusta (Sal) — dominant subtropical Terai forest tree, hard timber.
  • Rhododendron arboreum (lali gurans) — Nepal's national flower; upper-temperate forests.
  • Betula utilis (bhojpatra) — subalpine birch whose bark was used as paper.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Define flora.

    Answer. The complete plant life of a given region or period — the species list itself — as distinct from vegetation, which is the actual standing plant cover those species build.

  • Short answer (2m): What is a biodiversity hotspot?

    Answer. A region with exceptionally high endemism that is under severe threat of habitat loss (≥70% habitat already gone). The Himalaya is one of the world's 36 hotspots, which puts Nepal's flora among the planet's conservation priorities.

Key Points

  • Nepal: ~0.1% of world land but ~2–3% of world flowering plants (~6,000–7,000 species); ~500–600 ferns, ~1,100+ bryophytes, ~30+ gymnosperms.
  • Four reasons for richness: altitudinal range (60 m → 8,848 m), two-realm junction (Palaearctic + Indo-Malayan), monsoon climate, complex topography/microclimates.
  • Vegetation zones by altitude: subtropical (Sal) → lower temperate (pine) → upper temperate (oak–rhododendron) → subalpine (birch–fir) → alpine (grasses) → nival (lichens).
  • Himalaya = one of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots; many endemic flowering plants.

Summary

Nepal holds roughly 2–3% of the world's flowering plants on 0.1% of its land — about 7,000 angiosperms, 500–600 ferns, 1,100+ bryophytes and 30+ gymnosperms, many endemic to the Himalaya. The reasons are the country's enormous altitudinal range (60 m to 8,848 m), its position at the junction of the Palaearctic and Indo-Malayan realms, the monsoon climate and complex topography. Vegetation rises in zones — Sal, pine, oak–rhododendron, birch–fir, alpine grasses, nival lichens — and the Himalaya ranks among the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots, making this flora globally significant and conservation urgent.

Formulas
