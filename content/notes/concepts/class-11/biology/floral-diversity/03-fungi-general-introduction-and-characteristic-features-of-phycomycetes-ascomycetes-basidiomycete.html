Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Kingdom of Absorbers

    Fungi are the kingdom of life that eats by digesting outside its body. A mushroom is only the fruiting body; the real fungus is a web of microscopic threads spreading through soil, bread or skin, pouring out enzymes and soaking back the products. Fungi are everywhere — 100,000+ known species, more awaiting description — and they run the world's decomposition.

    • Why it matters: fungi decay dead matter (nutrient cycling), ferment our food, cause our crops' diseases and our skin infections, and gave us the first antibiotic.
    • Connection back: Topic 5's rule — absorptive nutrition + chitin walls = Fungi — is the definition this topic unpacks.

  • 3. The Four Classes of Fungi — Classification by Reproduction

    Traditional classification separates fungi by the type of sexual spore (and mycelium type). Learn each class by: mycelium → asexual spore → sexual spore → examples.

    3.1 Phycomycetes (algal fungi / lower fungi)

    FeatureDetail
    MyceliumCoenocytic (aseptate), multinucleate, branched
    HyphaeUnseptate — looks like algal filaments, hence "algal fungi"
    Asexual sporesSporangiospores (motile zoospores in water forms) inside sporangia
    Sexual reproductionBy gametes/gametangial contact — isogamous, anisogamous or oogamous; thick-walled resting spores (zygospores/oospores)
    HabitatMostly aquatic or very moist places; some obligate parasites
    ExamplesMucor (bread mould), Rhizopus, Albugo (white rust), Phytophthora (late blight of potato), Saprolegnia (water mould)

    3.2 Ascomycetes (sac fungi)

    FeatureDetail
    MyceliumSeptate, branched, multinucleate
    Asexual sporesConidia — naked spores borne on conidiophores (not enclosed)
    Sexual sporeAscospores — produced inside a sac called an ascus (usually 8 ascospores per ascus)
    Fruiting bodyAscocarp — cleistothecium, perithecium or apothecium
    ImportanceYeasts (fermentation), Penicillium (antibiotic), Aspergillus, morels, cup fungi; also powdery mildews
    ExamplesSaccharomyces (yeast), Penicillium, Aspergillus, Neurospora

    3.3 Basidiomycetes (club fungi)

    FeatureDetail
    MyceliumSeptate; long-lived dikaryotic phase (two nuclei per cell)
    Asexual sporesUsually absent (conidia rare)
    Sexual sporeBasidiospores — produced externally on a club-shaped basidium (usually 4 per basidium)
    Fruiting bodyBasidiocarp — the mushroom, bracket or puffball itself
    Clamp connectionsCharacteristic hook-like bridges between cells during dikaryotic growth
    ImportanceMushrooms (food), rusts and smuts (crop disease), Amanita (deadly poison)
    ExamplesAgaricus (mushroom), Puccinia (rust), Ustilago (smut), Amanita

    3.4 Deuteromycetes (fungi imperfecti)

    FeatureDetail
    MyceliumSeptate
    Sexual stageNot known (or lost) — hence "imperfect"
    Asexual sporesConidia on conidiophores, often in pycnidia/acervuli
    RealityMost are actually ascomycetes whose sexual phase was never observed; when found, they are reclassified
    ImportanceAntibiotics (Penicillium), disease (Alternaria, Fusarium wilts), industry (Aspergillus)
    ExamplesPenicillium, Alternaria, Fusarium, Colletotrichum

  • Deep dive — reading the class from the spore. The fastest exam skill in mycology: name the sexual spore and you have named the class. Zygospore → Phycomycetes. Ascospore in an ascus → Ascomycetes. Basidiospore on a basidium → Basidiomycetes. No known sexual stage → Deuteromycetes (imperfect fungi). The sexual structure is the class signature because it is the most conserved feature of the life cycle.

    6.2 Model answer

    Q (2m) — General characteristics of fungi + one beneficial and one harmful example. Answer: Fungi are eukaryotic, mostly multicellular organisms whose body is a mycelium of hyphae; their cells have chitin walls, they have no chlorophyll and feed by absorptive heterotrophism (external digestion, then absorption), they store glycogen, and they reproduce by spores (asexual conidia/sporangiospores and sexual spores). Beneficial: Saccharomyces (yeast) in bread and alcohol fermentation (or Penicillium for penicillin). Harmful: Puccinia causing wheat rust (or Amanita poisoning).

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying fungi are "plants without chlorophyll" — they are a separate kingdom with chitin walls and glycogen.
    2. Mixing up spore locations — ascospores are inside the ascus; basidiospores are outside on the basidium.
    3. Writing "deuteromycetes have no sexual reproduction" — better: the sexual stage is unknown/not observed.
    4. Calling Mucor an ascomycete — it is a phycomycete (coenocytic, sporangiospores).
    5. Confusing mycelium (the whole mass) with hypha (the single filament).

Confusion

  • Ascospore vs basidiospore: ascospores form INSIDE a sac (ascus, usually 8); basidiospores form OUTSIDE on a club (basidium, usually 4). Endogenous vs exogenous is the one-line separator.
  • Hypha vs mycelium: hypha = one filament; mycelium = the entire mass of hyphae.
  • Deuteromycetes are not a natural class: they are a temporary holding group of fungi whose sexual phase is unknown — mostly ascomycetes in reality.
  • Yeast is unicellular but still a fungus: it is an ascomycete; unicellularity does not disqualify it.
  • Coenocytic vs septate: coenocytic = one continuous tube with many nuclei (phycomycetes); septate = divided by cross-walls with pores (ascomycetes, basidiomycetes).

Practice

  • Why can't fungi live without moisture even though they grow on dry-looking bread?

    Answer. Every active stage of a fungus is a wall of water: absorption — its only way of feeding — works by digesting food externally and drawing the dissolved nutrients through the wall by osmosis, which requires a film of free water. Hyphal tip growth, spore germination and spore discharge are all equally water-dependent. What you see on "dry" bread is the fungus mining the bread's own moisture — the bread is not dry inside, and the mould spreads exactly along the damp zones. Only the spores tolerate dryness, and they are survival stages, not growth.

Universal Facts

  • Fleming discovered penicillin from Penicillium in 1928; mass production 1940s.
  • Mycorrhiza ("fungus root") occurs in ~90% of plant families.

Examples

  • Rhizopus stolonifer — black bread mould; phycomycete; sporangiospores.
  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae — baker's/brewer's yeast; ascomycete; budding.
  • Penicillium chrysogenum — penicillin source; conidiophore brush (ascomycete/deuteromycete).
  • Agaricus bisporus — cultivated mushroom; basidiomycete with basidiocarp.
  • Puccinia graminis — wheat rust; basidiomycete; alternating hosts (wheat + barberry).
  • Phytophthora infestans — late blight of potato; caused the Irish famine (1845).

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is a mycelium?

    Answer. The mass of hyphae — branching filaments — that forms the body of a fungus. Each hypha is a tube that grows at its tip; in lower fungi it is one continuous multinucleate tube (coenocytic), in higher fungi it is divided by septa.

  • Short answer (2m): Distinguish ascocarp and basidiocarp.

    Answer. | Basis | Ascocarp | Basidiocarp |

    |---|---|---|

    | Class | Ascomycetes | Basidiomycetes |

    | Spore-bearing organ | Ascus — a sac of (usually 8) endogenous ascospores | Basidium — a club bearing 4 exogenous basidiospores |

    | Example | Cup fungus, morel | Mushroom cap, bracket fungus |

  • Long answer (5m): Classify fungi into four classes with characters and examples.

    Answer. | Class | Mycelium | Asexual spore | Sexual spore | Fruiting body | Examples |

    |---|---|---|---|---|---|

    | Phycomycetes | Coenocytic (aseptate, multinucleate) | Sporangiospores in sporangia | Zygospore (conjugation) or oospore | None specialised | Mucor, Rhizopus, Phytophthora |

    | Ascomycetes | Septate | Conidia (exogenous) | Ascospores inside an ascus (8) | Ascocarp | Yeast, Penicillium, cup fungi |

    | Basidiomycetes | Septate, often dikaryotic (clamp connections) | Conidia rare | Basidiospores exogenous on a basidium (4) | Basidiocarp | Agaricus (mushroom), Puccinia (rust), Ustilago (smut) |

    | Deuteromycetes (imperfect) | Septate | Conidia | Sexual stage unknown/not observed | None | Alternaria, Fusarium, Colletotrichum |

    The classification runs on the sexual spore: zygospore → ascospore → basidiospore → unknown.

Key Points

  • Fungi: chitin wall, absorptive heterotrophs, glycogen reserve, mycelium of hyphae, spore reproduction, chief decomposers.
  • Phycomycetes: coenocytic, sporangiospores, zygospores/oospores — Mucor, Phytophthora.
  • Ascomycetes: septate, conidia, ascospores inside ascus (8), ascocarp — yeast, Penicillium.
  • Basidiomycetes: septate dikaryotic, basidiospores outside basidium (4), basidiocarp, clamp connections — Agaricus, Puccinia, Ustilago.
  • Deuteromycetes: sexual stage unknown, conidia — Penicillium, Alternaria, Fusarium.
  • Economic: yeast (bread/alcohol), Penicillium (penicillin), Agaricus (food), mycorrhiza (agriculture) vs Puccinia/Ustilago (crops), Aspergillus (spoilage), Candida (thrush), Amanita (poison).

Summary

Fungi are the absorptive kingdom: eukaryotic, chitin-walled, chlorophyll-less organisms whose mycelium of hyphae digests food externally and absorbs it, storing glycogen and reproducing by spores. Four classes separate by the sexual spore: phycomycetes (coenocytic, sporangiospores, zygospores — Mucor), ascomycetes (septate, conidia, ascospores inside an 8-spored ascus — yeast, Penicillium), basidiomycetes (dikaryotic, basidiospores outside a 4-spored basidium — Agaricus, rusts, smuts) and deuteromycetes (imperfect fungi with unknown sexual stage — Alternaria, Fusarium). Fungi feed humanity (bread, alcohol, antibiotics, mushrooms, mycorrhiza) and plague it (rusts, smuts, blights, mycoses, aflatoxin) — the same absorptive chemistry cuts both ways.

    6.2 Model answer

    Q (2m) — General characteristics of fungi + one beneficial and one harmful example. Answer: Fungi are eukaryotic, mostly multicellular organisms whose body is a mycelium of hyphae; their cells have chitin walls, they have no chlorophyll and feed by absorptive heterotrophism (external digestion, then absorption), they store glycogen, and they reproduce by spores (asexual conidia/sporangiospores and sexual spores). Beneficial: Saccharomyces (yeast) in bread and alcohol fermentation (or Penicillium for penicillin). Harmful: Puccinia causing wheat rust (or Amanita poisoning).

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying fungi are "plants without chlorophyll" — they are a separate kingdom with chitin walls and glycogen.
    2. Mixing up spore locations — ascospores are inside the ascus; basidiospores are outside on the basidium.
    3. Writing "deuteromycetes have no sexual reproduction" — better: the sexual stage is unknown/not observed.
    4. Calling Mucor an ascomycete — it is a phycomycete (coenocytic, sporangiospores).
    5. Confusing mycelium (the whole mass) with hypha (the single filament).
Confusion
  • Ascospore vs basidiospore: ascospores form INSIDE a sac (ascus, usually 8); basidiospores form OUTSIDE on a club (basidium, usually 4). Endogenous vs exogenous is the one-line separator.
  • Hypha vs mycelium: hypha = one filament; mycelium = the entire mass of hyphae.
  • Deuteromycetes are not a natural class: they are a temporary holding group of fungi whose sexual phase is unknown — mostly ascomycetes in reality.
  • Yeast is unicellular but still a fungus: it is an ascomycete; unicellularity does not disqualify it.
  • Coenocytic vs septate: coenocytic = one continuous tube with many nuclei (phycomycetes); septate = divided by cross-walls with pores (ascomycetes, basidiomycetes).
Key Points
  • Fungi: chitin wall, absorptive heterotrophs, glycogen reserve, mycelium of hyphae, spore reproduction, chief decomposers.
  • Phycomycetes: coenocytic, sporangiospores, zygospores/oospores — Mucor, Phytophthora.
  • Ascomycetes: septate, conidia, ascospores inside ascus (8), ascocarp — yeast, Penicillium.
  • Basidiomycetes: septate dikaryotic, basidiospores outside basidium (4), basidiocarp, clamp connections — Agaricus, Puccinia, Ustilago.
  • Deuteromycetes: sexual stage unknown, conidia — Penicillium, Alternaria, Fusarium.
  • Economic: yeast (bread/alcohol), Penicillium (penicillin), Agaricus (food), mycorrhiza (agriculture) vs Puccinia/Ustilago (crops), Aspergillus (spoilage), Candida (thrush), Amanita (poison).
Important Concepts
  • External digestion: fungi secrete hydrolytic enzymes into the substrate and absorb the digested products — the defining nutritional act of the kingdom.
  • Spore location as the classification key: where the sexual spore is made (inside a sac vs outside a club vs unknown) separates the four classes.
Examples
  • Rhizopus stolonifer — black bread mould; phycomycete; sporangiospores.
  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae — baker's/brewer's yeast; ascomycete; budding.
  • Penicillium chrysogenum — penicillin source; conidiophore brush (ascomycete/deuteromycete).
  • Agaricus bisporus — cultivated mushroom; basidiomycete with basidiocarp.
  • Puccinia graminis — wheat rust; basidiomycete; alternating hosts (wheat + barberry).
  • Phytophthora infestans — late blight of potato; caused the Irish famine (1845).
Universal Facts
  • Fleming discovered penicillin from Penicillium in 1928; mass production 1940s.
  • Mycorrhiza ("fungus root") occurs in ~90% of plant families.
Important Statements

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): What is a mycelium? — The mass of hyphae (filaments) forming the body of a fungus; each hypha is a tube that grows at its tip and, in higher fungi, is divided by septa.
  • Short answer (2m): Distinguish ascocarp and basidiocarp. — Ascocarp = fruiting body of ascomycetes bearing asci with ascospores (e.g. cup fungus); basidiocarp = fruiting body of basidiomycetes bearing basidia with basidiospores (e.g. mushroom cap).
  • Long answer (5m): Classify fungi into four classes with characters and examples. — Build the four-column table: mycelium type, asexual spore, sexual spore, fruiting body, examples — phycomycetes (coenocytic, sporangiospores, zygospore, Mucor), ascomycetes (septate, conidia, ascospore-in-ascus, ascocarp, yeast), basidiomycetes (septate dikaryotic, basidiospore-on-basidium, basidiocarp, Agaricus), deuteromycetes (septate, conidia, sexual unknown, Alternaria).
Summary

Fungi are the absorptive kingdom: eukaryotic, chitin-walled, chlorophyll-less organisms whose mycelium of hyphae digests food externally and absorbs it, storing glycogen and reproducing by spores. Four classes separate by the sexual spore: phycomycetes (coenocytic, sporangiospores, zygospores — Mucor), ascomycetes (septate, conidia, ascospores inside an 8-spored ascus — yeast, Penicillium), basidiomycetes (dikaryotic, basidiospores outside a 4-spored basidium — Agaricus, rusts, smuts) and deuteromycetes (imperfect fungi with unknown sexual stage — Alternaria, Fusarium). Fungi feed humanity (bread, alcohol, antibiotics, mushrooms, mycorrhiza) and plague it (rusts, smuts, blights, mycoses, aflatoxin) — the same absorptive chemistry cuts both ways.

1.

The fungal cell wall is made chiefly of:

Options
  • Cellulose
  • Chitin
  • Pectin
  • Lignin
Answer

B

Explanation

Chitin (fungal cellulose) — a nitrogen-containing polysaccharide — is the wall polymer of fungi, distinct from cellulose in plants.

2.

Ascospores are produced:

Options
  • Outside the basidium
  • Inside the ascus
  • In sporangia
  • Naked on conidiophores
Answer

B

Explanation

Ascospores (usually 8) form inside the sac-like ascus; basidiospores (usually 4) form outside the basidium.

3.

Which fungus class has a coenocytic (aseptate) mycelium?

Options
  • Ascomycetes
  • Basidiomycetes
  • Phycomycetes
  • Deuteromycetes
Answer

C

Explanation

Phycomycetes (lower fungi) have continuous multinucleate hyphae without septa — like Mucor.

4.

Fungi imperfecti is another name for:

Options
  • Phycomycetes
  • Ascomycetes
  • Basidiomycetes
  • Deuteromycetes
Answer

D

Explanation

Their sexual (perfect) stage is unknown, hence 'imperfect'; most are ascomycetes awaiting discovery of their sexual phase.
