  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Classification Is the First Question of Biology

    Biology names and organises life before it explains it. With an estimated 8–9 million species on Earth (about 1.8 million described so far), no science is possible without a system of naming and a system of grouping. This topic gives you both:

    • How life is organised at the highest level — the three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) and the five kingdoms (Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia).
    • How every organism gets its unique scientific name — binomial nomenclature.
    • Why it matters: every later topic in this unit (fungi, algae, bryophytes, angiosperms) assumes you can place the organism in its kingdom and write its name correctly.
    • Connection back: Topic 2's prokaryote/eukaryote split is exactly the line that separates Monera from every other kingdom.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding — What Classification Is

    2.1 Definition and purpose

    Classification (taxonomy) is the arrangement of organisms into groups (taxa) on the basis of their similarities, differences and evolutionary relationships.

    PurposeExplanation
    IdentificationPuts a name on an organism so everyone means the same thing
    OrganisationMillions of species become a searchable hierarchy
    Reveals relationshipsSimilar species share ancestry and features
    Predicts propertiesA newly found mushroom in genus Amanita warns of possible poison
    Universal communicationLatin names work across every language

    2.2 The basis of classification has evolved with knowledge

    EraBasisResult
    AncientUtility (edible, medicinal) and habitatFolk classifications
    Linnaeus (1735)Morphology (visible form)Two kingdoms; sexual system for plants
    Haeckel (1866)Added unicellular organismsThree kingdoms
    Copeland (1956)Added microbes properlyFour kingdoms
    Whittaker (1969)Cell structure, body organisation, nutritionFive kingdoms — the syllabus standard
    Woese (1990)rRNA gene sequencesThree domains — the modern super-kingdom level

    The deep principle: as instruments improved (microscope → electron microscope → DNA sequencing), classification moved from what organisms look like to what organisms are made of to what organisms' genes say.

    2.3 Taxonomic hierarchy — the ladder of ranks

    Kingdom → Division/Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. (Memory aid: "Kings Play Chess On Fine Gold Sets.") Each rank down is more specific; the species is the basic unit — a naturally interbreeding population reproductively isolated from others.

  • 3. Binomial Nomenclature — The Naming System

    3.1 What it is

    Binomial nomenclature is the two-word scientific naming system in which every organism receives a name of Genus + specific epithet, e.g. Mangifera indica (mango), Homo sapiens (human). It was introduced by Carolus Linnaeus in his work Species Plantarum (1753) — he is called the Father of Taxonomy.

    3.2 The rules (examiners check these exactly)

    1. Each organism has only one valid scientific name, in Latin or Latinised form.
    2. The first word = Genus (capitalised); the second word = specific epithet (lowercase).
    3. Both words are written in italics when printed (or underlined separately when handwritten).
    4. The name is often followed by the abbreviated name of the author who first described it — Mangifera indica Linn.
    5. Names must be unique — no two species may share the same binomial.
    6. The specific epithet alone means nothing — "indica" appears in many genera.

    Why not common names? Common names vary by language and region (the same plant may have a dozen Nepali names), and one common name may cover several species. Mangifera indica means exactly one organism, everywhere on Earth, forever.

    3.3 Key scientists of taxonomy

    ScientistContribution
    Carolus Linnaeus (1707–78)Binomial nomenclature; Species Plantarum (1753); two-kingdom system
    Ernst Haeckel (1866)Third kingdom (Protista)
    Robert Whittaker (1969)Five-kingdom system
    Carl Woese (1990)Three-domain system based on 16S/18S rRNA
  • Deep dive — why two names? A single local name can mean different plants in different districts; a single plant can carry many local names. Binomial nomenclature (Linnaeus, Species Plantarum, 1753) fixes this with one Latinised name of two words: the generic name (noun, capitalised) and the specific epithet (usually an adjective, lowercase), e.g. Mangifera indica. Both words are printed in italics (underlined when handwritten) and the author's abbreviated name may follow: Mangifera indica Linn. The name is governed by the International Code of Nomenclature (ICN), so the same organism carries the same name worldwide.

  • 4. The Three-Domain System (Woese, 1990)

    Based on differences in ribosomal RNA (16S/18S rRNA) sequences, Woese split all life into three domains — a level above kingdom:

    DomainMembersCell typeDefining features
    Bacteria (Eubacteria)True bacteria, cyanobacteriaProkaryotePeptidoglycan cell walls; membrane lipids with ester-linked fatty acids; sensitive to common antibiotics
    Archaea (Archaebacteria)Methanogens, halophiles, thermoacidophilesProkaryoteNo peptidoglycan; ether-linked branched membrane lipids; live in extreme habitats (hot springs, salt lakes, anaerobic guts); antibiotic-resistant
    EukaryaProtists, fungi, plants, animalsEukaryoteTrue nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, histone-bound linear chromosomes

    Conceptual point: Archaea look like bacteria under a microscope but their molecular machinery (rRNA, membrane lipids, RNA polymerase) is as different from Bacteria as both are from Eukarya — which is why genes, not appearance, decide the deepest splits.

  • 5. The Five-Kingdom System (R. H. Whittaker, 1969)

    5.1 The three criteria Whittaker used

    1. Cell structure — prokaryotic or eukaryotic.
    2. Level of body organisation — unicellular or multicellular; tissue/organ level.
    3. Mode of nutrition — autotrophic (photosynthesis), absorptive (absorb dissolved food), or ingestive (eat other organisms).

    Nutrition is the decisive separator of the four eukaryotic kingdoms: photosynthesis → Plantae, absorption → Fungi, ingestion → Animalia, mixed modes → Protista.

    5.2 The five kingdoms at a glance

    KingdomCell typeOrganisationNutritionKey members
    MoneraProkaryoticUnicellular, no organellesAutotrophic or heterotrophicBacteria, cyanobacteria, mycoplasma
    ProtistaEukaryoticMostly unicellularAutotrophic (algae-like) or heterotrophic (protozoa)Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium, Euglena, diatoms
    FungiEukaryoticMulticellular (except yeasts), filamentous mycelium of hyphaeAbsorptive heterotrophs — external digestion; cell wall of chitinMucor, yeast, mushroom, rusts
    PlantaeEukaryoticMulticellular, tissue/organ levelAutotrophic (photosynthesis); cellulose wallAlgae, bryophytes, ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms
    AnimaliaEukaryoticMulticellular, tissue→organ→organ-systemIngestive heterotrophs; no cell wallSponges to mammals

    5.3 Merits of the five-kingdom system

    1. Separated prokaryotes (Monera) from all eukaryotes — the single most fundamental biological divide.
    2. Moved fungi out of plants — their chitin wall, absorptive nutrition and glycogen reserve make them unlike plants.
    3. Created a separate Protista for simple eukaryotes that fit nowhere else.
    4. Used phylogenetic (evolutionary) direction: Monera → Protista → Fungi/Plantae/Animalia.
    5. Cell structure and nutrition are objective, measurable criteria.

    5.4 Demerits

    1. Monera and Protista are still "dumping-ground" kingdoms of unrelated groups.
    2. Some organisms straddle boundaries — Euglena (photosynthesises but ingests too), slime moulds, viruses (excluded entirely).
    3. Does not use molecular (rRNA) data — superseded at the top level by the three-domain system.
    4. Algae are split between Protista and Plantae, which is awkward.

    5.5 Comparison: two-kingdom vs five-kingdom

    7.2 Model answers

    Q1 (2m) — Binomial nomenclature. Answer: Carolus Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature (in Species Plantarum, 1753). The scientific name of mango is Mangifera indica Linn. — written in italics with the generic name capitalised and the specific epithet in lowercase.

    Q2/Q3 (5m) — Classify the five kingdoms with one feature each. Answer: R. H. Whittaker (1969) proposed the five-kingdom system on the basis of cell structure, body organisation and mode of nutrition.

    1. Monera — prokaryotic, unicellular, no membrane-bound organelles; e.g. bacteria, cyanobacteria.
    2. Protista — eukaryotic, mostly unicellular; autotrophic or heterotrophic; e.g. Amoeba, Euglena.
    3. Fungi — eukaryotic, multicellular mycelium of hyphae with chitin walls; absorptive heterotrophs; e.g. Mucor, mushroom.
    4. Plantae — eukaryotic, multicellular; autotrophic by photosynthesis; cellulose walls; e.g. mosses, ferns, mango.
    5. Animalia — eukaryotic, multicellular, no cell wall; ingestive heterotrophs; e.g. earthworm, frog, human.

    (Award structure: proposer + criteria + five kingdoms each with one clear feature + example.)

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Writing the scientific name without italics/capitalisation — Mangifera indica, never "mangifera Indica".
    2. Giving "cell wall" as the plant/fungi separator — fungi also have walls; the separator is nutrition (absorptive) and wall chemistry (chitin, not cellulose).
    3. Listing four criteria of Whittaker or inventing a sixth kingdom — stick to his three criteria.
    4. Placing cyanobacteria in Protista — they are Monera (prokaryotes).
    5. Forgetting that the three-domain system sits above the five kingdoms, not instead of the syllabus version.

Confusion

  • Domain vs kingdom: domains are a newer, higher rank above kingdoms; the five kingdoms are still taught, but Monera is split into Bacteria and Archaea at the domain level.
  • Fungi are not plants: chitin (not cellulose) walls, absorptive (not photosynthetic) nutrition, glycogen (not starch) reserve — Whittaker's main correction to the two-kingdom system.
  • Cyanobacteria are Monera, not algae: "blue-green algae" is a historical name; they are prokaryotic photosynthesizers, so they belong to Bacteria, not Protista or Plantae.
  • Species epithet alone is not a name: "indica" names nothing by itself; only the full binomial (Mangifera indica) identifies a species.
  • Protista vs Monera: both mostly unicellular, but Protista are eukaryotic (true nucleus, organelles) while Monera are prokaryotic.

Practice

  • Why did Whittaker need three criteria while Linnaeus needed only one?

    Answer. Linnaeus classified a world that knew only plants and animals, and a single visible criterion — overall form — was enough to sort them. Whittaker faced microbes: bacteria, fungi and single-celled eukaryotes look alike in size and simplicity, yet differ fundamentally in cell type and how they feed. Morphology alone would lump a photosynthetic alga with an amoeba. Only the trio of cell structure + organisation + nutrition separates prokaryote from eukaryote, absorber from photosynthesiser — the minimum set that makes the kingdom boundaries objective rather than cosmetic.

  • Why is Euglena a problem for the five-kingdom system?

    Answer. Euglena straddles the kingdom boundaries: it has chloroplasts and photosynthesises (a plant character) but no cell wall, swims with a flagellum and can ingest food in the dark (animal characters). Whittaker's criteria disagree with each other — its nutrition says Plantae, its organisation and locomotion say Protista. It is placed in Protista as a compromise, but it shows that sharp kingdom boundaries drawn from a few criteria will always have organisms sitting on the fence — one reason the system needed molecular revision.

  • A newly discovered microbe lives in boiling acid, has no peptidoglycan and ether-linked membrane lipids. Where does it go — and why not in Bacteria?

    Answer. Domain Archaea — specifically a thermo-acidophilic archaean (like Sulfolobus).

    Why not Bacteria: the two domains are distinguished at the molecular level, and every character here is archaeal —

    1. no peptidoglycan in the wall (all true bacteria build peptidoglycan);
    2. ether-linked, branched membrane lipids (bacteria use ester-linked fatty acids);
    3. growth at boiling acid — an archaeal extremophile speciality that no bacterium tolerates.

    Despite looking prokaryotic, its rRNA would also place it far from Bacteria — closer to Eukarya than to any bacterium.

  • Whittaker's system: 1969; Woese's three domains: 1990 (16S rRNA).

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is binomial nomenclature? Who introduced it?

    Answer. The system of giving every species a two-word scientific name — the Genus (capitalised) plus the specific epithet (lowercase), written in Latin and italicised (underlined when handwritten) — so that one universal, unambiguous name exists for each species. Introduced by Carolus Linnaeus in Species Plantarum (1753).

  • Short answer (2m): Write the scientific name of mango and human with the rules obeyed.

    Answer. ***Mangifera indica Linn. (mango) and Homo sapiens Linn.* (human).

    Rules obeyed: generic name capitalised, specific epithet in lowercase, both italicised in print (underlined separately when handwritten), and the author abbreviation (Linn. = Linnaeus) placed after the name.

  • Short answer (2m): On what three criteria is the five-kingdom system based?

    Answer. 1. Cell structure — prokaryotic or eukaryotic.

    1. Level of body organisation — unicellular, colonial or multicellular.
    2. Mode of nutrition — photosynthetic (autotrophic), absorptive or ingestive.
  • Short answer (2m): Why were fungi removed from Plantae?

    Answer. Three plant-unlike features:

    1. cell walls of chitin, not cellulose;
    2. absorptive heterotrophy — they digest food externally and absorb it, instead of photosynthesising;
    3. reserve food is glycogen, not starch.
  • Long answer (5m): Describe the five-kingdom classification with merits and demerits.

    Answer. Proposed by R. H. Whittaker (1969) on three criteria — cell structure, body organisation and mode of nutrition.

    KingdomCell typeOrganisationNutritionExamples
    MoneraProkaryoticUnicellularAutotrophic or heterotrophicBacteria, cyanobacteria
    ProtistaEukaryoticMostly unicellularAutotrophic or heterotrophicAmoeba, Paramecium, Euglena, diatoms
    FungiEukaryoticMulticellular/loose tissueAbsorptive heterotroph (chitin wall)Mucor, mushroom, yeast
    PlantaeEukaryoticMulticellular, tissuesPhotosynthetic autotrophMoss, fern, mango
    AnimaliaEukaryoticMulticellular, organ-systemsIngestive heterotrophSponge to man

    Merits:

    1. separated prokaryotes into their own kingdom;
    2. removed fungi from plants on sound evidence;
    3. broadly phylogenetic — arranged from simple to complex;
    4. used objective criteria (cell structure + nutrition), not just looks.

    Demerits:

    1. Monera and Protista are mixed bags — autotrophs and heterotrophs together;
    2. boundary organisms are awkward — Euglena (plant + animal characters), slime moulds, viruses not placed at all;
    3. based on visible characters, not molecular (rRNA) data — superseded by the three-domain system.
  • Long answer (5m): Explain the three-domain system and its relationship to the five-kingdom system.

    Answer. Proposed by Carl Woese (1990) from 16S/18S rRNA gene sequences — the most conserved molecules in life, so they record deep ancestry.

    DomainKey characters
    BacteriaProkaryotes with peptidoglycan walls and ester-linked membrane lipids (true bacteria, cyanobacteria)
    ArchaeaProkaryotes with no peptidoglycan, ether-linked branched lipids; many extremophiles (thermophiles, halophiles, methanogens)
    EukaryaTrue nucleus and membrane-bound organelles

    Relationship to the five kingdoms: the old kingdom Monera is split — its members divide into the two domains Bacteria and Archaea, which rRNA shows are as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes. The four eukaryotic kingdoms (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) all sit inside the single domain Eukarya. So the three-domain system is a higher, molecularly based layer above the five-kingdom scheme.

  • Conceptual (3m): Why is the species called the basic unit of classification?

    Answer. Because it is the only rank that corresponds to a real biological entity: a naturally interbreeding, reproductively isolated population sharing one gene pool. Every higher rank (genus, family, order) is a grouping of convenience built by humans from similarities; the species is where evolution actually happens — where variation arises and selection acts — and it is the unit that binomial nomenclature names.

  • Conceptual (3m): Why do scientific names remain stable while common names do not?

    Answer. Binomials are governed by international codes (ICBN/ICZN) with fixed rules — Latin (language-neutral), unique worldwide, published with a description and tied to a type specimen, and changed only by formal procedure. Common names drift with language and region: the same plant may carry a dozen local names, and one name may cover several different species — so only the binomial guarantees that scientists in Nepal, Brazil and Japan are discussing the same organism.

  • Application (2m): A farmer reports a new crop disease organism that has no nucleus and no peptidoglycan but lives in a hot spring. Predict its domain and one property.

    Answer. Domain: Archaea — prokaryotic (no nucleus) but lacking peptidoglycan rules out Bacteria, and a hot-spring habitat fits the archaeal extremophiles.

    One predictable property: thermophily — enzymes and ether-linked membrane lipids stable near boiling point; (also acceptable: antibiotic resistance, methanogenesis or halophily in related archaea).

Key Points

  • Classification = arranging organisms into taxa by similarities and evolutionary relationships; purposes: identification, organisation, relationship, prediction, universal naming.
  • Basis evolved: morphology (Linnaeus) → cell structure/nutrition (Whittaker) → rRNA genes (Woese).
  • Hierarchy: Kingdom → Division/Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species ("KPCOFGS"); species = basic unit.
  • Binomial nomenclature — Linnaeus, Species Plantarum 1753: Genus (capital) + specific epithet (lower), italicised/underlined, Latin, unique, with author abbreviation.
  • Three domains (Woese 1990, rRNA-based): Bacteria (peptidoglycan), Archaea (no peptidoglycan, extremophiles), Eukarya (true nucleus).
  • Five kingdoms (Whittaker 1969) on cell structure + organisation + nutrition: Monera (prokaryote), Protista (unicellular eukaryote), Fungi (absorptive, chitin), Plantae (photosynthetic), Animalia (ingestive).
  • Merits: separated prokaryotes, removed fungi from plants, phylogenetic direction; demerits: Protista/Monera are mixed groups, boundary organisms (Euglena, viruses) awkward.

Summary

Classification organises millions of species into a hierarchy (KPCOFGS) whose basic unit is the species. Linnaeus gave biology its naming system — binomial nomenclature (Genus + specific epithet, italicised, e.g. Mangifera indica) — and its first two-kingdom scheme. Whittaker (1969) rebuilt classification on cell structure, body organisation and nutrition into five kingdoms: prokaryotic Monera, unicellular eukaryotic Protista, absorptive chitin-walled Fungi, photosynthetic Plantae and ingestive Animalia. Woese (1990), reading rRNA genes, raised the deepest split to three domains — Bacteria, Archaea (no peptidoglycan, extremophiles) and Eukarya — with Monera split between the first two. Classification has therefore moved from appearance to chemistry to genes, and every later topic in this unit depends on placing organisms correctly in these kingdoms.

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Classification Is the First Question of Biology

    Biology names and organises life before it explains it. With an estimated 8–9 million species on Earth (about 1.8 million described so far), no science is possible without a system of naming and a system of grouping. This topic gives you both:

    • How life is organised at the highest level — the three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) and the five kingdoms (Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia).
    • How every organism gets its unique scientific name — binomial nomenclature.
    • Why it matters: every later topic in this unit (fungi, algae, bryophytes, angiosperms) assumes you can place the organism in its kingdom and write its name correctly.
    • Connection back: Topic 2's prokaryote/eukaryote split is exactly the line that separates Monera from every other kingdom.

  • 2. Fundamental Conceptual Understanding — What Classification Is

    2.1 Definition and purpose

    Classification (taxonomy) is the arrangement of organisms into groups (taxa) on the basis of their similarities, differences and evolutionary relationships.

    PurposeExplanation
    IdentificationPuts a name on an organism so everyone means the same thing
    OrganisationMillions of species become a searchable hierarchy
    Reveals relationshipsSimilar species share ancestry and features
    Predicts propertiesA newly found mushroom in genus Amanita warns of possible poison
    Universal communicationLatin names work across every language

    2.2 The basis of classification has evolved with knowledge

    EraBasisResult
    AncientUtility (edible, medicinal) and habitatFolk classifications
    Linnaeus (1735)Morphology (visible form)Two kingdoms; sexual system for plants
    Haeckel (1866)Added unicellular organismsThree kingdoms
    Copeland (1956)Added microbes properlyFour kingdoms
    Whittaker (1969)Cell structure, body organisation, nutritionFive kingdoms — the syllabus standard
    Woese (1990)rRNA gene sequencesThree domains — the modern super-kingdom level

    The deep principle: as instruments improved (microscope → electron microscope → DNA sequencing), classification moved from what organisms look like to what organisms are made of to what organisms' genes say.

    2.3 Taxonomic hierarchy — the ladder of ranks

    Kingdom → Division/Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. (Memory aid: "Kings Play Chess On Fine Gold Sets.") Each rank down is more specific; the species is the basic unit — a naturally interbreeding population reproductively isolated from others.

  • 3. Binomial Nomenclature — The Naming System

    3.1 What it is

    Binomial nomenclature is the two-word scientific naming system in which every organism receives a name of Genus + specific epithet, e.g. Mangifera indica (mango), Homo sapiens (human). It was introduced by Carolus Linnaeus in his work Species Plantarum (1753) — he is called the Father of Taxonomy.

    3.2 The rules (examiners check these exactly)

    1. Each organism has only one valid scientific name, in Latin or Latinised form.
    2. The first word = Genus (capitalised); the second word = specific epithet (lowercase).
    3. Both words are written in italics when printed (or underlined separately when handwritten).
    4. The name is often followed by the abbreviated name of the author who first described it — Mangifera indica Linn.
    5. Names must be unique — no two species may share the same binomial.
    6. The specific epithet alone means nothing — "indica" appears in many genera.

    Why not common names? Common names vary by language and region (the same plant may have a dozen Nepali names), and one common name may cover several species. Mangifera indica means exactly one organism, everywhere on Earth, forever.

    3.3 Key scientists of taxonomy

    ScientistContribution
    Carolus Linnaeus (1707–78)Binomial nomenclature; Species Plantarum (1753); two-kingdom system
    Ernst Haeckel (1866)Third kingdom (Protista)
    Robert Whittaker (1969)Five-kingdom system
    Carl Woese (1990)Three-domain system based on 16S/18S rRNA
  • Deep dive — why two names? A single local name can mean different plants in different districts; a single plant can carry many local names. Binomial nomenclature (Linnaeus, Species Plantarum, 1753) fixes this with one Latinised name of two words: the generic name (noun, capitalised) and the specific epithet (usually an adjective, lowercase), e.g. Mangifera indica. Both words are printed in italics (underlined when handwritten) and the author's abbreviated name may follow: Mangifera indica Linn. The name is governed by the International Code of Nomenclature (ICN), so the same organism carries the same name worldwide.

  • 4. The Three-Domain System (Woese, 1990)

    Based on differences in ribosomal RNA (16S/18S rRNA) sequences, Woese split all life into three domains — a level above kingdom:

    DomainMembersCell typeDefining features
    Bacteria (Eubacteria)True bacteria, cyanobacteriaProkaryotePeptidoglycan cell walls; membrane lipids with ester-linked fatty acids; sensitive to common antibiotics
    Archaea (Archaebacteria)Methanogens, halophiles, thermoacidophilesProkaryoteNo peptidoglycan; ether-linked branched membrane lipids; live in extreme habitats (hot springs, salt lakes, anaerobic guts); antibiotic-resistant
    EukaryaProtists, fungi, plants, animalsEukaryoteTrue nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, histone-bound linear chromosomes

    Conceptual point: Archaea look like bacteria under a microscope but their molecular machinery (rRNA, membrane lipids, RNA polymerase) is as different from Bacteria as both are from Eukarya — which is why genes, not appearance, decide the deepest splits.

    4.1 Three domains vs five kingdoms — how they relate

    Five kingdoms (Whittaker)Three domains (Woese)
    MoneraSplit into Bacteria and Archaea
    Protista, Fungi, Plantae, AnimaliaTogether = Eukarya

  • 5. The Five-Kingdom System (R. H. Whittaker, 1969)

    5.1 The three criteria Whittaker used

    1. Cell structure — prokaryotic or eukaryotic.
    2. Level of body organisation — unicellular or multicellular; tissue/organ level.
    3. Mode of nutrition — autotrophic (photosynthesis), absorptive (absorb dissolved food), or ingestive (eat other organisms).

    Nutrition is the decisive separator of the four eukaryotic kingdoms: photosynthesis → Plantae, absorption → Fungi, ingestion → Animalia, mixed modes → Protista.

    5.2 The five kingdoms at a glance

    KingdomCell typeOrganisationNutritionKey members
    MoneraProkaryoticUnicellular, no organellesAutotrophic or heterotrophicBacteria, cyanobacteria, mycoplasma
    ProtistaEukaryoticMostly unicellularAutotrophic (algae-like) or heterotrophic (protozoa)Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium, Euglena, diatoms
    FungiEukaryoticMulticellular (except yeasts), filamentous mycelium of hyphaeAbsorptive heterotrophs — external digestion; cell wall of chitinMucor, yeast, mushroom, rusts
    PlantaeEukaryoticMulticellular, tissue/organ levelAutotrophic (photosynthesis); cellulose wallAlgae, bryophytes, ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms
    AnimaliaEukaryoticMulticellular, tissue→organ→organ-systemIngestive heterotrophs; no cell wallSponges to mammals

    5.3 Merits of the five-kingdom system

    1. Separated prokaryotes (Monera) from all eukaryotes — the single most fundamental biological divide.
    2. Moved fungi out of plants — their chitin wall, absorptive nutrition and glycogen reserve make them unlike plants.
    3. Created a separate Protista for simple eukaryotes that fit nowhere else.
    4. Used phylogenetic (evolutionary) direction: Monera → Protista → Fungi/Plantae/Animalia.
    5. Cell structure and nutrition are objective, measurable criteria.

    5.4 Demerits

    1. Monera and Protista are still "dumping-ground" kingdoms of unrelated groups.
    2. Some organisms straddle boundaries — Euglena (photosynthesises but ingests too), slime moulds, viruses (excluded entirely).
    3. Does not use molecular (rRNA) data — superseded at the top level by the three-domain system.
    4. Algae are split between Protista and Plantae, which is awkward.

    5.5 Comparison: two-kingdom vs five-kingdom

    Unicellular eukaryotesForced into plants or animalsKingdom Protista

    7.2 Model answers

    Q1 (2m) — Binomial nomenclature. Answer: Carolus Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature (in Species Plantarum, 1753). The scientific name of mango is Mangifera indica Linn. — written in italics with the generic name capitalised and the specific epithet in lowercase.

    Q2/Q3 (5m) — Classify the five kingdoms with one feature each. Answer: R. H. Whittaker (1969) proposed the five-kingdom system on the basis of cell structure, body organisation and mode of nutrition.

    1. Monera — prokaryotic, unicellular, no membrane-bound organelles; e.g. bacteria, cyanobacteria.
    2. Protista — eukaryotic, mostly unicellular; autotrophic or heterotrophic; e.g. Amoeba, Euglena.
    3. Fungi — eukaryotic, multicellular mycelium of hyphae with chitin walls; absorptive heterotrophs; e.g. Mucor, mushroom.
    4. Plantae — eukaryotic, multicellular; autotrophic by photosynthesis; cellulose walls; e.g. mosses, ferns, mango.
    5. Animalia — eukaryotic, multicellular, no cell wall; ingestive heterotrophs; e.g. earthworm, frog, human.

    (Award structure: proposer + criteria + five kingdoms each with one clear feature + example.)

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Writing the scientific name without italics/capitalisation — Mangifera indica, never "mangifera Indica".
    2. Giving "cell wall" as the plant/fungi separator — fungi also have walls; the separator is nutrition (absorptive) and wall chemistry (chitin, not cellulose).
    3. Listing four criteria of Whittaker or inventing a sixth kingdom — stick to his three criteria.
    4. Placing cyanobacteria in Protista — they are Monera (prokaryotes).
    5. Forgetting that the three-domain system sits above the five kingdoms, not instead of the syllabus version.
Confusion
  • Domain vs kingdom: domains are a newer, higher rank above kingdoms; the five kingdoms are still taught, but Monera is split into Bacteria and Archaea at the domain level.
  • Fungi are not plants: chitin (not cellulose) walls, absorptive (not photosynthetic) nutrition, glycogen (not starch) reserve — Whittaker's main correction to the two-kingdom system.
  • Cyanobacteria are Monera, not algae: "blue-green algae" is a historical name; they are prokaryotic photosynthesizers, so they belong to Bacteria, not Protista or Plantae.
  • Species epithet alone is not a name: "indica" names nothing by itself; only the full binomial (Mangifera indica) identifies a species.
  • Protista vs Monera: both mostly unicellular, but Protista are eukaryotic (true nucleus, organelles) while Monera are prokaryotic.
Key Points
  • Classification = arranging organisms into taxa by similarities and evolutionary relationships; purposes: identification, organisation, relationship, prediction, universal naming.
  • Basis evolved: morphology (Linnaeus) → cell structure/nutrition (Whittaker) → rRNA genes (Woese).
  • Hierarchy: Kingdom → Division/Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species ("KPCOFGS"); species = basic unit.
  • Binomial nomenclature — Linnaeus, Species Plantarum 1753: Genus (capital) + specific epithet (lower), italicised/underlined, Latin, unique, with author abbreviation.
  • Three domains (Woese 1990, rRNA-based): Bacteria (peptidoglycan), Archaea (no peptidoglycan, extremophiles), Eukarya (true nucleus).
  • Five kingdoms (Whittaker 1969) on cell structure + organisation + nutrition: Monera (prokaryote), Protista (unicellular eukaryote), Fungi (absorptive, chitin), Plantae (photosynthetic), Animalia (ingestive).
  • Merits: separated prokaryotes, removed fungi from plants, phylogenetic direction; demerits: Protista/Monera are mixed groups, boundary organisms (Euglena, viruses) awkward.
Important Concepts
  • Nutrition as the kingdom separator — photosynthesis → Plantae; absorption → Fungi; ingestion → Animalia; mixed → Protista.
  • Molecular data outrank appearance — Archaea look bacterial but rRNA proves a separate domain.

Summary

Classification organises millions of species into a hierarchy (KPCOFGS) whose basic unit is the species. Linnaeus gave biology its naming system — binomial nomenclature (Genus + specific epithet, italicised, e.g. Mangifera indica) — and its first two-kingdom scheme. Whittaker (1969) rebuilt classification on cell structure, body organisation and nutrition into five kingdoms: prokaryotic Monera, unicellular eukaryotic Protista, absorptive chitin-walled Fungi, photosynthetic Plantae and ingestive Animalia. Woese (1990), reading rRNA genes, raised the deepest split to three domains — Bacteria, Archaea (no peptidoglycan, extremophiles) and Eukarya — with Monera split between the first two. Classification has therefore moved from appearance to chemistry to genes, and every later topic in this unit depends on placing organisms correctly in these kingdoms.
