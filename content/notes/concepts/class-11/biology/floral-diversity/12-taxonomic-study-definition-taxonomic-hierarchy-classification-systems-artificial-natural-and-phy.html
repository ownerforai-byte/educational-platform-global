  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Science of Naming and Classifying Flowering Plants

    Topic 5 gave the general principles of classification; this topic applies them to angiosperms: the taxonomic hierarchy in action, the three historical classification systems (artificial, natural, phylogenetic), and the tools taxonomists use — herbarium, botanical garden, keys, floras and monographs.

  • 3. The Three Classification Systems of Angiosperms

    3.1 Artificial classification

    AspectDetail
    BasisOne or few superficial, arbitrary characters — habit, leaf form, or (Linnaeus) number of stamens
    ExampleLinnaeus's sexual system (1735) — 24 classes by stamen number/arrangement
    MeritsFast, easy, practical for identification
    DemeritsGroups unrelated plants (a cactus and a pea could share stamen counts); ignores evolution and overall affinity

    3.2 Natural classification

    AspectDetail
    BasisOverall resemblance — all important characters: morphology, anatomy, embryology, chemistry
    ExamplesBentham and Hooker's system (1862–83) — still used in South Asian herbaria and textbooks; de Candolle's earlier natural system
    MeritsGroups reflect real affinity; practical keys; stable families (Bentham & Hooker's family circumscriptions persist)
    DemeritsPre-evolutionary — no phylogeny; some placements wrong by modern evidence

    3.3 Phylogenetic classification

    AspectDetail
    BasisEvolutionary relationships and descent — fossils, anatomy, and today DNA sequences
    ExamplesEngler and Prantl (1887–1915, first phylogenetic direction); Hutchinson; modern APG (Angiosperm Phylogeny Group) systems based on molecular data
    MeritsReflects true evolutionary history; predicts characters; the modern standard
    DemeritsChanges as data grow (unstable names); complex; requires molecular data

    3.4 Master comparison

    BasisArtificialNaturalPhylogenetic
    Characters usedFew, arbitraryMany, all-roundEvolutionary/molecular
    Reflects evolutionNoPartially (unintentionally)Explicitly
    StabilityStable but wrong groupingsStableImproves with data (names change)
    Best useQuick identificationHerbarium practice, teachingModern taxonomy
    ExampleLinnaeus's sexual systemBentham & HookerAPG systems
  • Deep dive — why phylogenetic classification won. Artificial systems (e.g. Linnaeus's sexual system) grouped plants by one or few characters — easy to use, but put unrelated plants together and split relatives apart. Natural systems (Bentham and Hooker) weighed overall similarity — better, but still arbitrary in character choice. Phylogenetic systems (Engler and Prantl onward, now APG) group by evolutionary descent, using fossils, anatomy, chemistry and now DNA sequences. The hierarchy of ranks stays the same; what changed is the evidence used to decide the grouping.

  • 5. Exam Orientation

    5.1 Previous year questions

    No taxonomic-study question exists in the authored bank (binomial nomenclature was asked in 2021 — Topic 5). Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    5.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — What is a taxonomic key? How does it work? Answer: A key is an identification device made of paired, contrasting statements (couplets). The user selects the statement matching the specimen at each step and proceeds to the next couplet, finally reaching the species name. Keys are artificial but fast — they work without prior knowledge of relationships. Good keys use stable, easily observable characters.

    Q2 (3m) — Distinguish artificial, natural and phylogenetic classification with one example of each. Answer: Artificial — few arbitrary characters, ignores affinity (Linnaeus's stamen-number system); natural — overall resemblance using all important characters (Bentham and Hooker, 1862–83); phylogenetic — evolutionary descent, today molecular (APG systems). Natural and phylogenetic group related plants; artificial may not.

    Q3 (2m) — What is a herbarium? State two uses. Answer: A herbarium is a collection of dried, pressed, mounted and labelled plant specimens arranged systematically. Uses: permanent reference for identification and research; source of data for floras, monographs and conservation assessments.

    Q4 (2m) — Give the complete classification of mango. Answer: Kingdom Plantae → Division Angiospermae → Class Dicotyledonae → Order Sapindales → Family Anacardiaceae → Genus Mangifera → Species indica.

    5.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling Bentham & Hooker's system phylogenetic — it is natural (pre-evolutionary).
    2. Writing keys as descriptions — keys are paired contrasting couplets.
    3. Confusing flora (regional account) with monograph (single taxon account).
    4. Giving the mango's family as Fabaceae — it is Anacardiaceae.

Confusion

  • Flora vs monograph: flora covers all plants of a region; a monograph covers all species of one taxon worldwide.
  • Natural vs phylogenetic: natural groups by overall similarity (may or may not match evolution); phylogenetic groups strictly by descent.
  • Herbarium sheet label: must carry scientific name, family, place and date of collection, collector — a specimen without data is scientifically worthless.

Practice

  • Why do herbaria still use Bentham & Hooker's family names despite modern APG systems?

    Answer. Practical continuity vs molecular truth. Bentham & Hooker (1862–83) built the natural system on overall affinity, and its family circumscriptions are stable, widely taught and embedded in a century and a half of herbarium sheets, floras, keys and literature — every specimen label and regional flora is filed by it. The modern APG phylogenetic systems keep reshuffling families as DNA evidence arrives (splitting, merging, renaming), which is scientifically better but would relabel millions of specimens and break every existing index. So herbaria run B&H for day-to-day curation and cite APG relationships in research — stability in the cabinets, evolution in the papers.

Universal Facts

  • "Flora of Nepal" is the national flora project cataloguing Nepal's ~7,000 flowering plants.

Examples

  • Bentham & Hooker's Genera Plantarum (1862–83) — the natural classification still cited in South Asian herbaria.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What are couplets?

    Answer. The paired, mutually contrasting statements that make up each step of a dichotomous key: at every step two opposite alternatives are offered (e.g. "leaves parallel-veined…" vs "leaves reticulate-veined…"); choosing the statement that fits leads either to the next couplet or to the final identification. Good couplets use one clear, positive character pair.

Key Points

  • Hierarchy: Kingdom → Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species; mango = Anacardiaceae, Sapindales, Dicotyledonae.
  • Taxonomic aids: herbarium, botanical garden, museum, key, flora, monograph, manual.
  • Key = paired contrasting couplets leading stepwise to identification.
  • Systems: artificial (Linnaeus — few arbitrary characters), natural (Bentham & Hooker 1862–83 — overall affinity), phylogenetic (Engler & Prantl; modern APG — molecular evolution).

Summary

Taxonomic study applies the hierarchy to angiosperms — Kingdom to Species, as in mango (Plantae → Angiospermae → Dicotyledonae → Sapindales → Anacardiaceae → Mangifera indica) — and equips taxonomists with herbaria, botanical gardens, dichotomous keys, floras and monographs. Three classification systems mark three eras: artificial (Linnaeus's stamen-based classes — fast but misleading), natural (Bentham & Hooker — overall affinity, still used in herbaria) and phylogenetic (Engler & Prantl to today's molecular APG systems — evolution made explicit). Identification is a tool; classification is a hypothesis of relationship — and the systems keep improving as data do.

Important Statements

  • "An artificial key identifies; a natural system explains."

Formulas

  • Linnaeus's sexual system was artificial: a few floral characters carried the whole classification. Bentham and Hooker built a natural system from many characters at once. A phylogenetic system tries to follow ancestry.
  • A taxonomic key works by paired contrasts. A herbarium sheet, a botanical garden and a flora manual are the aids that let someone else check the identification. The species remains the basic unit.
