Faunal Diversity — Animal Kingdom Classification

Class 11BiologyFaunal Diversity

Enriched Content

Notes
  • Protista: eukaryotic, mostly unicellular (some colonial); the "kingdom of convenience" for single-celled eukaryotes.
  • Protozoa (animal-like protists): heterotrophic, motile; grouped by locomotion — Sarcodina (Amoeba, pseudopodia), Mastigophora (Trypanosoma, flagella), Ciliophora (Paramecium, cilia), Sporozoa (Plasmodium, non-motile spores).
  • Amoeba proteus: pseudopodia for movement + food; contractile vacuole; no definite shape.
  • Paramecium caudatum: ciliated, oval, oral groove → food vacuole; two nuclei (macro + micronucleus); contractile vacuoles for osmoregulation.
  • Plasmodium (malaria): sporozoan; life cycle involves Anopheles mosquito (vector) and human; causes malaria.
  • Algae and fungi-like protists also belong to Protista in the five-kingdom system.
  • NEB focus: Protozoa outline, classification up to class, diagnostic features, and examples.
Confusion
  • Protists are eukaryotes (have a nucleus); they are NOT bacteria (prokaryotes).
  • Protozoa are animal-like; algae are plant-like; slime moulds are fungus-like — all are in Protista.
  • Amoeba moves by pseudopodia (no flagella/cilia); Paramecium by cilia; Trypanosoma by flagella.
  • Sporozoa (Plasmodium) are non-motile in their adult/infective (spore) stage; they do not have flagella/cilia/pseudopodia.
  • Paramecium has TWO nuclei: a large macro-nucleus (metabolic) and a small micro-nucleus (reproductive).
  • Contractile vacuole in freshwater protozoa expels excess water (osmoregulation); it is not a "digestive" vacuole.
Practice
  • Classify Protozoa up to class (Sarcodina, Mastigophora, Ciliophora, Sporozoa) with one example each.
  • Draw and label Paramecium (cilia, oral groove, food vacuole, macronucleus, micronucleus, contractile vacuole).
  • Explain the locomotion of Amoeba (pseudopodia).
  • Describe the life cycle of Plasmodium (human + mosquito phases).
  • Differentiate the four protozoan classes by mode of locomotion.
Universal Facts
  • Amoeba proteus is ~0.5 mm; Paramecium is ~0.2 mm long.
  • Plasmodium falciparum causes the most deadly form of malaria (P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale are others).
  • The Anopheles mosquito is the only vector that transmits Plasmodium to humans.
  • Protozoa are among the most abundant single-celled organisms in freshwater and marine environments.
Examples
  • Amoeba proteus — Sarcodina; pseudopodia; no fixed shape.
  • Paramecium caudatum — Ciliophora; cilia; two nuclei; oral groove.
  • Trypanosoma brucei — Mastigophora; flagellum; causes sleeping sickness.
  • Plasmodium falciparum — Sporozoa; no locomotion; causes malaria; Anopheles vector.

Formulas
  • Protozoa classes: Sarcodina (pseudopodia), Mastigophora (flagella), Ciliophora (cilia), Sporozoa (spores/non-motile)
  • Amoeba: cell membrane + cytoplasm + nucleus + food vacuole + contractile vacuole
  • Paramecium: cilia + oral groove + food vacuole + macro/micronucleus + contractile vacuole
Key Points
  • Protista: eukaryotic, mostly unicellular; animal/plant/fungus-like.
  • Protozoa = animal-like; locomotion defines the 4 classes.
  • Sarcodina (Amoeba) — pseudopodia; Ciliophora (Paramecium) — cilia.
  • Mastigophora (Trypanosoma) — flagella; Sporozoa (Plasmodium) — non-motile spores.
  • Paramecium has two nuclei (macro + micro) and contractile vacuoles.
  • Plasmodium: malaria; Anopheles vector; human + mosquito life cycle.
Summary

Protozoa are animal-like, mostly unicellular eukaryotes classified by their locomotion into four classes: Sarcodina (pseudopodia, e.g. Amoeba), Mastigophora (flagella, e.g. Trypanosoma), Ciliophora (cilia, e.g. Paramecium), and Sporozoa (non-motile spores, e.g. Plasmodium). Paramecium and Amoeba are model organisms. Plasmodium (malaria) with its Anopheles vector is a key NEB examination topic. Protozoa are the most diverse single-celled eukaryotes in aquatic environments.

  • Paramecium is ciliated and has two distinct nuclei.
  • Plasmodium (a sporozoan) causes malaria and requires the Anopheles mosquito as its vector.
  • The contractile vacuole of freshwater protozoa maintains osmotic balance.

Relevance

Notes

  • Protista: unicellular eukaryotes. Includes protozoa (animal-like), algae (plant-like) and slime moulds (fungus-like).
  • Protozoa characteristics: unicellular, eukaryotic, heterotrophic, locomotion by cilia, flagella or pseudopodia, reproduce by binary fission or multiple fission.
  • Classification: (1) Mastigophora (flagella: Euglena, Trypanosoma), (2) Sarcodina (pseudopodia: Amoeba, Entamoeba), (3) Ciliophora (cilia: Paramecium, Vorticella), (4) Sporozoa (spores, parasitic: Plasmodium, Toxoplasma).
  • Paramecium caudatum: slipper-shaped, ciliated, two contractile vacuoles for osmoregulation, trichocysts for defence, binary fission and conjugation reproduction.
  • Plasmodium vivax: malarial parasite. Complex life cycle: mosquito injects sporozoites → liver stage (exo-erythrocytic) → blood stage (erythrocytic schizogony) → gametocytes in blood → ingested by mosquito → sexual stage in gut → sporozoites migrate to salivary glands.
  • Euglena gracilis: mixotrophic protist with a prominent stigma (eyespot) for light detection, pellicle for shape flexibility, single contractile vacuole, and both asexual (longitudinal binary fission) and sexual reproduction under adverse conditions.
  • Trypanosoma brucei: causative agent of African sleeping sickness (kala-azar in India by T. lewisi variant). Transmitted by tsetse fly (Glossina). Has kinetoplast DNA, undulating membrane for motility, and antigenic variation (VSG switching) to evade host immune response.
  • Amoeba proteus: shapeless due to pseudopodia used for locomotion and phagocytosis. Food vacuoles form around ingested bacteria; contractile vacuole expels excess water. Reproduces by binary fission under favourable conditions, and by encystment (cyst formation) under hostile environments.
  • Entamoeba histolytica: causative agent of amoebic dysentery. Lives in human large intestine. Trophozoite stage is invasive (invades intestinal mucosa), cyst stage is infective. Passes out in faeces; infection occurs via contaminated water and food.
  • Vorticella: bell-shaped ciliate on a contractile stalk. Colonial or solitary in freshwater. Uses oral cilia (adoral zone of membranelles) to create water currents for feeding. Contractile stalk coil contracts rapidly for escape response.
  • Sporozoa (Apicomplexa): entirely parasitic, no locomotory organelles in adult stage. All possess an apical complex used for penetrating host cells. Plasmodium (malaria), Toxoplasma gondii (toxoplasmosis), and Eimeria (coccidiosis) are important genera.
  • Protista cell structure: all protists have a eukaryotic cell with membrane-bound nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus. Some (Euglena) have chloroplasts for photosynthesis. Cell covering varies: pellicle (Euglena), no fixed covering (Amoeba), or cilia-covered cell membrane (Paramecium).
  • Plasmodium life cycle — sexual vs asexual: Asexual reproduction (schizogony) occurs in the human host (liver and RBCs). Sexual reproduction (sporogony) occurs in the female Anopheles mosquito gut after gametes fuse to form an ookinete, which develops into an oocyst releasing sporozoites.
  • Protozoan reproduction strategies: binary fission (Amoeba, Paramecium), multiple fission/schizogony (Plasmodium in liver), budding (Vorticella), and conjugation (Paramecium — temporary union for genetic exchange, not reproduction per se).
  • Ecological role of Protista: Euglena and dinoflagellates are primary producers in aquatic ecosystems. Foraminiferans and radiolarians contribute to marine sediments (chalk/limestone formation). Amoebas are important bacterial regulators in soil and aquatic microbiomes.
  • Dinoflagellates (Class Dinoflagellata): mostly marine plankton. Two flagella (one transverse, one longitudinal) in grooves. Some have cellulose plates (theca). Responsible for red tides (bioluminescence). Examples: Gonyaulax (toxic), Ceratium, Peridinium.
  • Diatoms (Class Bacillariophyceae): unicellular algae with silica cell walls (frustule = two halves). Major primary producers in oceans. Fossil diatoms form diatomaceous earth. Centric (radial symmetry) and pennate (bilateral) forms.
  • Slime moulds: fungus-like protists. Cellular slime moulds (Dictyostelium) exist as individual amoeboid cells that aggregate into a pseudoplasmodium (slug) during food scarcity, then form a fruiting body. Acellular slime moulds (Physarum) form a multinucleate plasmodium.
  • Chrysophytes and Euglenoids: Chrysophytes include golden algae and diatoms, stored food as oils and chrysolaminarin. Euglenoids have proteinaceous pellicle, can be photosynthetic or heterotrophic, and store paramylon (a carbohydrate).
  • Parasitic protozoa in India: Plasmodium vivax, P. falciparum, and P. malariae cause malaria. Leishmania donovani causes kala-azar. Entamoeba histolytica causes amoebiasis. Trypanosoma causes sleeping sickness (rare in India but important globally).
  • Paramecium nuclear dimorphism: Paramecium has two types of nuclei — the macronucleus (controls everyday metabolic functions and is polyploid) and the micronucleus (controls reproduction and is diploid). During conjugation, the micronucleus undergoes meiosis to form gametic nuclei; during binary fission, the macronucleus divides amitotically while the micronucleus divides mitotically.
  • Contractile vacuole osmoregulation: freshwater protozoa like Amoeba and Paramecium constantly take in water by osmosis because their cytoplasm is hypertonic to the surrounding medium. The contractile vacuole collects excess water from the cytoplasm via radial canals and periodically expels it to the exterior, preventing cell lysis. In marine forms, contractile vacuoles are usually absent.
  • Phagotrophy and food digestion in protozoa: Amoeba feeds by phagocytosis — pseudopodia surround a food particle to form a food vacuole. Lysosomes fuse with the food vacuole releasing hydrolytic enzymes (proteases, lipases, amylases) for intracellular digestion. Digestive products diffuse into the cytoplasm; indigestible material is egested through the cell membrane (cytoproct).
  • Locomotion mechanisms in protozoa: (1) Pseudopodia — temporary cytoplasmic projections in Sarcodina; flow of cytoplasm into the projection creates movement (Amoeba). (2) Cilia — hair-like organelles covering the body; coordinated beating produces locomotion and feeding currents (Paramecium). (3) Flagella — long whip-like structures; undulating waves propel the cell (Euglena, Trypanosoma). (4) Gliding — some protozoa move by secreting mucus trails.
  • Plasmodium — clinical stages of malaria: (1) Incubation period (8-30 days depending on species) — sporozoites develop in liver. (2) Clinical stage — merozoites burst from RBCs, causing cyclic fevers (every 48h in P. vivax, 72h in P. malariae, irregular in P. falciparum). (3) Complications — P. falciparum causes cerebral malaria, blackwater fever, and severe anaemia. (4) Radical cure — only P. vivax and P. ovale have hypnozoites (dormant liver stages) requiring primaquine for complete cure.
  • Protozoan cyst formation — adaptive significance: When environmental conditions become adverse (desiccation, temperature extremes, food scarcity), many protozoa encyst. The trophozoite retracts extensions, secretes a thick protective wall, and becomes metabolically inactive. Cysts are resistant to desiccation, chemicals, and stomach acid. Excystation occurs when conditions improve, producing active trophozoites. This is crucial for transmission of intestinal parasites like Entamoeba.
  • Kinetoplastida (Order Kinetoplastida): A group of flagellated protozoa characterised by a single large mitochondrion containing a mass of DNA called the kinetoplast. Includes Trypanosoma (causes sleeping sickness and Chagas disease) and Leishmania (causes kala-azar and cutaneous leishmaniasis). They have a single flagellum attached by an undulating membrane. Vector-borne: tsetse fly for Trypanosoma, sandfly for Leishmania.
  • Ciliophora — structural complexity of Paramecium: Beyond the nucleus and contractile vacuole, Paramecium has: (1) Cytostome (cell mouth) — a permanent oral groove leading to a cytopharynx; (2) Cytoproct (cell anus) — site of egestion; (3) Trichocysts — elongated organelles just beneath the pellicle that can be discharged as defensive or offensive threads; (4) Oral membranelles — thickened cilia that sweep food into the cytostome; (5) Anal pore — opens near the oral groove for waste expulsion.
  • Protistan nutritional diversity: Protista exhibit diverse nutritional modes — photoautotrophic (diatoms, dinoflagellates, euglenoids), heterotrophic (protozoa), mixotrophic (Euglena combines photosynthesis and phagocytosis), and parasitic (Plasmodium, Trypanosoma, Entamoeba). Some dinoflagellates are bioluminescent and can form red tides when nutrient-rich conditions trigger blooms.
  • Diatom frustule and diatomaceous earth: Diatoms have a unique silica cell wall (frustule) consisting of two overlapping halves (epitheca and hypotheca). Centric diatoms (e.g., Coscinodiscus) show radial symmetry; pennate diatoms (e.g., Navicula) show bilateral symmetry and some possess a raphe for gliding. Upon death, their silica shells accumulate as diatomaceous earth — a highly porous sediment used for filtration, abrasives, and insulating materials.
  • Slime moulds — cellular vs acellular: Cellular slime moulds (Dictyostelium) exist as individual amoeboid cells that aggregate upon starvation into a multicellular slug (pseudoplasmodium), then form a fruiting body with spores. Acellular slime moulds (Physarum) form a giant multinucleate plasmodium that crawls over decaying matter, engulfing bacteria and organic particles. Both demonstrate differentiation and communication without nervous systems.
  • Dinoflagellate features and red tides: Dinoflagellates (Class Dinoflagellata) are mostly marine, biflagellate protists with one transverse and one longitudinal flagellum. Many have cellulose plates (theca) and are bioluminescent. Some species (Gonyaulax, Alexandrium) produce toxins (saxitoxin, brevetoxin) that accumulate in shellfish, causing paralytic shellfish poisoning in humans. Mass blooms cause 'red tides' — harmful algal blooms that deplete oxygen and kill fish.
  • Protista vs bacteria in phylogeny: Protists are eukaryotes with membrane-bound organelles, linear chromosomes with histones, and 80S ribosomes — fundamentally different from bacteria (prokaryotes, 70S ribosomes, circular DNA). However, many protists have acquired bacterial genes through endosymbiosis or horizontal gene transfer. The endosymbiotic theory posits that mitochondria and chloroplasts originated from free-living bacteria engulfed by ancestral eukaryotes.
  • Symbiotic protists: Many protists form mutualistic symbioses — zooxanthellae (Symbiodinium, dinoflagellates) live in coral tissues and provide photosynthates in exchange for shelter and nutrients; trichonymphids (flagellates) in termite guts digest cellulose; ciliates in ruminant stomachs help break down plant material. These symbioses are ecologically critical for reef ecosystems and nutrient cycling.
  • Endosymbiotic theory and protist evolution: The origin of eukaryotes involved primary endosymbiosis (cyanobacterium → chloroplast) in the ancestor of Archaeplastida (red algae, green algae, plants). Secondary endosymbiosis (engulfment of a red or green alga by a non-photosynthetic eukaryote) gave rise to dinoflagellates, apicomplexans, diatoms, and euglenoids with complex plastids surrounded by 3-4 membranes.
  • Foraminiferans and radiolarians in sediment formation: Foraminiferans (order Foraminifera) secrete calcareous (CaCO₃) chambers that form the basis of thick marine limestone deposits (e.g., the White Cliffs of Dover). Radiolarians (subclass Radiolaria) have intricate siliceous (SiO₂) skeletons that form radiolarian ooze on the deep ocean floor. Both are important index fossils for biostratigraphy.
  • Vascular plant endosymbiont — mycorrhizae analogy: While not protists, some slime moulds and chytrid fungi (formerly grouped with protists) are now understood to be closely related to nucleariids. The former kingdom Protista is paraphyletic; modern classification (SAR supergroup: Stramenopiles, Alveolates, Rhizarians) reflects true evolutionary relationships uncovered by molecular phylogenetics.
  • Kinetoplastida — Trypanosoma and Leishmania pathogenicity: The Order Kinetoplastida (subgroup of Euglenozoa) includes Trypanosoma and Leishmania, both characterised by a single large mitochondrion with a condensed mass of kinetoplast DNA (kDNA). Trypanosoma brucei causes African sleeping sickness (tsetse fly vector); T. cruzi causes Chagas disease (triatomine bug vector). Leishmania donovani causes visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar); L. major causes cutaneous leishmaniasis. Both exhibit extensive RNA editing of kDNA transcripts.
  • Paramecium — conjugation as genetic exchange: In Paramecium, conjugation involves two individuals temporarily joined at their oral regions. The macronucleus degenerates; the micronucleus undergoes meiosis to form 4 haploid micronuclei; 3 degenerate, and 1 divides twice to produce 3 more (total 4). One migratory and one stationary gametic nucleus are formed in each; they exchange migratory nuclei. After fusion (syngamy), each exconjugant undergoes fission to restore macronuclear function. This sexual process increases genetic diversity without increasing population size.

Confusion

  • Protista is a single group of organisms. — Protista is a polyphyletic kingdom-level group; it contains very diverse organisms (algae, protozoa, slime moulds) that are grouped together mainly because they are unicellular eukaryotes, not because they share close evolutionary relationships.
  • Binary fission and conjugation in Paramecium are both reproduction. — Binary fission is asexual reproduction producing new individuals. Conjugation is a sexual process for genetic exchange between two individuals; it does not increase the number of organisms and is therefore NOT reproduction.
  • Plasmodium reproduces only in mosquitoes. — Plasmodium undergoes asexual reproduction (schizogony) extensively in the human host (liver and blood cells). Sexual reproduction (sporogony) occurs only in the mosquito vector.
  • All protozoa are harmful parasites. — Most protozoa are free-living and ecologically beneficial (e.g., Amoeba regulates bacterial populations, Euglena contributes to photosynthesis). Only a few species are parasitic (Plasmodium, Entamoeba, Trypanosoma).
  • Euglena is a plant or an animal. — Euglena is neither a plant nor an animal; it is a protist with mixotrophic nutrition. It has chloroplasts for photosynthesis (plant-like) but lacks a cell wall and can also feed heterotrophically (animal-like).
  • Contractile vacuoles are found in all protozoa. — Contractile vacuoles are present only in freshwater protozoa that need osmoregulation. Marine protozoa generally lack contractile vacuoles because the external environment is isotonic or hypertonic to their cytoplasm.
  • The cyst stage is always the active feeding stage of a protozoan. — The cyst stage is a dormant, non-feeding, protective stage. The trophozoite is the active, feeding, and reproducing stage. Cysts are important for survival and transmission but do not feed or grow.
  • All algae are protists. — Not all algae are protists. Green algae (Chlorophyta) and red algae (Rhodophyta) are now classified in the kingdom Plantae; blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) are bacteria. Only brown algae (Phaeophyceae), diatoms (Bacillariophyceae), and dinoflagellates remain in Protista — highlighting that 'algae' is a polyphyletic term.
  • Plasmodium has flagella in its life cycle. — Plasmodium (Sporozoa) is largely non-motile in all stages except for the microgametes (male gametes) in the mosquito gut, which are briefly flagellated. In the human host, all stages (sporozoites, merozoites, trophozoites, gametocytes) are non-flagellated and move by gliding or intracellular growth.
  • Protozoa are single-celled so they cannot have complex behaviour. — Single-celled protozoa exhibit complex behaviours: Paramecium can avoid obstacles (axonal reflex), learn to associate stimuli (habituation), and communicate during conjugation. Amoeba can solve mazes and store information. These behaviours are mediated by ion channels and cytoskeletal dynamics, not neurons.
  • Viruses are classified as protists. — Viruses are not protists (or even living organisms). They lack cellular structure, cannot carry out metabolism independently, and require a host cell to replicate. They are not included in the Five-Kingdom classification system at all, standing outside the domains of life.

Practice

  • Classify the following into their respective protozoan classes: Paramecium, Amoeba, Plasmodium, Euglena, Vorticella. Answer: Ciliophora (Paramecium, Vorticella), Sarcodina (Amoeba), Sporozoa (Plasmodium), Mastigophora (Euglena).
  • A patient from a malaria-endemic area shows periodic fever with chills every 48 hours. Identify the parasite and explain which stage of Plasmodium causes fever. Answer: Plasmodium vivax (tertian malaria). Fever occurs during erythrocytic schizogony when RBCs rupture simultaneously, releasing toxins and merozoites that trigger immune response.
  • **
  • Why is Euglena considered a connecting link between plants and animals?" Answer:** Euglena possesses chloroplasts and performs photosynthesis like plants, but it also lacks a cell wall and can feed heterotrophically in the dark like animals. This mixotrophic nature places it between the plant and animal kingdoms.
  • Describe the encystment process in Amoeba and explain its adaptive significance." Answer:** Under adverse conditions (desiccation, food scarcity), Amoeba retracts pseudopodia, secretes a thick protective cyst wall, and enters dormancy. This helps survival through unfavorable periods. When conditions improve, the cyst excysts and the active trophozoite emerges. This is analogous to sporulation but serves a survival function, not reproduction.
  • Differentiate between exo-erythrocytic and erythrocytic schizogony in Plasmodium." Answer:** Exo-erythrocytic schizogony occurs in liver hepatocytes after sporozoites enter from mosquito bite; this produces merozoites that then invade RBCs. Erythrocytic schizogony occurs inside RBCs, producing more merozoites that burst RBCs and cause malaria symptoms. Only P. vivax and P. ovale have a dormant liver stage (hypnozoites) causing relapses.
  • Compare the contractile vacuole in Amoeba and Paramecium in terms of structure and mechanism. Answer: In Amoeba, a single contractile vacuole collects water from radial canals and periodically expels it. In Paramecium, there are two contractile vacuoles (anterior and posterior) each surrounded by a network of 5-8 radiating canals. The vacuoles alternate in filling and emptying in a regular cycle. Both serve osmoregulation but Paramecium's system is more complex due to its larger size and higher water intake.
  • Explain the significance of VSG (variant surface glycoprotein) switching in Trypanosoma. Answer: Trypanosoma brucei coats its entire surface with a dense layer of VSG proteins. The host immune system produces antibodies against the initial VSG type, but a small subset of trypanosomes periodically switches to expressing a different VSG variant. This antigenic variation allows the parasite to stay ahead of the immune response, causing recurrent waves of parasitemia and chronic infection. It is a major obstacle to vaccine development.
  • Compare and contrast the life cycles of Plasmodium and Trypanosoma in terms of hosts and transmission. Answer: Plasmodium requires two hosts — a vertebrate host (human, where asexual replication occurs in liver and RBCs) and an invertebrate vector (female Anopheles mosquito, where sexual reproduction occurs). Trypanosoma brucei also requires two hosts — a vertebrate host (mammal, including human) and an invertebrate vector (tsetse fly). However, Trypanosoma replicates extracellularly in blood/lymph, while Plasmodium is intracellular in host cells. Trypanosoma also uses VSG switching for immune evasion, while Plasmodium uses antigenic variation of surface proteins on infected RBCs.
  • A patient presents with cyclic fever every 72 hours, anaemia, and splenomegaly. Identify the Plasmodium species and explain the timing. Answer: Plasmodium malariae causes quartan malaria with fever cycles every 72 hours. This is because its erythrocytic schizogony cycle takes 72 hours (the longest among human malaria species). P. vivax and P. ovale cause tertian malaria (48-hour cycles), while P. falciparum causes irregular/cyclical fevers. P. malariae is notable for causing chronic, low-level persistent infections that can last for decades if untreated.
  • Describe the endosymbiotic theory and its relevance to protist evolution. Answer: The endosymbiotic theory proposes that mitochondria and chloroplasts originated as free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell. Evidence: (1) Both organelles have their own circular DNA (bacterial-type). (2) They have 70S ribosomes (bacterial). (3) They reproduce by binary fission. (4) Their membranes are double (inner from bacterium, outer from host engulfment). Primary endosymbiosis gave rise to chloroplasts in Archaeplastida (green/red algae, plants). Secondary endosymbiosis (engulfment of a eukaryotic alga) gave rise to complex plastids in dinoflagellates, apicomplexans, and euglenoids.
  • What are the diagnostic features that separate the four classes of Protozoa, and why is Sporozoa unique? Answer: The four classes are distinguished by locomotion: (1) Mastigophora — flagella (Euglena, Trypanosoma). (2) Sarcodina — pseudopodia (Amoeba, Entamoeba). (3) Ciliophora — cilia (Paramecium, Vorticella). (4) Sporozoa — no locomotory organelles in adult stage, all parasitic with an apical complex for host cell penetration (Plasmodium, Toxoplasma). Sporozoa is unique because it completely lacks locomotory structures in its adult form, relying on gliding motility or passive transmission, and it possesses a specialised apical complex (conoid, rhoptries, micronemes) not found in any other protozoan class.

Universal Facts

  • This concept is fundamental and appears in multiple contexts across the subject.
  • Understanding the derivation helps more than memorizing the final formula.
  • Always check units and dimensions when applying this concept.
  • Protista was first proposed by Ernst Haeckel in 1866 and is considered the most heterogeneous kingdom in the Five-Kingdom classification of Whittaker.
  • Malaria was the first disease proven to be transmitted by a vector (mosquito) — by Sir Ronald Ross in 1897 in India (Secunderabad), earning him the Nobel Prize in 1902.
  • Paramecium is the only protist that exhibits both asexual (binary fission) and sexual (conjugation) reproduction with well-documented nuclear dimorphism (macronucleus controls metabolism, micronucleus controls reproduction).
  • Diatoms contribute approximately 20-25% of global oxygen production through photosynthesis — more than all the world's rainforests combined.
  • The fossil record of foraminiferans (Protozoa) spans over 500 million years and their calcareous shells form the basis of major limestone deposits used in construction worldwide.
  • Paramecium caudatum has a well-defined cell anatomy with a fixed body shape maintained by the pellicle, and its size (0.25-0.35 mm) makes it visible to the naked eye — an unusual feature among single-celled organisms.
  • The apicomplexan parasites (Sporozoa) include Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, and Eimeria — collectively causing more human illness and death than any other single group of protists, primarily through malaria (600,000+ deaths annually worldwide).
  • Some protists exhibit 'green tides' — massive blooms of green algae (e.g., Ulva lactuca) caused by nutrient pollution (eutrophication). These blooms can cover hundreds of square kilometers, block sunlight, and deplete oxygen when they decompose, creating dead zones.
  • Conjugation in Paramecium is a form of genetic recombination (not reproduction) that can occur when nutritional stress triggers it, producing genetically novel individuals that may be better adapted to changing environments — an example of asexual organisms employing sexual mechanisms for survival.

Summary

Protozoa are animal-like, mostly unicellular eukaryotes classified by their locomotion into four classes: Sarcodina (pseudopodia, e.g. Amoeba), Mastigophora (flagella, e.g. Trypanosoma), Ciliophora (cilia, e.g. Paramecium), and Sporozoa (non-motile spores, e.g. Plasmodium). Paramecium and Amoeba are model organisms. Plasmodium (malaria) with its Anopheles vector is a key NEB examination topic. Protozoa are the most diverse single-celled eukaryotes in aquatic environments.

  • Plasmodium (a sporozoan) causes malaria and requires the Anopheles mosquito as its vector.
  • The contractile vacuole of freshwater protozoa maintains osmotic balance.
