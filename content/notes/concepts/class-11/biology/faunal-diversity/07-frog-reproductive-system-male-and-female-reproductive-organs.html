Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Dioecious Amphibian Reproduction

    Unlike the hermaphrodite earthworm, the frog is dioecious — separate sexes with distinct organs — and fertilisation is external: eggs and sperm are shed into water. This topic covers the male and female reproductive organs and their roles in breeding.

  • 2. Male Reproductive Organs

    OrganLocationFunction
    Two testesYellowish ovoid bodies attached to the kidneys (by mesorchium)Produce sperm and testosterone
    Vasa efferentiaSeveral ducts from each testis entering the kidneyConduct sperm through the kidney
    Bidder's canalLongitudinal canal through the kidneyCollects sperm from vasa efferentia
    Urino-genital ductsRun along the kidney's outer edge to the cloacaCarry sperm (and urine) to the cloaca
    Spermatic sacs / seminal vesiclesTerminal swelling near the cloacaTemporary sperm storage before mating

    Sperm path: testes → vasa efferentia → kidney (Bidder's canal) → urino-genital duct → cloaca. The urinary and genital tracts share the duct — hence "urino-genital".

  • Male frog urogenital pathway — testis to cloaca

  • 3. Female Reproductive Organs

    OrganLocationFunction
    Two ovariesNear the kidneys, attached by mesovarium; size varies seasonallyProduce ova and oestrogen
    Two oviductsLong, coiled tubes running independently on either sideTransport ova; secrete albumen and jelly
    Ovisac / uterusEnlarged posterior end of each oviduct opening into the cloacaTemporarily stores eggs before laying

    Egg structure: each ovum is black-and-white pigmented (animal pole dark), surrounded by a protective jelly coat secreted by the oviduct — the jelly swells in water, protecting and anchoring the eggs.

  • 4. Breeding and Fertilisation

    1. Breeding season: monsoon; males croak (vocal sacs) to attract females.
    2. Amplexus: the male clasps the female's back with his nuptial pads.
    3. External fertilisation: the female sheds hundreds of eggs into water; the male releases sperm over them simultaneously.
    4. Development in water: jelly swells; the zygote develops into embryo → tadpole (aquatic larva with gills) → metamorphosis into an adult frog.

    Contrast with earthworm: frog is dioecious with external fertilisation and indirect development; earthworm is hermaphrodite with internal (cocoon) fertilisation and direct development.

  • Deep dive — why external fertilisation still works. Frogs trade parental care for numbers: one amplexus releases thousands of eggs, and the jelly coat swells in water to protect and oxygenate them. The gamble is that enough tadpoles survive pond predators. Note the NEB-favourite contrast with the earthworm: earthworm = hermaphrodite, internal exchange, cocoon, direct development; frog = dioecious, external fertilisation, larval stage. Same goal — gene delivery to the next generation — via opposite strategies.

    6.1 Previous year questions

    6.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Describe the male reproductive system of the frog. Answer: Two yellowish testes are attached to the kidneys by the mesorchium; they produce sperm and testosterone. Several vasa efferentia carry sperm from each testis into the kidney, where the Bidder's canal collects them; sperm then travel along the urino-genital ducts (shared with the ureters) to a small seminal vesicle near the cloaca, and are released through the cloaca during mating. The shared duct is why the tract is called urino-genital.

    Q2 (3m) — Describe the female reproductive system of the frog and the structure of its egg. Answer: Two ovaries near the kidneys produce ova and oestrogen; their size varies with the breeding season. Two long coiled oviducts run independently to the cloaca, enlarging into ovisacs (uterus) that store eggs before laying; their walls secrete the albumen and the jelly coat. Each ovum is pigmented — dark animal pole, light vegetative pole — and is released in water, where the jelly swells to protect and anchor the egg mass; fertilisation is external.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying fertilisation is internal — frog fertilisation is external, in water.
    2. Giving the testes an independent duct — sperm travel through the kidney (Bidder's canal) in the shared urino-genital duct.
    3. Calling the jelly coat part of the ovum — it is secreted by the oviduct.
    4. Saying development is direct — the frog has an aquatic tadpole larva (indirect development).
Confusion
  • Frogs have EXTERNAL fertilisation (in water), NOT internal.
  • The male does NOT have a copulatory organ; he releases sperm over the eggs during amplexus.
  • Amplexus: male grasps the female (axillary in Rana, inguinal in some frogs) to coordinate egg and sperm release.
  • The oviduct adds a jelly coat to the eggs; the jelly protects the eggs in water.
  • Tadpoles have gills + tail; adults have lungs + no tail (metamorphosis).
  • The testes are kidney-shaped and lie along the dorsal body wall; the ovaries are lobed and also on the dorsal wall.

Summary

The frog is dioecious, contrasting with the hermaphrodite earthworm. The male's yellowish testes sit on the kidneys; sperm travel through vasa efferentia and the kidney's Bidder's canal along the shared urino-genital ducts to a seminal vesicle and the cloaca. The female's ovaries swell seasonally with pigmented ova; long coiled oviducts add albumen and the protective jelly coat before eggs reach the ovisacs and the cloaca. In the monsoon, males croak with vocal sacs, amplexus positions the pair, and eggs and sperm are shed into water for external fertilisation; the jelly-swollen eggs hatch into tadpoles that metamorphose into adults — indirect development in the amphibian way.

Summary

The frog's reproductive system is adapted for external fertilisation in water. The male has two kidney-shaped testes, vasa deferentia, seminal vesicles, and a cloaca (no copulatory organ). The female has two lobed ovaries, oviducts that add a jelly coat, a uterus, and a cloaca. During amplexus, the male grasps the female and both release gametes into the water, where external fertilisation occurs. Development proceeds from the zygote to the tadpole (gills + tail) to the adult frog (lungs, no tail) via metamorphosis. Frogs are ecologically important as pest controllers and water-quality bio-indicators.

Practice

  • Why does the frog's ovary change size through the year?

    Answer. The ovary is seasonal: it swells enormously before the monsoon as thousands of ova mature and fill it with yolk, then shrinks to a small resting organ after spawning empties it. The cycle is driven by photoperiod and monsoon cues relayed through the pituitary–gonad hormone axis (gonadotropins), timing gamete production to the one period when water is available for external fertilisation and tadpole development. A permanently large ovary would waste energy; the frog builds gametes only when they can succeed.
