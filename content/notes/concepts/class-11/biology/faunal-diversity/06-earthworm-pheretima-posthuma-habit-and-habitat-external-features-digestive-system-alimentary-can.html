Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Earthworm as a Type Study

    Pheretima posthuma, the common Nepali earthworm, is the syllabus's type specimen of phylum Annelida: a coelomate, metameric animal studied organ by organ. This topic covers habit and habitat, external features and the digestive system; the excretory, nervous and reproductive systems follow in Topic 44.

  • 2. Habit and Habitat

    • Lives in moist, humus-rich soil; burrows by peristalsis; nocturnal, emerging at night and after rain.
    • Feeds on decaying organic matter in the soil (detritivore); casts are the worm's excreta.
    • Sensitive to light, dryness and waterlogging — comes to surface when burrows flood.

    4.2 Physiology of digestion

    Food = decaying organic matter + soil. Salivary protease starts protein digestion in the pharynx; the gizzard grinds; in the intestine, enzymes complete digestion — amylase (from the intestinal caecum), protease, lipase — and the typhlosole and villi absorb the products into the blood. Undigested matter is expelled as castings through the anus.

    6.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Describe the external features of Pheretima posthuma. Answer: The body is a long, cylindrical, metameric tube of 100–120 segments, dark brown dorsally. The prostomium overhangs the mouth; S-shaped chitinous setae project from setigerous sacs in most segments for locomotion; the glandular clitellum covers segments 14–16; male genital pores open on segment 18, the female pore on 14, spermathecal pores in the grooves of segments 5/6–8/9; dorsal pores, nephridiopores and genital papillae complete the external anatomy.

    Q2 (3m) — Describe the alimentary canal of Pheretima. Answer: Mouth and buccal chamber (1–3) → muscular pharynx (4) with salivary glands, which sucks food in → oesophagus (5–7) → muscular gizzard (8) that grinds food with soil → stomach (9–14) whose calciferous glands neutralise humic acids → intestine (15 onward) with an intestinal caecum at segment 26 (amylase) and a dorsal typhlosole from segment 26 doubling the absorptive surface → rectum → anus. Digestion: salivary protease in the pharynx; intestinal amylase, protease and lipase complete it; absorption via typhlosole and villi.

    Q3 (2m) — What is the typhlosole? Answer: A dorsal fold of the intestinal wall in Pheretima, present from segment 26 backward; it doubles the absorptive surface of the intestine and helps in enzyme distribution.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Placing the gizzard in segment 9–14 (that is the stomach) — the gizzard is at segment 8.
    2. Putting the clitellum at 17–19 — it is 14–16.
    3. Saying the salivary glands secrete amylase — earthworm salivary secretion is mucus and protease.
    4. Forgetting the calciferous glands' acid-neutralising role.
    5. Confusing the typhlosole (dorsal fold) with the intestinal caecum (lateral pouches at 26).

  • Extra depth — physiology of digestion, step by step

    Ingestion is by the muscular pharynx sucking decaying leaves with soil. Pharyngeal glands add mucus and proteolytic enzymes; the oesophagus merely conducts. The gizzard grinds mechanically (with sand grains) — no enzymes. The stomach secretes calciferous glands' calcium carbonate to neutralise humic acid, so intestinal enzymes work near neutral pH. The intestine adds amylase, lipase and protease (extracellular digestion); the typhlosole (dorsal intucking, segments 26–95) doubles absorptive surface, and the chloragogen cells around the intestine store glycogen and deaminate amino acids — a 'liver equivalent'. Absorbed nutrients enter the dorsal blood vessel for distribution.

  • Extra depth — external features worth quoting

    Body: 100–150 segments (metameres), 12–30 cm, brownish, bilaterally symmetric. Clitellum — glandular saddle on segments 14–16, secretes the cocoon. Setae — S-shaped chitinous locomotor bristles, 80–120 per segment (except first, last and clitellar), arranged in two pairs per segment half. Dorsal pores, nephridiopores, and the median female pore (14), paired male pores (18), and spermiducal openings mark the reproductive geography. Respiration is cutaneous — the mucus-moistened skin must stay damp; that single fact explains the worm's nocturnal, humid-habitat lifestyle.

Confusion

  • The clitellum is segments 14 to 16, not a random swelling in the middle of the body. The female pore is on segment 14, inside that region.
  • Earthworm blood is red. Haemoglobin is dissolved in the plasma. It is not carried in red cells, and it is not absent.
  • The typhlosole is a fold of the intestine, not a separate gland. The caeca at segment 26 are the glandular outgrowths.
  • Setae are not parapodia. Parapodia belong to Nereis. Pheretima has a ring of small setae.

Practice

  • Why does the earthworm come to the surface after heavy rain?

    Answer. Oxygen, not water, is the problem. Flooded soil has its air spaces filled with water; diffusion of oxygen into waterlogged soil is far too slow for the worm's moist cutaneous respiration, so a burrow becomes an airless trap. Coming to the surface restores access to oxygen (and lets the worm migrate when its burrow is unusable). The trade-off is danger: on the surface the worm meets light, desiccation and birds — which is why it surfaces mainly at night or during rain.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is the function of the gizzard?

    Answer. A muscular, thick-walled chamber at segment 8 that grinds food mechanically with the help of ingested soil particles, compensating for the earthworm's lack of teeth — it precedes the chemically digesting stomach.

Exercises

1.

A student says the earthworm chews leaves in its mouth and that its blood is colourless. Correct both claims with the organs involved.

Steps
  • The mouth and buccal cavity only take soil in. They do not chew.
  • Grinding is the work of the gizzard in segments 8 and 9.
  • Chemical digestion of the organic matter happens in the intestine, with amylase from the caeca at segment 26.
  • Absorption uses the typhlosole. The blood that receives the products contains haemoglobin dissolved in the plasma, so it is red.
  • There are no red blood cells. Colourless blood would mean no respiratory pigment, which is the wrong description of Pheretima.
Answer

Grinding is gizzard, not mouth. Blood is red because haemoglobin is dissolved in the plasma.

Summary

Pheretima posthuma, the common earthworm, is a nocturnal detritivore of moist humus-rich soil, a metameric cylinder of 100–120 segments bearing S-shaped setae, with the glandular clitellum at segments 14–16 and genital pores at defined segments (male 18, female 14, spermathecal 5/6–8/9). Its alimentary canal runs from the buccal chamber through a suction pharynx with salivary protease, a grinding gizzard at segment 8, a calciferous-gland stomach neutralising humic acids, and an intestine with an amylase-secreting caecum and a dorsal typhlosole doubling absorption, to the anus. Every organ is metamerised — the coelomate, segmented body plan of Annelida in working order.
