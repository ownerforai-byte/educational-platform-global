Desc

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Three-Chambered Heart, Three Breathing Surfaces

    The frog's circulation and respiration show the amphibian compromise: a three-chambered heart mixing oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, and three respiratory surfaces — skin, buccal cavity and lungs — used according to the animal's state of activity and submersion. The respiratory system is a repeated 5m question (2022 and 2024).

  • 2. Blood Vascular System

    2.1 Structure of the heart

    ChamberDetail
    Two atria (auricles)Right atrium receives deoxygenated blood; left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs
    Single ventriclePumps both streams; internal ridges (trabeculae) and the spiral valve of the conus arteriosus help keep streams partly separate

    Pericardium encloses the heart; sinus venosus collects venous blood into the right atrium; truncus/conus arteriosus leaves the ventricle with the spiral valve.

    2.2 Circulation (double but incomplete)

    1. Pulmonary circulation: ventricle → conus → pulmocutaneous arches → lungs and skin → oxygenated blood → pulmonary veins → left atrium.
    2. Systemic circulation: ventricle → conus → systemic arches (paired, joining to form the dorsal aorta) → body → venous return via sinus venosus → right atrium.
    3. Portal systems: hepatic portal (gut → liver) and renal portal (limbs/kidneys → kidneys).

    Because the single ventricle mixes the two streams, the frog has incomplete double circulation — blood reaching the body is partly mixed. This suffices for a poikilotherm's low metabolic rate.

    2.3 Blood

    Plasma + cells: RBCs (nucleated, elliptical, carry haemoglobin), WBCs (defence), platelet-like cells (clotting).

  • 3. Respiratory System (NEB 2022 & 2024 — 5m)

    The frog uses three respiratory surfaces:

    ModeWhen usedMechanism
    Cutaneous (skin)In water and at rest on landMoist, vascular, thin skin; O₂ diffuses through the moist surface into capillaries; CO₂ diffuses out
    Buccopharyngeal (buccal cavity lining)On land at restFloor of the buccal cavity rhythmically rises and falls, drawing air in and out through the nostrils; gas exchange across its moist vascular lining
    Pulmonary (lungs)On land, during activitySimple paired sacs with thin walls and low internal partitioning (not alveoli-rich); exchange across the moist lining

    3.1 Mechanism of pulmonary breathing

    1. Inspiration: the floor of the buccal cavity lowers → air enters through the external nostrils into the buccal cavity; then the glottis opens, the buccal floor rises, and air is forced into the lungs; nostrils close.
    2. Expiration: the buccal floor lowers again, the glottis opens, and elastic recoil of the lungs expels air into the buccal cavity, out through the nostrils.
    3. The buccal cavity can breathe independently between lung ventilations.

    Respiratory passages: external nostrils → nasal cavity → internal nostrils (choanae) → buccopharyngeal cavity → glottis → laryngotracheal chamber (with vocal cords — the male's croaking) → paired simple lungs → exchange across the moist vascular lining → pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood to the left atrium.

    5.2 Model answer (5m)

    Q — Describe the respiratory system of the frog. Answer: The frog respires through three surfaces. (1) Cutaneous respiration — the skin is thin, moist, richly supplied with capillaries; O₂ and CO₂ diffuse through the moist surface; it operates in water and at rest on land, contributing most of the exchange in water. (2) Buccopharyngeal respiration — the lining of the buccal cavity is moist and vascular; the floor rhythmically lowers and rises, drawing air through the external nostrils and exchanging gases, used at rest on land. (3) Pulmonary respiration — paired simple lungs, thin-walled sacs with low internal partitioning, connected by the laryngotracheal chamber to the glottis. In pulmonary breathing the buccal floor lowers and air enters through the nostrils; the nostrils close, the glottis opens and the buccal floor rises, forcing air into the lungs; expiration follows by elastic recoil. Oxygenated blood returns by pulmonary veins to the left atrium. (Draw the three surfaces and the inspiration–expiration sequence.)

    5.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Describing only the lungs — the frog uses three surfaces, and cutaneous respiration dominates in water.
    2. Saying the lungs have alveoli — they are simple sacs with low partitioning.
    3. Reversing the inspiration sequence (glottis must open after the nostrils close).
    4. Forgetting the glottis and laryngotracheal chamber (vocal cords) in the passage.
    5. Saying circulation is complete double circulation — the single ventricle gives incomplete double circulation.

Confusion

  • The ventricle is single. Two atria do not make a four-chambered heart. The chambers that count are two atria plus one ventricle.
  • Oxygenated blood from the skin enters the right atrium, via the sinus venosus. Only pulmonary blood enters the left atrium.
  • The truncus arteriosus is part of the arterial exit. It is not a vein, and it is not a fourth chamber.
  • Adult frogs do not breathe with gills. Gills are larval. Hibernating adults rely on skin.

Key Points

  • Heart: two atria + single ventricle; sinus venosus → right atrium; pulmonary veins → left atrium; conus arteriosus with spiral valve; incomplete double circulation (pulmocutaneous + systemic arches); hepatic and renal portal systems.
  • Respiration: cutaneous (skin — dominant in water), buccopharyngeal (buccal lining, at rest), pulmonary (simple sac lungs, during activity).
  • Pulmonary breathing: buccal floor lowers, air in via nostrils → nostrils close, glottis opens, floor rises → air forced into lungs; expiration by elastic recoil.

Important Statements

  • "A three-chambered heart is a compromise between fish and mammal."

Examples

  • Cutaneous respiration — the moist vascular skin exchanges gases in water and at rest.

Universal Facts

  • In water, the frog obtains most of its oxygen through the skin — a frog can survive long dives without surfacing.

Formulas

  • Body to sinus venosus to right atrium. Lungs to pulmonary vein to left atrium. Both to the ventricle. Ventricle to truncus.
  • Three arches on each side: carotid, systemic, pulmocutaneous.
  • Inspiration: nostrils open, floor down, then nostrils shut, floor up, air into lungs.

Practice

  • Why can a frog survive long dives without surfacing?

    Answer. Because it carries a backup breathing surface: the skin. Cutaneous respiration continues underwater — the highly vascular, moist skin draws oxygen directly from the water and dumps CO₂, supplying enough for the frog's low, poikilothermic metabolic rate while it rests on the bottom. Combined with a large oxygen reserve stored in the lungs before diving and a tolerance for oxygen debt, the frog simply switches to skin breathing until it next surfaces.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Why is frog circulation called incomplete double circulation?

    Answer. Double because blood passes through the heart twice per complete circuit — once to the lungs/skin (pulmocutaneous arch) and once to the body (systemic arches). Incomplete because the two atria empty into a single ventricle, where oxygenated and deoxygenated blood partly mix, so the body receives a blended stream. Only the spiral valve in the conus arteriosus keeps the separation roughly functional.

Exercises

1.

Trace a volume of blood from the posterior vena cava until part of it reaches the lungs, and say what is oxygenated on the way back.

Steps
  • The posterior vena cava enters the sinus venosus.
  • The sinus venosus empties into the right atrium.
  • The right atrium contracts into the single ventricle.
  • Ventricular contraction sends the less oxygenated blood into the pulmocutaneous arch.
  • That arch supplies the lung. Blood refreshed in the lung returns by the pulmonary vein to the left atrium, not to the sinus venosus.
Answer

Posterior vena cava to sinus venosus to right atrium to ventricle to pulmocutaneous arch to the lung, and back by the pulmonary vein to the left atrium.

Bounds

  • Digestive organs are the previous note. The reproductive system of the frog is not this topic.
  • Arch names are limited to carotid, systemic and pulmocutaneous.

Summary

The frog's cardiovascular and respiratory systems show the amphibian compromise. A three-chambered heart — two atria and a single ventricle with a spiral-valved conus — drives incomplete double circulation: pulmocutaneous arches send blood to lungs and skin, systemic arches to the body, with some mixing in the single ventricle. Respiration uses three surfaces: the moist vascular skin (dominant in water), the buccal lining (at rest), and paired simple sac lungs (during activity), ventilated by the buccal floor's pumping — nostrils open as the floor lowers, then close as the glottis opens and the floor rises, forcing air into the lungs. Every feature reflects the double life of an amphibian.
