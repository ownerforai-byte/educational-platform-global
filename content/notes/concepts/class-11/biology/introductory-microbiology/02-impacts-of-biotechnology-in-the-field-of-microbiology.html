Enriched Content

Notes
  • DETAILED RECOMBINANT DNA (rDNA) TECHNOLOGY AND ITS APPLICATIONS.
  • rDNA (gene cloning) — 7 steps:
  • Step 1 — Identification + isolation: the gene of interest (e.g., human insulin) is identified and isolated from the source DNA (or synthesised by PCR).
  • Step 2 — Cutting: restriction endonucleases (e.g., EcoRI, BamHI) cut both the source DNA and the vector (plasmid) at specific recognition sites, producing sticky (or blunt) ends.
  • Step 3 — Insertion (ligation): DNA ligase joins the gene into the vector (plasmid), forming recombinant DNA (rDNA).
  • Step 4 — Transformation: the rDNA is introduced into a competent host cell (E. coli) by heat shock, electroporation, or a calcium-chloride method.
  • Step 5 — Selection: the host is grown on a selective medium (e.g., ampicillin); only cells with the plasmid (carrying the antibiotic-resistance gene) survive; blue-white screening (lacZ) identifies recombinants.
  • Step 6 — Expression: the host cell transcribes + translates the inserted gene, producing the desired protein (e.g., insulin).
  • Step 7 — Harvest + purification: the protein is secreted into the culture medium (or inside the cell), harvested, purified (chromatography), and formulated.
  • Vectors: plasmids (most common), bacteriophages, cosmids, BACs, YACs; features: origin of replication (ori), selectable marker (antibiotic resistance), multiple cloning site (MCS), and a high copy number.
  • Host cells: E. coli (prokaryotic, fast, cheap), yeast (eukaryotic, for proteins needing glycosylation), mammalian cells (for complex human proteins).
  • Applications in medicine: recombinant insulin (E. coli, 1982), growth hormone, hepatitis B + HPV vaccines, monoclonal antibodies, gene therapy.
  • Applications in agriculture: Bt cotton (Cry toxin gene → insect resistance), herbicide-tolerant soybean, tissue culture (rapid propagation), biofertilisers (Rhizobium, Azotobacter), biopesticides.
  • Applications in industry: enzyme production (amylase, protease in detergents), bioremediation (oil degradation by Pseudomonas), biofuels (ethanol by yeast), biogas (methanogens).
  • Gene therapy: a functional copy of a defective gene is delivered to the patient's cells via a viral vector (e.g., adeno-associated virus); examples: SCID (1990), cystic fibrosis (trials).
  • Gene editing (CRISPR-Cas9): a programmable nuclease cuts DNA at a specific site; the cell's repair machinery introduces the desired change; derived from the bacterial immune system (2012 Nobel).
  • Ethical considerations: GMO food safety, gene patenting, genetic privacy, environmental release of transgenics, and the creation of "designer babies."
  • NEB focus: detailed rDNA steps, vectors, host cells, insulin production, gene therapy, CRISPR, and the ethical issues of biotechnology.
Confusion
  • Restriction enzymes CUT DNA at specific sequences; DNA ligase JOINS DNA fragments; they are different tools.
  • A plasmid is a small circular DNA molecule used as a VECTOR; it is NOT the gene of interest itself.
  • Transformation (in rDNA) = introducing foreign DNA into a competent cell; it is DIFFERENT from transformation (natural DNA uptake in bacteria).
  • Gene therapy treats a disease by CORRECTING a faulty gene in the patient's cells; gene editing (CRISPR) is a TOOL to make that correction.
  • Bt toxin (from Bacillus thuringiensis) is harmless to humans but lethal to specific insect larvae; it is used in Bt crops.
  • Monoclonal antibodies are produced by a single B-cell clone; they are highly specific and used in diagnostics + therapy.
  • CRISPR-Cas9 is a programmable nuclease; it is NOT the same as a restriction enzyme (which cuts at a fixed, natural sequence).
  • A transgenic organism (GMO) carries a gene from a DIFFERENT species; it is created in the laboratory.

Universal Facts
  • Recombinant human insulin was the first biotech drug approved for human use (1982).
  • The HPV vaccine, derived from recombinant virus-like particles, can prevent cervical cancer.
  • CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, derived from bacterial immune systems, revolutionised genetics in 2012 (Nobel Prize 2020 to Doudna + Charpentier).
  • Bt cotton reduced pesticide use by ~50% in India after its introduction in 2002.

  • Gene therapy: healthy gene → viral vector (AAV) → patient cell → expression → treatment (SCID, 1990).
  • CRISPR-Cas9: guide RNA + Cas9 → cut DNA at a specific site → cell repair → desired change.

Summary

Recombinant DNA (rDNA) technology follows 7 detailed steps: isolate the gene, cut it with restriction enzymes, ligate it into a vector, transform a host cell, select the transformants, express the gene, and harvest the product. Plasmids are the most common vectors (ori, selectable marker, MCS). E. coli is the standard host for simple proteins; yeast and mammalian cells are used for complex, glycosylated proteins. Applications span medicine (insulin, vaccines, gene therapy), agriculture (Bt crops, tissue culture, biofertilisers), and industry (enzymes, bioremediation, biofuels). Gene therapy corrects faulty genes in the patient's cells; gene editing (CRISPR-Cas9) is the tool that makes the correction. Ethical issues (GMO safety, patenting, privacy, environment) are integral to the field. NEB focuses on the detailed rDNA steps, vectors, host cells, insulin production, gene therapy, CRISPR, and the ethical issues of biotechnology.

  • NEB expects the 7 rDNA steps clearly, with the role of each tool (restriction enzyme, ligase, vector, host).
  • The plasmid vector structure (ori, selectable marker, MCS, copy number) is a common exam point.
  • The insulin production pathway (gene → E. coli → fermentation → purification) is a very frequent application question.
  • Gene therapy vs gene editing (CRISPR): know the distinction.
  • Bt crops: mechanism of insect resistance + one environmental concern.
  • Ethical issues of GMOs: environmental, health, and social dimensions.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: single B-cell clone; highly specific; diagnostics + therapy.

Summary

Recombinant DNA (rDNA) technology follows 7 detailed steps: isolate the gene, cut it with restriction enzymes, ligate it into a vector, transform a host cell, select the transformants, express the gene, and harvest the product. Plasmids are the most common vectors (ori, selectable marker, MCS). E. coli is the standard host for simple proteins; yeast and mammalian cells are used for complex, glycosylated proteins. Applications span medicine (insulin, vaccines, gene therapy), agriculture (Bt crops, tissue culture, biofertilisers), and industry (enzymes, bioremediation, biofuels). Gene therapy corrects faulty genes in the patient's cells; gene editing (CRISPR-Cas9) is the tool that makes the correction. Ethical issues (GMO safety, patenting, privacy, environment) are integral to the field. Steps

    1. Ligate: DNA ligase joins the gene into the plasmid
