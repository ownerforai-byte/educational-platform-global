  • 1. Topic Introduction — Life at Its Simplest and Most Successful

    Monera is the kingdom of prokaryotes — organisms whose genetic material is not enclosed in a nucleus. Bacteria are the most successful life forms on Earth: they existed 3.5 billion years ago, live from deep-sea vents to your intestine, and run the planet's nutrient cycles. The syllabus covers bacterial structure, nutrition, growth, and the cyanobacteria.

    2.1 Structure of the bacterial cell (parts in order)

    1. Capsule/slime layer (some): polysaccharide coat — protection against phagocytosis and drying (virulence factor).
    2. Cell wall: rigid peptidoglycan; Gram-positive = thick peptidoglycan, stains violet, teichoic acids; Gram-negative = thin peptidoglycan + outer lipopolysaccharide (LPS) membrane, stains pink, more drug-resistant.
    3. Plasma membrane: selectively permeable; carries respiratory enzymes (no mitochondria — mesosomes are infoldings associated with respiration and cell division).
    4. Cytoplasm: no organelles; contains 70S ribosomes, storage granules (volutin, glycogen), and the nucleoid.
    5. Nucleoid: circular DNA, not enclosed.
    6. Plasmids: small accessory circular DNAs — carry antibiotic resistance, fertility (F) factors.
    7. Flagella: rotary hooks for motility; pili/fimbriae: protein hairs for attachment and conjugation (sex pilus).
    8. **Endospore (some, e.g. Bacillus, Clostridium):** highly resistant resting spore surviving boiling and decades of dormancy.

  • 4. Bacterial Growth

    Binary fission: the chromosome replicates, the cell elongates, a septum divides it — two identical daughter cells in 20–30 minutes under ideal conditions.

    Growth curve (batch culture, four phases):

    PhaseEventNote

    8.2 Model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Characteristic features of bacteria + one beneficial and one harmful example. Answer: Bacteria are unicellular prokaryotes: (1) no nuclear membrane — naked circular DNA (nucleoid) plus plasmids; (2) cell wall of peptidoglycan; (3) 70S ribosomes, no membrane-bound organelles; (4) nutrition autotrophic or heterotrophic; (5) reproduce by binary fission (~20 min). Beneficial: Lactobacillus converts milk to curd (or Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in legumes). Harmful: Vibrio cholerae causes cholera (or Mycobacterium tuberculosis — TB).

    Q2 (3m) — Gram staining; Gram-positive vs Gram-negative. Answer: Gram staining is a differential staining technique (crystal violet → iodine → alcohol decolourisation → safranin) that divides bacteria by wall structure. Gram-positive: thick peptidoglycan retains the crystal-violet–iodine complex, stays violet; teichoic acids present; sensitive to penicillin (e.g. Bacillus, Staphylococcus). Gram-negative: thin peptidoglycan plus an outer LPS membrane loses the dye on decolourisation and takes safranin pink; more resistant to antibiotics (e.g. E. coli, Salmonella).

    Q3 (3m) — Cyanobacteria and nitrogen fixation. Answer: Cyanobacteria are oxygenic photosynthetic prokaryotes with chlorophyll a and phycocyanin ("blue-green algae"), occurring as unicells, colonies or filaments with heterocysts. They fix atmospheric nitrogen with nitrogenase inside the thick-walled, low-oxygen heterocysts, converting N₂ → NH₃ usable by plants. Example: Anabaena azollae lives symbiotically in the water fern Azolla and enriches rice paddies with nitrogen (also Nostoc in soil crusts).

    Q4 (2m) — Four economic importances of microbes. Answer: (1) Dairy — Lactobacillus in curd and cheese; (2) Medicine — antibiotics from Streptomyces; (3) Agriculture — Rhizobium nitrogen fixation and composting; (4) Environment — sewage treatment and biogas production (methanogens).

    8.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling bacteria eukaryotes or giving them mitochondria — prokaryotes have no membrane-bound organelles.
    2. Reversing Gram colours — Gram-positive = violet, Gram-negative = pink.
    3. Saying heterocysts photosynthesise — they fix nitrogen (they lack the oxygen-evolving photosystem).
    4. Writing binary fission as "sexual reproduction" — it is asexual; recombination (conjugation) is separate.
    5. Giving vague "useful/harmful" answers without named examples — examples carry the marks.

Confusion

  • Nucleoid vs nucleus: the nucleoid is an unenclosed DNA region; a nucleus has a double membrane — bacteria have the former only.
  • Heterocyst vs vegetative cell: heterocyst = thick-walled nitrogen-fixing cell without oxygenic photosynthesis; vegetative cells photosynthesise.
  • Pili vs flagella: pili are attachment/conjugation hairs; flagella are rotary motility organelles.
  • Mesosome vs mitochondrion: mesosome = plasma-membrane infolding in bacteria; mitochondrion = eukaryotic organelle absent in prokaryotes.

Practice

  • Why does penicillin kill bacteria but not human cells?

    Answer. Penicillin blocks the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan, the polymer unique to the bacterial cell wall. A bacterium whose wall cannot cross-link bursts under its own osmotic pressure. Human cells have no cell wall at all — our cells are supported by flexible cholesterol-containing membranes — so penicillin has no target in us. This selectivity (attack a structure the pathogen has and the host lacks) is the basic principle of antibiotic therapy.

Universal Facts

  • Bacteria were the only life forms for roughly the first 2 billion years of Earth's history.

Examples

  • Lactobacillus — milk → curd.
  • Rhizobium — legume nodule nitrogen fixation.
  • Nitrosomonas/Nitrobacter — nitrification steps of the nitrogen cycle.
  • Anabaena azollae — symbiont of Azolla; rice biofertiliser.
  • Bacillus/Clostridium — endospore formers; survive boiling.

Practice Questions

  • Q. What are plasmids?

    Answer. Small, circular, self-replicating accessory DNA molecules in bacteria, separate from the nucleoid chromosome. They carry non-essential but useful genes — antibiotic resistance, fertility factors, toxin genes — and copy independently, which is exactly why engineered plasmids serve as the vectors of genetic engineering.

  • Q. What is an endospore?

    Answer. A highly resistant, dehydrated resting structure formed inside certain bacteria (Bacillus, Clostridium). It survives boiling, drying, disinfectants and even years of waiting, and germinates back into a vegetative cell when conditions improve — the reason autoclaving, not mere boiling, sterilises instruments.

Key Points

  • Monera: prokaryotes — no nuclear envelope, 70S ribosomes, peptidoglycan wall, circular DNA + plasmids, binary fission in 20–30 min.
  • Shapes: coccus (diplo/strepto/staphylo), bacillus, spirillum, vibrio. Gram-positive violet/thick peptidoglycan; Gram-negative pink/thin + LPS.
  • Nutrition: photoautotrophic (cyanobacteria), chemoautotrophic (Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter), saprophytic, parasitic, symbiotic (Rhizobium, E. coli).
  • Growth curve: lag → log → stationary → decline.
  • Cyanobacteria: chlorophyll a + phycocyanin, heterocysts fix N2 with nitrogenase (Anabaena in Azolla for rice).

Summary

Monera holds the prokaryotes: bacteria are unicellular cells of 0.5–5 µm with a peptidoglycan wall, 70S ribosomes, a naked circular nucleoid plus plasmids, and no membrane-bound organelles; they take coccus, bacillus, spirillum and vibrio shapes, divide by binary fission in about twenty minutes, and grow through lag, log, stationary and decline phases. They feed as photoautotrophs (cyanobacteria), chemoautotrophs (Nitrosomonas), saprophytes, parasites or symbionts (Rhizobium, E. coli). Cyanobacteria add chlorophyll a, phycocyanin and heterocysts — nitrogenase chambers that fix N2 for rice paddies via Anabaena in Azolla. Economically bacteria give curd, antibiotics, compost, sewage treatment and insulin, but also cholera, typhoid, TB and food spoilage — the twin faces of the most successful cells on Earth.

    5. Giving vague "useful/harmful" answers without named examples — examples carry the marks.
Confusion
  • Nucleoid vs nucleus: the nucleoid is an unenclosed DNA region; a nucleus has a double membrane — bacteria have the former only.
  • Heterocyst vs vegetative cell: heterocyst = thick-walled nitrogen-fixing cell without oxygenic photosynthesis; vegetative cells photosynthesise.
  • Pili vs flagella: pili are attachment/conjugation hairs; flagella are rotary motility organelles.
  • Mesosome vs mitochondrion: mesosome = plasma-membrane infolding in bacteria; mitochondrion = eukaryotic organelle absent in prokaryotes.
Key Points
  • Monera: prokaryotes — no nuclear envelope, 70S ribosomes, peptidoglycan wall, circular DNA + plasmids, binary fission in 20–30 min.
  • Shapes: coccus (diplo/strepto/staphylo), bacillus, spirillum, vibrio. Gram-positive violet/thick peptidoglycan; Gram-negative pink/thin + LPS.
  • Nutrition: photoautotrophic (cyanobacteria), chemoautotrophic (Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter), saprophytic, parasitic, symbiotic (Rhizobium, E. coli).
  • Growth curve: lag → log → stationary → decline.
  • Cyanobacteria: chlorophyll a + phycocyanin, heterocysts fix N2 with nitrogenase (Anabaena in Azolla for rice).
Important Concepts
  • Prokaryote = pre-nucleus: all defining Monera features (size, ribosomes, wall, replication speed) follow from lacking a nuclear envelope and organelles.
Examples
  • Lactobacillus — milk → curd.
  • Rhizobium — legume nodule nitrogen fixation.
  • Nitrosomonas/Nitrobacter — nitrification steps of the nitrogen cycle.
  • Anabaena azollae — symbiont of Azolla; rice biofertiliser.
  • Bacillus/Clostridium — endospore formers; survive boiling.
Universal Facts
  • Bacteria were the only life forms for roughly the first 2 billion years of Earth's history.

Practice Questions
  • Short answer (2m): What are plasmids? — Small, circular, self-replicating accessory DNA molecules in bacteria carrying non-essential but useful genes (antibiotic resistance, fertility factors); the vectors of genetic engineering.
  • Short answer (2m): What is an endospore? — A highly resistant, dehydrated resting structure formed inside certain bacteria (Bacillus, Clostridium) that survives boiling, drying and disinfectants for years; germinates when conditions improve.
