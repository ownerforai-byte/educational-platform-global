Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Borderline of Life

    Viruses straddle the line between living and non-living: inside a host they replicate, evolve and respond; outside it they are inert crystals of protein and nucleic acid. They are the smallest obligate intracellular parasites known, and the bacteriophage that attacks bacteria became the workhorse of molecular biology. The syllabus covers virus structure, importance, and the T2/T4 bacteriophage.

  • 2. General Characteristics of Viruses

    FeatureDetail
    Size20–300 nm — pass through bacterial filters; visible only by electron microscopy
    Nucleic acidEither DNA or RNA, never both; single- or double-stranded
    StructureNucleocapsid = nucleic acid core + capsid (protein coat of capsomeres); some have an envelope (lipid from host membrane with spikes)
    EnzymesAlmost none — host cell supplies everything (some carry reverse transcriptase, neuraminidase, lysozyme)
    MetabolismNone of their own — no ribosomes, no ATP generation
    ReproductionOnly inside a living host cell; assembly-line style (parts made separately, then assembled)
    Host rangeSpecific — animal, plant, bacterial (phage), fungal viruses
    CrystallisationCan be crystallised like chemicals (TMV crystals) — yet retain infectivity

    Why "borderline living" (NEB 2024): viruses show life only inside a host — they replicate, mutate and evolve there; outside, they are inert, non-metabolising, crystallisable particles. They fail most definitions of life (metabolism, independent reproduction, cellular organisation) yet possess heredity and evolution.

  • 3. Structure of T2/T4 Bacteriophage

    The phage is a complex (head-tail) virus attacking E. coli:

    PartDetailFunction
    HeadIcosahedral protein capsid (~95 nm) containing double-stranded DNAProtects and stores the genome
    Collar/whiskersDisc under the headConnects head and tail
    SheathContractile protein tubeDrives genome injection by contraction
    Base plateHexagonal plate with pinsAnchors to host wall
    Tail fibres6 long fibresRecognise and bind host receptor sites

    3.1 Life cycles of the bacteriophage

    Lytic cycle (NEB 2024, 4m):

    1. Adsorption: tail fibres attach to specific receptors on the E. coli wall; base plate settles.
    2. Penetration: sheath contracts; the tail core pierces the wall; phage DNA is injected into the host (the protein coat stays outside — "syringe" action).
    3. Biosynthesis: phage DNA takes over the host machinery; host DNA is degraded; phage nucleic acids and capsid proteins are mass-produced.
    4. Maturation/assembly: DNA heads, tails and fibres assemble into complete virions (~25 min post-infection).
    5. Lysis/release: phage-encoded lysozyme ruptures the wall; ~100–200 new phages burst out and infect other cells.

    Lysogenic cycle: phage DNA integrates into the host chromosome as a prophage; it replicates with the host for generations without killing it; stress (UV etc.) can induce it to enter the lytic cycle (temperate phages, e.g. λ phage).

    6.2 Model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Structure and reproduction of T2 phage + two uses. Answer: Structure: the T2 phage has an icosahedral head holding double-stranded DNA, a collar, a contractile sheath, a hexagonal base plate and six tail fibres that recognise the E. coli host. Reproduction (lytic): adsorption → penetration (DNA injected, sheath contracts) → biosynthesis of phage DNA and proteins using host machinery → maturation/assembly of new virions → lysis by lysozyme releasing 100–200 phages. Uses: (1) phage therapy against antibiotic-resistant bacteria; (2) vectors and tools in genetic engineering/molecular biology (Hershey–Chase proof of DNA as genetic material).

    Q2 (3m) — Why are viruses borderline living/non-living? Answer: Living characters (inside host): they reproduce, mutate and evolve, possess genetic material (DNA or RNA), and show host specificity. Non-living characters (outside host): no metabolism, no respiration, no ribosomes or enzymes of their own, can be crystallised like chemicals (TMV), and remain inert until infection. Because they display life only within a host cell, viruses are placed on the borderline between living and non-living.

    Q3 (4m) — Lytic cycle with diagram description. Answer: (1) Adsorption — tail fibres bind E. coli receptors; (2) Penetration — sheath contracts, DNA injected through the pierced wall; (3) Biosynthesis — host DNA degraded; phage DNA and capsid proteins synthesised on host ribosomes; (4) Maturation — heads, tails and fibres assemble into complete phages; (5) Lysis — lysozyme breaks the wall and ~100–200 virions are released to infect new cells. (Draw: head with DNA, sheath contracting, host cell, intracellular assembly, burst release.)

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying viruses contain both DNA and RNA — only one, never both.
    2. Writing that the whole phage enters the cell — only the DNA is injected; the coat stays outside.
    3. Omitting lysozyme in the lysis step.
    4. Confusing lysogenic (prophage, no host death) with lytic (host lysis).
    5. Forgetting that the capsid is made of capsomeres.

Confusion

  • Lytic vs lysogenic: lytic kills the host immediately; lysogenic integrates as prophage and replicates with the host until induced to lyse.
  • Capsid vs envelope: capsid = protein coat made of capsomeres; envelope = host-derived lipid membrane with spikes (present in influenza, HIV; absent in phage).
  • Virion vs virus: virion = the complete infectious particle outside a cell; "virus" is the broader term.

Practice

  • Why can't viruses be cultured on artificial media like bacteria?

    Answer. Viruses have no metabolism of their own — no ribosomes, no ATP generation, almost no enzymes. They can only be assembled by hijacking a living host cell's machinery, so they replicate solely inside living cells. Bacteria grow on inert nutrient agar because they feed and divide independently; viruses must instead be grown in living systems — bacterial lawns, chick embryos or cell cultures.

Universal Facts

  • Viruses are the most abundant biological entities on Earth — about 10^31 particles, ten times more than bacteria.

Examples

  • T4 phage — complex head-tail virus of E. coli; the lytic-cycle textbook specimen.
  • TMV — helical rod virus; first virus crystallised (Stanley, 1935).
  • HIV — enveloped RNA retrovirus using reverse transcriptase; causes AIDS.

Practice Questions

  • Q. What is a prophage?

    Answer. Phage DNA integrated into the host bacterial chromosome during the lysogenic cycle. It replicates passively with the host's DNA at every cell division and can later be induced (by stress) to excise and enter the lytic cycle.

Key Points

  • Viruses: 20–300 nm, DNA or RNA (never both), nucleocapsid, no metabolism/ribosomes, replicate only in living host cells, crystallisable — borderline of life.
  • T2 phage: head (dsDNA) + collar + contractile sheath + base plate + 6 tail fibres; host = E. coli.
  • Lytic cycle: adsorption → penetration (DNA injection) → biosynthesis → maturation → lysis (lysozyme, 100–200 phages). Lysogenic: prophage integration.
  • Importance: phage therapy, genetic-engineering vectors, Hershey–Chase DNA proof; but influenza, HIV, polio, TMV, foot-and-mouth.

Summary

Viruses are the borderline of life: 20–300 nm particles of a single nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) wrapped in a capsid of capsomeres, sometimes within a host-derived envelope, with no metabolism, no ribosomes and no independent life — yet capable of replication, mutation and evolution inside a host. The T2 bacteriophage adds a contractile tail: head with dsDNA, collar, sheath, base plate and tail fibres that lock onto E. coli. Its lytic cycle — adsorption, penetration, biosynthesis, maturation, lysis — turns one cell into ~200 phages; its lysogenic cycle hides as a prophage. Viruses give medicine phage therapy and molecular biology its greatest tools, but also influenza, AIDS, polio and tobacco mosaic — heredity stripped to its barest form.

Original Content

Notes
  • Viruses: acellular, obligate intracellular parasites; not classified in any kingdom; consist of a nucleic acid core (DNA or RNA) + protein coat (capsid); some have a lipid envelope.
  • Structure: capsomeres (protein subunits of capsid); bacteriophages have a head (icosahedral) + tail; animal viruses may have an envelope with glycoprotein spikes.
  • Types by nucleic acid: DNA viruses (e.g., T4 phage, smallpox) and RNA viruses (e.g., influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2).
  • Bacteriophage (T4): lytic life cycle — adsorption → injection → replication → assembly → release (lysogeny is the lysogenic cycle in lambda phage).
  • Replication: viruses hijack the host cell machinery; they cannot replicate independently.
  • Importance: many human and animal diseases (common cold, flu, HIV/AIDS, hepatitis, rabies, smallpox — eradicated in 1980).
  • Applications: viral vectors in gene therapy, bacteriophages in food safety testing, viruses in molecular biology tools.
  • NEB focus: virus structure, lytic cycle of T4 phage, and importance of viruses.
Confusion
  • Viruses are NOT living organisms: they lack metabolism, cannot reproduce outside a host, and are not made of cells.
  • A virus has EITHER DNA or RNA, never both (with rare exceptions like some retroviruses that convert RNA to DNA).
  • Bacteriophages infect BACTERIA; animal viruses infect animals; plant viruses infect plants.
  • The capsid is protein; the core is nucleic acid; the envelope (if present) is lipid + glycoproteins from the host membrane.
  • Lytic cycle destroys the host cell; lysogenic cycle integrates viral DNA into the host genome (prophage) without immediate lysis.
  • Viruses are NOT visible under a light microscope; they require an electron microscope (~20–300 nm size).

Universal Facts
  • The largest virus (Pithovirus) is ~1.2 µm in diameter — visible only under a light microscope.
  • Smallpox was the first human disease eradicated by vaccination (1980).
  • Retroviruses (e.g., HIV) carry RNA but use reverse transcriptase to make DNA in the host.
  • Viruses are ~1/100th the size of a typical bacterium.
Examples
  • T4 bacteriophage — DNA virus, infects E. coli; lytic cycle model.
  • Influenza virus — RNA virus, causes seasonal flu.
  • HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) — retrovirus, causes AIDS.
  • SARS-CoV-2 — RNA virus, causes COVID-19.

Formulas
  • Virus = Nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) + Capsid (protein) [+ Envelope (lipid + glycoprotein)]
  • Lytic cycle: Adsorption → Penetration (injection) → Biosynthesis → Assembly → Release
  • Lysogenic cycle: Prophage integration → Replication with host → Induction → Lytic cycle
Key Points
  • Viruses are acellular, obligate intracellular parasites.
  • Structure: nucleic acid + capsid (+ envelope in some).
  • DNA or RNA (not both); capsomeres form the capsid.
  • Bacteriophage: icosahedral head + tail + baseplate + tail fibers.
  • Lytic cycle: 5 steps, destroys host; Lysogenic: integrates, delays lysis.
  • Not living: no metabolism, no independent reproduction.
  • Importance: cause many diseases; used in gene therapy and research.
Summary

Viruses are acellular, obligate intracellular parasites that contain either DNA or RNA enclosed in a protein capsid (and sometimes a lipid envelope). They cannot replicate outside a host cell and hijack host machinery. The T4 bacteriophage lytic cycle (adsorption, injection, replication, assembly, release) is the NEB model. Viruses cause many important diseases but are also valuable tools in gene therapy and molecular biology. NEB examinations focus on virus structure, the lytic cycle, and the distinction between viruses and living organisms.

Special Notes
  • NEB expects a detailed T4 phage diagram and the 5-step lytic cycle.
  • The "are viruses alive?" debate is a favourite exam discussion; present both arguments.
  • DNA vs RNA viruses: know the key difference and one example of each.
  • Mention the significance of the viral envelope (immune evasion, cell entry).
Important Statements
  • Viruses are the smallest infectious agents known.
  • They contain either DNA or RNA, but never both.
  • The lytic cycle destroys the host cell; the lysogenic cycle integrates into the host genome.
  • Viruses are not classified in any biological kingdom.
