Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Circles That Never End

    While energy leaks away as heat, matter is forever reused: carbon, nitrogen, water and minerals pass from environment to organism and back in biogeochemical cycles. Meanwhile ecosystems themselves change over centuries — ecological succession — marching toward a stable climax. This topic pairs the two great "circles" of ecology: the circle of matter and the arrow of time.

  • 2. The Carbon Cycle

    ReservoirProcess moving carbon
    Atmosphere (CO₂, ~0.04%)Photosynthesis removes CO₂; respiration returns it
    ProducersFix CO₂ into carbohydrates
    ConsumersEat producers; respire CO₂
    DecomposersBreak dead matter → CO₂ (aerobic) or CH₄ (anaerobic)
    Fossil fuels (coal, oil)Buried carbon; combustion releases CO₂
    OceanDissolves CO₂; shells/corals → limestone (carbonate sink)

    Balance and disruption: volcanic emission, respiration and decomposition release CO₂; photosynthesis and ocean dissolution absorb it. Burning fossil fuels and deforestation add CO₂ faster than sinks absorb it → rising atmospheric CO₂ → greenhouse effect and climate change (Topic 26).

  • 3. The Nitrogen Cycle

    Nitrogen is 78% of the atmosphere but unusable as N₂ by most life — it must be fixed.

    StepProcessAgents
    1. Nitrogen fixationN₂ → NH₃/ammoniumRhizobium (legume nodules), Azotobacter (free-living soil), cyanobacteria heterocysts (Anabaena, Nostoc), lightning, Haber process (industrial fertiliser)

  • 4. Ecological Succession (NEB 2022 & 2024)

    Definition: the directional, progressive and predictable change in the species composition of a community over time, culminating in a stable climax community.

    BasisPrimary successionSecondary succession
    Starts onBare, lifeless substrate (rock, lava, sand)Disturbed area with soil and seeds (burnt forest, abandoned field)
    TimeVery slow (centuries–millennia)Faster (decades) — soil already present
    ExampleXerosere on rock; hydrarch in a pondRegrowth after fire or shifting cultivation

    4.1 Xerosere — succession on dry rock (NEB 2022 & 2024 example)

    1. Pioneer stage — lichens: acids etch the rock; dead lichen matter forms the first thin soil.
    2. Mosses: deeper soil and moisture accumulate.
    3. Herbs and grasses: roots break rock further; more humus.
    4. Shrubs: woody colonisers shade out herbs.
    5. Small trees → climax forest: the stable, self-maintaining community limited by climate.

    Each stage modifies the environment (soil depth, moisture, shade), making it suitable for the next — facilitation — while earlier stages are displaced.

    4.2 Key terms

    • Sere: the whole sequence; each step is a seral stage.
    • Climax community: stable end point in equilibrium with climate.
    • Pioneer species: first colonisers (lichens on rock; phytoplankton in water).
    • Hydrarch succession: in water — phytoplankton → submerged → floating → reed-swamp → marsh → forest (pond fills in).

    6.2 Model answer (3m)

    Q — What is ecological succession? Explain with the example of xerosere. Answer: Ecological succession is the progressive, directional and predictable change in the species composition of a community over time, ending in a stable climax community. Xerosere is primary succession on dry bare rock: (1) lichens — the pioneers — etch the rock with acids and build the first thin soil from their dead bodies; (2) mosses colonise the thin soil, adding organic matter; (3) herbs and grasses follow as soil deepens; (4) shrubs then trees establish, finally forming a stable climax forest. Each stage changes the habitat (soil, moisture, shade) to favour the next.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling succession random — it is directional and predictable.
    2. Confusing primary (bare rock, no soil) with secondary (soil present, e.g. after fire).
    3. Starting xerosere with mosses — lichens are the pioneers.
    4. Writing that the climax is permanent forever — it is stable but can shift with climate change.
    5. Omitting the facilitation logic (each stage prepares the next) — the concept's core.

Confusion

  • Nitrification vs nitrogen fixation: fixation = N₂ → NH₃ (Rhizobium, cyanobacteria); nitrification = NH₃ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ (Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter).
  • Denitrification vs ammonification: denitrification returns NO₃⁻ to N₂ gas (Pseudomonas); ammonification releases NH₃ from dead matter.
  • Primary vs secondary succession: primary starts lifeless (no soil); secondary restarts after disturbance with soil intact.

Practice

  • Why does a waterlogged soil lose fertility through denitrification?

    Answer. Waterlogging fills soil pores with water, so oxygen disappears and the soil turns anaerobic. Anaerobic bacteria (Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus) then reduce nitrate (NO₃⁻) to gaseous N₂ and N₂O, which escape to the air — stripping the soil of the nitrate crops need. Nitrification is blocked at the same time. Remedies: drain and aerate the field, add compost and leguminous green manure (or Rhizobium/cyanobacteria inoculants) to restore fixed nitrogen.

Universal Facts

  • The Haber process now fixes more nitrogen than all natural terrestrial sources combined — humanity's biggest intervention in a biogeochemical cycle.

Examples

  • Lichen on bare rock — the pioneer of xerosere.
  • Rhizobium–legume — biological nitrogen fixation feeding crop rotation.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is a climax community?

    Answer. The stable, self-perpetuating final community of ecological succession, in equilibrium with the regional climate. It has maximum biomass and biodiversity and changes little unless the climate or substrate itself changes (e.g. the mixed forest that ends a xerosere).

Summary

Two great circles govern ecosystems: matter cycles while time marches. In the carbon cycle, photosynthesis and respiration trade CO₂ between air and life, with fossil fuels and limestone as deep sinks — until combustion and deforestation overload the balance. In the nitrogen cycle, N₂ must be fixed — by Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria heterocysts, lightning or the Haber process — then nitrified by Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter, assimilated into proteins, ammonified from dead matter and finally returned to air by denitrifying Pseudomonas. Meanwhile the community itself evolves through succession: on bare rock, xerosere proceeds from lichen pioneers through mosses, herbs and shrubs to a climax forest, each stage preparing the soil for the next — the predictable arrow of ecological time.

Original Content

Notes
  • Biogeochemical cycles: the movement of elements (C, N, P, H, O) through biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) compartments.
  • Carbon cycle: atmospheric CO2 → photosynthesis (plants) → respiration (animals + plants) → decomposition → soil → oceans; combustion (fossil fuels) releases CO2; carbonate rocks (limestone) store carbon long-term.
  • Nitrogen cycle: atmospheric N2 (78%) → nitrogen fixation (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, lightning) → ammonia (NH3) → nitrification (Nitrosomonas → nitrite; Nitrobacter → nitrate) → assimilation (plants) → ammonification (decomposers) → denitrification (Pseudomonas → N2).
  • Phosphorus cycle: rock (apatite) → weathering → soil → plants → animals → decomposition → sediment; no gaseous phase (unlike C, N).
  • Water cycle: evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff → oceans.
  • Succession: the process by which an ecosystem changes over time; primary (on bare rock, no soil) vs secondary (on existing soil).
  • Primary succession: bare rock → lichens/mosses → grasses → shrubs → forest (climax).
  • Secondary succession: after a disturbance (fire, flood, logging) → grasses → shrubs → forest (faster than primary).
  • Climax community: stable, mature community in equilibrium with the environment (e.g., mature oak-rhododendron forest in Nepal).
  • NEB focus: carbon and nitrogen cycles; concept of succession.
Confusion
  • Nitrogen fixation converts N2 to ammonia (NH3/NH4+); nitrification converts ammonia to nitrite then nitrate; denitrification converts nitrate back to N2.
  • Rhizobium (symbiotic, in legume root nodules) and Azotobacter (free-living, in soil) are both nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
  • The carbon cycle has a gaseous phase (CO2); the phosphorus cycle does NOT have a gaseous phase.
  • Primary succession starts on BARE ROCK (no soil); secondary succession starts on EXISTING SOIL (after disturbance).
  • Succession is a PREDICTABLE, directional process toward a stable climax community.
  • Decomposition releases nutrients (C, N, P) back into the soil; without it, cycles would stop.

  • Atmospheric nitrogen (N2) makes up ~78% of the air but is unusable by most organisms until fixed.
  • Carbon dioxide is the main gaseous form of carbon in the carbon cycle.

Examples
  • Carbon cycle: forest (photosynthesis absorbs CO2) → animals (respiration releases CO2) → decomposers (release CO2) → atmosphere → ocean (dissolved CO2) → limestone (long-term storage).
  • Nitrogen cycle: Rhizobium in pea root nodules fixes N2 → plants assimilate nitrate → animals eat plants → decomposers ammonify → denitrifying bacteria release N2.
  • Primary succession: bare rock after a volcanic eruption → lichens → mosses → grasses → shrubs → forest.
  • Secondary succession: after a forest fire in Nepal → regrowth of grasses → shrubs → trees (faster than primary).
  • Climax community: a mature, stable oak-rhododendron forest on the Nepal hills.

Formulas
  • Carbon cycle: CO2 ↔ organic C (photosynthesis/respiration/decomposition); fossil fuel combustion → CO2
  • Nitrogen cycle: N2 → (fixation) NH3 → (nitrification) NO2- → NO3- → (assimilation) plants → (ammonification) NH3 → (denitrification) N2
  • Succession: primary (bare rock) / secondary (existing soil) → climax community
Key Points
  • Biogeochemical cycles: C, N, P, H, O move through biotic + abiotic compartments.
  • Carbon cycle: gaseous (CO2); photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion.
  • Nitrogen cycle: N2 → NH3 → NO2- → NO3- → assimilation → ammonification → N2.
  • Bacteria: Rhizobium (fixation), Nitrosomonas (nitrification), Pseudomonas (denitrification).
  • Phosphorus cycle: no gaseous phase; slowest cycle.
  • Succession: primary (bare rock) vs secondary (existing soil); climax community is stable.
  • Climax: mature, stable community in equilibrium with the environment.
Summary

Biogeochemical cycles move essential elements through living and non-living compartments. The carbon cycle (gaseous CO2) and the nitrogen cycle (N2 → NH3 → NO2- → NO3- → N2) are the most important; bacteria (Rhizobium, Nitrosomonas, Pseudomonas) drive key steps. The phosphorus cycle has no gaseous phase and is the slowest. Succession is the directional change of an ecosystem toward a stable climax community: primary succession starts on bare rock, secondary on existing soil. NEB focuses on the carbon and nitrogen cycles and the concept of succession.

  • Primary vs secondary succession: key difference is the starting point (bare rock vs existing soil).
  • Climax community: give a Nepali example (mature oak-rhododendron forest).
  • Note that the phosphorus cycle has no gaseous phase.
Important Statements
  • Biogeochemical cycles recycle essential elements through biotic and abiotic compartments.
  • Nitrogen fixation converts atmospheric N2 to usable ammonia.
  • Primary succession begins on bare rock; secondary succession on existing soil.
  • A climax community is a stable, mature ecosystem in equilibrium with its environment.

Exercises
1.

In the nitrogen cycle, a plant absorbs nitrate (NO3-). What does the plant use it for, and how does the nitrogen eventually return to the atmosphere?

ID

1

Steps
  • Plant uses NO3- to make amino acids/proteins (assimilation)
  • Animal eats plant; N moves to animal proteins
  • Decomposers break down dead tissue → ammonia (ammonification)
  • Nitrosomonas → nitrite; Nitrobacter → nitrate
  • Pseudomonas (denitrifying bacteria) convert NO3- to N2 gas
Answer

The plant uses nitrate to build proteins; nitrogen returns to the atmosphere via denitrifying bacteria (Pseudomonas) converting nitrate to N2
