Desc

Two separate problems: greenhouse gases warm the lower air, and chlorine thins stratospheric ozone.

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — When the Balance Breaks

    Ecosystems self-regulate — until humans push them past their limits. Ecological imbalances are human-driven disruptions: the greenhouse effect warming the planet, the ozone hole letting in UV, acid rain poisoning forests and lakes, invasive species rewriting communities, and eutrophication suffocating waters. This topic is the syllabus's bridge to environmental responsibility — and a frequent source of short-answer questions.

  • 2. Greenhouse Effect and Climate Change

    Mechanism: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, water vapour and CFCs are transparent to incoming sunlight but absorb outgoing infrared heat — like glass in a greenhouse. Human activities add these gases: fossil-fuel burning (CO₂), livestock and rice paddies and landfills (CH₄), fertilisers (N₂O), refrigerants (CFCs).

    EffectDetail
    Global warming~1.1 °C average rise since pre-industrial times; CO₂ above 420 ppm
    Melting ice and sea-level riseHimalayan glaciers shrink; coastal flooding threat to low-lying Terai and island nations
    Extreme weatherErratic monsoons, floods (e.g. 2021 Melamchi disaster), droughts, heatwaves
    Ecosystem shiftsSpecies ranges move upslope; coral bleaching; phenology mismatch
    AgricultureReduced yields, new pests, food insecurity

    Mitigation: renewable energy, afforestation, energy efficiency, international agreements (Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement 2015 — limit warming well below 2 °C).

  • 3. Depletion of the Ozone Layer

    AspectDetail
    OzoneO₃ in the stratosphere (15–35 km) absorbs harmful UV-B/UV-C — "Earth's sunscreen"
    CulpritsCFCs (refrigerants, aerosols, foam), halons, methyl bromide — one chlorine atom destroys ~100,000 ozone molecules
    Effect of depletionMore UV-B → skin cancer, cataracts, immune suppression; reduced phytoplankton and crop yields; damage to amphibian eggs
    ResponseMontreal Protocol (1987) — phase-out of CFCs; layer now slowly recovering

    Antarctic ozone hole: seasonal severe thinning discovered in 1985 — the reason the Montreal Protocol became the most successful environmental treaty.

  • 4. Acid Rain

    Mechanism: SO₂ (coal-burning, smelters) and NOₓ (vehicle exhaust) react with water vapour → H₂SO₄ and HNO₃, falling as rain with pH < 5.6.

    EffectDetail
    ForestsLeaf damage, soil nutrient leaching (Ca, Mg), aluminium release kills roots
    LakespH drop kills fish eggs and invertebrates ("dead lakes")
    Soil and monumentsNutrient loss; marble and limestone erosion (Taj Mahal damage)
    Human healthRespiratory irritation

    Control: scrubbers on chimneys, catalytic converters, low-sulphur fuel, renewable energy.

  • Three imbalances — greenhouse, ozone hole, acid rain

  • 5. Biological Invasion

    Definition: introduction of species outside their native range that establish, spread and harm native ecosystems, economies or health.

    StageDetail
    IntroductionDeliberate (ornamentals, aquaculture) or accidental (ship ballast, timber, soil)
    Establishment and spreadFew predators/parasites in new range → rapid growth (enemy-release)
    ImpactOutcompete natives, prey on them, alter habitats, hybridise

    Examples: Eichhornia crassipes (water hyacinth) choking lakes and hydropower canals of Nepal; Mikania micrantha (banmara) smothering Chitwan's forests; Ageratum, lantana; brown tree snake on Guam; water lettuce.

    Control: quarantine and border inspection, mechanical removal, biological control (weevils against water hyacinth), early-detection rapid response.

  • 6. Eutrophication (NEB 2024)

    Definition: the over-enrichment of water bodies with nutrients (mainly N and P), causing excessive algal growth and oxygen depletion.

    Causes: fertiliser runoff from farms, sewage and detergents (phosphates), livestock waste, industrial effluent.

    Sequence of effects:

    1. Nutrient influx → algal bloom (surface green scum; cyanobacteria).
    2. Bloom blocks sunlight → submerged plants die.
    3. Dead algae and plants sink → decomposers multiply and consume dissolved oxygen.
    4. Hypoxia/anoxia → fish and invertebrates die ("dead zones"); anaerobic decay releases foul-smelling gases (H₂S, CH₄).
    5. Water becomes turbid, toxic (some cyanobacterial toxins), unusable.

    Control: treat sewage, phosphate-free detergents, buffer strips and controlled fertilisation, restore wetlands (natural nutrient filters).

    8.2 Model answer

    Q (3m) — What is eutrophication? Explain its causes and effects on aquatic life. Answer: Eutrophication is the over-enrichment of a water body with nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus), leading to excessive algal growth and oxygen depletion. Causes: fertiliser runoff from farmland, sewage and phosphate detergents, livestock and industrial effluents. Effects: (1) algal blooms block sunlight, killing submerged plants; (2) decomposers breaking the dead bloom consume dissolved oxygen, causing hypoxia; (3) fish and other aquatic organisms die of oxygen starvation — the water becomes turbid, foul-smelling and sometimes toxic (cyanobacterial toxins), collapsing the aquatic ecosystem.

    8.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Calling the greenhouse effect purely bad — the natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth habitable (~15 °C instead of −18 °C); the problem is its enhancement.
    2. Confusing ozone depletion (CFCs, UV) with global warming (CO₂, heat) — different gases, different effects.
    3. Writing acid rain as "acid in factories" — it is SO₂/NOₓ reacting with atmospheric water.
    4. Omitting the oxygen-depletion sequence in eutrophication — the causal chain carries the marks.
    5. Naming invasive species without their impact.
Confusion
  • Ozone hole vs global warming: ozone depletion = CFCs destroying stratospheric O₃ → more UV; global warming = greenhouse gases trapping heat → higher temperature. Different problems, different treaties (Montreal vs Paris).
  • Eutrophication vs pollution generally: eutrophication is specifically nutrient over-enrichment — not any contamination.

Practice

  • Why is the Montreal Protocol considered more successful than climate treaties?

    Answer. Because its problem was chemically narrow and its solution was cheap: only a single family of chemicals (CFCs/halons), made by a few companies, with ready substitutes and a measurable atmospheric target. Trade sanctions and a Multilateral Fund paid developing countries to switch, so compliance was near-universal and the ozone layer is now recovering. CO₂, by contrast, is emitted by every economy's core energy system, has no single substitute and its costs and benefits fall on different generations and countries — which is why climate treaties struggle where Montreal succeeded.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Why is the natural greenhouse effect beneficial?

    Answer. Without any greenhouse gases the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C; the natural effect (CO₂, CH₄, water vapour) keeps it near +15 °C, making liquid water and life possible. The problem is not the natural effect but the human-enhanced addition of CO₂, CH₄, N₂O and CFCs, which is pushing the balance past the comfortable range.

Exercises

1.

By how many degrees does the natural greenhouse effect warm the Earth, using −18 °C and +15 °C?

Steps
  • A planet with no greenhouse gases would average about −18 °C.
  • The observed mean is about +15 °C.
  • The difference is 15 − (−18) = 33.
  • So the natural blanket is worth about 33 °C.
Answer

About 33 °C.

Summary

Human activity pushes five imbalances past nature's feedback limits. The enhanced greenhouse effect — CO₂, CH₄, N₂O and CFCs trapping outgoing heat — drives warming, melting Himalayan glaciers, rising seas and erratic monsoons. CFCs shred stratospheric ozone, Earth's UV sunscreen, inviting skin cancer and plankton loss until the Montreal Protocol acted. SO₂ and NOₓ become sulfuric and nitric acid in rain, poisoning forests, lakes and marble. Biological invaders — water hyacinth, banmara, lantana — arrive without their enemies and smother native communities. And eutrophication runs its lethal sequence: nutrient runoff, algal bloom, shaded death, oxygen-hungry decomposition, fish kill. Each is a cycle the syllabus taught — carbon, nitrogen, community balance — now broken by excess.

  • Noise

  • Oxygen depletion from decomposition
  • High pH
  • Cold water
