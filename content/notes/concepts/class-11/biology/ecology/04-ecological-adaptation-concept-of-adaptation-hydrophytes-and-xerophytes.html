Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Life Tailored to Water and Drought

    Adaptation is the fit between organism and habitat, achieved by evolution. Plants forced to live in water (hydrophytes) or deserts (xerophytes) solve opposite problems with opposite equipment — and comparing them is the classic examination exercise. The syllabus names three habitat types: hydrophytes, xerophytes and (between them) mesophytes.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts — The Three Plant Habit Types

    TypeHabitatProblemExamples
    HydrophytesIn or on water (ponds, lakes, streams)Excess water; little support; low light underwater; poor aerationHydrilla, Eichhornia (water hyacinth), Nymphaea (lotus), Typha, Vallisneria
    MesophytesModerate water (most land plants)None extremeMango, wheat, garden plants
    XerophytesDry habitats (deserts, rocks, saline soils)Water deficit, heat, high lightOpuntia, Aloe, Casuarina, Nerium (oleander), Acacia, Euphorbia

  • 7. Exam Orientation

    7.1 Previous year questions

    No adaptation question exists in the authored bank. Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Describe three adaptations of hydrophytes. Answer: (1) Aerenchyma — air-filled spaces in stem and petioles give buoyancy and conduct oxygen to roots in oxygen-poor mud; (2) stomata on the upper surface only of floating leaves (lotus) or no stomata in submerged forms, with a thin cuticle — gas exchange with air without excess tissue; (3) reduced roots and vascular tissue — water bathes the body, so support and conduction are minimised (e.g. Hydrilla, Nymphaea).

    Q2 (3m) — Describe three adaptations of xerophytes. Answer: (1) Leaves reduced to spines (Opuntia) with the green succulent stem taking over photosynthesis (phylloclade) — transpiring surface minimised, water stored; (2) sunken stomata with a thick waxy cuticle (Nerium) — humid air is trapped in pits, cutting water loss; (3) deep extensive tap roots (Acacia) reaching the water table. (Also: CAM — night-open stomata; ephemeral life cycles.)

    Q3 (2m) — Why do floating leaves have stomata only on the upper surface? Answer: The lower surface touches water and cannot exchange gases with air; stomata on the upper surface open directly to the atmosphere, allowing transpiration and gas exchange.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Giving hydrophytes "thick cuticle" — it is thin or absent.
    2. Calling spines xerophyte leaves modified for defence only — their primary role here is reducing transpiration.
    3. Forgetting aerenchyma — the signature hydrophyte tissue.
    4. Mixing mesophyte traits into both lists.

Confusion

  • Aerenchyma vs parenchyma: aerenchyma is parenchyma with large air spaces — buoyancy and aeration; ordinary parenchyma packs tissue.
  • Phylloclade vs cladode: phylloclade = flattened stem doing leaf's job (Opuntia); cladode = one-internode stem-leaf (Asparagus).
  • Xerophyte vs halophyte: xerophytes face water deficit generally; halophytes face salt stress (mangroves with salt glands).

Practice

  • Why do submerged leaves take dissected or ribbon shapes?

    Answer. Underwater leaves face weak light, little CO₂ and moving water. A finely dissected or ribbon-like blade (e.g. Vallisneria, Hydrilla)

    1. offers minimal resistance to the current, so it does not tear,
    2. gives a very large surface-to-volume ratio for light absorption and direct gas and mineral diffusion through the thin surface, and
    3. avoids self-shading in dim water.

    Stomata would be useless underwater, so the whole thin surface does the exchange instead.

Universal Facts

  • CAM plants lose 3–6 times less water per unit carbon fixed than ordinary C3 plants.

Examples

  • Opuntia — phylloclade: the cactus "leaf" that is really a water-storing photosynthetic stem.
  • Nymphaea (lotus) — floating leaf with upper-surface stomata and aerenchymatous petioles.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): What is aerenchyma?

    Answer. A modified parenchyma tissue with large intercellular air spaces, characteristic of hydrophytes. It gives buoyancy to floating organs and conducts oxygen down to the roots buried in oxygen-poor anaerobic mud.

Formulas

  • Hydrophyte adaptations: aerenchyma + floating leaves + reduced roots + upper-surface stomata + thin cuticle
  • Xerophyte adaptations: thick cuticle + spines (modified leaves) + succulent stem + deep roots + CAM photosynthesis
  • CAM photosynthesis: fix CO2 at night (malic acid) → decarboxylate by day (reduced water loss)

Key Points

  • Hydrophytes: aerenchyma, reduced roots/xylem, thin cuticle, stomata upper surface (floating) or absent (submerged), dissected submerged leaves, mucilage — Hydrilla, Nymphaea, Eichhornia.
  • Xerophytes: deep tap roots, succulent stems (phylloclade), spiny leaves, sunken stomata, thick cuticle, CAM, ephemeral cycles — Opuntia, Aloe, Nerium, Acacia.
  • Mesophytes: moderate water, no special equipment.

Summary

Plants adapt to their water regime with opposite toolkits. Hydrophytes — Hydrilla, lotus, water hyacinth — live with excess: aerenchyma for buoyancy and aeration, reduced roots and xylem, thin cuticles, floating leaves with upper-surface stomata, dissected submerged leaves. Xerophytes — Opuntia, Aloe, Nerium, Acacia — live with deficit: deep tap roots, succulent water-storing stems, leaves reduced to spines, sunken stomata under thick cuticles, CAM night-breathing and ephemeral life cycles. Between them stand the unspecialised mesophytes. The contrast table — root, stem, leaf, cuticle, vascular tissue — is the exam's favourite framework, and each row teaches the same lesson: evolution solves the problem the habitat poses, no more and no less.

The characteristic tissue of hydrophytes providing buoyancy is:

In Opuntia, photosynthesis is performed by the:

  • Phylloclade (flattened stem)

B

Explanation

Leaves are reduced to spines; the succulent green stem (phylloclade) photosynthesises and stores water.

Sunken stomata are found in:

Options
  • Hydrophytes
  • Xerophytes
  • Mesophytes
  • All plants
Answer

B

Explanation

Stomata in pits trap humid air, reducing transpiration — a xerophyte trait (Nerium).

Xerophyte roots are typically:

Options
  • Reduced or absent
  • Deep and extensive
  • Floating
  • Absent with rhizoids

B

Deep tap roots reach distant water tables — the opposite of reduced hydrophyte roots.

Stomata in floating leaves like lotus occur:

Options
  • On both surfaces
  • Only on the upper surface
  • Only on the lower surface
  • Not at all

CAM metabolism helps xerophytes by:

  • Storing salt

Xylem is poorly developed in hydrophytes because:

Original Content

Notes
  • Ecological adaptation: the structural, physiological, and behavioural features that allow an organism to survive in a particular environment.
  • Adaptation types: structural (body form), physiological (internal processes), behavioural (activities).
  • Hydrophytes (water plants): adapted to aquatic life; examples: water hyacinth (Nymphaea lotus), lotus, Vallisneria.
  • Hydrophyte adaptations: aerenchyma (air spaces in tissues for gas exchange), floating leaves, reduced root system, stomata on upper surface, no cuticle or thin cuticle.
  • Xerophytes (dry-land plants): adapted to arid conditions; examples: cacti, Casuarina, Prosopis.
  • Xerophyte adaptations: thick cuticle, reduced leaves (spines in cacti), deep or extensive root system, succulent stem, CAM photosynthesis, stomata in pits.
  • Mesophytes: plants of moderate moisture (most temperate plants); intermediate adaptations.
  • NEB focus: concept of adaptation, hydrophytes, and xerophytes with their adaptations.
  • Adaptation is the result of natural selection over generations; it is not "intentional" or "goal-directed".
Confusion
  • Hydrophytes have AERENCHYMA (air spaces) for gas exchange; xerophytes have reduced surface area to conserve water.
  • Cacti's "spines" are modified LEAVES (to reduce water loss); the stem is the photosynthetic organ (succulent).
  • Xerophytes often use CAM photosynthesis (open stomata at night to reduce water loss); hydrophytes use the C3 pathway with stomata on the upper surface.
  • Hydrophytes may have REDUCED roots (they float; roots are for anchorage, not water uptake).
  • Adaptation is a POPULATION-LEVEL feature (evolution over generations), NOT an individual changing during its life.
  • Acclimatisation (short-term, individual adjustment) is different from adaptation (long-term, evolutionary).

Universal Facts
  • Cacti can survive with as little as 10% of the water a typical plant needs.
  • Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) is an invasive species in many tropical water bodies, including Nepal.
  • Xerophytes can close their stomata during the day to reduce transpiration (stomatal closure).
  • Aerenchyma in hydrophytes provides buoyancy and gas exchange in waterlogged environments.
Examples
  • Hydrophyte (water hyacinth): aerenchyma, floating leaves, reduced roots, stomata on upper surface, thin cuticle.
  • Xerophyte (cactus): thick cuticle, spines (modified leaves), succulent stem, deep roots, CAM photosynthesis.
  • Mesophyte (oak, rhododendron): moderate water; normal cuticle, normal leaves, roots in moist soil.
  • Structural adaptation: cactus spines (reduced leaf area).
  • Physiological adaptation: CAM photosynthesis in cacti.

Summary

Ecological adaptation refers to the structural, physiological, and behavioural features that allow an organism to survive in its environment. Hydrophytes (water plants) have aerenchyma, floating leaves, reduced roots, and thin cuticles for aquatic life. Xerophytes (dry-land plants) have thick cuticles, spines (modified leaves), succulent stems, deep roots, and CAM photosynthesis for arid conditions. Mesophytes of moderate moisture have intermediate adaptations. Adaptation is an evolutionary (population-level) process, distinct from short-term acclimatisation. NEB focuses on the concept of adaptation, hydrophytes, and xerophytes with their specific adaptations.

Special Notes
  • NEB expects a clear hydrophyte vs xerophyte comparison with specific adaptations.
  • The cactus spines (modified leaves) and succulent stem are key exam points.
  • CAM photosynthesis (xerophytes) is a frequent physiological question.
  • Differentiate structural, physiological, and behavioural adaptations.
  • Note that adaptation is evolutionary, not individual (acclimatisation is individual).
Important Statements
  • Adaptation is the result of natural selection over generations.
  • Hydrophytes have aerenchyma for gas exchange in water.
  • Xerophytes conserve water via thick cuticles, reduced leaves, and CAM photosynthesis.
  • Adaptation is a population-level (evolutionary) feature, not an individual change.

Exam Short Tricks
  • Hydrophyte = water + air spaces (aerenchyma) + thin cuticle.
  • Xerophyte = dry + thick cuticle + spines + CAM.
  • Cactus spines = modified leaves (not needles).
  • Adaptation = evolution; Acclimatisation = individual.
