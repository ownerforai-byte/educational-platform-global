Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Science of Home

    Ecology (Greek oikos = house) studies how organisms interact with each other and with their physical surroundings. It is organised in levels — organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere — and every environmental question of our time, from climate change to conservation, is answered at one of these levels.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts — The Levels of Ecological Organisation

    LevelDefinitionExample
    OrganismOne individual and its responses to environmentA single tiger and its territory
    PopulationGroup of the same species in an area at a timeAll the tigers of Bardiya National Park
    CommunityAll populations of different species in an area interactingTigers + deer + grasses + microbes of Bardiya
    EcosystemCommunity + its physical environment functioning as a unitThe Terai grassland ecosystem
    BiomeLarge regional community defined by climateTropical deciduous forest biome
    BiosphereAll life-bearing parts of Earth (land + water + air)The global sum of ecosystems

    2.1 Abiotic components (the physical stage)

    ComponentRoleExamples
    ClimaticEnergy and water regimeLight, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind
    EdaphicSoil conditionsSoil texture, pH, minerals, moisture
    TopographicLandscape effectsAltitude, slope, aspect
    ChemicalElement availabilityDissolved O₂, CO₂, salinity, nutrients (N, P)

    2.2 Biotic components (the living cast)

    TypeRoleExamples
    Producers (autotrophs)Make food from sunlight/inorganic matterGreen plants, algae, cyanobacteria
    Consumers (heterotrophs)Eat other organismsPrimary (herbivores — deer), secondary (frog), tertiary (snake), top carnivore (tiger)
    Decomposers (saprotrophs)Break dead matter into nutrientsBacteria, fungi — return elements to the cycle

    InteractionSpecies ASpecies BNatureExample
    Mutualism (+/+)BenefitsBenefitsObligate or facultative cooperationBee–flower (pollination for nectar); lichen (alga + fungus); Rhizobium–legume

    5.2 Model answer

    Q (3m) — Any three types of population interaction with one example each. Answer: (1) Mutualism (+/+) — both benefit: bees get nectar while flowers get pollinated (also Rhizobium in legume nodules). (2) Parasitism (+/−) — one benefits at the other's expense: Plasmodium vivax parasitises human blood, causing malaria. (3) Commensalism (+/0) — one benefits, other unaffected: epiphytic orchids grow on mango branches gaining support without harming the tree. (Other acceptable pairs: predation — tiger–deer; competition — Paramecium species; amensalism — Penicillium antibiosis.)

    5.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Confusing commensalism with mutualism — check whether both benefit.
    2. Calling predation "parasitism" — predation kills quickly; parasites usually keep the host alive.
    3. Omitting the +/- notation, which examiners reward.
    4. Giving plant examples only — mix animals, microbes and plants.

Confusion

  • Population vs community: population = one species; community = all species in the area.
  • Commensalism vs mutualism: commensalism benefits one partner only (+/0); mutualism benefits both (+/+).
  • Niche vs habitat: habitat = where it lives (address); niche = its role and resource use (profession).

Practice

  • Why is a predator-prey cycle always out of phase with each other?

Universal Facts

  • Decomposers recycle the chemicals that producers need — without them, ecosystems would drown in dead matter and starve.

Examples

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Distinguish habitat from niche. — Habitat is the physical place a species lives (its address); niche is its functional role — what it eats, when it is active, how it reproduces (its profession). Two species may share a habitat but differ in niche.

Formulas

  • Ecosystem = Biotic (producers + consumers + decomposers) + Abiotic (light, water, soil, gases)
  • Species interactions: mutualism (+/+), commensalism (+/0), parasitism (+/-), predation (+/-), competition (-/-)
  • Energy flow: Sun → Producer → Primary consumer → Secondary consumer → ... → Heat (unidirectional)

Key Points

  • Levels: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere.
  • Abiotic: climatic, edaphic, topographic, chemical. Biotic: producers, consumers (1°/2°/3°), decomposers.
  • Interactions: mutualism (+/+ bee-flower), commensalism (+/0 orchid-tree), parasitism (+/− Plasmodium), predation (+/− tiger-deer), competition (−/− Gause's Paramecium), amensalism (−/0 Penicillium).

Summary

Ecology studies life's levels — from a single organism through populations, communities and ecosystems to biomes and the biosphere. Ecosystems combine abiotic factors (light, temperature, soil, topography, chemicals) with biotic ones: producers capture energy, consumers pass it along trophic levels, and decomposers return nutrients. Between species, six interactions define community structure: mutualism (bee–flower), commensalism (orchid on mango), parasitism (Plasmodium), predation (tiger–deer), competition (Gause's Paramecium, driving competitive exclusion) and amensalism (Penicillium antibiosis). Each interaction is a sign pattern (+/+, +/0, +/−, −/−, −/0) — and each shapes who lives, who loses and who coexists.

Original Content

Notes
  • Ecology: the study of the interactions between organisms and their environment (biotic + abiotic).
  • Biotic factors: living components (plants, animals, microorganisms); Abiotic factors: non-living (light, temperature, water, soil, pH, gases).
  • Species interactions: mutualism (+/+), commensalism (+/0), parasitism (+/-), predation (+/-), competition (-/-), amensalism (-/0), neutralism (0/0).
  • Ecosystem: a functional unit of biotic + abiotic components interacting; types: aquatic (pond, lake, ocean) and terrestrial (grassland, forest, desert).
  • Structural aspects: producers (autotrophs), consumers (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores), decomposers (bacteria, fungi); trophic levels.
  • Functional aspects: energy flow (unidirectional, sun → producers → consumers → heat), nutrient cycling (biogeochemical), and food chains/webs.
  • Pond and forest ecosystems: producers (phytoplankton / trees), consumers (zooplankton / herbivores / carnivores), decomposers; abiotic factors (light, temperature, water, soil).
  • Nepal: Terai, hills, and mountains each have distinct ecosystems (e.g., Chitwan Terai, temperate oak-rhododendron hills, alpine zone).
  • NEB focus: concept of ecology, biotic/abiotic factors, species interactions, and structural + functional aspects of pond and forest ecosystems.
Confusion
  • Predation (+/-) and parasitism (+/-) both harm one partner; predation usually KILLS the prey quickly, parasitism is long-term and usually does not kill the host immediately.
  • Mutualism (+/+) benefits both; commensalism (+/0) benefits one, affects neither; parasitism (+/-) benefits one, harms the other.
  • Competition (-/-) harms BOTH species (interspecific); intra-specific competition is within the same species.
  • An ecosystem is a FUNCTIONAL unit (energy flow + nutrient cycling); a community is just the assemblage of species.
  • Energy flows ONEDIRECTIONALLY through an ecosystem (sun → producers → consumers → heat); it is NOT cycled.
  • Nutrients (C, N, P) ARE cycled (biogeochemical); energy is not cycled.

Universal Facts
  • A single hectare of a tropical forest can contain 100+ tree species.
  • The sun is the primary energy source of almost all ecosystems.
  • Decomposers recycle nutrients back into the soil; without them, life would stop.
  • Nepal's Chitwan National Park is a Terai ecosystem; Sagarmatha is a high-mountain ecosystem.
Examples
  • Pond ecosystem: phytoplankton (producer) → zooplankton (herbivore) → small fish (carnivore) → big fish; bacteria + fungi (decomposer).
  • Forest ecosystem: trees + grass (producers) → deer, insects (herbivores) → tiger, owl (carnivores) → fungi, bacteria (decomposers).
  • Mutualism: bee + flower; commensalism: barnacle + whale; parasitism: tapeworm + human.
  • Competition: two grass species competing for light in a meadow.

Summary

Ecology studies the interactions between organisms and their environment. Biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) factors shape every ecosystem. Species interact through mutualism, commensalism, parasitism, predation, and competition. An ecosystem is a functional unit of biotic and abiotic components; its structural aspects (producers, consumers, decomposers, trophic levels) and functional aspects (unidirectional energy flow + cyclic nutrient cycling) define how it operates. Pond and forest ecosystems (e.g., Chitwan Terai, temperate hills) illustrate these principles in the Nepali context.
