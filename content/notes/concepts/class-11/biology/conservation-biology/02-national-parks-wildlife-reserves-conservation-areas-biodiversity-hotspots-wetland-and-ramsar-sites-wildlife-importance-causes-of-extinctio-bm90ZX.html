Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Wildlife Matters and Why It Vanishes

    Wildlife's value spans ecology (pollination, seed dispersal, nutrient cycling), economy (tourism, medicine, genetics), science (research, indicator species) and ethics (every species' right to exist). Extinction is natural — but the current rate, driven by humans, is hundreds of times the background rate. This topic covers wildlife's importance, the causes of extinction, conservation strategies and the IUCN categories that rank threat.

  • 2. Importance of Wildlife

    DomainContributions
    EcologicalPollination (bees, bats), seed dispersal (birds, monkeys), nutrient cycling, pest control (insectivorous birds), population regulation
    EconomicTourism revenue (tiger- and rhino-watching in Nepal), timber and non-timber forest products, fisheries
    ScientificModel organisms for research; bioindicators of environmental health
    GeneticWild relatives of crops and livestock — reservoirs for future breeding
    Aesthetic-ethicalCultural and spiritual values; every species' intrinsic worth
    MedicinalSources of drugs — many derived first from wild species

  • 3. Causes of Extinction

    CauseMechanismExample
    Habitat destruction and fragmentationForests cleared, rivers dammed, land convertedTerai forest loss; road fragmentation
    OverexploitationHunting, poaching, overfishing beyond recoveryRhino poaching for horn; tiger poaching for bone
    PollutionToxins accumulate or degrade habitatPesticide residues thinning raptor eggshells
    Invasive speciesAliens outcompete or prey on nativesMikania in Chitwan; water hyacinth in lakes
    DiseasePathogens spread as contact with humans/livestock growsAvian malaria in native birds
    Climate changeHabitats shift faster than species can moveUpward shift of alpine species; glacier retreat
    Natural causesVolcanism, asteroid impacts, disease (background rate)Mass extinctions of geological history

    3.1 The extinction vortex

    Small populations suffer inbreeding, lose genetic diversity, produce fewer viable offspring, shrink further — a feedback loop driving them extinct even after threats are removed.

  • 4. Conservation Strategies

    1. In-situ: national parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas, buffer zones (Topic 53).
    2. Ex-situ: zoos, captive breeding and reintroduction (e.g. rhino translocations from Chitwan to Bardia), botanical gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation.
    3. Legal protection: national laws (National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act 2029), international conventions — CITES (trade in endangered species), CBD (biodiversity convention), Ramsar (wetlands), World Heritage.
    4. Community-based conservation: community forestry, buffer-zone user groups, conservation area management by locals.
    5. Restoration ecology: replanting, wetland restoration, corridor establishment between fragments.
    6. Education and research: population monitoring, awareness, citizen science.

  • 5. IUCN Categories of Threatened Species

    CategoryMeaning
    Extinct (EX)No reasonable doubt the last individual has died
    Extinct in the Wild (EW)Survives only in cultivation or captivity
    Critically Endangered (CR)Extremely high risk of extinction in the wild (≥90% decline or tiny range/population)
    Endangered (EN)Very high risk of extinction in the wild
    Vulnerable (VU)High risk of extinction in the medium term
    Near Threatened (NT)Close to qualifying for a threatened category
    Least Concern (LC)Widespread and abundant
    Data Deficient (DD)Not enough information to assess

    Threatened = CR + EN + VU (the three categories of high risk).

  • 7. Exam Orientation

    7.1 Previous year questions

    No wildlife-extinction/IUCN question exists in the authored bank. Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Explain the extinction vortex. Answer: When a population becomes small, individuals are forced to inbreed; homozygosity rises and harmful recessive alleles are expressed, while genetic diversity is lost by drift. Fertility, survival and disease resistance fall, the population shrinks further, and inbreeding deepens — a self-reinforcing extinction vortex. Even if external threats are removed, the population may be trapped genetically unless new individuals (gene flow) or habitat are restored.

    Q2 (3m) — Differentiate between the IUCN categories Endangered and Vulnerable. Answer: Both are "threatened" categories, but Endangered (EN) species face a very high risk of extinction in the wild — typically severe population decline, small range or few mature individuals (Nepal's Bengal tiger, red panda) — while Vulnerable (VU) species face a high risk over the medium term, being less acutely imperilled (Nepal's one-horned rhinoceros, snow leopard). EN sits one step closer to Critically Endangered than VU.

    Q3 (3m) — List three conservation strategies and give one example of each. Answer: (1) In-situ conservation — protecting species in their habitats, e.g. Chitwan National Park. (2) Ex-situ conservation — conserving off-site, e.g. captive breeding and rhino translocation from Chitwan to Bardia, or seed banks for plants. (3) Legal/international protection — CITES regulating trade in endangered species, backed by Nepal's National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act. (Community-based conservation and restoration ecology are further strategies.)

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Treating "threatened" as one IUCN category — it is the umbrella for CR + EN + VU.
    2. Confusing Extinct in the Wild (EW) with Extinct (EX).
    3. Listing extinction causes without mechanisms or examples.
    4. Forgetting natural (background) extinction alongside human causes.
    5. Missing the extinction vortex when asked why small populations are at risk.

Confusion

  • Extinct (EX) vs Extinct in the Wild (EW): EX means no individuals survive anywhere; EW means survivors exist only in captivity/cultivation.
  • Threatened vs endangered: "threatened" is the umbrella for CR, EN and VU; "endangered" is one specific category within it.

Key Points

  • Wildlife value: ecological (pollination, dispersal, pest control), economic (tourism), scientific (bioindicators), genetic (wild crop relatives), aesthetic-ethical, medicinal.
  • Extinction causes: habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, invasives, disease, climate change (plus natural background causes).

Summary

Wildlife sustains ecology and economy alike — pollinating crops, dispersing seeds, drawing tourists, storing genetic resources and carrying ethical worth. Its loss accelerates through habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, invasive species, disease and climate change, on top of the natural background rate; small populations then fall into the extinction vortex of inbreeding and drift. Conservation answers with in-situ parks, ex-situ zoos, seed banks and translocations, legal shields from CITES to Nepal's own Wildlife Conservation Act, community forestry and restoration. The IUCN ranks the danger — Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern, Data Deficient — with "threatened" meaning CR, EN or VU: Nepal's gharial is Critically Endangered, its tiger and red panda Endangered, its rhino and snow leopard Vulnerable.

Notes
  • Protected areas: designated areas for conservation; in Nepal: 10 national parks, 6 conservation areas, 1 hunting reserve, and Ramsar wetlands.
  • National parks: Chitwan (1973, Bengal tiger + one-horned rhino), Bardia, Banke, Shuklaphanta, Koshi Tappu, Parsa, Rara, Langtang (1976), Sagarmatha (1976), Annapurna (1986), Manaslu (1986), Makalu (1992), Kanchenjunga (1989). NEB commonly cites 10 national parks; several sources list 11–14 depending on inclusion of the newer parks.
  • Conservation areas (6): Kanchenjunga, Manaslu, Shey-Phoksundo, Khoratadi, Khaptad, and Barun.
  • Ramsar sites: Koshi Tappu, Rara, Chitwan (Terai wetlands) — internationally important wetlands.
  • IUCN Red List categories: Extinct (EX), Extinct in the Wild (EW), Critically Endangered (CR), Endangered (EN), Vulnerable (VU), Near Threatened (NT), Least Concern (LC).
  • Threatened species: CR, EN, VU (at risk of extinction); Near Threatened; Rare; Extinct.
  • Endangered species in Nepal: Bengal tiger (EN), one-horned rhino (VU), snow leopard (VU), Asian elephant (EN), goral, musk deer, rhesus macaque, red panda (VU), Himalayan tahr (NT), kingfisher (some CR).
  • Causes of extinction: habitat loss, poaching, pollution, climate change, invasive species, overexploitation.
  • Conservation strategies: protected areas, community forestry, anti-poaching laws (NTNC), ex-situ (zoos, seed banks), awareness, international agreements (CITES).
  • NEB focus: national parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas, Ramsar sites, IUCN categories, endangered species in Nepal, causes of extinction, and conservation strategies.
  • Wildlife: importance (ecological services, economy, culture) and causes of extinction.
Confusion
  • Chitwan National Park (1973) is the FIRST and most famous; it is home to the Bengal tiger and one-horned rhino.
  • Sagarmatha National Park (1976) includes Mount Everest; it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • IUCN "CR" = Critically Endangered (highest risk); "EN" = Endangered; "VU" = Vulnerable.
  • A national park is more strictly protected (no human habitation) than a conservation area (allows local people inside).
  • Ramsar sites are internationally important WETLANDS (not the same as national parks).
  • Community forests (in-situ) and national parks are BOTH conservation strategies; they differ in management (community vs government).
  • The one-horned rhinoceros (Rhinoceros unicornis) is VU (Vulnerable) globally; the Bengal tiger is EN (Endangered).

Summary

Nepal protects its biodiversity through 10 national parks (Chitwan, Bardia, Banke, Shuklaphanta, Koshi Tappu, Parsa, Ranipurna, Sagarmatha, Langtang, Annapurna, Makalu), 6 conservation areas (Kanchenjunga, Manaslu, etc.), 1 hunting reserve, and Ramsar wetlands. Chitwan (1973) and Sagarmatha (1976) are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The IUCN Red List (EX, EW, CR, EN, VU, NT, LC) tracks species risk; in Nepal, the Bengal tiger (EN), one-horned rhino (VU), snow leopard (VU), and Asian elephant (EN) are key endangered species. Threats include habitat loss, poaching, pollution, and climate change. Conservation strategies (in-situ, ex-situ, community forestry, anti-poaching, CITES) are essential to prevent extinction. NEB focuses on protected areas, IUCN categories, endangered species, causes of extinction, and conservation strategies.
