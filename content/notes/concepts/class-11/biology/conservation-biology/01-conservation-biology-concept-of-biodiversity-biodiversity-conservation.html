Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Conservation Biology as Crisis Discipline

    Conservation biology is the scientific study of maintaining Earth's biodiversity — a crisis discipline that applies ecology, genetics and social science to prevent extinctions. Its object is biodiversity at every level, from genes to ecosystems.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts (NEB 2024)

    Biodiversity is the variety and variability of life at all levels of organisation.

    2.1 Levels of biodiversity and their significance

    LevelDefinitionSignificanceExample
    Genetic diversityVariation of genes within a species or populationAdaptability to change and disease; basis of crop and livestock improvementDifferent rice landraces of Nepal (Jumli marshi, Pokhreli masino)
    Species diversityVariety of species in a regionEcosystem function, productivity, resilience; national heritageNepal's ~208 mammals, ~900 birds, ~6,500 flowering plants
    Ecosystem diversityVariety of habitats, communities and ecological processesProvides all ecosystem services — water, soil, climate regulation, pollinationTerai sal forests to alpine meadows to wetlands

    Significance overall: every level underpins ecosystem services, food security, medicine, and ethical-aesthetic value; losing genes weakens species, losing species weakens ecosystems.

  • 3. Conservation Biology's Goals and Approaches

    1. Document biodiversity (inventories, monitoring, IUCN Red List assessment).
    2. Understand the effects of human activity on species, communities and ecosystems.
    3. Prevent extinctions and restore populations and habitats.

    In-situ conservation — protecting species in their natural habitats: national parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas, biosphere reserves.

    Ex-situ conservation — conserving genetic material outside the natural habitat: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, tissue culture, cryopreservation, captive breeding.

    AspectIn-situEx-situ
    SiteNatural habitatArtificial/off-site
    ProtectsWhole ecosystems and evolutionary processesIndividual species or genes
    CostLarge areas neededCheaper per species
    EvolutionOngoing natural selectionSelection paused or altered
    ExamplesSagarmatha NP, Chitwan NP, Annapurna CACentral Zoo Kathmandu, National Herbarium, seed banks

  • 4. Why Biodiversity Is Threatened — HIPPO

    DriverMeaningNepal example
    Habitat lossDeforestation, farmland expansion, urbanisationTerai forest conversion; road building through forests
    Invasive speciesAlien species outcompete nativesMikania micrantha smothering Chitwan's grasslands; Eichhornia (water hyacinth) in lakes

    Climate change compounds all five — shifting habitats faster than species can move.

    6.2 Model answers

    Q1 (3m, 2024) — Biodiversity and its levels with significance. Answer: Biodiversity is the variety and variability of life at all levels of organisation. Levels: (1) Genetic diversity — variation of genes within a species; it gives populations adaptability to disease and climate change and underpins crop improvement (Nepal's rice landraces). (2) Species diversity — the variety of species in a region; it drives productivity and resilience and is national heritage (Nepal's ~208 mammals, ~900 birds). (3) Ecosystem diversity — the variety of habitats and communities; it sustains ecosystem services such as water regulation, soil formation and pollination (Terai sal forests to alpine meadows).

    Q2 (3m, 2023) — In-situ vs ex-situ with two examples each. Answer: In-situ conservation protects species within their natural habitats, conserving whole ecosystems and evolutionary processes — e.g. Chitwan National Park and Annapurna Conservation Area. Ex-situ conservation conserves species or genetic material outside their natural habitat — e.g. the Central Zoo, Kathmandu (captive animals) and seed banks/botanical gardens (preserved plant genetic resources). In-situ needs large areas and keeps evolution running; ex-situ is cheaper per species but suspends natural selection.

    Q3 (5m, 2022) — Five threats to biodiversity in Nepal with one conservation measure each. Answer: (1) Habitat loss (deforestation, road building) → protected areas and community forestry that keep forests standing. (2) Invasive species (Mikania smothering Chitwan's grasslands) → manual/mechanical removal and restoration of native grassland. (3) Pollution (pesticides, river sewage) → effluent treatment, regulated pesticide use and monitoring. (4) Human population pressure (encroachment) → land-use planning, buffer-zone programmes sharing park benefits with local people. (5) Overexploitation (rhino and tiger poaching, illegal logging) → anti-poaching patrols and strict wildlife-crime law enforcement (army-assisted patrols in national parks).

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Listing biodiversity's levels without their significance — the question asks for both.
    2. Confusing in-situ (habitat) with ex-situ (off-site) examples.
    3. Giving threats without a matching conservation measure — the 2022 question demands both.
    4. Naming HIPPO drivers vaguely ("human activities") instead of specific ones.
    5. Forgetting that genetic diversity is the level below species.
Confusion
  • Biodiversity vs conservation biology: biodiversity is the object (variety of life at gene, species and ecosystem levels); conservation biology is the discipline protecting it.
  • In-situ vs ex-situ: in-situ conserves species in their habitats (parks); ex-situ conserves them off-site (zoos, seed banks).

Summary

Conservation biology is the crisis discipline protecting biodiversity — the variety of life at three levels: genetic diversity, whose variation gives populations adaptability and underpins crop improvement; species diversity, which drives ecosystem function and national heritage; and ecosystem diversity, which supplies the services — water, soil, climate, pollination — that all life depends on. Threats follow HIPPO: habitat loss, invasive species like Mikania in Chitwan, pollution, human population pressure and overexploitation such as rhino poaching, compounded by climate change. Conservation answers in two modes: in-situ, protecting whole habitats in parks and conservation areas where evolution continues; and ex-situ, preserving species and genes in zoos, botanical gardens and seed banks. Every threat needs a matched measure.

Practice

  • Why does a small population face higher extinction risk than a large one?

    Answer. Small populations are hit by chance from every direction:

    1. Demographic stochasticity — a few deaths or a skewed sex ratio can crash a handful of animals (all it takes is one bad year).
    2. Genetic drift and inbreeding — rare alleles are lost, homozygosity rises, fertility and disease resistance fall (inbreeding depression).
    3. Environmental and demographic shocks — disease, fire, poaching can wipe out the whole count at once.
    4. Allee effects — below a threshold, finding mates or mounting cooperative defence fails even as numbers rise.

    Large populations buffer all four: chance averages out, variation is preserved, and local losses are replaced. This is why minimum viable population size, not just total count, drives conservation planning.

Summary

Conservation biology is the crisis discipline protecting biodiversity — the variety of life at three levels: genetic diversity, whose variation gives populations adaptability and underpins crop improvement; species diversity, which drives ecosystem function and national heritage; and ecosystem diversity, which supplies the services — water, soil, climate, pollination — that all life depends on. Threats follow HIPPO: habitat loss, invasive species like Mikania in Chitwan, pollution, human population pressure and overexploitation such as rhino poaching, compounded by climate change. Conservation answers in two modes: in-situ, protecting whole habitats in parks and conservation areas where evolution continues; and ex-situ, preserving species and genes in zoos, botanical gardens and seed banks. Every threat needs a matched measure.

1.

A village in the Annapurna region manages a 500-hectare forest with local rules. Is this in-situ or ex-situ conservation, and what is its benefit?

ID

1

Steps
  • Forest managed in its natural location → in-situ
  • Local community rights + rules → sustainable use
  • Benefit: protects habitat + provides resources + livelihood
Answer

In-situ (community forest); protects habitat while providing sustainable resources and livelihood
