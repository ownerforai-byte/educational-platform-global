Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Nepal's Protected-Area Network

    Nepal conserves biodiversity through a legally protected network — 12 national parks, 1 wildlife reserve, 1 hunting reserve, 6 conservation areas and 13 buffer zones — covering nearly 24% of the country's area, plus Ramsar-listed wetlands. This topic maps that network, the global concept of biodiversity hotspots, and wetland conservation.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    TermDefinition
    National parkLarge area protecting ecosystems and wildlife; no consumptive use; managed by the government
    Wildlife reserveProtects particular wildlife and habitat; human activities more restricted than parks
    Hunting reserveOnly area where regulated hunting of certain species is permitted (Dhorpatan)
    Conservation areaArea managed with local communities for both biodiversity and sustainable use
    Buffer zoneCommunity-use fringe around parks that channels benefits (revenue share, forests) to local people, reducing conflict
    Biodiversity hotspotRegion with exceptional endemic species that has lost ≥70% of original habitat
    Ramsar siteWetland of international importance designated under the Ramsar Convention (1971, Iran)

    2.1 Nepal's two biodiversity hotspots

    • Himalaya hotspot — the whole Nepalese Himalayan arc; exceptional endemism in plants, birds and mammals along altitude gradients.
    • Sundaland/Indo-Burma edge — Nepal's far-eastern Terai (Kanchenjunga–Singalila and eastern plains) lies in the Indo-Burma hotspot.

    2.2 Nepal's Ramsar sites

    Nine wetlands of international importance, including Bishazari Tal (Chitwan), Ghodaghodi Tal (Kailali), Jagadishpur Reservoir (Kapilvastu), Koshi Tappu (wildlife reserve and wetland), Gosaikunda (alpine lake), Rara Lake, Mai Pokhari, Phoksundo Lake and Gokyo lakes — protecting migratory waterbirds, fish and unique alpine wetlands.

  • 3. Nepal's Major Protected Areas

    Protected areaNotable biodiversity
    Chitwan National Park (Terai)One-horned rhinoceros, Bengal tiger, gharial; sal forest and grasslands
    Bardia National Park (western Terai)Tiger, wild elephant, Gangetic dolphin in the Karnali
    Sagarmatha National Park (Khumbu)High-altitude ecosystem; musk deer, snow leopard, Himalayan tahr; World Heritage Site
    Langtang National ParkRed panda, Himalayan black bear; alpine flora
    Koshi Tappu Wildlife ReserveLast wild water buffalo (arna); migratory waterbird paradise
    Dhorpatan Hunting ReserveBlue sheep; regulated hunting
    Annapurna Conservation AreaLargest CA; community-managed; alpine to subtropical gradients
    Kanchenjunga Conservation AreaEastern Himalayan endemism; snow leopard corridor
    Gaurishankar, Krishnasar, Shivapuri–Nagarjun, Banke, Parsa, Shuklaphanta, Khaptad, Shey-Phoksundo NPs/CAsComplete the altitudinal coverage

    3.1 Why protected areas work

    They remove the main threats (habitat conversion, hunting) from core zones, maintain ecological processes (watersheds, migration corridors), and anchor tourism revenue that funds conservation — with buffer zones sharing benefits so local communities become stakeholders rather than adversaries.

  • 5. Exam Orientation

    5.1 Previous year questions

    No protected-area question exists in the authored bank. Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    5.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Define biodiversity hotspot and name the hotspots relevant to Nepal. Answer: A biodiversity hotspot is a region with exceptional concentrations of endemic species that has lost at least 70% of its original natural habitat. Nepal's territory falls in the Himalaya hotspot (the whole mountain arc, with exceptional endemism along altitude gradients) and the edge of the Indo-Burma hotspot in the far-eastern Terai. Hotspots set priorities: limited conservation money goes where endemism and threat are both highest.

    Q2 (3m) — What is a Ramsar site? Name any three of Nepal's Ramsar wetlands. Answer: A Ramsar site is a wetland designated as being of international importance under the Ramsar Convention (1971) for its waterbirds and wetland biodiversity. Nepal has nine, including Bishazari Tal, Ghodaghodi Tal, Koshi Tappu, Gosaikunda, Rara Lake, Phoksundo Lake and the Gokyo lakes. Wetlands filter water, buffer floods and support migratory birds.

    Q3 (3m) — How do buffer zones help conserve biodiversity? Answer: Buffer zones are community-use fringes around protected areas. They channel a share of park revenue and resources (community forests, grazing, tourism jobs) to local people, so communities gain more from conservation than from encroachment; they absorb human pressure that would otherwise enter core zones, and they support restoration and corridors connecting habitats.

    5.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Saying a hunting reserve bans all hunting — Dhorpatan permits regulated hunting of certain species.
    2. Confusing conservation areas (community-managed, sustainable use) with national parks (strict protection).
    3. Listing hotspots without the 70% habitat-loss criterion.
    4. Giving park names without their flagship species or altitudinal zone.
    5. Forgetting that buffer zones exist to benefit local communities, not only wildlife.

Key Points

  • Nepal's network: 12 national parks, 1 wildlife reserve (Koshi Tappu), 1 hunting reserve (Dhorpatan), 6 conservation areas, 13 buffer zones — ~24% of the country.
  • Hotspots: exceptional endemism + ≥70% habitat loss; Nepal lies in the Himalaya hotspot (and Indo-Burma edge).
  • Ramsar wetlands (9): Bishazari Tal, Ghodaghodi Tal, Jagadishpur, Koshi Tappu, Gosaikunda, Rara, Mai Pokhari, Phoksundo, Gokyo.

Confusion

  • National park vs conservation area: parks are strictly protected with no consumptive use; conservation areas are community-managed with sustainable use.
  • Buffer zone vs core zone: the core is strictly protected; the buffer fringe shares benefits with local communities to reduce pressure and conflict.

Examples

  • Chitwan National Park — rhino, tiger, gharial; Nepal's first national park (1973).

Universal Facts

  • Sagarmatha National Park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site — the park protecting Everest's slopes.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Name the only hunting reserve of Nepal and its target species.

    Answer. Dhorpatan Hunting Reserve (western Nepal) — the country's only hunting reserve, where regulated hunting of blue sheep (Pseudois nayaur) and a few other species is permitted under licence, with revenue funding conservation.

Practice

  • Why does the matriarch elephant's range exceed a single park?

    Answer. Elephants are wide-ranging mega-herbivores: one herd needs hundreds of square kilometres of seasonal food and water, far more than any single protected area contains. The matriarch's memory maps traditional corridors linking wet-season and dry-season ranges across park, forest and farmland. When corridors are blocked by settlements, herds raid crops and conflict flares — which is why Nepal manages buffer zones and transboundary corridors (e.g. Terai Arc Landscape) instead of relying on parks alone.

Summary

Nepal conserves about a quarter of its land in a protected-area network — 12 national parks (Chitwan, Bardia, Sagarmatha, Langtang), a wildlife reserve (Koshi Tappu with its wild water buffalo), Dhorpatan Hunting Reserve, six community-managed conservation areas (Annapurna, Kanchenjunga) and 13 buffer zones that share park benefits with local people. The country falls within the Himalaya biodiversity hotspot — exceptional endemism plus severe habitat loss — with the Indo-Burma edge in the far-eastern Terai, and holds nine Ramsar wetlands of international importance, from Bishazari Tal to the Gokyo lakes. Protected areas work because they remove core threats, keep ecological processes running, and pay their way through tourism — with buffer zones turning neighbours into stakeholders.

Important Statements

  • "A park protects the habitat; a buffer zone protects the park from people and the people from poverty."

Formulas

  • A national park is managed for wildlife and visitors, and people do not live in the core. Chitwan, gazetted in 1973, was Nepal's first.
  • Sagarmatha National Park protects the Everest region and is also a World Heritage site.
