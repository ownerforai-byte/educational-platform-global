Notes

  • Wildlife includes all non-domesticated plants, animals, and microorganisms living in natural habitats, forming essential components of ecosystems.
  • Wildlife provides ecosystem services: pollination, seed dispersal, nutrient cycling, pest control, and maintenance of food webs.
  • Wildlife has economic value through ecotourism, medicinal resources, genetic diversity for agriculture, and sustainable harvest of timber and non-timber products.
  • Wildlife holds cultural, aesthetic, and ethical value, supporting indigenous knowledge and spiritual traditions.
  • Major causes of extinction: habitat destruction and fragmentation, overexploitation (hunting, poaching), invasive species, pollution, climate change, and disease.
  • Habitat loss is the leading cause of species extinction, driven by deforestation, agriculture, urbanization, and infrastructure development.
  • Overexploitation occurs when harvesting exceeds reproduction rates, as seen in ivory poaching and overfishing.
  • Invasive alien species outcompete native species for resources and alter ecosystem dynamics (e.g., Lantana camara in Nepal).
  • Conservation strategies include legal protection (CITES, national laws), protected areas, captive breeding, habitat restoration, community-based conservation, and international cooperation.
  • CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species) regulates cross-border trade in threatened species through Appendix I, II, and III listings.

Key Points

  • Wildlife = non-domesticated organisms in natural habitats
  • Ecosystem services: pollination, seed dispersal, nutrient cycling
  • Leading extinction cause: habitat destruction
  • Other causes: overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, climate change
  • Strategies: legal protection, protected areas, captive breeding, restoration
  • CITES regulates international wildlife trade

Confusion

  • ❌ Extinction and endangerment are the same. ✅ Extinct means no living individuals remain; endangered means at high risk of extinction.
  • ❌ Poaching and hunting are identical. ✅ Hunting may be legal and regulated; poaching is illegal.
  • ❌ Invasive species always increase biodiversity. ✅ They usually reduce native biodiversity.
  • ❌ Conservation only means protecting animals. ✅ It includes plants, fungi, and ecosystems.

Examples

  • One-horned rhinoceros — recovered from near-extinction in Chitwan through anti-poaching and habitat protection
  • Bengal tiger — protected under CITES Appendix I
  • Red panda — endangered due to habitat fragmentation in eastern Nepal
  • Snow leopard — threatened by poaching and climate change in Himalayas

Universal Facts

  • Over 1 million species are currently threatened with extinction (IPBES)
  • Habitat loss affects ~85% of threatened species
  • CITES has 184 member countries
  • Nepal's wildlife is protected under National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act 1973

Practice Questions

  • Q. List five importance of wildlife to humans and ecosystems.

    Answer. 1. Pollination and seed dispersal — bees, birds and bats maintain crop yields and forest regeneration.

    1. Nutrient cycling and soil health — decomposers, earthworms and dung beetles keep soils fertile.
    2. Food and livelihoods — fisheries, bushmeat and NTFPs support millions; tourism revenue (tiger and rhino viewing) funds rural Nepal.
    3. Pest and disease regulation — predators, bats and vultures suppress pests, rodents and carcass-borne disease.
    4. Scientific, cultural and aesthetic value — genetic resources for medicine, sacred and national symbols, and the psychological value of wild nature.
  • Q. Explain the major causes of wildlife extinction with examples.

    Answer. HIPPO + climate:

    1. Habitat destruction (the leading cause — affects ~85% of threatened species): forests cleared for farms and roads; wetlands drained; e.g. grassland loss pushing the Bengal florican to CR.
    2. Invasive species: Mikania (banmara) smothering natives; water hyacinth choking wetlands.
    3. Pollution: pesticides (DDT thinning eggshells), plastic, industrial effluent.
    4. Population pressure (human overgrowth): expanding demand converts and fragments habitat.
    5. Overexploitation: poaching (rhino horn, tiger parts), illegal trade, overfishing.
    6. Climate change: shifting zones, melting wetlands, coral bleaching — the rising accelerant.

    Over 1 million species are currently threatened worldwide (IPBES).

  • Q. What is CITES? How does it help wildlife conservation?

    Answer. CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, 1973; 184 member countries) — a treaty that regulates or bans international trade in wild animals and plants.

    • Appendix I — trade prohibited (tiger, rhino, gharial);
    • Appendix II — trade allowed only with permits and proof it is not detrimental;
    • Appendix III — one country requests others' help controlling trade.

    How it helps: it removes the profit from smuggling by making cross-border trade of listed species a crime with paperwork trails, forcing consumer countries to share enforcement — the key international brake on poaching-driven extinction.

  • Q. Describe community-based conservation with a Nepali example.

    Answer. Idea: conservation succeeds when local communities gain more from protecting wildlife than from using it — locals become stakeholders, not adversaries.

    Nepali example — Annapurna Conservation Area (ACAP): the country's largest conservation area is managed with local communities: village conservation committees set rules, tourism and lodge fees fund schools and alternative energy, and in return communities police poaching and protect forest and wildlife. Buffer-zone user groups around Chitwan work the same way, sharing park tourism revenue. The result: lower poaching pressure and local ownership of conservation rules.

  • Q. Distinguish between endangered and vulnerable species.

    Answer. | Basis | Endangered (EN) | Vulnerable (VU) |

    |---|---|---|

    | Risk of extinction in the wild | Very high | High, but less immediate |

    | Typical trigger | Numbers crashed or range collapsed (>50% decline) | Declining steadily (~30% decline) or restricted range |

    | Example (Nepal) | Tiger, red panda, wild buffalo (arna) | One-horned rhino, snow leopard |

    Both sit inside the IUCN "threatened" block, EN being the more severe.

Practice

  • State the definition in one sentence, then give the example from the note.

    Answer. Definition: wildlife means all undomesticated organisms living in natural habitats — not only large mammals but also insects, birds, plants and microbes. Example from the note: the one-horned rhinoceros of Chitwan National Park — a wild, non-domesticated species protected under the National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act 1973.

  • Build the contrast the note uses.

    Answer. Conservation vs preservation-style protection: the note's contrast is between sustainable use (regulated harvest, community forestry, trophy licences at Dhorpatan that fund conservation) and strict exclusion (national parks where use is barred). Modern strategy blends both: strict cores for critical species, sustainable multiple-use surroundings for people.

  • Name one limit or cost.

    Answer. Cost: strict protection can impose real costs on local people — crop raiding by protected wildlife, lost access to forest products, and relocation — which, if uncompensated, breeds resentment and poaching. Conservation must therefore pay its way locally (tourism revenue sharing, buffer-zone benefits) or it will not last.

Summary

Wildlife underpins ecosystem function and human well-being, yet faces severe threats from habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species, and climate change. Effective conservation combines legal frameworks, protected areas, captive breeding, and community engagement.
