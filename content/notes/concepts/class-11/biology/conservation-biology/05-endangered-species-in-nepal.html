Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Nepal's Flagship Species on the Red List

    Nepal's altitudinal sweep — Terai grasslands to alpine peaks — shelters species found nowhere so concentrated elsewhere: the greater one-horned rhinoceros, Bengal tiger, red panda, snow leopard, gharial, Asiatic wild buffalo and the spiny babbler (Nepal's only endemic bird). Each carries an IUCN category, a story of threat and, in several cases, a story of recovery.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    Endangered species are species facing a very high risk of extinction in the wild (IUCN EN), often used loosely for all threatened species (CR, EN, VU). Nepal's list also includes nationally protected schedules under the National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act.

    3.1 Success and cautionary stories

    • Success: the rhinoceros population grew from ~100 in the 1960s to ~750+ through zero-poaching years; Nepal nearly doubled its tigers (from 121 in 2009 to 355 in 2022) — meeting the Tx2 goal ahead of schedule.
    • Caution: the gharial remains Critically Endangered despite decades of captive release — wild recruitment is low; the arna's gene pool is diluted by hybridisation.
  • Nepal's flagship endangered species — where they live

  • 4. Why Flagship Species Matter

    Flagship species (rhino, tiger, snow leopard) anchor public appeal and funding; protecting their large habitats automatically shelters co-occurring species (the umbrella effect). Indicator species (gharial for river health) signal ecosystem condition. Keystone species (elephants opening paths, seed dispersers) maintain the systems others depend on.

  • 6. Exam Orientation

    6.1 Previous year questions

    No endangered-species question exists in the authored bank. Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    6.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Name three endangered species of Nepal with their habitats and one threat each. Answer: (1) Bengal tiger — Terai and Churia forests (Chitwan, Bardia); threatened by poaching for bones and skins and by depletion of its prey base. (2) Red panda — temperate bamboo forests of Langtang, Annapurna and Kanchenjunga; threatened by habitat loss and the pet trade. (3) Gharial — Karnali, Rapti and Narayani rivers; threatened by sand mining, fishing nets and altered river flows (Critically Endangered).

    Q2 (3m) — Explain the umbrella species concept with a Nepalese example. Answer: An umbrella species is one whose protection automatically protects many co-occurring species because it needs large, intact habitats. The Bengal tiger requires vast Terai forests with abundant prey; conserving tiger landscapes — Chitwan, Bardia, Parsa, Banke — simultaneously protects rhinoceros, deer, gharial rivers and the whole food web. Flagship appeal plus large habitat needs makes umbrella species efficient conservation anchors.

    Q3 (2m) — What conservation actions have helped Nepal's rhinoceros recover? Answer: Army-assisted anti-poaching patrols in Chitwan and Bardia, translocation of rhinos to establish new subpopulations, regular census monitoring, and habitat (grassland) management — together raising the population from near 100 in the 1960s to more than 750, moving the species from Endangered to Vulnerable.

    6.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Giving species without habitats or threats — the question asks for both.
    2. Confusing snow leopard (Vulnerable) with tiger (Endangered).
    3. Calling the spiny babbler endangered — it is Nepal-endemic but Least Concern.
    4. Missing the umbrella/keystone concept when asked why flagship species matter.
    5. Ignoring success stories (rhino, tiger recovery) that examiners reward.

Key Points

  • Nepal's threatened species: gharial CR (rivers); tiger, red panda, arna EN; rhino, snow leopard VU; Bengal florican CR (grasslands).
  • Success: rhino ~100 (1960s) → 750+; tigers 121 (2009) → 355 (2022) — Tx2 met early. Caution: gharial recruitment low; arna hybridisation.
  • Flagship/umbrella logic: conserving tiger landscapes shelters whole communities.

Confusion

  • Endangered (EN) vs endemic: endangered describes extinction risk; endemic describes geographic restriction (the spiny babbler is endemic to Nepal but Least Concern).
  • Umbrella vs keystone species: umbrella species protect others through large habitat needs (tiger); keystone species have outsized ecological roles (elephants as habitat engineers).

Examples

  • Gharial — Critically Endangered river crocodilian; captive breeding at Kasara, Chitwan.

Universal Facts

  • Nepal met the international Tx2 goal — doubling tigers — ahead of the 2022 deadline, one of the few countries to do so.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Why is the arna's conservation complicated?

    Answer. The Koshi Tappu wild water buffalo (arna) interbreeds with domestic buffalo, diluting the wild gene pool; the reserve is small, and the population faces disease from livestock and poaching pressure. Conserving genetically pure wild stock therefore demands continuous monitoring of hybrids plus strict reserve protection — a genetics problem as much as a protection problem.

Practice

  • Why is a growing rhino population still listed Vulnerable?

    Answer. IUCN status is judged against long-term risk criteria, not just the current count. Nepal's rhinos recovered from ~100 in the 1960s to 750+ (752 in the 2021 count: 694 Chitwan, 38 Bardiya, 17 Shuklaphanta, 3 Parsa), but the species still qualifies as Vulnerable because:

    1. the entire national population is concentrated in a few Terai lowland populations — one disease outbreak, flood or poaching wave could cut it severely;
    2. the historical decline far exceeds the VU threshold;
    3. habitat is limited and poaching pressure for horn never disappears.

    A rising trend is encouraging, but the category changes only when the species no longer meets VU criteria across its whole range.

Summary

Nepal's endangered species map its altitudinal range: Terai grasslands hold the rhinoceros (Vulnerable, recovered from ~100 to 750+ by anti-poaching and translocation), the Bengal tiger (Endangered, 355 after meeting Tx2 early), and the Bengal florican (Critically Endangered); rivers hold the gharial (Critically Endangered, sustained by captive release); high Himalaya holds the snow leopard (Vulnerable); bamboo forests hold the red panda (Endangered); and Koshi Tappu's wetlands hold the hybridisation-threatened arna. Each species pairs a habitat with a threat and a conservation action — patrols, corridors, grassland management, nest protection. Flagship species earn their keep through the umbrella effect: protecting the tiger's forests protects everything beneath them.

Important Statements

  • The Bengal tiger is Endangered. Nepal's Terai parks hold the breeding population.
