1.

DDT belongs to which pesticide class?

Options
  • Organophosphates
  • Organochlorines
  • Carbamates
  • Pyrethroids
Answer

B

Explanation

DDT is an organochlorine — highly persistent and lipophilic.

2.

Organophosphates kill insects by:

Options
  • Thinning eggshells
  • Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase
  • Blocking photosynthesis
  • Disrupting cell walls
Answer

B

Explanation

Acetylcholine accumulates at synapses; nerves over-fire and the insect is paralysed.

3.

Biomagnification means:

Options
  • Pesticide build-up in one organism
  • Concentration increasing at higher trophic levels
  • Faster pest growth
  • Larger crop yields
Answer

B

Explanation

Persistent pollutants concentrate up the food chain — top predators get the highest dose.

4.

DDT caused reproductive failure in fish-eating birds by:

Options
  • Killing the adults
  • Thinning eggshells
  • Starving the chicks
  • Sterilising the males
Answer

B

Explanation

DDT interferes with calcium metabolism — eggshells thin and break during incubation.

5.

Pest resurgence after spraying occurs because:

Options
  • Pests mutate faster
  • Natural enemies are killed along with pests
  • Pests eat more
  • The pesticide fertilises crops
Answer

B

Explanation

With their enemies dead, surviving pest populations rebound harder.

6.

Ladybird beetles used against aphids are an example of:

Options
  • Chemical control
  • Biological control
  • Cultural control
  • Genetic control
Answer

B

Explanation

Using natural enemies against pests is biological control.

7.

Pesticide resistance evolves because spraying:

Options
  • Kills all pests
  • Applies selection pressure favouring tolerant individuals
  • Weaken pests
  • Sterilises the soil
Answer

B

Explanation

Survivors carry resistance genes and breed — resistance spreads through the population.

8.

A central principle of IPM is to:

Options
  • Spray on a fixed calendar
  • Use pesticides only when pests cross the economic threshold
  • Maximise chemical use
  • Ban all pest control
Answer

B

Explanation

IPM integrates cultural, biological and chemical tools, spraying only at threshold levels.

Enriched Content

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Chemical Arms Race in Agriculture

    Pesticides are chemicals used to kill pests — insects (insecticides), weeds (herbicides), fungi (fungicides), rodents (rodenticides). They protect crops and save lives from vector-borne disease, but their persistence, biomagnification and resistance make them a central environmental problem.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    TermDefinition
    PesticideAny chemical agent used to destroy pests
    PersistenceHow long a pesticide remains active in the environment (DDT: decades)
    BiomagnificationIncrease in pesticide concentration at successively higher trophic levels
    BioaccumulationBuild-up of a pesticide within one organism's tissues over its lifetime
    Pesticide resistanceSelection of tolerant pest populations after repeated spraying
    Broad-spectrum vs narrow-spectrumKilling many species vs targeting specific pests

  • 3. Types of Pesticides

    ClassTargetExamplesNotes
    OrganochlorinesInsectsDDT, aldrin, dieldrin, endosulfanHighly persistent, lipophilic, banned in many countries; DDT still permitted for malaria control in some settings
    OrganophosphatesInsectsMalathion, parathion, chlorpyrifosBreak down faster but acutely toxic (nerve poisons — inhibit acetylcholinesterase)
    CarbamatesInsectsCarbaryl, aldicarbAlso acetylcholinesterase inhibitors; moderate persistence
    PyrethroidsInsectsPermethrin, deltamethrin (synthetic; pyrethrin from chrysanthemum)Low mammalian toxicity, fast breakdown
    HerbicidesWeeds2,4-D, glyphosate, atrazineSome persist in soil and water
    FungicidesFungiCopper compounds, sulphur, carbendazimUsed in orchards and seed treatment

    3.1 How insecticides act

    • Organochlorines and pyrethroids: disrupt nerve impulse transmission along axons.
    • Organophosphates and carbamates: inhibit acetylcholinesterase, so acetylcholine accumulates and nerves over-fire — paralysis and death.

  • 4. Effects of Pesticides

    4.1 Environmental effects

    • Biomagnification: DDT concentrations rise from water → plankton → small fish → large fish → fish-eating birds, where it thins eggshells (the classic DDT → bald eagle/osprey eggshell thinning story).
    • Persistence: organochlorines remain in soil for decades and travel through food webs.
    • Killing non-target organisms: pollinators (bees), natural enemies of pests, and aquatic life from runoff.
    • Eutrophication link: herbicide and fertiliser runoff degrade water quality.

    4.2 Effects on humans

    • Acute poisoning (organophosphate poisoning among farm workers — nausea, convulsions, respiratory failure).
    • Chronic effects: some pesticides are suspected carcinogens or endocrine disruptors; residues in food and milk.

    4.3 Ecological backfire

    • Pest resurgence: spraying kills natural enemies; pest populations rebound harder.
    • Resistance: repeated spraying selects resistant pests — the pesticide treadmill (more chemical, less effect).
    • Secondary pests: new pests emerge when their competitors or enemies are killed.

  • 5. Alternatives and Control

    ApproachDescription
    Integrated Pest Management (IPM)Combines tolerant varieties, crop rotation, biological control, pheromone traps, and pesticides only as a last resort at economic threshold levels
    Biological controlUsing natural enemies — ladybird beetles against aphids; Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) against caterpillars
    Pheromone trapsSpecies-specific sex pheromones lure and trap pests
    Cultural practicesCrop rotation, resistant varieties, clean seed, timely sowing

  • 6. Conceptual Relationships

    • Topic 50 → here: pesticides are one of the sources of soil and water pollution.
    • Here → Unit 4 (ecology): biomagnification is a food-chain phenomenon; pest resurgence is a community-ecology failure.
    • Here → evolution: resistance is natural selection observed in real time — the pesticide treadmill.
    • Here → Unit 10: sustainable agriculture reduces the pressure driving biodiversity loss.

  • 7. Exam Orientation

    7.1 Previous year questions

    No pesticide question exists in the authored bank. Questions below are NEB-style practice, clearly labelled.

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — What is biomagnification? Explain with the DDT example. Answer: Biomagnification is the increase in concentration of a persistent pollutant at successively higher trophic levels. DDT, sprayed against mosquitoes, washes into water and is taken up by plankton at very low concentration; small fish eating plankton accumulate it; large fish eating small fish concentrate it further; fish-eating birds at the top accumulate the highest doses, where DDT interferes with calcium metabolism and thins eggshells, causing reproductive failure. The pollutant cannot be metabolised or excreted, so it climbs the food chain.

    Q2 (3m) — Why do pest problems often worsen after repeated pesticide spraying? Answer: Repeated spraying kills the pest's natural enemies as well as the pest, so surviving pest populations rebound without control (pest resurgence). It also applies strong selection pressure, favouring resistant individuals — resistance spreads through the population and higher doses are needed (the pesticide treadmill). Killing competitors can release secondary pests. The ecological solution is Integrated Pest Management, using pesticides only as a last resort.

    Q3 (2m) — How do organophosphate insecticides act? Answer: They inhibit the enzyme acetylcholinesterase at nerve synapses; acetylcholine is not broken down and accumulates, causing continuous nerve firing — tremors, paralysis and death of the insect (and, at high doses, of humans).

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Confusing bioaccumulation (within one organism) with biomagnification (up the food chain).
    2. Calling DDT a fast-degrading pesticide — organochlorines are the most persistent class.
    3. Saying pesticides affect only pests — non-target organisms and natural enemies suffer first.
    4. Missing the resistance/pest-resurgence argument when asked why spraying backfires.
    5. Naming no IPM component when asked for alternatives.
Confusion
  • Bioaccumulation vs biomagnification: bioaccumulation is build-up within one organism; biomagnification is concentration rising up trophic levels.
  • Organochlorines vs organophosphates: organochlorines are persistent axon poisons (DDT); organophosphates degrade faster but are acute acetylcholinesterase-inhibiting nerve poisons (malathion).

Key Points

  • Effects: biomagnification (DDT → eggshell thinning in birds), non-target kills (bees, natural enemies), human poisoning, pest resurgence and resistance.
  • Alternatives: IPM — tolerant varieties, rotation, biological control (ladybirds, Bt), pheromone traps, pesticides only at economic threshold.
Summary

Pesticides — insecticides, herbicides, fungicides — protect crops but carry ecological costs. Organochlorines like DDT persist for decades and biomagnify up food chains until fish-eating birds lay thin-shelled eggs; organophosphates and carbamates poison nerves by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase; pyrethroids offer lower mammalian toxicity. Beyond target pests, sprays kill pollinators and natural enemies, poison farm workers, and trigger resurgence and resistance — the pesticide treadmill in which each spray selects tougher pests. The way out is Integrated Pest Management: tolerant varieties, rotation, biological control with ladybirds and Bt, pheromone traps, and chemicals only as a measured last resort.

Original Content

Notes
  • Environmental pollution: the addition of substances or energy to the environment at a rate that exceeds its capacity to dilute, degrade, or absorb them.
  • Air pollution: SO2 (coal), NOx (vehicles), CO (incomplete combustion), PM2.5/PM10 (particulates), ozone (ground-level), CFCs; sources: industry, vehicles, power plants, biomass burning.
  • Effects of air pollution: respiratory diseases (asthma, bronchitis), acid rain, smog, global warming, ozone depletion.
  • Kathmandu Valley: PM2.5 exceeds WHO limits ~10× in winter (stove smoke, vehicles, dust).
  • Water pollution: domestic waste, industrial effluent, agricultural runoff (pesticides, fertilisers), oil spills, thermal pollution; effects: eutrophication, oxygen depletion, disease (cholera, typhoid).
  • Soil pollution: heavy metals, pesticides, plastics, industrial waste; effects: reduced fertility, contamination of food, leaching to groundwater.
  • Pesticides: chemicals to control pests; types: insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, rodenticides; effects: biomagnification, resistance, non-target species death, soil/water contamination.
  • Control measures: reduce emissions, renewable energy, tree planting, waste treatment, organic farming, international agreements (Montreal, Paris).
  • NEB focus: sources, effects, and control measures of air, water, and soil pollution; pesticides and their effects.
  • Nepal: vulnerable to air (Kathmandu), water (river pollution), and soil (pesticide) pollution; deforestation and urbanisation are major contributors.
Confusion
  • Acid rain (pH < 5.6) is caused by SO2 and NOx, NOT by CO2 alone.
  • Ozone is beneficial in the STRATOSPHERE (blocks UV) but harmful at ground level (smog, air pollutant).
  • Biomagnification: toxin concentration INCREASES at higher trophic levels (e.g., DDT in fish-eating birds).
  • Pesticide resistance: pests evolve resistance over time; higher doses are needed.
  • Eutrophication: excess nutrients (N, P) → algal bloom → O2 depletion → fish kill.
  • PM2.5 (fine particulates) are MORE harmful than PM10 because they penetrate deeper into the lungs.

Universal Facts
  • WHO guideline for PM2.5 is 10 µg/m³ (annual mean); Kathmandu Valley often exceeds 100 µg/m³ in winter.
  • DDT (banned in many countries) biomagnifies up food chains; it caused eggshell thinning in raptors (eagles, falcons).
  • Eutrophication is the #1 cause of "dead zones" (hypoxic zones) in lakes and coastal waters.
  • Nepal's community forestry program manages ~36% of the country's forest land.
Examples

  • Water pollution: domestic sewage in Bagmati and Karnali rivers; agricultural runoff (fertilisers, pesticides).

  • Pesticides: DDT (insecticide) biomagnifies in the food chain; non-target species (bees, fish) die.
  • Control: tree planting, renewable energy, waste treatment, organic farming, international agreements.

Summary

Environmental pollution — air, water, and soil — is a major human-induced threat. Air pollution (SO2, NOx, CO, PM2.5, O3, CFCs) causes respiratory disease, acid rain, smog, and global warming; Kathmandu Valley's PM2.5 exceeds WHO limits ~10× in winter. Water pollution (domestic, industrial, agricultural) leads to eutrophication, O2 depletion, and disease. Soil pollution (heavy metals, pesticides, plastics) reduces fertility and contaminates food. Pesticides biomagnify up food chains, cause resistance, and harm non-target species. Control measures include reducing emissions, renewable energy, tree planting, waste treatment, organic farming, and international agreements.

Summary

Pesticides — insecticides, herbicides, fungicides — protect crops but carry ecological costs. Organochlorines like DDT persist for decades and biomagnify up food chains until fish-eating birds lay thin-shelled eggs; organophosphates and carbamates poison nerves by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase; pyrethroids offer lower mammalian toxicity. Beyond target pests, sprays kill pollinators and natural enemies, poison farm workers, and trigger resurgence and resistance — the pesticide treadmill in which each spray selects tougher pests. The way out is Integrated Pest Management: tolerant varieties, rotation, biological control with ladybirds and Bt, pheromone traps, and chemicals only as a measured last resort.

Exercises

1.

A lake has an algal bloom followed by mass fish death. Explain the cause and mechanism.

ID

1

Steps
  • Excess nutrients (N, P) from agriculture/sewage
  • Algal bloom (eutrophication)
  • Algae die → decomposers use O2
  • O2 depletion (hypoxia) → fish die
Answer

Eutrophication: excess nutrients → algal bloom → O2 depletion → fish death
