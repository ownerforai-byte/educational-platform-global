Enriched Content

Notes
  • Animal adaptation: structural, physiological, and behavioural features that allow an animal to survive in a particular environment.
  • Aquatic adaptation: primary aquatic (fishes, fully aquatic) and secondary aquatic (whales, seals — originally terrestrial).
  • Aquatic features: streamlined (fusiform) body, fins, gills (countercurrent exchange), lateral line (fish), webbed feet, dense bones, salt glands.
  • Terrestrial adaptation: cursorial (running — cheetah, horse), fossorial (burrowing — earthworm, mole), arboreal (tree-dwelling — monkey, sloth).
  • Terrestrial features: waterproof skin (fur, scales, feathers), lungs, internal fertilisation, nitrogenous waste (urea/ammonia), strong limbs.
  • Volant adaptation: flight; features: wings (feathers), hollow (pneumatic) bones, air sacs (double respiration), strong pectoral muscles, streamlined body, beak.
  • Adaptations increase survival and reproduction in a given habitat; they are the result of natural selection.
  • NEB focus: aquatic (primary + secondary), terrestrial (cursorial, fossorial, arboreal), and volant adaptations.
  • Nepal examples: fish (Terai), rhododendron forest birds (volant), snow leopard (terrestrial cursorial), pangolin (fossorial).
Confusion
  • Primary aquatic animals were ALWAYS aquatic (fishes); secondary aquatic animals (whales, seals) evolved FROM terrestrial ancestors.
  • A fusiform (streamlined) body REDUCES water resistance; it is an adaptation for fast swimming.
  • Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 uptake efficiency (blood and water flow in opposite directions).
  • Hollow bones in birds reduce weight for flight; they are still strong (internal struts).
  • Arboreal animals (monkeys) have grasping hands/feet, forward-facing eyes (stereoscopic vision), and a prehensile tail (New World monkeys).
  • Fossorial animals (moles, earthworms) have reduced eyes, strong forelimbs for digging, and cylindrical bodies.
Practice
  • List aquatic adaptations of a fish (5).
  • Differentiate primary from secondary aquatic animals.
  • List terrestrial adaptations (cursorial, fossorial, arboreal) with examples.
  • Explain the volant adaptations of a bird (5).
  • Explain countercurrent exchange in fish gills.
Universal Facts
  • The fusiform body shape reduces drag by up to ~40% in fast swimmers.
  • Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 extraction; parallel flow would extract only ~50%.
  • Birds have a double respiration system (air sacs + lungs) that delivers fresh air through the lungs on BOTH in- and exhalation.
  • A hummingbird's wings beat ~50 times per second; some migrate thousands of km.
Examples
  • Fish (primary aquatic): fusiform body, fins, gills, lateral line, scale-covered skin.
  • Whale (secondary aquatic): streamlined body, dense bones, blubber, baleen, modified limbs (flippers).
  • Cheetah (cursorial): long limbs, enlarged nostrils, non-retractable claws for grip, S-spine.
  • Mole (fossorial): reduced eyes, strong digging forelimbs, cylindrical body, no tail.
  • Orangutan (arboreal): long arms, grasping hands, stereoscopic vision, prehensile tail (New World).
  • Eagle (volant): feathers, hollow bones, air sacs, strong pectoral muscles, sharp beak and talons.

Formulas
  • Aquatic: fusiform body + fins + gills (countercurrent) + lateral line + webbed feet
  • Terrestrial: waterproof skin + lungs + internal fertilisation + strong limbs
  • Volant: wings + hollow bones + air sacs + strong pectoral muscles + streamlined body + beak
Key Points
  • Aquatic: fusiform body, gills (countercurrent), lateral line, webbed feet, fins.
  • Primary aquatic = always aquatic (fish); secondary = evolved from land (whales, seals).
  • Terrestrial: cursorial (run), fossorial (dig), arboreal (tree-dwell); waterproof skin, lungs.
  • Volant: wings, hollow bones, air sacs, strong pectoral muscles, beak.
  • Countercurrent exchange: ~80% O2 uptake in fish gills.
  • Adaptation is evolutionary (population-level), not individual.
  • Nepal examples: fish (Terai), snow leopard (cursorial), birds (volant).
Summary

Animal adaptations reflect their habitat. Aquatic animals (primary, e.g. fishes; secondary, e.g. whales) have streamlined bodies, gills with countercurrent exchange, lateral lines, and webbed feet. Terrestrial animals show cursorial (running), fossorial (burrowing), or arboreal (tree-dwelling) adaptations, with waterproof skin and lungs. Volant (flying) animals have wings, hollow bones, air sacs, and strong pectoral muscles. Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 uptake. These adaptations are evolutionary, not intentional. NEB focuses on aquatic, terrestrial, and volant adaptations with Nepali examples.

Special Notes
  • NEB expects a clear aquatic (primary + secondary), terrestrial (cursorial, fossorial, arboreal), and volant adaptation list.
  • Countercurrent exchange in fish gills is a very common exam point.
  • Differentiate primary from secondary aquatic animals.
  • Hollow bones + air sacs + double respiration are key volant adaptations.
  • Give a Nepali example for each type (fish, snow leopard, eagle).
Important Statements
  • Aquatic animals have streamlined bodies and gills with countercurrent exchange.
  • Primary aquatic animals were always aquatic; secondary ones evolved from land.
  • Volant animals have wings, hollow bones, and air sacs for efficient flight.
  • Adaptations are evolutionary features, not individual adjustments.
Important Notes
