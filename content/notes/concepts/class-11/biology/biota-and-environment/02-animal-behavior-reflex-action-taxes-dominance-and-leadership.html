  • 1. Topic Introduction — Why Animals Move and Act

    Behaviour is the coordinated response of an organism to stimuli. Some responses are hard-wired reflexes; others are oriented movements (taxes); some are social (dominance and leadership); and some move whole populations across continents (fish and bird migration). The syllabus groups them here.

  • 2. Reflex Action

    Definition: a rapid, automatic, involuntary response of an organ to a stimulus, mediated by the reflex arc.

    Component of the reflex arcRole
    ReceptorSense organ detects the stimulus
    Sensory (afferent) neuronCarries the impulse to the spinal cord
    Integration centre (spinal cord)Synapse; the impulse is redirected
    Motor (efferent) neuronCarries the impulse to the effector
    EffectorMuscle or gland produces the response

    Example: the knee-jerk (patellar) reflex — tapping the tendon stretches the muscle; the sensory neuron fires; the spinal cord directly excites the motor neuron; the muscle contracts and the leg kicks. Withdrawal of the hand from a hot object is another spinal reflex.

    Properties: rapid, involuntary, stereotyped; the brain is bypassed (though informed afterwards) — spinal reflexes survive even when the brain is absent.

  • Reflex arc — the fastest circuit in the body

  • 3. Taxes — Oriented Movements

    Taxis (plural taxes): an oriented locomotor response toward (+) or away from (−) a stimulus.

    TaxisStimulusExample
    PhototaxisLightMoths fly toward light (+); cockroaches flee light (−)
    ChemotaxisChemicalsSperm toward the egg; mosquitoes toward body odours (+); planaria away from harmful chemicals (−)
    GeotaxisGravityEarthworms move toward gravity (+); some insects fly against it (−)
    ThigmotaxisTouchEarthworms and woodlice press against surfaces (+)
    RheotaxisWater currentFish orienting against the current (+)
    ThermotaxisTemperatureSnakes seeking warm rocks (+)

    Taxes are innate (instinctive) behaviours — the whole animal orients; contrast with tropisms, which are growth responses of plants.

  • 4. Dominance and Leadership

    4.1 Dominance hierarchy (peck order)

    Definition: a social ranking within a group in which some individuals (dominants) gain priority of access to food, mates and space while subordinates defer.

    FeatureDetail
    Established byAgonistic displays, fights, ritualised threats — settled once and remembered
    BenefitsReduces repeated fighting; group stability; priority access for dominants
    ExamplesHens' pecking order; wolf packs with alpha pair; langur troops

    4.2 Leadership

    Definition: an individual or individuals that guide group movement and decisions.

    FeatureDetail
    BasisExperience, strength, age or knowledge — not always dominance
    FunctionGuides migration routes, warns of danger, leads to water and food
    ExamplesThe lead elephant matriarch (oldest female, remembers water sources); lead fish in schools; experienced lead goose in flight

    Dominance ≠ leadership: a dominant individual may win fights without leading; a leader may guide by knowledge without dominating.

    7.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

    Q1 (3m) — Define reflex action and describe the reflex arc with an example. Answer: A reflex action is a rapid, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus. The reflex arc: a receptor detects the stimulus; a sensory (afferent) neuron carries the impulse to the spinal cord, where it synapses; a motor (efferent) neuron carries it to the effector (muscle or gland), which responds. Example: withdrawing the hand from a hot object — the arm is pulled away before the brain consciously registers the heat.

    Q2 (2m) — Define taxis and give two examples. Answer: Taxis is an innate oriented movement of an organism toward (+) or away from (−) a stimulus. Examples: phototaxis — moths flying toward light; chemotaxis — sperm swimming toward chemicals released by the egg.

    Q3 (3m) — Explain dominance hierarchy and leadership in animals. Answer: A dominance hierarchy is a social ranking in which dominant individuals gain priority access to food, mates and space while subordinates defer; it is established by fights or displays and then reduces conflict (hens' pecking order, wolf packs). Leadership is the guidance of group movement and decisions by an individual with experience or knowledge — e.g. the elephant matriarch leading the herd to water. Dominance is won by force; leadership rests on knowledge — one individual may be dominant without leading, or lead without dominating.

    Q4 (3m) — What is bird migration? State the triggers and navigation cues. Answer: Bird migration is the regular seasonal movement of birds between breeding and wintering grounds. Triggers: changing photoperiod, temperature and food availability, acting through hormonal changes. Navigation cues: the sun compass, star patterns, the Earth's magnetic field and landmark memory. Example: demoiselle cranes crossing the Himalaya to winter in the Indian subcontinent.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Confusing taxis (whole-animal movement) with tropism (plant growth response).
    2. Saying reflexes are controlled by the brain — spinal reflexes bypass the brain.
    3. Treating dominance and leadership as the same — dominance is won in conflict; leadership rests on knowledge.
    4. Calling migration random dispersal — it is regular, seasonal and navigated.
    5. Confusing anadromous (sea → river, salmon) with catadromous (river → sea, eel).

Confusion

  • Taxis vs tropism: taxis is the whole animal orienting its movement; tropism is a plant's growth response.
  • Anadromous vs catadromous: anadromous fish spawn in rivers after living at sea (salmon); catadromous fish spawn at sea after living in rivers (eel).

Key Points

  • Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → motor neuron → effector; rapid, involuntary; brain bypassed.
  • Taxes: photo-, chemo-, geo-, thigmo-, rheo-, thermotaxis — oriented movement toward (+) or away (−) from a stimulus.
  • Dominance hierarchy: peck order, priority access, reduces fighting. Leadership: guides by experience/knowledge (elephant matriarch).
  • Fish migration: anadromous (salmon, sea→river), catadromous (eel, river→sea). Bird migration: photoperiod-triggered; sun/star/magnetic navigation; demoiselle crane over Himalaya.

Important Statements

  • "A reflex is faster than thought because it never reaches thought."

Examples

  • Knee-jerk reflex — spinal reflex arc with only two synapse levels.

Universal Facts

  • The bar-headed goose crosses the Himalaya at altitudes near 8,000 m — the highest bird migration known.

Formulas

  • Reflex arc order: receptor → sensory neuron → coordinator → motor neuron → effector.
  • Sign of a taxis: positive = toward the stimulus, negative = away from it.

Practice

  • Why does the spinal cord, not the brain, run spinal reflexes?

    Answer. Speed and reliability. Routing the arc through the spinal cord alone (receptor → sensory neuron → cord → motor neuron → effector) removes the delay of carrying the signal to the brain and back — the withdrawal hand jerk fires in milliseconds, before damage deepens. It is also automatic: the brain is informed afterwards and can override later, but the protective response cannot wait for conscious decision. This is why reflexes are rapid, involuntary and stereotyped.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Distinguish anadromous from catadromous migration.

    Answer. Anadromous — fish that live most of their life at sea and migrate up rivers to spawn (salmon, Hilsa).

    Catadromous — fish that live in rivers and migrate to the sea to spawn (eel).

    Both are two-way journeys timed to reproduction; the direction of the spawning run is what differs.

Exercises

1.

Woodlice walk quickly in dry bright air and almost stop under a damp stone. Is this best called a taxis or a kinesis, and why?

Steps
  • A taxis would be movement aimed toward or away from a stimulus, such as walking straight to the dark.
  • Here the animal changes its rate of movement as conditions change.
  • It slows where it is damp, so it spends more time there without steering along a compass direction.
  • A change in speed or turning, without a set direction, is kinesis.
Answer

It is a kinesis: activity falls in the damp place, so the animal accumulates there without a directed path.

Summary

Behaviour spans reflexes, oriented movements, social order and mass movement. Reflex actions — rapid, involuntary responses through the five-part reflex arc (receptor, sensory neuron, spinal cord, motor neuron, effector) — bypass the brain, as in the knee-jerk. Taxes orient the whole animal toward or away from stimuli: moths to light, sperm to egg chemicals, earthworms to gravity and touch. In societies, dominance hierarchies rank access to resources and dampen conflict, while leadership guides group movement by knowledge, as with the elephant matriarch. Fish migrate anadromously (salmon, sea to river) or catadromously (eel, river to sea); birds answer photoperiod and navigate by sun, stars and magnetic field — demoiselle cranes cross the Himalaya each year.
