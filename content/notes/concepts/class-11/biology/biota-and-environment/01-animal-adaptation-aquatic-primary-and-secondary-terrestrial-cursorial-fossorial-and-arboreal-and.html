Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Animals Tailored to Their Habitats

    Like plants, animals evolve body plans and behaviours matched to their habitats. Aquatic animals solve buoyancy and oxygen; cursorial runners solve speed; fossorial diggers solve darkness; arboreal climbers solve grip; volant fliers solve weight. The syllabus lists aquatic, primary and secondary terrestrial, cursorial, fossorial, arboreal and volant adaptations.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts — Habitat and Niche (NEB 2023)

    Habitat is the physical place where an organism lives — its address (pond, soil, canopy). Ecological niche is its functional role — what it eats, when it is active, how it uses resources — its profession. Two species can share a habitat but differ in niche (two fish in one pond, one feeding by day on insects, the other by night on detritus).

    2.1 Biotic and abiotic components of the environment (NEB 2024)

    Biotic: living components — producers (plants), consumers (animals), decomposers (bacteria, fungi). Abiotic: non-living components — light, temperature, water, soil, air, minerals.

    7.2 Model answers

    Q1 (3m, 2023) — Habitat vs ecological niche. Answer: The habitat is the physical place where an organism lives — its address (a pond, a tree canopy, a burrow). The ecological niche is the organism's functional role in that habitat — what it eats, when it is active, how it uses resources and interacts with other species — its profession. Example: two fish species may share the same pond (habitat) yet differ in niche — one feeds on insects by day near the surface, the other on detritus by night on the bottom. Habitat is where; niche is how.

    Q2 (5m, 2022) — Four interspecific relationships with examples. Answer: (1) Mutualism (+/+) — both benefit: bees obtain nectar while flowers are pollinated; Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in legume nodules. (2) Commensalism (+/0) — one benefits, the other is unaffected: epiphytic orchids on mango branches gain support only. (3) Parasitism (+/−) — one benefits at the host's expense: Plasmodium vivax in human blood causes malaria. (4) Predation (+/−) — the predator kills and eats the prey: tiger–deer; ladybird beetles eating aphids (used in biocontrol). (Also acceptable: competition −/− — Gause's Paramecium; amensalism −/0 — Penicillium antibiosis.)

    Q3 (2m, 2021) — Role of decomposers with two examples. Answer: Decomposers break down dead organic matter and wastes into simple inorganic nutrients, returning them to the soil and air for reuse by producers — they close the nutrient cycle and prevent the accumulation of dead matter. Examples: saprophytic bacteria (e.g. Bacillus) and fungi (e.g. Penicillium, mushrooms) decomposing leaf litter.

    Q4 (2m, 2024) — Biotic and abiotic components with two examples each. Answer: Biotic components are the living parts of the environment — e.g. producers (green plants) and decomposers (bacteria, fungi). Abiotic components are the non-living physical and chemical factors — e.g. temperature and soil water (also light, air, minerals).

    Q5 (3m, 2024) — Hydrophytes and xerophytes with two adaptive features each. Answer: Hydrophytes live in water — e.g. Hydrilla, lotus. Features: (1) aerenchyma — air spaces give buoyancy and aerate roots in oxygen-poor mud; (2) reduced roots and vascular tissue, thin cuticle, and stomata only on the upper surface of floating leaves. Xerophytes live in dry habitats — e.g. Opuntia, Nerium. Features: (1) leaves reduced to spines with a thick waxy cuticle, minimising transpiration; (2) sunken stomata and deep extensive roots reaching distant water.

    Q6 (3m, 2024) — Natality, mortality, population density and growth. Answer: Natality is the birth rate — the number of individuals born per unit time; mortality is the death rate — the number dying per unit time; population density is the number of individuals per unit area or volume. They are related to growth by: change in density = (natality + immigration) − (mortality + emigration). When natality exceeds mortality the population grows; when mortality exceeds natality it declines; when they balance, density is stable.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Confusing habitat (address) with niche (profession).
    2. Calling a whale a primary aquatic animal — it is secondary aquatic (evolved on land, breathes air).
    3. Writing "fitness = strength" — fitness is reproductive success.
    4. Giving adaptation examples without naming the animal.
    5. Confusing natality (births) with population density (individuals per area).

Confusion

  • Primary vs secondary aquatic animals: primary (fish) evolved in water with gills; secondary (whale, seal) evolved on land, breathe lungs, return to water.
  • Adaptation vs acclimatisation: adaptation is inherited (evolutionary); acclimatisation is a short-term physiological adjustment within an individual's lifetime.

Practice

  • Why are fossorial animals' eyes reduced?

    Answer. Underground there is no light to see by. Eyes are expensive tissue to build and maintain, and in a burrow they are useless — worse, they let soil in and get damaged. Natural selection therefore favours individuals that invest less in eyes and more in the senses a tunnel actually needs: touch (vibrissae), smell and hearing — the mole's tiny covered eyes and powerful digging claws are the textbook result.

Universal Facts

  • Birds' hollow (pneumatic) bones and keeled sternum cut weight while anchoring flight muscles — weight is the enemy of flight.

Examples

  • Horse — cursorial: single hoofed digit, long limbs.
  • Mole — fossorial: spade-like forelimbs, reduced eyes.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Define population density and give its formula of change.

    Answer. Population density — the number of individuals per unit area or volume (e.g. tigers per 100 km²).

    Change in density = (natality + immigration) − (mortality + emigration)

    Natality and immigration add individuals; mortality and emigration remove them.

Key Points

  • Habitat = address; niche = profession. Biotic: producers, consumers, decomposers; abiotic: light, temperature, water, soil.
  • Aquatic: streamlined body, gills/swim bladder, fins; secondary aquatic (whale) keep lungs.
  • Terrestrial: cursorial (long limbs, reduced digits — horse), fossorial (claws, reduced eyes — mole), arboreal (opposable thumbs, prehensile tail, binocular vision), volant (wings, hollow bones, keel — birds; patagium — bat).
  • Population: natality +, mortality −, density = individuals/area; growth = (natality + immigration) − (mortality + emigration).

Summary

Animals are tailored to their habitats as plants are. Aquatic animals — primary like fish with gills, fins and swim bladders, or secondary like whales that returned with lungs — solve buoyancy and oxygen; terrestrial animals solve drying with waterproof skins, internal fertilisation and lungs. Among terrestrial specialists, cursorial runners lengthen limbs and lose digits, fossorial diggers gain claws and lose eyes, arboreal climbers gain opposable thumbs, prehensile tails and binocular vision, and volant fliers gain wings, hollow bones and keeled sternums. Habitat is the address, niche the profession; populations grow as natality plus immigration exceeds mortality plus emigration. Adaptation — inherited, not acquired — is natural selection's signature in every body plan.

Options

The ecological niche of a species is its:

Options

  • Functional role in the community
  • Population size
  • Genome

B

The niche is the profession — resource use, timing and interactions; the habitat is the address.

If natality exceeds mortality and there is no migration, the population:

Options
  • Declines
  • Grows
  • Stays constant
  • Becomes extinct
Answer

B

Original Content

Notes
  • Animal adaptation: structural, physiological, and behavioural features that allow an animal to survive in a particular environment.
  • Aquatic adaptation: primary aquatic (fishes, fully aquatic) and secondary aquatic (whales, seals — originally terrestrial).
  • Aquatic features: streamlined (fusiform) body, fins, gills (countercurrent exchange), lateral line (fish), webbed feet, dense bones, salt glands.
  • Terrestrial adaptation: cursorial (running — cheetah, horse), fossorial (burrowing — earthworm, mole), arboreal (tree-dwelling — monkey, sloth).
  • Terrestrial features: waterproof skin (fur, scales, feathers), lungs, internal fertilisation, nitrogenous waste (urea/ammonia), strong limbs.
  • Volant adaptation: flight; features: wings (feathers), hollow (pneumatic) bones, air sacs (double respiration), strong pectoral muscles, streamlined body, beak.
  • Adaptations increase survival and reproduction in a given habitat; they are the result of natural selection.
  • NEB focus: aquatic (primary + secondary), terrestrial (cursorial, fossorial, arboreal), and volant adaptations.
  • Nepal examples: fish (Terai), rhododendron forest birds (volant), snow leopard (terrestrial cursorial), pangolin (fossorial).
Confusion
  • Primary aquatic animals were ALWAYS aquatic (fishes); secondary aquatic animals (whales, seals) evolved FROM terrestrial ancestors.
  • A fusiform (streamlined) body REDUCES water resistance; it is an adaptation for fast swimming.
  • Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 uptake efficiency (blood and water flow in opposite directions).
  • Hollow bones in birds reduce weight for flight; they are still strong (internal struts).
  • Arboreal animals (monkeys) have grasping hands/feet, forward-facing eyes (stereoscopic vision), and a prehensile tail (New World monkeys).
  • Fossorial animals (moles, earthworms) have reduced eyes, strong forelimbs for digging, and cylindrical bodies.

Universal Facts
  • The fusiform body shape reduces drag by up to ~40% in fast swimmers.
  • Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 extraction; parallel flow would extract only ~50%.
  • Birds have a double respiration system (air sacs + lungs) that delivers fresh air through the lungs on BOTH in- and exhalation.
  • A hummingbird's wings beat ~50 times per second; some migrate thousands of km.
Examples
  • Fish (primary aquatic): fusiform body, fins, gills, lateral line, scale-covered skin.
  • Whale (secondary aquatic): streamlined body, dense bones, blubber, baleen, modified limbs (flippers).
  • Cheetah (cursorial): long limbs, enlarged nostrils, non-retractable claws for grip, S-spine.
  • Mole (fossorial): reduced eyes, strong digging forelimbs, cylindrical body, no tail.
  • Orangutan (arboreal): long arms, grasping hands, stereoscopic vision, prehensile tail (New World).
  • Eagle (volant): feathers, hollow bones, air sacs, strong pectoral muscles, sharp beak and talons.

Formulas
  • Aquatic: fusiform body + fins + gills (countercurrent) + lateral line + webbed feet
  • Terrestrial: waterproof skin + lungs + internal fertilisation + strong limbs
  • Volant: wings + hollow bones + air sacs + strong pectoral muscles + streamlined body + beak
Key Points
  • Aquatic: fusiform body, gills (countercurrent), lateral line, webbed feet, fins.
  • Primary aquatic = always aquatic (fish); secondary = evolved from land (whales, seals).
  • Terrestrial: cursorial (run), fossorial (dig), arboreal (tree-dwell); waterproof skin, lungs.
  • Volant: wings, hollow bones, air sacs, strong pectoral muscles, beak.
  • Countercurrent exchange: ~80% O2 uptake in fish gills.
  • Adaptation is evolutionary (population-level), not individual.
  • Nepal examples: fish (Terai), snow leopard (cursorial), birds (volant).
Summary

Animal adaptations reflect their habitat. Aquatic animals (primary, e.g. fishes; secondary, e.g. whales) have streamlined bodies, gills with countercurrent exchange, lateral lines, and webbed feet. Terrestrial animals show cursorial (running), fossorial (burrowing), or arboreal (tree-dwelling) adaptations, with waterproof skin and lungs. Volant (flying) animals have wings, hollow bones, air sacs, and strong pectoral muscles. Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 uptake. These adaptations are evolutionary, not intentional. NEB focuses on aquatic, terrestrial, and volant adaptations with Nepali examples.

Important Statements

  • Primary aquatic animals were always aquatic; secondary ones evolved from land.
  • Volant animals have wings, hollow bones, and air sacs for efficient flight.
  • Adaptations are evolutionary features, not individual adjustments.
