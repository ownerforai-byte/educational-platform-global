Notes

  • Animal adaptation: structural, physiological, and behavioural features that allow an animal to survive in a particular environment.
  • Aquatic adaptation: primary aquatic (fishes, fully aquatic) and secondary aquatic (whales, seals — originally terrestrial).
  • Aquatic features: streamlined (fusiform) body, fins, gills (countercurrent exchange), lateral line (fish), webbed feet, dense bones, salt glands.
  • Terrestrial adaptation: cursorial (running — cheetah, horse), fossorial (burrowing — earthworm, mole), arboreal (tree-dwelling — monkey, sloth).
  • Terrestrial features: waterproof skin (fur, scales, feathers), lungs, internal fertilisation, nitrogenous waste (urea/ammonia), strong limbs.
  • Volant adaptation: flight; features: wings (feathers), hollow (pneumatic) bones, air sacs (double respiration), strong pectoral muscles, streamlined body, beak.
  • Adaptations increase survival and reproduction in a given habitat; they are the result of natural selection.
  • NEB focus: aquatic (primary + secondary), terrestrial (cursorial, fossorial, arboreal), and volant adaptations.
  • Nepal examples: fish (Terai), rhododendron forest birds (volant), snow leopard (terrestrial cursorial), pangolin (fossorial).

  • Animal adaptation: Aquatic (primary and secondary), terrestrial (cursorial, fossorial and arboreal) and volant adaptation

Confusion

  • Primary aquatic animals were ALWAYS aquatic (fishes); secondary aquatic animals (whales, seals) evolved FROM terrestrial ancestors.
  • A fusiform (streamlined) body REDUCES water resistance; it is an adaptation for fast swimming.
  • Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 uptake efficiency (blood and water flow in opposite directions).
  • Hollow bones in birds reduce weight for flight; they are still strong (internal struts).
  • Arboreal animals (monkeys) have grasping hands/feet, forward-facing eyes (stereoscopic vision), and a prehensile tail (New World monkeys).
  • Fossorial animals (moles, earthworms) have reduced eyes, strong forelimbs for digging, and cylindrical bodies.

1.

Why do birds have hollow bones, and how does this help in flight?

ID

1

Steps
  • Hollow bones are lighter than solid bones
  • Reduced body mass = less energy to fly
  • Internal struts keep bones strong
Answer

Hollow bones reduce body weight, making flight more efficient while remaining strong
