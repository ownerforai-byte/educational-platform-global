  • 1. Topic Introduction — When Waste Outruns Assimilation

    Environmental pollution is the addition of substances or energy to the environment at a rate faster than it can be dispersed, diluted or decomposed into harmless forms. The syllabus covers air, water and soil pollution — their sources, effects and control — and their consequences: greenhouse effect, acid rain and eutrophication.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    TermDefinition
    PollutantAny substance or agent added to the environment that causes harmful effects
    Primary pollutantEmitted directly (CO, SO₂, dust)
    Secondary pollutantFormed in air by reactions (ozone, PAN in smog)
    BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand)Oxygen (mg/L) needed by microbes to decompose organic matter in water over 5 days at 20°C — a measure of organic pollution
    EutrophicationNutrient (N, P) enrichment of water → algal bloom → oxygen depletion → death of aquatic life

  • 3. Air Pollution

    3.1 Sources

    SourcePollutants
    Vehicle exhaustCO, NOₓ, hydrocarbons, lead
    Industry and power plantsSO₂, particulates, CO₂
    Brick kilns (major in Nepal)CO, SO₂, particulates
    Burning of solid waste and crop residueCO₂, CO, dioxins, smoke
    Indoor cooking (biomass, kerosene)CO, particulates — a major household killer

    3.2 Effects

    • Respiratory disease (asthma, bronchitis); CO binds haemoglobin → tissue hypoxia.
    • Greenhouse effect: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O trap infrared radiation → global warming, glacier retreat (Himalaya), sea-level rise, erratic monsoon.
    • Acid rain: SO₂ + NOₓ dissolve in rain → H₂SO₄/HNO₃ — acidifies lakes and soils, corrodes buildings.
    • Photochemical smog: NOₓ + hydrocarbons + sunlight → ozone and PAN — eye irritation, plant damage.
    • Damage to crops, materials and visibility (haze over Kathmandu valley).

    3.3 Control

    • Catalytic converters and vehicle emission testing; electrostatic precipitators and scrubbers on chimneys; filters for particulates.
    • Cleaner fuels (LPG, electricity), public transport, electric vehicles; brick kilns upgraded to improved technology.
    • Afforestation; ban on open burning.
  • Pollution pathways — air, water, soil

  • 4. Water Pollution

    4.1 Sources

    SourcePollutants
    SewageOrganic waste, pathogens
    IndustryHeavy metals (lead, mercury, cadmium, arsenic), acids, dyes
    AgricultureNitrates and phosphates from fertilisers; pesticides
    Solid waste dumpingLeachate into rivers and groundwater

    4.2 Effects

    • High BOD: sewage and organic waste raise BOD; microbes consume oxygen → dissolved oxygen falls → fish and other aquatic life suffocate; water turns anaerobic and foul-smelling.
    • Eutrophication: fertiliser runoff enriches lakes → algal blooms → oxygen depletion on decay → fish kills (e.g. Phewa Lake's creeping eutrophication).
    • Heavy metals: mercury biomagnifies up the food chain (Minamata disease); arsenic in Terai groundwater.
    • Pathogens: cholera, typhoid, dysentery.

    4.3 Control

    • Sewage treatment: primary (screening, sedimentation) → secondary (activated sludge/trickling filter — microbial digestion) → tertiary (chemical polishing).
    • Effluent treatment before discharge; laws and monitoring; constructed wetlands; community sanitation.

  • 5. Soil Pollution and Erosion (NEB 2024)

    Sources: excess fertilisers and pesticides, industrial waste, mining spoil, plastic dumping, sewage sludge.

    Soil erosion: removal of topsoil by water and wind, accelerated by deforestation, overgrazing and unscientific farming. Topsoil is the most fertile layer — its loss degrades productivity and silts rivers and reservoirs.

    Conservation measures practised in Nepal:

    1. Terracing and contour farming on slopes — slows runoff.
    2. Afforestation and community forestry — root systems hold soil (Nepal's community forests reversed erosion).
    3. Cover crops, mulching and crop rotation — protect and rebuild soil.
    4. Gully plugging, check dams and embankments on steep slopes and river banks.
    5. Controlled grazing; banning slope cutting without retaining walls.

    7.2 Model answers

    Q1 (3m, 2024) — Major causes and effects of air pollution. Answer: Causes: vehicle exhaust (CO, NOₓ, hydrocarbons), industry and brick kilns (SO₂, particulates), burning of fossil fuels and solid waste, and indoor biomass cooking. Effects: respiratory diseases (asthma, bronchitis) and CO poisoning; the greenhouse effect from CO₂ and CH₄ driving global warming and glacier retreat; acid rain from SO₂ and NOₓ acidifying lakes, soils and corroding buildings; photochemical smog (ozone, PAN) irritating eyes and damaging crops.

    Q2 (2m, 2024) — What is BOD? Why is high BOD harmful? Answer: BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand) is the amount of dissolved oxygen, in mg/L, consumed by microorganisms while decomposing the organic matter in a water sample over 5 days at 20°C — a measure of organic pollution. High BOD is harmful because microbes consume the dissolved oxygen that fish and other aquatic organisms need; oxygen falls, anaerobic decomposition takes over producing foul-smelling gases, and aquatic life dies.

    Q3 (3m, 2024) — What is soil erosion? Suggest three conservation measures practised in Nepal. Answer: Soil erosion is the removal of the fertile topsoil by water and wind, accelerated by deforestation, overgrazing and unscientific farming. Measures: (1) terracing and contour farming on slopes to slow runoff; (2) afforestation and community forestry, whose roots bind the soil; (3) cover crops, mulching and crop rotation to protect and rebuild soil — with check dams and controlled grazing as further options.

    7.3 Common examination mistakes

    1. Defining BOD as "oxygen in water" — it is oxygen demanded by microbes to decompose organics.
    2. Confusing primary pollutants (emitted directly) with secondary pollutants (formed in air).
    3. Listing pollution effects without naming the pollutant responsible.
    4. Saying eutrophication enriches water with oxygen — nutrients enrich, then oxygen collapses.
    5. Giving "plant trees" alone for soil conservation — name the specific mechanism (roots bind soil, terraces slow runoff).

Confusion

  • Primary vs secondary pollutants: primary are emitted directly (CO, SO₂); secondary form in air by reactions (ozone, PAN).
  • BOD vs DO: BOD is the oxygen demanded by decomposers; DO is the oxygen actually available — high BOD means low DO.

Key Points

  • Air: vehicles/industry/kilns → CO, SO₂, NOₓ, particulates → respiratory disease, greenhouse effect, acid rain, smog; control by catalytic converters, scrubbers, cleaner fuels.
  • Water: sewage, industry (heavy metals), fertiliser runoff → high BOD, eutrophication, biomagnification, pathogens; control by primary–secondary–tertiary sewage treatment.
  • Soil: excess fertiliser/pesticide, erosion of topsoil by water/wind; Nepal measures — terracing, community forestry, cover crops, check dams, controlled grazing.

Important Statements

  • "Pollution is a resource in the wrong place at the wrong concentration."

Examples

  • Phewa Lake — creeping eutrophication from nutrient runoff.

Universal Facts

  • Lichens die where SO₂ is high — bioindicators of air quality.

Formulas

  • Eutrophication sequence: nutrients → algal bloom → death and decay → oxygen used up → fish death.
  • Biomagnification: concentration rises at each step of the food chain for persistent fat-soluble substances.

Practice

  • Why does eutrophication kill fish even though nutrients 'enrich' the water?

    Answer. The enrichment is a chain reaction that ends in suffocation:

    1. N and P runoff triggers an algal bloom on the surface.
    2. The bloom blocks light, so submerged plants die.
    3. When the algae themselves die, decomposers multiply and consume the water's dissolved oxygen.
    4. Oxygen collapses — fish and bottom life suffocate; anaerobic decay adds foul smells and toxins.

    The nutrients feed algae, not fish; the fish die of oxygen debt, not of plenty.

Practice Questions

  • Short answer (2m): Name two secondary pollutants and how they form.

    Answer. Ozone (O₃) and PAN (peroxyacetyl nitrate) — both form in photochemical smog when primary pollutants (NOₓ and unburnt hydrocarbons from vehicles) react under strong sunlight. Neither is emitted directly; sunlight manufactures them in the air.

Exercises

1.

A lake receives fertiliser from fields. Algae cover the surface, then die. Fish are found dead although the fertiliser itself is not a nerve poison. Explain the chain.

Steps
  • Extra nutrients enter the lake with the runoff.
  • Algae multiply into a bloom because nutrients were the limit on their growth.
  • The bloom dies and bacteria decompose that mass of organic matter.
  • Decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, and fish die of oxygen shortage.
Answer

The fish die from oxygen loss after the nutrient-driven algal bloom collapses and decays. This is eutrophication.

Summary

Pollution is waste added faster than the environment can assimilate it. Air pollution from vehicles, industry, brick kilns and indoor biomass burning drives respiratory disease, the greenhouse effect, acid rain and photochemical smog, controlled by catalytic converters, scrubbers, cleaner fuels and afforestation. Water pollution from sewage, industrial heavy metals and fertiliser runoff raises BOD — microbes strip the dissolved oxygen, fish suffocate — and drives eutrophication and biomagnification, controlled by staged sewage treatment and effluent laws. Soil pollution and erosion strip the fertile topsoil; Nepal answers with terracing and contour farming, community forestry, cover crops, check dams and controlled grazing. Every pollution problem is a nutrient or energy cycle pushed out of balance.

Options
  • Fish excrete it
  • Concentration increases at each trophic level
  • It dilutes up the chain
  • Plants fix it
Answer

B

Predators accumulate the mercury of all their prey — concentration rises up the food chain.

  • Parent rock

Options

  • Slowing runoff on slopes
  • Deepening topsoil
  • Killing pests
