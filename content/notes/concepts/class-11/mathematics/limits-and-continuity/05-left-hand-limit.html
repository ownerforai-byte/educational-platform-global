Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Approaching From the Left

    The mirror of the right-hand limit: lim_{x→a⁻} f(x) reads behaviour as x approaches a through values less than a. Together with a⁺ it completes the one-sided pair whose agreement (or disagreement) decides every two-sided question.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition

    • lim_{x→a⁻} f(x) = L ⟺ f(x) → L as x runs through a − δ < x < a — from the left of a.
    • Graphically: trace the curve toward a moving rightward (from smaller x).

    2.2 Computation

    • Use the branch governing the left neighbourhood (piecewise); for blow-ups, the sign of the denominator for x < a decides ±∞.
    • Example: 1/(x−2) as x → 2⁻: denominator negative-small ⟹ −∞.

    2.3 The pair in action

    • Existence theorem: lim_{x→a} f(x) = L ⟺ LHL = RHL = L. Disagreement ⟹ DNE (jump or blow-up).
    • Jump size: RHL − LHL measures the step at a jump discontinuity.

    2.4 Left-side-only cases

    • Functions like √(−x) or log(x) at domain edges may have only a left-hand limit; the missing side is a domain fact, not a value disagreement.

  • 3. Scientific Definitions

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The left branch rule

    • Piecewise definitions assign each side its formula; the left-hand limit always consults the branch whose interval ends at a from below. Boundary ownership (≤ vs <) matters for f(a), not for the limit — limits ignore the point.

    4.2 Blow-up bookkeeping from the left

    • For x < a, x − a < 0: odd-power denominators give −∞, even powers +∞. The multiplicity of the zero (odd/even) is the whole sign story.

    4.3 Disagreement taxonomy

    • LHL ≠ RHL (both finite) ⟹ jump; one side infinite ⟹ infinite discontinuity; both infinite, opposite signs ⟹ the two-sided limit DNE with a "split asymptote".

  • 6. Common Confusions

    a⁻ means x < a: the minus locates the approach side, not the limit's sign.

  • Boundary ownership vs limits: whether a belongs to the ≤ branch affects f(a), never the one-sided limits.
  • Jump ≠ asymptote: finite disagreement is a jump; infinite behaviour is an infinite discontinuity — classify before concluding.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→2⁻} 1/(x − 2). — For x slightly less than 2, x − 2 is negative and small ⟹ 1/(x−2) is large negative: lim = −∞. Paired with the right-hand +∞, the two-sided limit does not exist — a split asymptote at x = 2.

  • NEB-style (2m): For f(x) = { x + 1 if x < 2; x² if x ≥ 2 }, evaluate lim_{x→2⁻} f(x). — The left neighbourhood belongs to the first branch: lim_{x→2⁻} (x + 1) = 3. Together with RHL = 4: jump discontinuity, size 1.
  • NEB-style (3m): For f(x) = { x² if x < 1; 3 if x = 1; 2 − x if x > 1 }, evaluate both one-sided limits at 1 and discuss. — LHL: first branch, x² → 1. RHL: third branch, 2 − x → 1. Both sides agree at 1 ⟹ lim_{x→1} f(x) = 1 exists. Value: f(1) = 3 ≠ 1 ⟹ discontinuous, but removable — redefining f(1) = 1 repairs it. The one-sided pair did the diagnosis: agreement of sides, mismatch of value.

  • 8. Deep Conceptual — The Left Side as the Sceptic's Probe

    • The LHL asks: "if the function's only history were values below a, where would it be heading?" It strips away the right side entirely — which is why it exposes step functions, piecewise joints, and one-sided definitions that a casual two-sided glance misses.
    • Sign work on the left: for c/(x − a) with c > 0, the LHL is −∞ (denominator small negative) — the mirror image of the RHL's +∞. Every c/0 sign reading flips when you cross to the left side; the side determines the denominator's sign, and the denominator's sign decides the infinity.
    • LHL in the wild: prices step down at deadlines, tax brackets step at thresholds, floor(x) steps at integers — each is a function whose left approach tells the pre-step story. Reading the left side is reading "just before the change."

  • 9. Deep Conceptual — One-Sided Limits Compose With Everything

    • One-sided limits respect the algebraic properties: LHL of a sum is the sum of LHLs, of a product the product — because each side is just an ordinary limit on a restricted domain (a punctured interval from the left).
    • Composition works too: if LHL of g at a is M and f is continuous at M, then LHL of f(g(x)) at a is f(M). This licenses the substitution-within-a-side moves used in trig and log limit work.
    • The hierarchy of verdicts: LHL alone gives a one-sided answer; LHL + RHL agreeing gives the two-sided limit; LHL + RHL disagreeing gives a jump classification. Each additional piece of information upgrades the conclusion — which is why exam solutions compute both sides before naming any limit.

  • 10. Deep Conceptual — Left Limits and the Definition of the Derivative's Mirror

    • The left-hand derivative f′₋(a) = lim_{h→0⁻} [f(a+h) − f(a)]/h is the LHL idea applied to slopes — and |x| at 0 (LHD −1, RHD +1) is the canonical case where the sides disagree.
    • Left-continuity: f is left-continuous at a iff f(a) = LHL. Closed intervals' left endpoints are tested with right-continuity and right endpoints with left-continuity — the side is determined by which side is inside the interval.
    • Deep unity: one-sidedness is not a special topic but a lens — every limit notion (value, continuity, slope) has a left and right version, and the two-sided concept is always their agreement. Master the lens once and it applies everywhere.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→2⁻} 1/(x − 2). — Side selection: x → 2⁻ means x runs through values slightly less than 2, so x − 2 is negative and shrinking toward 0. Behaviour: 1/(small negative) = large negative ⟹ lim_{x→2⁻} 1/(x − 2) = −∞. Pairing: the right-hand limit is +∞ (positive small denominator), so the two one-sided limits are infinite with opposite signs — the two-sided limit does not exist, and x = 2 is a vertical asymptote split by sign: the hyperbola dives on the left, climbs on the right.
  • NEB-style (2m): For f(x) = x + 1 (x < 2) and f(x) = x² (x ≥ 2), evaluate lim_{x→2⁻} f(x) and the jump size at 2. — Left-hand limit: the left neighbourhood x < 2 belongs to the first branch, so lim_{x→2⁻} f(x) = 2 + 1 = 3. Right-hand limit (for the comparison): the second branch gives 2² = 4. Jump size: RHL − LHL = 4 − 3 = 1 — the graph steps up by exactly 1 unit at x = 2. Verdict: both one-sided limits exist but disagree ⟹ the two-sided limit does not exist and f has a jump discontinuity at x = 2. Note that f(2) = 4 (the ≥ branch owns the boundary) — but the limits ignore the point's value entirely.
  • NEB-style (3m): Let f(x) = x² for x < 1, f(1) = 3, and f(x) = 2 − x for x > 1. Evaluate both one-sided limits at x = 1 and classify the discontinuity. — Left-hand limit: the first branch is continuous: lim_{x→1⁻} x² = 1. Right-hand limit: the third branch: lim_{x→1⁺} (2 − x) = 2 − 1 = 1. Agreement: LHL = RHL = 1 ⟹ the two-sided limit exists: lim_{x→1} f(x) = 1. Value check: f(1) = 3 ≠ 1 ⟹ f is discontinuous at x = 1, but since the limit exists the discontinuity is removable — redefining f(1) = 1 would make f continuous there. Diagnostic moral: the one-sided pair decides existence (agreement ✓), and the value decides continuity (mismatch ✗) — the two checks are independent, exactly as the three-part continuity test demands.
  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = 4 − x for x < 2, f(2) = 0: compute the LHL at 2, f(2), and determine whether f is left-continuous at 2. — LHL (x → 2⁻): the condition x < 2 governs the left side ⟹ lim (4 − x) = 4 − 2 = 2 (polynomial ⟹ substitution licensed). Value: f(2) = 0 (the middle rule fires at exactly 2). Left-continuity test: f is left-continuous at 2 iff f(2) = LHL — here 0 ≠ 2 ⟹ not left-continuous. Repair: redefining f(2) = 2 would make f left-continuous (and, if the right side also agreed at 2, fully continuous). Deep point: left-continuity is the three-part test restricted to the left side — value exists, LHL exists, they match — and it is the only continuity notion that makes sense at a right endpoint of a domain, where the left side is the interior.
  • NEB-style (5m): Evaluate lim_{x→3⁻} 5/(x − 3) and lim_{x→3⁺} 5/(x − 3), and conclude on lim_{x→3} 5/(x − 3). — LHL: as x → 3⁻, x − 3 is a small negative number; 5 divided by a small negative is a large negative ⟹ lim_{x→3⁻} 5/(x−3) = −∞. RHL: as x → 3⁺, x − 3 is small positive ⟹ 5/(small positive) → +∞. Conclusion: the sides run to opposite infinities ⟹ lim_{x→3} 5/(x−3) does not exist — the wall splits (odd power of (x−3) in the denominator). Deep point: the side determines the denominator's sign, the numerator's sign fixes the direction of the blow-up; for an even power like 5/(x−3)² both sides give +∞ (a unified wall) — the parity of the denominator's zero is the whole classification.
  • NEB-style (6m): For the piecewise function f(x) = x² for x ≤ 1, f(x) = 2x − 1 for x > 1: compute the LHL and RHL at 1, the limit if it exists, f(1), and the continuity verdict. — LHL (x → 1⁻, first piece — note ≤ includes 1): lim x² = 1. RHL (x → 1⁺, second piece): lim (2x − 1) = 2 − 1 = 1. Limit: LHL = RHL = 1 ⟹ lim_{x→1} f(x) = 1. Value: f(1) = 1² = 1 (the ≤ condition puts 1 in the first piece). Continuity: value 1 = limit 1, both parts exist ⟹ f is continuous at 1 — the joint is seamless. Deep point: which piece owns the boundary point is decided by the condition column (≤ vs <), and here the ownership mattered for f(1) but not for the limit, because the pieces' limits happened to agree — the seam is invisible exactly when the weld is good.
  • NEB-style (5m): A student computes lim_{x→0⁻} sin(1/x) by substituting x = 0 and reports the limit is 0. Diagnose the error and give the correct analysis. — The error: substitution at x = 0 is invalid twice over — sin(1/0) is undefined (1/0 is not a number), and more deeply, the function has no limiting behaviour to substitute into. Correct analysis: as x → 0⁻, 1/x → −∞, so sin(1/x) oscillates through every value in [−1, 1] infinitely often, faster and faster — values like sin(−100) and sin(−1000.5) land anywhere in the band with no settling trend. Verdict: lim_{x→0⁻} sin(1/x) does not exist (oscillatory failure — the sides never approach any single value). Contrast: multiplying by x fixes it — lim_{x→0} x·sin(1/x) = 0 by squeeze, because the amplitude x collapses even though the oscillation persists. Deep point: substitution is licensed only where the function is continuous (or the limit provably exists); oscillatory pathology is the third failure mode beyond 0/0 and c/0, and it defeats transformation too — only damping (amplitude collapse) or the squeeze theorem tames it.

Key Points

Piecewise: the branch owning the left neighbourhood decides; boundary ownership affects f(a), not the limits. Taxonomy: finite disagreement = jump; infinite side = infinite discontinuity; both sides infinite opposite = split asymptote.

Summary

The left-hand limit lim_{x→a⁻} f(x) tracks f as x approaches a from below — completing the one-sided pair. Its computation follows the branch owning the left neighbourhood (x + 1 gives 3 at the sample joint) and, at blow-ups, the denominator's sign for x < a: odd-power zeros split the asymptote (1/(x−2) → −∞ from the left vs +∞ from the right), even powers unify it. The pair's agreement is the existence theorem: LHL = RHL = L ⟺ the two-sided limit exists; finite disagreement sizes a jump (4 − 3 = 1), infinite disagreement a split asymptote, and matched sides with a mismatched value a removable break (x² vs 2−x both → 1 while f(1) = 3). Boundary ownership shapes f(a) but never the limits — limits ignore the point. Both sides, then the verdict: the one-sided pair is the diagnostic instrument of elementary calculus.

Continuity: value 1 = limit 1, both parts exist ⟹ f is continuous at 1 — the joint is seamless. Deep point: which piece owns the boundary point is decided by the condition column (≤ vs <), and here the ownership mattered for f(1) but not for the limit, because the pieces' limits happened to agree — the seam is invisible exactly when the weld is good.
  • NEB-style (5m): A student computes lim_{x→0⁻} sin(1/x) by substituting x = 0 and reports the limit is 0. Diagnose the error and give the correct analysis. — The error: substitution at x = 0 is invalid twice over — sin(1/0) is undefined (1/0 is not a number), and more deeply, the function has no limiting behaviour to substitute into. Correct analysis: as x → 0⁻, 1/x → −∞, so sin(1/x) oscillates through every value in [−1, 1] infinitely often, faster and faster — values like sin(−100) and sin(−1000.5) land anywhere in the band with no settling trend. Verdict: lim_{x→0⁻} sin(1/x) does not exist (oscillatory failure — the sides never approach any single value). Contrast: multiplying by x fixes it — lim_{x→0} x·sin(1/x) = 0 by squeeze, because the amplitude x collapses even though the oscillation persists. Deep point: substitution is licensed only where the function is continuous (or the limit provably exists); oscillatory pathology is the third failure mode beyond 0/0 and c/0, and it defeats transformation too — only damping (amplitude collapse) or the squeeze theorem tames it.
