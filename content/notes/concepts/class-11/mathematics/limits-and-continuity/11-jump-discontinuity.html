Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Step in the Graph

    A jump discontinuity occurs when both one-sided limits exist finitely but disagree. The graph steps from one height to another; no redefinition can repair it, because the limit itself does not exist.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition and detection

    • Jump at a ⟺ lim_{x→a⁻} f(x) = L and lim_{x→a⁺} f(x) = M both exist finitely with L ≠ M.
    • Detection: compute both one-sided limits (branch selection for piecewise functions); compare.

    2.2 Jump size and classification

    • Jump size = M − L (RHL − LHL) — the height of the step.
    • Jump discontinuities are non-removable: no single value f(a) can make LHL = RHL.
    • Special case — the Heaviside step: f(x) = 0 for x < 0, 1 for x ≥ 0: jump of size 1 at 0, the prototype switch.

    2.3 Where jumps live

    • Piecewise-defined functions (tax brackets, shipping rates, sign functions).
    • Floor/ceiling functions at every integer.
    • Digitised/quantised signals — sampling turns continuous waves into staircases.

    2.4 One-sided continuity

    • A jump function can still be left-continuous or right-continuous at the step: f continuous from the right at a ⟺ f(a) = RHL. The Heaviside step is right-continuous at 0.

  • 3. Scientific Definitions

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why no repair exists

    • Removability needs a limit to copy. Here the two sides report different values — there is no single L to assign; the defect is structural, in the neighbourhoods themselves.

    4.2 Reading jumps on graphs

    • A jump shows as a vertical gap: closed dot on the side owning f(a), open dot on the other. The step's height is measurable — RHL − LHL.

    4.3 Jumps in the wild

    • Switches (on/off), thresholds (tax, shipping), quantisation (audio samples): the mathematics of "below vs above" is exactly the theory of jump discontinuities.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Finite sides ≠ existence: the two-sided limit needs equal finite sides — unequal finite is still DNE.

  • f(a) doesn't matter for the jump: the step is between the sides; whether f(a) matches one of them decides one-sided continuity, not the jump's existence.
  • Jump ≠ hole: a hole keeps the sides agreeing; a jump breaks the agreement — categorically different repairs (none vs one value).

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): For f(x) = { x + 2 if x < 1; 5 − x if x > 1; 3 if x = 1 }, find both one-sided limits at 1, the jump size, and discuss continuity. — LHL: x + 2 → 3. RHL: 5 − x → 4. Jump: 4 − 3 = 1 — the two-sided limit does not exist ⟹ jump discontinuity at x = 1, non-removable. Value: f(1) = 3 = LHL ⟹ f is left-continuous at 1 but not right-continuous. No repair possible: changing f(1) can match at most one side.

  • NEB-style (2m): Show that the floor function f(x) = ⌊x⌋ has a jump discontinuity at every integer. — At n ∈ ℤ: for x ↑ n, ⌊x⌋ = n − 1 ⟹ LHL = n − 1; for x ↓ n, ⌊x⌋ = n ⟹ RHL = n. Sides finite but unequal ⟹ jump of size 1 at every integer; between integers ⌊x⌋ is constant, hence continuous. The staircase graph shows a unit step at each integer — closed dot on the right side (⌊n⌋ = n), open on the left.
  • NEB-style (2m): Construct a function with a jump of size 2 at x = 0, right-continuous there. — Take f(x) = 0 for x < 0 and f(x) = 2 for x ≥ 0. LHL = 0, RHL = 2, jump size 2 ✓. Right-continuity: f(0) = 2 = RHL ✓ — the closed dot sits on the right branch. (Any pair of constants c, c + 2 with the boundary assigned to the upper branch works.)

  • 8. Deep Conceptual — Jumps as Decisions, Not Defects

    • Some jumps are engineered: tax brackets, postage tiers, signal thresholds, on/off switches. The function's rule legitimately changes at the threshold, and the jump size measures the change. A jump is a defect only when the underlying quantity is conserved (position, money, temperature) — then a jump would mean teleportation.
    • The jump size is data: J = RHL − LHL quantifies the discontinuity. In piecewise models it is the "fee," "surcharge," or "phase change" magnitude — often the quantity the question actually cares about.
    • One-sided continuity is the natural habitat: step functions are usually continuous from one side (the dot sits on one end of each segment). Asking "continuous from the left/right" is more informative at a jump than the two-sided verdict "not continuous."

  • 9. Deep Conceptual — Jumps Defeat Every Limit Tool, By Design

    • No transformation repairs a jump: factoring, conjugates, and stars all assume the neighbourhood is trying to agree. At a jump the sides are not trying to agree — the failure is structural, in the definitions, not computational.
    • L'Hôpital does not apply: the 0/0 and ∞/∞ forms require both contestants to be approaching (the same kind of) trouble. At a jump the one-sided limits are finite and unequal — there is nothing indeterminate to resolve, only a verdict to record.
    • What still works: one-sided limits fully describe the jump; the existence theorem correctly reports failure; the graph convention (filled dot on the side that owns the value) records the assignment. The tools don't fail — they correctly certify a non-removable break.

  • 10. Deep Conceptual — Jumps and the Boundaries of the Big Theorems

    • The IVT dies at jumps: a function stepping from 3 to 7 never equals 5, even though 5 lies between its endpoint values. Continuity is exactly the hypothesis that forces the graph to pass through intermediate values — the jump is the counterexample that motivates the hypothesis.
    • Integration survives jumps: a finite number of jump discontinuities does not break the definite integral — the area reading just splits at each jump. This is why piecewise functions are integrable while being discontinuous: Riemann's theory tolerates finitely many breaks.
    • Derivatives cannot jump (Darboux): derivatives, though possibly discontinuous, never have jump discontinuities — they satisfy the intermediate value property themselves. Jumps belong to functions, not to their rates; the asymmetry is a deep structural fact.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): For f(x) = x + 2 (x < 1), f(x) = 5 − x (x > 1), f(1) = 3: find the one-sided limits at 1, the jump size, and discuss continuity. — Left-hand limit: the left branch is continuous: lim_{x→1⁻} (x + 2) = 3. Right-hand limit: lim_{x→1⁺} (5 − x) = 4. Existence: both one-sided limits exist finitely but 3 ≠ 4 ⟹ the two-sided limit does not exist and f has a jump discontinuity at x = 1, with jump size = RHL − LHL = 1. Value analysis: f(1) = 3 = LHL ⟹ f is left-continuous at 1 (the closed dot sits on the left branch) but not right-continuous. Repair verdict: none — redefining f(1) can equal at most one of {3, 4}, never both, so the jump is non-removable: the disagreement lives in the neighbourhoods, not the point.
  • NEB-style (2m): Show that the floor function f(x) = ⌊x⌋ has a jump discontinuity at every integer and is continuous elsewhere. — At an integer n: approaching from the left, x ∈ [n−1, n) gives ⌊x⌋ = n − 1 constantly ⟹ LHL = n − 1; approaching from the right, x ∈ [n, n+1) gives ⌊x⌋ = n ⟹ RHL = n. Both finite, n − 1 ≠ n ⟹ jump discontinuity of size 1 at every integer. Between integers: on any open interval (n, n+1), ⌊x⌋ is constant = n, and constants are continuous ⟹ continuous there. Graph reading: the staircase — at each integer the graph steps up one unit, with the closed dot on the right (⌊n⌋ = n, so f is right-continuous at integers) and the open dot on the left. An infinite family of unit jumps on one curve.
  • NEB-style (2m): Construct a function with a jump discontinuity of size 2 at x = 0 that is right-continuous at 0. — Construction: f(x) = 0 for x < 0 and f(x) = 2 for x ≥ 0. Verification of the jump: lim_{x→0⁻} f(x) = 0 (constant branch) and lim_{x→0⁺} f(x) = 2 — both finite and unequal ⟹ jump discontinuity with jump size = 2 − 0 = 2 ✓. Right-continuity: f(0) = 2 = RHL ✓ — the boundary value is claimed by the upper branch, placing the closed dot on the right and the open dot at height 0 on the left. (General template: constants c below and c + 2 from 0 onward, with the boundary owned by the upper branch, always works; assigning the boundary to the lower branch instead would make it left-continuous with the same jump.)
  • NEB-style (6m): A parking fee is modelled as F(t) = 5 for 0 < t ≤ 2 and F(t) = 8 for 2 < t ≤ 4 (in hours). Evaluate LHL and RHL at t = 2, the jump size, and F(2). — LHL (t → 2⁻): the first piece governs ⟹ 5. RHL (t → 2⁺): the second piece governs ⟹ 8. Jump size: J = RHL − LHL = 8 − 5 = 3 — the fee leaps by 3 the moment the second hour begins. Value: F(2) = 5 (the ≤ condition assigns 2 to the first piece — you pay the base rate through the end of the second hour). One-sided continuity: F(2) = 5 = LHL ⟹ F is left-continuous at 2 (the filled dot sits at the left segment's right end); not right-continuous (5 ≠ 8). Deep point: the ≤ in the condition is the business rule "the second hour is charged after it starts," encoded mathematically; the jump size 3 is the surcharge, and one-sided continuity records which side the dot lives on — all three readings come from the same piecewise table.
  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = x + 3 for x < 1, f(x) = 2x for x ≥ 1: show f has a jump discontinuity at 1, give the jump size, and determine whether any redefinition of f(1) can make f continuous. — LHL: lim_{x→1⁻} (x + 3) = 4. RHL: lim_{x→1⁺} 2x = 2. Verdict: both one-sided limits exist but 4 ≠ 2 ⟹ jump discontinuity at 1, jump size |4 − 2| = 2. Value: f(1) = 2 (≥ puts 1 in the second piece) — matching the RHL, so f is right-continuous at 1. Repair question: no redefinition of f(1) can help — the sickness is in the neighbourhood (the sides disagree), not at the point; any value assigned at 1 leaves LHL = 4 ≠ RHL = 2, so the two-sided limit never exists ⟹ non-removable. Deep point: the repair test ("can point-values fix it?") is what separates removable from jump: a jump needs the sides changed, which is a different function, not a different dot.
  • NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = ⌊x⌋ (floor function), analyse continuity at x = 2 and x = 1.5, and describe the general pattern at integers. — At x = 2 (an integer, a step edge): LHL = lim_{x→2⁻} ⌊x⌋ = 1 (just below 2, floor is 1); RHL = lim_{x→2⁺} ⌊x⌋ = 2; f(2) = 2. Sides disagree (1 ≠ 2) ⟹ jump discontinuity at 2, jump size 1; f(2) = 2 = RHL ⟹ right-continuous. At x = 1.5 (interior of a step): nearby x ∈ (1, 2) all have ⌊x⌋ = 1, so LHL = RHL = f(1.5) = 1 ⟹ continuous at 1.5. General pattern: ⌊x⌋ is continuous at every non-integer and has a jump of size 1 at every integer, right-continuous at each — the discontinuity set is exactly ℤ. Deep point: a function can be discontinuous at infinitely many points and still be perfectly integrable and drawable — the jumps are isolated (no accumulation in any bounded interval's interior... they accumulate only at infinity), which is why the floor function is the standard example that continuity failures can be orderly and infinite.
  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1) for x ≠ 1 with f(1) = 0, a student classifies the discontinuity at 1 as a jump. Correct the classification with full working. — Compute the sides: (x²−1)/(x−1) = x + 1 for x ≠ 1 ⟹ LHL = 2, RHL = 2. Jump requires: finite, unequal one-sided limits — here they are equal, so this is not a jump. Correct classification: the limit exists (2) but f(1) = 0 ≠ 2 ⟹ removable discontinuity — a detached dot at (1, 0) over the hole at (1, 2); repair by f(1) = 2. Why the error matters: the student skipped the one-sided computation and guessed from the picture of "a break"; the taxonomy's categories are decided by computed side values, not by the mere presence of a discontinuity. Deep point: removable and jump are distinguished by exactly one comparison — LHL vs RHL — which is why computing both sides is non-negotiable in every discontinuity classification.

    • jump size = M − L; Heaviside: jump 1 at 0, right-continuous
    • ⌊x⌋: LHL = n − 1, RHL = n at each integer n

Key Points

  • Jump discontinuity: LHL = L and RHL = M both finite with L != M; two-sided limit DNE; non-removable - no single f(a) repairs it. Jump size = M - L. Detection: compute both one-sided limits (branch selection) and compare. Homes: piecewise functions, floor/ceiling at integers, quantised signals, Heaviside step (0 below, 1 at/above 0, jump 1, right-continuous). One-sided continuity: f(a) = RHL => right-continuous; f(a) = LHL => left-continuous. Graph: vertical gap, closed dot on the owning side.

Summary

A jump discontinuity is the finite disagreement: LHL = L and RHL = M both exist with L ≠ M, so the two-sided limit does not exist and — unlike the removable case — no redefinition can help: there is no single limit value to copy. The jump size M − L measures the step (x+2 vs 5−x step by 1 at x = 1, with f(1) = 3 giving left-continuity only). Jumps live where thresholds live: piecewise rules, the floor/ceiling staircases (unit jumps at every integer, right-continuous with closed dots on the right), the Heaviside step (prototype switch, jump 1), and quantised signals. One-sided continuity survives jumps: f(a) = RHL makes f right-continuous even mid-step. Finite sides, unequal, unrepairable — the jump is the honest discontinuity of every threshold in mathematics and policy.

  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = x + 3 for x < 1, f(x) = 2x for x ≥ 1: show f has a jump discontinuity at 1, give the jump size, and determine whether any redefinition of f(1) can make f continuous. — LHL: lim_{x→1⁻} (x + 3) = 4. RHL: lim_{x→1⁺} 2x = 2. Verdict: both one-sided limits exist but 4 ≠ 2 ⟹ jump discontinuity at 1, jump size |4 − 2| = 2. Value: f(1) = 2 (≥ puts 1 in the second piece) — matching the RHL, so f is right-continuous at 1. Repair question: no redefinition of f(1) can help — the sickness is in the neighbourhood (the sides disagree), not at the point; any value assigned at 1 leaves LHL = 4 ≠ RHL = 2, so the two-sided limit never exists ⟹ non-removable. Deep point: the repair test ("can point-values fix it?") is what separates removable from jump: a jump needs the sides changed, which is a different function, not a different dot.
  • NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = ⌊x⌋ (floor function), analyse continuity at x = 2 and x = 1.5, and describe the general pattern at integers. — At x = 2 (an integer, a step edge): LHL = lim_{x→2⁻} ⌊x⌋ = 1 (just below 2, floor is 1); RHL = lim_{x→2⁺} ⌊x⌋ = 2; f(2) = 2. Sides disagree (1 ≠ 2) ⟹ jump discontinuity at 2, jump size 1; f(2) = 2 = RHL ⟹ right-continuous. At x = 1.5 (interior of a step): nearby x ∈ (1, 2) all have ⌊x⌋ = 1, so LHL = RHL = f(1.5) = 1 ⟹ continuous at 1.5. General pattern: ⌊x⌋ is continuous at every non-integer and has a jump of size 1 at every integer, right-continuous at each — the discontinuity set is exactly ℤ. Deep point: a function can be discontinuous at infinitely many points and still be perfectly integrable and drawable — the jumps are isolated (no accumulation in any bounded interval's interior... they accumulate only at infinity), which is why the floor function is the standard example that continuity failures can be orderly and infinite.
  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1) for x ≠ 1 with f(1) = 0, a student classifies the discontinuity at 1 as a jump. Correct the classification with full working. — Compute the sides: (x²−1)/(x−1) = x + 1 for x ≠ 1 ⟹ LHL = 2, RHL = 2. Jump requires: finite, unequal one-sided limits — here they are equal, so this is not a jump. Correct classification: the limit exists (2) but f(1) = 0 ≠ 2 ⟹ removable discontinuity — a detached dot at (1, 0) over the hole at (1, 2); repair by f(1) = 2. Why the error matters: the student skipped the one-sided computation and guessed from the picture of "a break"; the taxonomy's categories are decided by computed side values, not by the mere presence of a discontinuity. Deep point: removable and jump are distinguished by exactly one comparison — LHL vs RHL — which is why computing both sides is non-negotiable in every discontinuity classification.
