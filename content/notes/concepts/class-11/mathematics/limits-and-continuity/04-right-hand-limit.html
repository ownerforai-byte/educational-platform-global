Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Approaching From the Right

    The right-hand limit lim_{x→a⁺} f(x) reads the function's behaviour as x slides toward a from values greater than a. Piecewise functions, domain edges and blow-ups make one-sided thinking unavoidable.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition

    • lim_{x→a⁺} f(x) = L ⟺ f(x) → L as x takes values a < x < a + δ with δ → 0 — from the right side of a on the number line.
    • Graphically: follow the curve toward x = a moving leftward (from larger x); the y-height approached is L.

    2.2 When the right-hand limit is the whole story

    • Domain edge: √x at 0 exists only from the right (x ≥ 0) — lim_{x→0⁺} √x = 0 and no left approach is even defined.
    • Piecewise boundary: on [a, ∞) pieces, a⁺ reads the branch that owns the right neighbourhood.

    2.3 Computation

    • Substitute a value slightly greater than a into the branch/rule governing the right side; for blow-ups, track the sign of the denominator for x > a.

    2.4 The existence theorem (preview)

    • lim_{x→a} f(x) = L ⟺ both one-sided limits exist and equal L. One-sided limits are the atoms; two-sided limits are molecules.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why one side at a time

    • Functions may behave differently on either side of a — different branch formulas, different signs, different rates. One-sided limits isolate each story before the existence test merges them.

    4.2 Reading the graph right-to-left

    • The finger traces the curve from the right toward a: the height it settles toward is lim_{x→a⁺}. Open dots, jumps and asymptotes all become readable.

    4.3 Blow-up signs

    • For 1/(x − 2): x > 2 ⟹ denominator positive ⟹ +∞; the right-hand limit is +∞ while the left is −∞ — sign bookkeeping done side by side.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    a⁺ means x > a: the plus is about where x comes from, not the sign of the limit.

  • Domain edges are one-sided by nature: √x at 0 has no left-hand limit because the left side isn't in the domain — not because it disagrees.
  • ∞ on one side still kills the two-sided limit: the existence theorem needs finite agreement.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→2⁺} 1/(x − 2). — For x slightly greater than 2, x − 2 is positive and small ⟹ 1/(x−2) is large positive: lim = +∞. (The left-hand limit would be −∞ — the sides disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist.)

  • NEB-style (2m): For f(x) = { x + 1 if x < 2; x² if x ≥ 2 }, evaluate lim_{x→2⁺} f(x). — The right neighbourhood x > 2 belongs to the second branch: lim_{x→2⁺} x² = 4. (The left-hand limit via the first branch is 3 — the mismatch at 2 is a jump discontinuity of size 1.)
  • NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→0⁺} √x and explain why no left-hand limit exists. — √x is defined for x ≥ 0; approaching 0 from the right: √x → 0. From the left, √x is undefined — not a disagreement but an absence: the left-hand limit cannot even be formed. Hence the two-sided limit equals the right-hand limit 0 by default (the domain's edge makes the one-sided value the whole story).

  • 8. Deep Conceptual — One-Sided Limits as Domain Edges

    • RHL is not just half a two-sided limit — it is the full tool for domain boundaries. At the left edge of a domain (like x = 0 for √x), only the right side has data, so RHL is the only meaningful limit and continuity there is defined one-sided.
    • Piecewise definitions make RHL the primary instrument: the piece governing x ≥ a determines the RHL. Reading the condition column of a piecewise table tells you instantly which side each formula controls.
    • The existence theorem's fine print: a two-sided limit needs both sides to exist and agree. A domain edge fails the first requirement before agreement is even asked — a different failure from the jump's disagreement, and it produces different conclusions.

  • 9. Deep Conceptual — RHL and Behaviour at Infinity's Mirror

    • x → a⁺ means x slides down toward a from above: values like a + 0.1, a + 0.01, a + 0.001. The notation encodes direction of travel, not size — a⁺ is just above a, however close.
    • RHL at a vertical asymptote: for c/(x − a) with c > 0, the RHL is +∞ — the wall's right face. Knowing each face's sign (RHL vs LHL) is what classifies the wall as splitting (odd power) or unifying (even power).
    • Notation transfer: the same superscript convention reappears in derivatives (f′₊) and one-sided continuity (continuous from the right) — learning x → a⁺ once equips every one-sided question in calculus.

  • 10. Deep Conceptual — Why the Right Side Can Lie While the Left Tells Truth

    • A function can behave perfectly on one side and pathologically on the other: f(x) = x for x ≥ 0, sin(1/x) for x < 0 has RHL 0 at 0 but no LHL. One-sided limits exist precisely so such asymmetric pathology can be described at all.
    • Engineering the asymmetry: exam questions hide pathology on one side only — the side with the clean formula gives a clean limit, lulling you into skipping the other side. The existence theorem is the defence: never certify a two-sided limit without both sides.
    • RHL in definitions of record: right-continuity at endpoints, floor/ceiling behaviour, and step functions all live on the right side by convention — the convention exists because open/closed interval edges need a definite side to test.

Examples

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→2⁺} 1/(x − 2). — Side selection: x → 2⁺ means x takes values slightly greater than 2, so x − 2 is positive and shrinking. Behaviour: 1/(small positive) = large positive ⟹ lim_{x→2⁺} 1/(x − 2) = +∞. Twin fact: from the left, x − 2 is negative and small, giving −∞ — the two one-sided limits are both infinite but of opposite signs, so the two-sided limit does not exist. The function's graph shows the classic hyperbola split by a vertical asymptote at x = 2.
  • NEB-style (2m): For f(x) = x + 1 (x < 2) and f(x) = x² (x ≥ 2), evaluate lim_{x→2⁺} f(x) and lim_{x→2⁻} f(x), and describe the joint. — Right-hand: the right neighbourhood x > 2 belongs to the second branch f(x) = x², which is continuous, so lim_{x→2⁺} f(x) = 2² = 4. Left-hand: the left neighbourhood uses f(x) = x + 1: lim_{x→2⁻} f(x) = 2 + 1 = 3. Joint: the one-sided limits both exist but disagree (3 ≠ 4) ⟹ the two-sided limit does not exist and f has a jump discontinuity at x = 2 of size 4 − 3 = 1 — the graph steps up by 1 as x crosses 2.
  • NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→0⁺} √x and explain why a left-hand limit does not exist. — Right-hand limit: √x is defined for x ≥ 0, and as x → 0⁺, √x → 0 — substitution at the (right) edge works. Why no left-hand limit: for x < 0, √x is undefined over the reals — the left approach cannot even be attempted; this is an absence of domain, not a disagreement of values. Consequence: since the right-hand limit is the only one that exists, it serves as the two-sided limit relative to the domain — lim_{x→0⁺} √x = 0 is the complete story, and √x is continuous on its domain [0, ∞) (continuity at a domain endpoint needs only the one-sided test).
  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = √(x − 1), evaluate lim_{x→1⁺} f(x) and explain why no left-hand limit exists at 1. — Domain: even root needs x − 1 ≥ 0 ⟹ x ≥ 1, so the domain is [1, ∞) — the point x = 1 is the left edge. RHL: as x → 1 through values above 1 (1.1, 1.01, …), √(x−1) → √0 = 0. No LHL: for x < 1 the radicand is negative and the function has no values at all — there is nothing on the left side to approach through, so the left-hand limit is undefined (not merely different). Conclusion: only the one-sided limit exists; f is continuous at 1 from the right (value 0 = RHL 0). Deep point: domain edges automatically convert limit questions to one-sided questions — the existence theorem is truncated to the side the domain supplies, which is why interval continuity is defined with one-sided tests at endpoints.
  • NEB-style (6m): For the piecewise function f(x) = x + 2 for x > 3, f(3) = 1: compute the RHL at 3, f(3), and state whether f is right-continuous at 3. — RHL (x → 3⁺): the condition x > 3 governs the right side ⟹ lim (x + 2) = 3 + 2 = 5 (substitution licensed — the piece is a polynomial, continuous everywhere). Value: f(3) = 1 (the middle rule fires at exactly 3). Right-continuity test: f is right-continuous at 3 iff f(3) = RHL — here 1 ≠ 5 ⟹ not right-continuous. What would repair it: redefining f(3) = 5 would make f right-continuous (value matching the right side's approach); the left side's behaviour is a separate question answered by the LHL. Deep point: right-continuity is the one-sided mirror of the three-part test — value exists, RHL exists, they match — and at a point where only one side has data (or only one side is in question), it is the entire continuity verdict.
  • NEB-style (5m): Evaluate lim_{x→2⁺} (x − 2)/(x − 2)·(x + 5) — that is, lim_{x→2⁺} (x + 5) after cancellation — and lim_{x→2⁺} 3/(x − 2), contrasting the two failure modes. — First limit: (x − 2)/(x − 2) = 1 for x ≠ 2, so the expression is x + 5 for x ≠ 2 ⟹ RHL = 2 + 5 = 7. The 0/0 form hid a hole: cancellation removed the break and the limit survives. Second limit: 3/(x − 2) as x → 2⁺: the denominator is a small positive number, the numerator is fixed positive 3 ⟹ the quotient grows without bound ⟹ RHL = +∞ — a wall, not a hole. Contrast: both fail substitution at x = 2, but the first failure (0/0) is repairable by transformation while the second (c/0⁺) is a genuine unbounded blow-up whose one-sided sign is read from the sign of c and the side. Deep point: diagnosing which substitution failure you have — quiet 0/0 or loud c/0 — is the triage step that selects the technique.
  • NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = x² for x ≥ 0 and f(x) = undefined (not part of the rule) for x < 0, a student claims lim_{x→0} f(x) = 0. Assess the claim fully. — What the student has: the RHL — for x → 0⁺, x² → 0 by substitution (polynomial, continuous). What the claim overreaches: the student wrote a two-sided limit. For x < 0 the function has no values (outside its domain), so the LHL does not exist. Existence theorem: a two-sided limit requires both one-sided limits to exist and agree; with the LHL missing, lim_{x→0} f(x) does not exist — the correct statement is lim_{x→0⁺} f(x) = 0. Continuity reading: f is continuous on (0, ∞) and right-continuous at 0 (f(0) = 0 = RHL if f(0) = 0 is defined); continuity at 0 in the two-sided sense is not applicable on a domain edge. Deep point: the existence theorem has two gates — both sides must exist, then agree — and a domain edge fails the first gate; confusing "the limit I computed" with "the limit" is the exact error the two-gate structure exists to catch.

Key Points

  • Right-hand limit lim_{x->a+} f(x) = L: f(x) -> L as x -> a through values greater than a; read the graph moving leftward toward a. Domain edges (sqrt x at 0) are one-sided by nature. Piecewise: the branch owning the right neighbourhood decides. Blow-up sign: denominator's sign for x > a (+inf or -inf). Summary

The right-hand limit lim_{x→a⁺} f(x) tracks f as x approaches a from above — reading the graph right-to-left. It is the whole story at domain edges (√x at 0 → 0, with no left approach defined at all), the branch-picker at piecewise boundaries (x² branch gives 4 while x + 1 gives 3 — a jump of size 1), and the sign-reader at blow-ups (1/(x−2) → +∞ from the right, −∞ from the left). Computation is branch selection plus substitution — or denominator-sign bookkeeping for c/0 forms. The existence theorem binds the atoms: a two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist and agree (finitely); one infinite side already kills it. One side at a time, then merge — the disciplined order that every piecewise and asymptote question demands.

Continuity reading: f is continuous on (0, ∞) and right-continuous at 0 (f(0) = 0 = RHL if f(0) = 0 is defined); continuity at 0 in the two-sided sense is not applicable on a domain edge. Deep point: the existence theorem has two gates — both sides must exist, then agree — and a domain edge fails the first gate; confusing "the limit I computed" with "the limit" is the exact error the two-gate structure exists to catch.
