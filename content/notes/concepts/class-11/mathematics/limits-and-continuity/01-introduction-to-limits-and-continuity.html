Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Idea of Approaching

    A limit asks what value f(x) approaches as x nears a — not what it reaches. This opening topic installs the intuition, the notation, and the one-sided/existence scaffolding the whole calculus unit stands on.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 The limit idea

    • lim_{x→a} f(x) = L means f(x) can be made arbitrarily close to L by taking x sufficiently close to a (from either side), x ≠ a.
    • The value at a is irrelevant: f may be undefined at a (the hole case) or take a different value — the limit reads the neighbourhood, not the point.

    2.1b Notation and reading

    • x → a⁺ (from the right), x → a⁻ (from the left); the two-sided limit exists only if both one-sided limits agree (row 32's theorem).
    • Indeterminate forms 0/0, ∞/∞, 0·∞ signal work needed, not an answer: factor, rationalise, or use standard limits.

    2.2 Continuity preview

    • A function is continuous at a when three things line up: f(a) exists, lim_{x→a} f(x) exists, and the two are equal. Graphically: pen never lifts.

    2.3 Why limits matter

    • Derivatives are limits of difference quotients; integrals are limits of sums; asymptotes are limits at infinity. Limits are calculus's atomic operation.

  • 3. Scientific Definitions

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 The hole is the point

    • (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is undefined at x = 2 yet approaches 4: factoring to x + 2 (valid since x ≠ 2) exposes the approach value. Limits see through holes.

    4.2 Indeterminate ≠ undefined

    • 0/0 is indeterminate: many values possible, decided only by transforming the expression. 5/0 is not indeterminate — it signals blow-up (infinite limit or DNE).

    4.3 The three-part continuity test

    • Memorise as a checklist: exists at a; limit exists; they match. Each can fail independently — and each failure mode is a named discontinuity (rows 36–38).

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Limit ≠ value: lim can exist where f(a) doesn't — the neighbourhood decides, not the point.

  • 0/0 is not 1 or 0: it is a request for transformation, never an answer.
  • x ≠ a is part of the definition: the approach excludes the destination by design.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→2} (x² − 4)/(x − 2). — Direct substitution gives 0/0 (indeterminate — transform, don't conclude). Factor: (x−2)(x+2)/(x−2) = x + 2 for x ≠ 2. Limit: 2 + 2 = 4. The cancellation is licensed precisely because x ≠ 2 in the limiting process.

  • NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→3} (2x + 1). — Polynomials are continuous everywhere: the limit equals the value by direct substitution — 2(3) + 1 = 7. The general theorem: for any polynomial P, lim_{x→a} P(x) = P(a); the same holds for |x| and roots at interior points.
  • NEB-style (2m): The function f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1) for x ≠ 1, f(1) = 3. Discuss continuity at x = 1. — Limit: factoring gives x + 1 for x ≠ 1, so lim_{x→1} f(x) = 2. Value: f(1) = 3. Test: limit exists (2) but ≠ value (3) ⟹ discontinuous at x = 1 — a removable discontinuity: redefining f(1) = 2 would repair it (row 36's theme).

  • 8. Deep Conceptual — The Neighbourhood, Not the Point

    • The definition builds x ≠ a into the approach: the limit is decided by what happens around a, never at a. This is why a hole, a wrong value, or even no value at a cannot stop a limit from existing.
    • Consequence: limits and evaluation are independent questions. f(2) can be undefined, wrong, or 100 while lim_{x→2} f(x) = 4 — the three-part continuity test exists precisely because these two answers can disagree.
    • Reading direction: a limit is a prediction — "if you walked along the curve toward a, where would you arrive?" — not a report of what is there. Every transformation technique (factoring, conjugates) is just clearing the view of the road, not changing the destination.

  • 9. Deep Conceptual — Why One Number Must Be Approached From Both Sides

    • A real line has two directions of arrival, so a single "approaching value" only exists if both directions agree. This is the deep reason the existence theorem (LHL = RHL) is not a convention but a necessity: with two sides, one value each, agreement is the only way to name one limit.
    • The asymmetry of failure: when the sides disagree, nothing is produced — not the average, not the closer one. Disagreement destroys the limit entirely, which is why piecewise joints are the standard stress test.
    • Two-sided thinking powers everything later: derivatives (two-sided difference quotients), continuity (two-sided match with f(a)), and differentiability (two-sided slope agreement) all reuse this one architectural idea.

  • 10. Deep Conceptual — Limits as the Contract Calculus Rests On

    • Every calculus object is a limit in costume: the derivative is a limit of slopes, the integral a limit of sums, continuity a limit matching a value. Mastering the neighbourhood idea once buys the whole subject.
    • Indeterminate forms are work orders, not verdicts: 0/0 does not mean "fails" — it means "not yet decided; transform and retry." Reading 0/0 as a stop sign is the single most common beginner error; reading it as a task is the skill.
    • The determinate forms carry information too: c/0⁺ → +∞ says the function is unbounded near a (a wall, not a hole); 0/c → 0 says the numerator dies faster than the denominator. Limits classify behaviour, they don't just compute numbers.

Examples

  • Seeing through the hole - (x^2 - 4)/(x - 2) is undefined at 2 yet limits to 4: factoring is legal because the definition excludes x = 2.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→2} (x² − 4)/(x − 2). — Step 1 — substitute: (4 − 4)/(2 − 2) = 0/0 — an indeterminate form, which demands transformation rather than a verdict. Step 2 — factor: x² − 4 = (x − 2)(x + 2), so the expression is (x − 2)(x + 2)/(x − 2) = x + 2 for x ≠ 2 — the cancellation is licensed because the limiting process never uses x = 2 itself. Step 3 — substitute again: lim = 2 + 2 = 4. The function has a hole at (2, 4): the limit exists even though f(2) does not — the neighbourhood, not the point, decides.
  • NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→3} (2x + 1), citing the relevant theorem. — Theorem: every polynomial P is continuous on all of ℝ, so lim_{x→a} P(x) = P(a) — direct substitution is licensed. Compute: P(3) = 2(3) + 1 = 7. The same theorem covers roots and |x| at interior points of their domains; substitution fails only at the special spots (denominator zeros, radicand boundaries, piecewise joints) where the machinery of indeterminate forms and one-sided limits takes over.
  • NEB-style (2m): The function f is given by f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1) for x ≠ 1 and f(1) = 3. Discuss the continuity of f at x = 1. — Three-part test. Part 1: f(1) = 3 exists ✓. Part 2: the limit — factoring (x² − 1)/(x − 1) = x + 1 for x ≠ 1 gives lim_{x→1} f(x) = 2 ✓. Part 3: f(1) = 3 ≠ 2 = limit ✗ — the test fails at the third step, so f is discontinuous at x = 1. Classification: since the limit exists, the failure is purely at the point's value — a removable discontinuity: redefining f(1) = 2 would make f continuous there (the "pen lift" is a single detached dot, easily re-attached).
  • NEB-style (5m): Evaluate lim_{x→−3} (x² + 5x + 6)/(x + 3), explaining each step and the meaning of the 0/0 form. — Step 1 — substitute: (9 − 15 + 6)/(0) = 0/0 — an indeterminate form, which is a work order, not an answer; it says the numerator and denominator both die at x = −3 and their rates of dying decide the limit. Step 2 — factor: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 3)(x + 2), so the expression = x + 2 for x ≠ −3 (cancellation licensed — the definition only uses x ≠ −3). Step 3 — substitute: lim = −3 + 2 = −1. Meaning: f has a hole at (−3, −1); the curve's two branches would meet there if the point existed. Deep point: the 0/0 form is the signature of a common factor — whenever substitution yields 0/0, the denominator's root divides the numerator, so factoring is guaranteed to make progress.
  • NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = (x² − 9)/(x − 3) for x ≠ 3, f(3) = 5: find lim_{x→3} f(x), f(3), and state whether the limit depends on f(3). — Limit: factoring, (x−3)(x+3)/(x−3) = x + 3 for x ≠ 3 ⟹ lim_{x→3} f(x) = 6. Value: f(3) = 5 (given). Independence: the limit does not depend on f(3) — the definition approaches through x ≠ 3, so the point's value is invisible to the limit. Consequence for continuity: limit 6 ≠ value 5 ⟹ f is discontinuous at 3, but the mismatch is a single wrong value, so the break is removable: redefining f(3) = 6 repairs it. Deep point: this question is the neighbourhood principle in miniature — the limit reads the road, f(3) reads the destination sign, and continuity is the statement that they tell the same story.
  • NEB-style (5m): Using the squeeze theorem, evaluate lim_{x→0} x²·sin(1/x). — The obstacle: sin(1/x) oscillates infinitely fast near 0 — no side limit exists for it alone, so substitution and transformation both fail. The squeeze setup: −1 ≤ sin(1/x) ≤ 1 for every x ≠ 0. Multiply by x² ≥ 0: −x² ≤ x² sin(1/x) ≤ x². Take the limits of the rails: lim_{x→0} (−x²) = 0 and lim_{x→0} x² = 0 — both rails converge to 0. Squeeze conclusion: the middle function is pinned between two rails converging to 0, so lim_{x→0} x² sin(1/x) = 0. Deep point: the squeeze theorem converts bounded chaos into a limit — the oscillation never settles, but its amplitude x² collapses to 0 fast enough to drag the product along; this is exactly the mechanism that later makes x·sin(1/x) continuous at 0.
  • NEB-style (6m): The function f(x) = (x² − 4x + 3)/(x − 1) for x ≠ 1. Find the limit at x = 1, sketch-reason the graph's behaviour there, and state what single value at x = 1 would make f continuous. — Substitute first: (1 − 4 + 3)/(0) = 0/0 ⟹ transform. Factor: x² − 4x + 3 = (x − 1)(x − 3) ⟹ f(x) = x − 3 for x ≠ 1. Limit: lim_{x→1} f(x) = −2. Graph behaviour: f is the line y = x − 3 with a single hole at (1, −2) — the two branches approach that point from both sides but the point itself is missing (open dot). Repair value: defining f(1) = −2 makes value = limit, satisfying all three continuity parts ⟹ f becomes continuous at 1 (the line with the dot filled in). Deep point: the whole analysis flows from one factoring — a 0/0 form always means a shared root, and once the shared root is divided out, the limit, the graph's shape, and the repair value all fall out of the simplified expression.

Formulas

    • lim_{x→a} f(x) = L ⟺ lim_{x→a⁻} = lim_{x→a⁺} = L
    • (x² − a²)/(x − a) = x + a (x ≠ a) ⟹ limit 2a
    • continuity at a: f(a) = lim_{x→a} f(x) (both exist)

Key Points

  • Limit: lim_{x->a} f(x) = L means f(x) approaches L as x approaches a from either side, x != a; the value at a is irrelevant. One-sided notation x -> a+, x -> a-. Indeterminate forms 0/0, inf/inf demand transformation (factor, rationalise, standard limits); 5/0 signals blow-up, not indeterminate. Continuity at a (three-part test): f(a) exists, limit exists, and they are equal - pen never lifts. Polynomial theorem: lim P(x) at a = P(a). Hole example: (x^2-4)/(x-2) -> x + 2 -> limit 4. Limits are the atomic operation behind derivatives, integrals and asymptotes.

Summary

The limit lim_{x→a} f(x) = L is the value f(x) approaches as x nears a from both sides — with x ≠ a built into the definition, so holes and redefinitions at a don't block the approach: (x²−4)/(x−2) limits to 4 through its hole, and (x²−1)/(x−1) with f(1) = 3 fails continuity only because value 3 ≠ limit 2 (a removable break). One-sided notation x → a⁺, a⁻ sets up the existence theorem (both sides must agree). Indeterminate forms — 0/0 chief among them — are work orders, not answers: factor, rationalise, or invoke standard limits, then substitute. Continuity at a is the three-part checklist: f(a) exists, the limit exists, they're equal — the pen never lifts. Polynomials pass everywhere (lim P(x) = P(a)), making substitution the default and transformation the exception that proves the rule. Limits are calculus's atom: derivatives, integrals and asymptotes are all limits in costume.

Repair value: defining f(1) = −2 makes value = limit, satisfying all three continuity parts ⟹ f becomes continuous at 1 (the line with the dot filled in). Deep point: the whole analysis flows from one factoring — a 0/0 form always means a shared root, and once the shared root is divided out, the limit, the graph's shape, and the repair value all fall out of the simplified expression.
