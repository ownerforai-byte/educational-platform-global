Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Vertical Wall

    An infinite discontinuity occurs when f blows up at a: at least one one-sided limit is ±∞. The graph sprouts a vertical asymptote x = a — the line the curve approaches but never touches.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Definition and detection

    • Infinite discontinuity at a ⟺ lim_{x→a⁻} f(x) or lim_{x→a⁺} f(x) is +∞ or −∞.
    • Detection: c/0 forms (numerator → c ≠ 0, denominator → 0); also log x at 0⁺, and unbounded growth like e^{1/x} compositions.

    2.2 The two species of blow-up

    • Split asymptote: sides infinite with opposite signs — 1/x at 0 (−∞ left, +∞ right); caused by odd-multiplicity zeros of the denominator.
    • Unified blow-up: both sides the same infinity — 1/x² at 0 (+∞ both); even multiplicity zeros.
    • Either way the two-sided limit does not exist — infinity is not a value.

    2.3 The vertical asymptote

    • x = a is a vertical asymptote ⟺ |f(x)| → ∞ as x → a from at least one side. The curve hugs the line without meeting it (or crosses it far away — asymptotes describe local behaviour).
    • Denominator zeros of rational functions (after cancelling common factors) are the standard asymptote factories.

    2.4 Non-removability

    • No redefinition helps: the neighbourhood values are unbounded, so no finite f(a) reconciles them. Infinite discontinuities are non-removable by nature.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Multiplicity is the sign story

    • Near a, (x − a)ᵐ changes sign across a iff m is odd. Dividing c by it: odd m ⟹ the sides split (±∞); even m ⟹ they unify (+∞ after squaring). The multiplicity of the denominator zero is the entire classification.

    4.2 Cancel first, then triage

    • Cancel common factors (removable holes at those zeros), then the surviving denominator zeros give asymptotes. The (x+2)/(x²−4) pattern: hole at −2, wall at 2.

    4.3 Logs and composites

    • ln x → −∞ as x → 0⁺: a one-sided wall at the domain edge. Compositions inherit blow-ups: e^{1/x} → ∞ from the right, → 1·... → e^{−∞} = 0 from the left — walls can wear disguises.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    ∞ is not a limit value: "limit = ∞" is shorthand for unbounded behaviour; the two-sided limit still DNE.

  • Every denominator zero is an asymptote: only after cancelling common factors — shared zeros are holes first.
  • Asymptotes can be crossed: the curve may cross x = a far from a; the asymptote constrains behaviour near a.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB-style (3m): Find the vertical asymptotes of f(x) = (x + 1)/(x² − 3x + 2) and classify each. — Factor: x² − 3x + 2 = (x − 1)(x − 2); numerator −1 at both ⟹ no cancellation. At x = 1: denominator (x−1)(x−2) ≈ (small)(−1) — sign flips across 1 (odd multiplicity) ⟹ split asymptote: −∞/∞ sides. At x = 2: (x−2) odd power ⟹ split asymptote. Both are infinite discontinuities; f → ±∞ near each.

  • NEB-style (2m): Show that lim_{x→0⁺} ln x = −∞ and interpret graphically. — For x → 0⁺, ln x = −|ln x| → −∞: the log dives without bound at its domain's left edge. Graph: x = 0 (the y-axis) is a one-sided vertical asymptote — the curve hugs it downward; no left side exists (domain x > 0), so the wall is visible only from the right.
  • NEB-style (2m): Discuss lim_{x→0} e^{1/x}. — Right side: x → 0⁺ ⟹ 1/x → +∞ ⟹ e^{1/x} → +∞. Left side: 1/x → −∞ ⟹ e^{1/x} → e^{−∞} = 0. Verdict: RHL = +∞ (infinite), LHL = 0 — the right side blows up ⟹ infinite discontinuity at 0 (non-removable), with a disguised wall: the exponential turns the split of 1/x into a one-sided blow-up against a finite left approach.

  • 8. Deep Conceptual — Walls Are Real Features, Not Failures of Nerve

    • An infinite discontinuity encodes unboundedness, which is genuine mathematical information: near a wall the function takes arbitrarily large values. Writing "the limit is ∞" is a description of that behaviour; writing "the limit does not exist" is the strict verdict — ∞ is not a real number, so the limit still fails to exist. Both statements belong in a full answer.
    • The two wall geometries: an odd-power zero in the denominator (x − a)¹, (x − a)³ sends the sides to opposite infinities (splitting wall); an even-power zero (x − a)² sends both sides to the same infinity (unifying wall). The parity of the denominator's root is the entire classification, readable before any computation.
    • Vertical asymptotes in context: 1/x at 0, tan x at π/2 + kπ, ln x at 0⁺ — walls are where the model's quantity divides by something vanishing, and the side-by-side sign reading is the physicist's "which way does it blow up?"

  • 9. Deep Conceptual — Infinite Limits Compose With the Algebra of Signs

    • The limit laws partially extend to infinities: ∞ + c = ∞, ∞·c > 0 = ∞, ∞/c = ∞ — but ∞ − ∞, 0·∞, ∞/∞ stay indeterminate: two infinities can compete, and their race's outcome needs the rates, not the signs.
    • Degree comparison is the race referee: for rational functions at infinity, higher numerator degree wins (∞), higher denominator degree loses (0), ties resolve by leading coefficients. At a finite wall, the numerator's nonzero value at a against the denominator's zero decides the blow-up.
    • Why c/0 is not ∞/∞: c/0 has one contestant vanishing and one standing still — no race, just a verdict read sign-by-side. Confusing the two is what makes students try L'Hôpital on c/0, where it does not apply.

  • 10. Deep Conceptual — Infinite Discontinuity vs Removable: The Value of the Numerator

    • At a denominator zero x = a, the numerator's behaviour decides everything: numerator(a) ≠ 0 ⟹ wall (infinite, non-removable); numerator(a) = 0 ⟹ 0/0, a shared root ⟹ removable after cancellation. One evaluation of the numerator sorts the cases.
    • This is why factoring is the master move: full factorisation displays which roots cancel (removable holes) and which survive in the denominator (walls). The discontinuity classification and the limit computation are the same act of factoring read two ways.
    • Walls and integration: an infinite discontinuity inside an interval makes the ordinary definite integral undefined — the area reading needs improper-integral machinery. Walls are where even the definite integral's default licence expires.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (3m): Find the vertical asymptotes of f(x) = (x + 1)/(x² − 3x + 2) and classify the behaviour at each. — Factor: x² − 3x + 2 = (x − 1)(x − 2). Cancellation check: the numerator x + 1 vanishes at neither 1 nor 2 (x + 1 = 0 ⟹ x = −1, not a denominator zero) — no common factors, so both zeros survive as asymptote candidates. At x = 1: near 1, (x − 2) ≈ −1 (negative), so the denominator ≈ −(x − 1): an odd power of (x − 1) ⟹ the sign flips across 1 ⟹ split asymptote: lim_{x→1⁻} f = +∞ (small negative denominator over +2 numerator... numerator ≈ 2 > 0, denominator → 0⁻ ⟹ −∞; from the right +∞) — the sides are −∞ and +∞. At x = 2: numerator ≈ 3 > 0, denominator ≈ 3(x − 2) — again odd ⟹ split asymptote (−∞ left, +∞ right). Verdict: vertical asymptotes x = 1 and x = 2, both with split (odd-multiplicity) behaviour; both discontinuities infinite and non-removable.
  • NEB-style (2m): Show that lim_{x→0⁺} ln x = −∞ and interpret the graph. — Computation: for 0 < x < 1, ln x is negative, and as x shrinks, |ln x| grows without bound (e.g. ln 0.001 ≈ −6.9, ln 10⁻⁶ ≈ −13.8): hence lim_{x→0⁺} ln x = −∞. Graphical interpretation: the y-axis (x = 0) is a one-sided vertical asymptote — the curve plunges downward hugging the axis, never touching it. Why one-sided: ln x is defined only for x > 0; the left approach does not exist (a domain absence, not a disagreement), so the wall is visible only from the right — the log's wall stands at the edge of its world.
  • NEB-style (2m): Discuss lim_{x→0} e^{1/x} using one-sided analysis. — Right side: x → 0⁺ ⟹ 1/x → +∞ ⟹ e^{1/x} → e^{+∞} = +∞ — unbounded growth. Left side: x → 0⁻ ⟹ 1/x → −∞ ⟹ e^{1/x} → e^{−∞} = 0 — a quiet, finite approach. Verdict: the right-hand limit is infinite ⟹ f has an infinite discontinuity at x = 0 (non-removable), and the two-sided limit does not exist — but note the disguise: 1/x alone splits into −∞/+∞, while its exponential converts the left side's −∞ into the finite 0 (e^{−∞} = 0) and amplifies the right side's +∞. The wall survives on one side only — composition can transmute but not always erase a blow-up.
  • NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = (x + 1)/(x² − 3x + 2), find all vertical asymptotes and describe the one-sided behaviour at each. — Factor the denominator: x² − 3x + 2 = (x − 1)(x − 2) ⟹ walls at x = 1 and x = 2 (check the numerator there: 1 + 1 = 2 ≠ 0 and 2 + 1 = 3 ≠ 0 ⟹ no cancellation, both walls are genuine/infinite). At x = 1: denominator ≈ (x−1)(1−2) = −(x−1); numerator → 2. For x → 1⁺: (x−1) > 0 small ⟹ denominator small negative ⟹ quotient → −∞; for x → 1⁻: denominator small positive ⟹ → +∞. Splitting wall (odd power). At x = 2: denominator ≈ (x−2)(2−1) = (x−2); numerator → 3. For x → 2⁺: small positive denominator ⟹ → +∞; for x → 2⁻: small negative ⟹ → −∞. Splitting wall. Deep point: the sign chart near each wall needs only three ingredients — the numerator's nonzero sign, the denominator's factored form, and the side — and the odd/even parity of each root's power predicts splitting vs unifying before any values are computed.
  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x² − 4x + 4), classify the discontinuity at x = 2 completely. — Factor: numerator (x−2)(x+2); denominator (x−2)² ⟹ f(x) = (x+2)/(x−2) for x ≠ 2. One factor cancelled, one survived: the cancelled (x−2) would have been removable, but the surviving (x−2) in the denominator dominates. Behaviour at 2: numerator → 4 ≠ 0; denominator → 0⁺ from both sides (a square) ⟹ f → +∞ from both sides — a unifying wall (even power), lim = +∞ (description), limit does not exist (verdict). Classification: infinite (non-removable) discontinuity at x = 2. Deep point: when a root appears in both numerator and denominator, compare powers: numerator's power 1 vs denominator's power 2 ⟹ one power cancels, one remains ⟹ the break is infinite, not removable — removability requires the denominator's root to be fully cancelled.
  • NEB-style (6m): Evaluate the one-sided limits of f(x) = 1/(x − 2)² + 3 at x = 2 and sketch-reason the graph's behaviour there. — Both sides: (x − 2)² > 0 for every x ≠ 2, regardless of side ⟹ 1/(x−2)² → +∞ from both the left and the right. Shift: adding 3 does not change the blow-up — f → +∞ from both sides; the limit does not exist (no finite value), but the wall is unifying (even power) rather than splitting. Graph reading: both branches of the curve rise along the vertical line x = 2, hugging it upward; the +3 lifts the branches' tails so the curve tends to the horizontal level y = 3 far from the wall. Deep point: the even power makes the two sides agree at infinity — a rare case where the existence theorem's agreement condition is satisfied at an infinite level; convention records this as lim = +∞ while the strict finite-limit verdict remains "does not exist."
  • NEB-style (5m): A student applies L'Hôpital's rule to lim_{x→0⁺} 1/x and obtains lim (0)/(−1/x²) = 0. Diagnose the error completely. — The form check fails: at x → 0⁺, the numerator 1 is a nonzero constant, the denominator x → 0 — the form is c/0⁺ → +∞, not 0/0 and not ∞/∞. L'Hôpital's hypotheses: the rule applies only to 0/0 or ∞/∞; applying it to c/0 is outside its licence, so the "answer" 0 is meaningless. Correct evaluation: numerator positive constant, denominator small positive ⟹ quotient large positive ⟹ lim_{x→0⁺} 1/x = +∞ (blow-up; the limit does not exist as a finite value). Deep point: the form check is not paperwork — it is the hypothesis test that gates the rule; every L'Hôpital solution must open by naming the indeterminate form, and c/0 is precisely the common case where a student's shortcut manufactures a wrong finite answer from a genuine wall.

Summary

An infinite discontinuity is the wall: at least one one-sided limit is ±∞, the two-sided limit does not exist, and no redefinition can help — the neighbourhood itself is unbounded. The visible signature is the vertical asymptote x = a, and the multiplicity rule tells the two species apart: odd-power denominator zeros split the sides (1/x: −∞ left, +∞ right), even-power zeros unify them (1/x²: +∞ both). Protocol: factor, cancel common factors first (shared zeros are removable holes), then triage the survivors — (x+1)/(x²−3x+2) has split walls at x = 1 and x = 2. Walls also appear at domain edges (ln x → −∞ at 0⁺, one-sided) and in disguise (e^{1/x}: +∞ right, 0 left — composition transmutes the split). Infinity is behaviour, not a value; the wall is approached, never touched.

  • NEB-style (2m): Discuss lim_{x→0} e^{1/x} using one-sided analysis. — Right side: x → 0⁺ ⟹ 1/x → +∞ ⟹ e^{1/x} → e^{+∞} = +∞ — unbounded growth. Left side: x → 0⁻ ⟹ 1/x → −∞ ⟹ e^{1/x} → e^{−∞} = 0 — a quiet, finite approach. Verdict: the right-hand limit is infinite ⟹ f has an infinite discontinuity at x = 0 (non-removable), and the two-sided limit does not exist — but note the disguise: 1/x alone splits into −∞/+∞, while its exponential converts the left side's −∞ into the finite 0 (e^{−∞} = 0) and amplifies the right side's +∞. The wall survives on one side only — composition can transmute but not always erase a blow-up.
  • NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = (x + 1)/(x² − 3x + 2), find all vertical asymptotes and describe the one-sided behaviour at each. — Factor the denominator: x² − 3x + 2 = (x − 1)(x − 2) ⟹ walls at x = 1 and x = 2 (check the numerator there: 1 + 1 = 2 ≠ 0 and 2 + 1 = 3 ≠ 0 ⟹ no cancellation, both walls are genuine/infinite). At x = 1: denominator ≈ (x−1)(1−2) = −(x−1); numerator → 2. For x → 1⁺: (x−1) > 0 small ⟹ denominator small negative ⟹ quotient → −∞; for x → 1⁻: denominator small positive ⟹ → +∞. Splitting wall (odd power). At x = 2: denominator ≈ (x−2)(2−1) = (x−2); numerator → 3. For x → 2⁺: small positive denominator ⟹ → +∞; for x → 2⁻: small negative ⟹ → −∞. Splitting wall. Deep point: the sign chart near each wall needs only three ingredients — the numerator's nonzero sign, the denominator's factored form, and the side — and the odd/even parity of each root's power predicts splitting vs unifying before any values are computed.
  • NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x² − 4x + 4), classify the discontinuity at x = 2 completely. — Factor: numerator (x−2)(x+2); denominator (x−2)² ⟹ f(x) = (x+2)/(x−2) for x ≠ 2. One factor cancelled, one survived: the cancelled (x−2) would have been removable, but the surviving (x−2) in the denominator dominates. Behaviour at 2: numerator → 4 ≠ 0; denominator → 0⁺ from both sides (a square) ⟹ f → +∞ from both sides — a unifying wall (even power), lim = +∞ (description), limit does not exist (verdict). Classification: infinite (non-removable) discontinuity at x = 2. Deep point: when a root appears in both numerator and denominator, compare powers: numerator's power 1 vs denominator's power 2 ⟹ one power cancels, one remains ⟹ the break is infinite, not removable — removability requires the denominator's root to be fully cancelled.
  • NEB-style (6m): Evaluate the one-sided limits of f(x) = 1/(x − 2)² + 3 at x = 2 and sketch-reason the graph's behaviour there. — Both sides: (x − 2)² > 0 for every x ≠ 2, regardless of side ⟹ 1/(x−2)² → +∞ from both the left and the right. Shift: adding 3 does not change the blow-up — f → +∞ from both sides; the limit does not exist (no finite value), but the wall is unifying (even power) rather than splitting. Graph reading: both branches of the curve rise along the vertical line x = 2, hugging it upward; the +3 lifts the branches' tails so the curve tends to the horizontal level y = 3 far from the wall. Deep point: the even power makes the two sides agree at infinity — a rare case where the existence theorem's agreement condition is satisfied at an infinite level; convention records this as lim = +∞ while the strict finite-limit verdict remains "does not exist."
  • NEB-style (5m): A student applies L'Hôpital's rule to lim_{x→0⁺} 1/x and obtains lim (0)/(−1/x²) = 0. Diagnose the error completely. — The form check fails: at x → 0⁺, the numerator 1 is a nonzero constant, the denominator x → 0 — the form is c/0⁺ → +∞, not 0/0 and not ∞/∞. L'Hôpital's hypotheses: the rule applies only to 0/0 or ∞/∞; applying it to c/0 is outside its licence, so the "answer" 0 is meaningless. Correct evaluation: numerator positive constant, denominator small positive ⟹ quotient large positive ⟹ lim_{x→0⁺} 1/x = +∞ (blow-up; the limit does not exist as a finite value). Deep point: the form check is not paperwork — it is the hypothesis test that gates the rule; every L'Hôpital solution must open by naming the indeterminate form, and c/0 is precisely the common case where a student's shortcut manufactures a wrong finite answer from a genuine wall.
