Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Statics and Dynamics: The Two Halves of Mechanics

    Mechanics splits by one question: is the body accelerating? Statics studies bodies at rest or in uniform motion — forces in balance; dynamics studies accelerated motion — forces causing change. This canonical is the overview that anchors both halves in Newton's three laws.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • First law (inertia): a body remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless a net external force acts — rest and uniform motion are the same state, distinguished by frame only.
    • Second law: net force = rate of change of momentum = ma (for constant mass) — the quantitative engine of dynamics.
    • Third law: forces occur in mutual pairs, equal and opposite, acting on different bodies — never cancelling on one body.
    • Statics' condition: equilibrium ⟺ ΣF = 0 (and Σmoment = 0 for extended bodies); dynamics' condition: ΣF = ma.
    • Kinematics toolkit: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as — the three equations of uniformly accelerated motion.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Statics as the Degenerate Case of Dynamics

    • Setting a = 0 in ΣF = ma collapses dynamics into statics: the second law doesn't retire at equilibrium — it reads "the forces sum to zero." Every statics problem is a dynamics problem whose acceleration is known to be zero.
    • Why the third law never cancels forces: action and reaction act on different bodies, so they never appear together in one body's ΣF — the common misconception that they "balance" confuses the bookkeeping of two bodies with the balance of one. Equilibrium is about one body's force inventory; the third law is about pairs of bodies.
    • Inertia as the zero-force behaviour: the first law is not a preamble to the second but its a = 0 reading plus a claim about frames — it defines the inertial frames in which ΣF = ma is even true.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — The Three Equations as a Complete System

    • For uniform acceleration, the three kinematic equations connect the five quantities (u, v, a, t, s); any problem supplies three and asks for the others. Choosing the equation that omits the unknown you don't care about is the whole solution strategy: no t ⟹ v² = u² + 2as; no s ⟹ v = u + at; no v ⟹ s = ut + ½at².
    • Sign discipline is the hidden content: gravity down, initial velocity up — the equations are vector statements along one axis; every "tricky" projectile-style answer error is a sign error, not a physics error. Declaring the positive direction first is the discipline that makes the algebra mechanical.

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — statics identification: decide equilibrium versus acceleration from ΣF.
    • Type B — equation selection: pick the kinematic equation omitting the irrelevant quantity.
    • Type C — sign discipline: motion under gravity with declared positive direction.
    • Type D — law application: identify which Newton law answers a given scenario.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Action–reaction cancel": they act on different bodies — only forces on the same body can cancel. "An object at rest has no forces": it has forces in balance — ΣF = 0, not F = 0 on each.

Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (5m): A book rests on a table. Identify all forces acting on it and show they balance; explain why the book's weight and its reaction on the table do not cancel. — Forces on the book: weight W (down, the Earth's pull) and the table's normal reaction N (up). Balance: no acceleration ⟹ ΣF = 0 ⟹ N = W. The non-cancellation: the book's weight and the book's reaction on the table (equal to N by the third law) act on different bodies — table and book — so they never enter the same force sum; the book is in equilibrium because its own two forces (W and N) cancel, not because of any action–reaction pair. Deep point: equilibrium is one body's ledger; the third law is a two-body pairing rule — confusing them produces the classic "everything cancels so nothing moves" paradox, dissolved by asking "forces on whom?"
  • NEB-style (6m): A particle starts from rest with uniform acceleration 4 m/s². Find its velocity and displacement after 3 s, and verify with a third equation. — Data: u = 0, a = 4, t = 3. Velocity: v = u + at = 0 + 12 = 12 m/s. Displacement: s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½·4·9 = 18 m. Cross-check via v² = u² + 2as: 144 = 0 + 2·4·s ⟹ s = 144/8 = 18 m ✓ — two independent routes agree. Deep point: the verification used the equation that omits t — the one quantity already consumed; a three-equation system on three knowns is overdetermined, and the spare equation is a free correctness check worth one line.
  • NEB-style (6m): A stone is dropped from a height of 80 m (take g = 10 m/s²). Find the time of fall and the impact speed, declaring your positive direction. — Positive direction: downward (the motion's direction). Data: u = 0, a = g = 10, s = 80. Time (omitting v): s = ut + ½at² ⟹ 80 = 5t² ⟹ t² = 16 ⟹ t = 4 s. Impact speed (omitting t): v² = u² + 2as = 0 + 2·10·80 = 1600 ⟹ v = 40 m/s. Consistency: v = u + at = 0 + 10·4 = 40 ✓ — all three equations agree. Deep point: declaring "downward positive" before computing is what lets g enter as +10 and every sign follow mechanically; the same problem with "upward positive" works identically with g = −10 — the physics never depended on the choice, only the signs did.
  • NEB-style (6m): A ball is thrown vertically upward at 20 m/s (g = 10 m/s²). Find the maximum height, the time to reach it, and the total flight time — and explain the symmetry. — Positive direction: upward. At the top v = 0: v² = u² + 2as ⟹ 0 = 400 − 20s ⟹ h = 20 m. Time up: v = u + at ⟹ 0 = 20 − 10t ⟹ t = 2 s. Total flight: by symmetry the fall from 20 m takes the same 2 s ⟹ 4 s total; algebraically s = ut + ½at² with s = 0 (return to start): 0 = 20t − 5t² ⟹ t = 4 (the non-zero root). Symmetry explained: with constant g and no air resistance, the upward and downward legs traverse the same height profile with accelerations equal in magnitude — time up = time down, and the impact speed equals the launch speed (energy conservation's kinematic face). Deep point: the s = 0 root of the displacement equation is where the "total flight" answer hides — reading a quadratic's roots as physical events (launch and return) is the sign-disciplined way to see symmetry instead of assuming it.

Key Points

  • Statics: equilibrium, sum F = 0. Dynamics: acceleration, sum F = ma - the second law covers both, statics is the a = 0 case. Three laws: inertia (and inertial frames), F = dp/dt = ma, mutual pairs on different bodies. Kinematics: v = u + at, s = ut + at^2/2, v^2 = u^2 + 2as - choose the equation omitting the unneeded quantity. Sign discipline first: declare the positive direction, then the algebra is mechanical.

Summary

Mechanics' two halves share one law: statics is ΣF = ma with a = 0, dynamics is the full equation. Newton's three laws supply the frame (inertia), the engine (F = ma), and the bookkeeping rule (pairs on different bodies — never cancelling within one body's ledger). The kinematic system v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as is complete: three knowns of (u, v, a, t, s) fix the rest, the choice of equation omits the irrelevant quantity, and the spare equation verifies. Declare the positive direction first; the signs then do the physics for you.
