Practice Questions

  • NEB-style (5m): Find the distance between P(1, 2, 3) and Q(4, 6, 15) and exhibit the two-step Pythagoras. — Differences: Δx = 3, Δy = 4, Δz = 12. Base diagonal: √(3² + 4²) = 5. Space diagonal: √(5² + 12²) = √169 = 13. Direct check: √(9 + 16 + 144) = √169 = 13 ✓. Deep point: the numbers were chosen as two Pythagorean triples stacked (3-4-5 then 5-12-13) — the box picture turns the formula into two visible right triangles, and the "direct check" is the same computation written in one line.
  • NEB-style (6m): A line has direction ratios (2, −3, 6). Find its direction cosines and verify the fundamental identity. — Magnitude: √(4 + 9 + 36) = √49 = 7. Cosines: l = ±2/7, m = ∓3/7, n = ±6/7 — the signs move together (both triples (2/7, −3/7, 6/7) and (−2/7, 3/7, −6/7) describe the same line). Identity: l² + m² + n² = 4/49 + 9/49 + 36/49 = 49/49 = 1 ✓. Deep point: normalisation is the whole computation — divide by the magnitude and the identity is automatic (it is |û| = 1 restated); the ± pair is the line's two directions, and choosing one converts the line into a directed ray.
  • NEB-style (6m): Find the angle between the lines with direction ratios (1, 1, 2) and (2, −2, 1). — Normalise: |u₁| = √6, |u₂| = √9 = 3. Dot product: (1)(2) + (1)(−2) + (2)(1) = 2 − 2 + 2 = 2. Cosine: cos θ = |2/(√6·3)| = 2/(3√6) = √6/9. Angle: θ = cos⁻¹(√6/9) ≈ 74.2°. Perpendicularity note: the dot product 2 ≠ 0, so the lines are not perpendicular — the raw dot product (before normalising) already decides that question. Deep point: the cosine formula is the 3D dot product wearing trigonometric clothes; for perpendicularity skip the normalisation (zero is zero at any scale), but for the actual angle the magnitudes must enter.
  • NEB-style (6m): Find the coordinates of the point that divides the segment from A(1, −2, 3) to B(5, 6, −1) internally in the ratio 3 : 5, and its distance from A. — Section formula: P = ((3·5 + 5·1)/8, (3·6 + 5·(−2))/8, (3·(−1) + 5·3)/8) = (20/8, 8/8, 12/8) = (5/2, 1, 3/2). Distance AP by ratio: |AB| = √(4² + 8² + (−4)²) = √96 = 4√6; the ratio 3 : 5 ⟹ AP = (3/8)·4√6 = 3√6/2. Direct check: Δ = (5/2 − 1, 1 + 2, 3/2 − 3) = (3/2, 3, −3/2) ⟹ AP = √(9/4 + 9 + 9/4) = √(27/2) = √(81/6) = 9/√6 = 3√6/2 ✓ — both routes agree. Deep point: the section formula is the 2D formula with a third passenger, and the ratio-to-distance conversion (AP = fraction × |AB|) is often cheaper than recomputing the distance — but the shortcut earns trust only when the direct check confirms it, which here it does exactly.

Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — Coordinates in Space, Distance and Direction Cosines

    This canonical carries the three-dimensional foundation: points in space, the distance formula, direction cosines and direction ratios. Everything in space geometry — lines, planes, angles — is built from these three tools, and each is a direct descendant of its two-dimensional ancestor.

  • 2. Theorem Mirror

    • Coordinates in space: a point P is an ordered triple (x, y, z) — perpendicular distances from the three coordinate planes; eight octants partition space.
    • Distance formula: d = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²] — Pythagoras applied twice (the diagonal of the box, then the space diagonal).
    • Direction cosines: l = cos α, m = cos β, n = cos γ for a directed line's angles with the axes; l² + m² + n² = 1 always.
    • Direction ratios: any triple (a, b, c) proportional to (l, m, n); l = ±a/√(a²+b²+c²), and so on — the sign tracks direction, the magnitude only the line.
    • Section formula / midpoint: ((mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), …) — the 2D formula with a third passenger.

  • 3. Deep Conceptual — Direction Cosines Are a Unit Vector Read Componentwise

    • The triple (l, m, n) is exactly the unit vector along the line: cosines of the axis angles are the components of û = (cos α, cos β, cos γ), and l² + m² + n² = 1 is the statement |û| = 1. The identity is not a formula to memorise but the Pythagorean theorem on the unit vector.
    • Ratios vs cosines: direction ratios (a, b, c) are any vector along the line — unnormalised; dividing by the magnitude produces the cosines. The ± ambiguity in l = ±a/√(…) is real: a line (undirected) has two cosine triples, opposite in sign; a directed line has one.
    • The angle between two lines: cos θ = |l₁l₂ + m₁m₂ + n₂n₁| — the dot product of unit vectors; perpendicularity is the vanishing of the dot product, parallelism the proportionality of the triples.

  • 4. Deep Conceptual — Distance in Space Is Pythagoras Iterated

    • The space diagonal formula is two applications of Pythagoras: first in the base plane (√[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]), then in the vertical plane containing that diagonal and the z-difference. Three squared differences, one square root — the pattern extends to any dimension, which is why the formula "looks the same" in n dimensions.
    • The box picture: every distance computation is the space diagonal of a rectangular box with edges Δx, Δy, Δz — drawing the box converts the formula into a visible diagonal and makes the two-step Pythagoras derivation instant.

  • 5. Worked Example Types

    • Type A — distance and midpoint: three-coordinate arithmetic, box picture as a check.
    • Type B — cosines from ratios: normalise (a, b, c), attach ±, verify l²+m²+n² = 1.
    • Type C — angles between lines: dot product of normalised triples.
    • Type D — section formula: internal/external division in space.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    "Direction cosines are unique": a line has two triples (±); only a directed ray has one. "Ratios must satisfy l²+m²+n² = 1": that identity belongs to cosines; ratios satisfy it only after normalisation.
