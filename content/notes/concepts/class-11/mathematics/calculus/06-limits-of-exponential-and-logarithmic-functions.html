Notes

  • Exponential function limits: limx→a​ex=ea for all real a. The exponential function is continuous everywhere.
  • Important exponential limit: limx→0​xex−1​=1. This is the exponential analogue of limx→0​(sinx)/x=1.
  • Generalised form: limx→0​bxeax−1​=ba​.
  • Logarithmic function limits: limx→a​lnx=lna for all a>0. The natural logarithm is continuous on (0,∞).
  • Important logarithmic limit: limx→0​xln(1+x)​=1. Equivalently, limx→0​xln(1+x)​=1.
  • Generalised form: limx→0​bxln(1+ax)​=ba​.
  • Limits at infinity for exponential: limx→∞​ex=∞ and limx→−∞​ex=0.
  • Limits at infinity for logarithmic: limx→∞​lnx=∞ (slow growth) and limx→0+​lnx=−∞.
  • The number e defined as a limit: e=limn→∞​(1+1/n)n≈2.71828.
  • Fundamental trigonometric limit: limx→0​xsinx​=1, where x is measured in radians. This is the most important limit in introductory calculus.
  • Corollary: limx→0​xtanx​=1 and limx→0​x1−cosx​=0.
  • Generalised form: limx→0​bxsin(ax)​=ba​ and limx→0​sin(bx)sin(ax)​=ba​.
  • Limits at π/2: limx→π/2​π/2−xcosx​=1 and limx→π/2​cosxsinx​= straightforward — use substitutions.
  • Working strategy: When you see sin(something) in a limit, try to make the "something" match the other factor by multiplying and dividing.
  • Example: limx→0​xsin3x​=limx→0​3xsin3x​⋅3=1⋅3=3.
  • Example: limx→0​x3sinx−x​ requires Taylor expansion or repeated applications of L'Hopital's rule (advanced).
  • Reciprocal forms: limx→0​sinxx​=1 follows immediately from the fundamental limit.
  • Sum rule: limx→a​[f(x)+g(x)]=limx→a​f(x)+limx→a​g(x), provided both limits exist.
  • Difference rule: limx→a​[f(x)−g(x)]=limx→a​f(x)−limx→a​g(x).
  • Product rule: limx→a​[f(x)⋅g(x)]=limx→a​f(x)⋅limx→a​g(x).
  • Quotient rule: limx→a​g(x)f(x)​=limx→a​g(x)limx→a​f(x)​, provided the denominator limit is non-zero.
  • Constant multiple rule: limx→a​[c⋅f(x)]=c⋅limx→a​f(x) for any constant c.
  • Power rule: limx→a​[f(x)]n=[limx→a​f(x)]n for any positive integer n.
  • Root rule: limx→a​nf(x)​=nlimx→a​f(x)​, provided the limit is positive when n is even.
  • Sandwich (Squeeze) theorem: If g(x)≤f(x)≤h(x) near a and limx→a​g(x)=limx→a​h(x)=L, then limx→a​f(x)=L.
  • What is an indeterminate form? When direct substitution into a limit gives an expression like 0/0, ∞/∞, 0⋅∞, ∞−∞, 1∞, 00, or ∞0, the result is indeterminate — the limit may exist but cannot be determined from this form alone.
  • 0/0 form: The most common indeterminate form. Factor or simplify the expression to cancel the zero-producing factor, then substitute.
  • ∞/∞ form: Common in limits at infinity. Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of x, or use L'Hopital's rule (studied later).
  • ∞−∞ form: Rationalize, combine fractions, or use a common denominator to convert into a 0/0 or ∞/∞ form.
  • 0⋅∞ form: Rewrite as 0/∞1​=0/0 or ∞/01​=∞/∞, then apply standard techniques.
  • 1∞, 00, ∞0 forms: Take the natural logarithm of the expression, evaluate the limit of the logarithm, then exponentiate back.
  • Important note: Not every indeterminate form evaluates to zero or infinity. For example, limx→0​(sinx)/x=1 despite being a 0/0 form.
  • Polynomial limit theorem: For any polynomial p(x), limx→a​p(x)=p(a). Simply substitute x=a.
  • Rational function theorem: For a rational function r(x)=p(x)/q(x), if q(a)=0, then limx→a​r(x)=p(a)/q(a). Direct substitution works.
  • When direct substitution fails (0/0 form): Factor the numerator and denominator, cancel common factors, then substitute.
  • Limits at infinity for polynomials: limx→∞​an​xn+⋯={∞​if an​>0 −∞​if an​<0​ (for n≥1).
  • Limits at infinity for rational functions: Divide every term by the highest power of x in the denominator. If degree of numerator < degree of denominator, limit = 0. If equal, limit = ratio of leading coefficients.
  • Example: limx→∞​2x2−73x2+5x−1​=23​ (same degree, ratio of leading coefficients).
  • Nested radicals: For limx→a​(f(x)​−L) type limits, rationalize by multiplying by the conjugate f(x)​+L.
  • Definition of limit: limx→a​f(x)=L means that as x approaches a (but x=a), the value of f(x) approaches L.
  • One-sided limits: The left-hand limit limx→a−​f(x) considers values of x less than a; the right-hand limit limx→a+​f(x) considers values greater than a. For the limit to exist, both one-sided limits must be equal.
  • Existence of a limit: limx→a​f(x)=L exists if and only if limx→a−​f(x)=limx→a+​f(x)=L.
  • Geometric interpretation: On the graph of y=f(x), the limit limx→a​f(x)=L means the y-value approaches L as the curve is traced toward x=a from both sides. The function need not be defined at x=a itself.
  • Physical interpretation: If f(t) represents position at time t, then limt→a​f(t) gives the position approached at time a, regardless of whether the object is actually at that position.

Confusion

  • lnx is only defined for x>0. Never attempt ln(0) or ln(−1).
  • The limit limx→0​(ex−1)/x=1 requires the exponent to be exactly x. For e2x, adjust: limx→0​(e2x−1)/x=2.
  • ex grows much faster than any polynomial xn as x→∞, and lnx grows much slower than any positive power xn.
  • The limit limx→0​(sinx)/x=1 only works when x is in RADIANS. In degrees, the limit would be π/180.
  • Do not confuse limx→0​(sinx)/x=1 with limx→∞​(sinx)/x=0 — these are completely different situations.
  • limx→0​xsinx​=1 does NOT mean sinx=x. It means they are approximately equal for very small x.
  • The quotient rule requires the denominator limit to be non-zero. If both numerator and denominator approach zero, you have a 0/0 indeterminate form and must simplify first.
  • These properties hold only when the individual limits on the right side exist (except for the quotient rule, which also requires a non-zero denominator limit).
  • 0/0 is indeterminate, but k/0 (where k=0) is NOT indeterminate — the limit is ±∞ (does not exist as a finite number).
  • ∞/∞ is indeterminate, but k/∞=0 for any finite constant k — this IS determinate.
  • For rational functions where both numerator and denominator approach 0 at x=a, do NOT simply say the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients — you must factor and simplify first.
  • At infinity, only the highest-degree terms matter. Lower-degree terms become negligible.
  • The limit limx→a​f(x) does NOT require f(a) to exist. The limit is about the behaviour of f(x) near a, not at a.
  • Do not substitute x=a directly as a first step — many limits yield 0/0 which requires algebraic simplification before substitution.

Universal Facts

  • The two key limits limx→0​(ex−1)/x=1 and limx→0​ln(1+x)/x=1 are the foundations for differentiating exponential and logarithmic functions.
  • The exponential function's unique property is that it is its own derivative: dxd​ex=ex, which follows directly from the limit limh→0​(eh−1)/h=1.
  • The small-angle approximation sinx≈x (for small x) comes directly from this limit and is widely used in physics.
  • All other trigonometric limits at 0 can be derived from the fundamental limit limx→0​(sinx)/x=1.
  • These algebraic properties allow you to break complex limits into simpler ones, which is the primary strategy for limit evaluation.
  • The properties extend to finite sums and products of any number of functions.
  • The seven indeterminate forms are: 0/0, ∞/∞, 0⋅∞, ∞−∞, 1∞, 00, ∞0.
  • Recognizing an indeterminate form is the critical first step — it tells you that more work is needed, not that the limit does not exist.
  • For polynomials and rational functions, the first strategy is always direct substitution. Only when it fails do you need algebraic manipulation.
  • The degree comparison rule for limits at infinity is a quick diagnostic tool that avoids unnecessary computation.
  • Limits are the foundation of all calculus — derivatives and integrals are both defined via limits.
  • The concept of a limit extends naturally to sequences: limn→∞​an​=L means the sequence values approach L.
