Notes

  • 1. Topic Introduction — The Differentiation Toolkit

    Rules of differentiation — product, quotient, chain — plus parametric, implicit and higher-order derivatives: the machinery that makes every function differentiable in practice.

  • 2. Fundamental Concepts

    2.1 Product and quotient rules

    • Product: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′ (proved by adding and subtracting f(x+h)g(x)). Quotient: (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′)/g² (g ≠ 0).
    • Memory: product keeps order symmetry; quotient is "low d-high minus high d-low over low squared".

    2.2 The chain rule

    • Theorem: if g is differentiable at x and f differentiable at g(x), then (f∘g)′(x) = f′(g(x))·g′(x) — dy/dx = dy/du · du/dx.
    • The engine of composite differentiation: outer table, inner derivative, multiply.

    2.3 Parametric differentiation

    • x = x(t), y = y(t): dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt) (dx/dt ≠ 0); second derivative: d²y/dx² = d/dx(dy/dx) = [d/dt(dy/dx)]/(dx/dt) — divide by dx/dt again, not by itself.

    2.4 Implicit differentiation

    • For F(x, y) = 0 defining y(x): differentiate both sides in x, treating y as y(x) (chain rule inserts dy/dx), then solve for dy/dx.
    • Logarithmic differentiation (the power-tool): y = u^v ⟹ ln y = v ln u ⟹ y′/y = v′ ln u + v·u′/u ⟹ y′ = y(v′ ln u + vu′/u) — handles x^x and (sin x)^{cos x}.

    2.5 Higher-order derivatives

    • f′′, f′′′, …, f⁽ⁿ⁾: differentiate repeatedly. Notation: d²y/dx², Dⁿy. Leibniz (statement): (fg)⁽ⁿ⁾ = Σ C(n,k) f⁽ⁿ⁻ᵏ⁾g⁽ᵏ⁾ — the product rule's binomial generalisation.

  • 4. Detailed Explanation

    4.1 Why the chain rule is the master rule

    • Product and quotient rules are special cases of treating expressions as composites; every "hard" derivative is a chain rule in disguise. Master dy/du · du/dx and the table becomes universal.

    4.2 Logarithmic differentiation's domain

    • Use when y = u(x)^{v(x)} with both base and exponent variable (xˣ, (sin x)^{cos x}, x^{1/x}) — neither the power rule (variable base) nor the exponential rule (variable exponent) applies alone.

    4.3 The parametric second-derivative trap

    • d²y/dx² ≠ (d²y/dt²)/(d²x/dt²). The correct route: differentiate dy/dx with respect to t, then divide by dx/dt — the operator d/dx = (1/(dx/dt))·d/dt applied twice.

  • 6. Common Confusions

    Chain rule amnesia: ln(sin x) is cot x only because the inner derivative cos x survives — dropping u′ is the classic error.

  • Parametric second derivative: divide by dx/dt again after differentiating dy/dx in t — not the ratio of second t-derivatives.
  • x^x is not x·x^{x−1}: the power rule needs constant exponent; xˣ demands logarithmic differentiation.

  • 7. Exam Application

    NEB 2023 (verified PYQ): Find the derivative of y = sin x · cos x using the product rule. (3m) — Product rule: y′ = (sin x)′·cos x + sin x·(cos x)′ = cos x·cos x + sin x·(−sin x) = cos²x − sin²x = cos 2x. (Cross-check: y = ½ sin 2x ⟹ y′ = ½·2cos 2x = cos 2x ✓.)

  • NEB 2022 (verified PYQ): Differentiate (i) y = xˣ, (ii) y = sin²x·eˣ, (iii) y = (1+x)^{1/x}. (3m) — (i) Logarithmic: ln y = x ln x ⟹ y′/y = ln x + 1 ⟹ y′ = xˣ(ln x + 1). (ii) Product + chain: y′ = (2 sin x cos x)·eˣ + sin²x·eˣ = eˣ(sin 2x + sin²x). (iii) Logarithmic: ln y = (1/x)ln(1+x) ⟹ y′/y = [x/(1+x) − ln(1+x)]/x² ⟹ y′ = (1+x)^{1/x}·[x/(1+x) − ln(1+x)]/x².
  • NEB 2023 (verified PYQ): If y = (sin x)^{cos x}, show that dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)). (3m) — Logarithmic differentiation: ln y = cos x·ln(sin x). Differentiate: y′/y = (−sin x)·ln(sin x) + cos x·(cos x/sin x) = −sin x·ln(sin x) + cos x·cot x. Multiply by y: dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)) ∎ — exactly the claimed form.

  • 8. Deep Conceptual — The Parametric Second-Derivative Trap, Resolved

    • Wrong: d²y/dx² = (d²y/dt²)/(d²x/dt²). Right: d²y/dx² = [d/dt(dy/dx)]/(dx/dt) — because d/dx is the operator (1/(dx/dt))·d/dt, so differentiating dy/dx with respect to x means differentiating it with respect to t and dividing by dx/dt again.
    • Why the trap exists: the first derivative genuinely is a ratio of first derivatives — the pattern seduces you into extending it. But d²y/dx² is d(dy/dx)/dx, and dy/dx is itself a function of t: the chain rule demands one more division by dx/dt.
    • Cycloid check: x = a(θ − sin θ), y = a(1 − cos θ) gives dy/dx = cot(θ/2) and d²y/dx² = −1/(4a sin⁴(θ/2)) — the wrong formula would give (−a cos θ)/(a sin θ) = −cot θ, a completely different (and wrong) curve.

  • 9. Deep Conceptual — Logarithmic Differentiation as Linearisation

    • ln converts powers to products: ln(u^v) = v ln u — the log is an isomorphism from multiplication to addition, and it linearises the hardest differentiation problems. After one ln, every u^v question is a product rule question plus one multiplication by y.
    • The universal shape: y′ = y·(d/dx ln y) — differentiate the log, multiply by the original. The answer's architecture is fixed before any computation; only the bracket varies.
    • Bonus uses: differentiating long products f₁f₂⋯fₙ (ln splits it into a sum of logs — each term's derivative is simple); checking answers numerically (log-differentiate both sides of a candidate identity).

  • 10. Deep Conceptual — Higher-Order Derivatives as Differential Equations

    • Second derivatives measure acceleration (position → velocity → acceleration), curvature, and concavity — each application extracts one more layer of the function's structure.
    • The pattern behind y₂ + m²y = 0: y = A cos mx + B sin mx satisfies y′′ = −m²y — sinusoids are the eigenfunctions of d²/dx². Every "show that y satisfies …" question is verifying that a function solves a differential equation: the derivative table's deepest application.
    • Leibniz's rule (fg)⁽ⁿ⁾ = Σ C(n,k) f⁽ⁿ⁻ᵏ⁾g⁽ᵏ⁾ is the product rule with binomial coefficients — differentiation of products obeys the same algebra as powers of sums, which is why (D) behaves like a number in operator algebra.

Practice Questions

  • NEB 2023 (verified PYQ): Find the derivative of y = sin x · cos x using the product rule. (3m) — Product rule: y′ = (sin x)′·cos x + sin x·(cos x)′ = cos x·cos x + sin x·(−sin x) = cos²x − sin²x. Simplify: by the double-angle identity, cos²x − sin²x = cos 2x. Cross-check: sin x·cos x = ½·sin 2x, so differentiating directly gives ½·2cos 2x = cos 2x ✓ — the two routes agree, and the product rule has delivered a double angle.
  • NEB 2022 (verified PYQ): Differentiate with respect to x: (i) y = xˣ, (ii) y = sin²x · eˣ, (iii) y = (1+x)^{1/x}. (3m) — (i) Logarithmic differentiation (both base and exponent variable — neither single rule applies): ln y = x ln x ⟹ y′/y = 1·ln x + x·(1/x) = ln x + 1 ⟹ y′ = xˣ(ln x + 1). (ii) Product + chain: y′ = [d/dx(sin²x)]·eˣ + sin²x·(eˣ)′ = (2 sin x cos x)·eˣ + sin²x·eˣ = eˣ(sin 2x + sin²x) (using 2 sin x cos x = sin 2x). (iii) Logarithmic: ln y = (1/x)·ln(1+x); differentiate the product: y′/y = (−1/x²)·ln(1+x) + (1/x)·(1/(1+x)) ⟹ y′ = (1+x)^{1/x}·[x/(1+x) − ln(1+x)]/x². Three different rule-combinations, one page — the toolkit triage in a single question.
  • NEB 2023 (verified PYQ): If y = (sin x)^{cos x}, show that dy/dx = y(cos x · cot x − sin x · log_e(sin x)). (3m) — Step 1 — take logs (u^v with both parts variable): ln y = cos x · ln(sin x). Step 2 — differentiate both sides in x (product rule on the right; chain rule inserts y′/y on the left): y′/y = (−sin x)·ln(sin x) + cos x·[cos x/sin x] = −sin x·ln(sin x) + cos x·cot x. Step 3 — solve for y′: multiply by y: dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)) ∎ — matching the required form exactly, with y retained (the target keeps y unexpanded; substituting (sin x)^{cos x} back is optional but the shown form is the mark).
  • NEB-style (6m): For the cycloid x = a(θ − sin θ), y = a(1 − cos θ), find dy/dx and d²y/dx². — First derivative (parametric rule): dx/dθ = a(1 − cos θ); dy/dθ = a sin θ ⟹ dy/dx = a sin θ/[a(1 − cos θ)] = sin θ/(1 − cos θ). Simplify with half-angles: sin θ = 2 sin(θ/2)cos(θ/2) and 1 − cos θ = 2sin²(θ/2) ⟹ dy/dx = cot(θ/2). Second derivative (the correct route): d²y/dx² = [d/dθ(dy/dx)]/(dx/dθ). Differentiate cot(θ/2) in θ: d/dθ[cot(θ/2)] = −cosec²(θ/2)·(1/2). Divide by dx/dθ = a(1 − cos θ) = 2a sin²(θ/2): d²y/dx² = [−½cosec²(θ/2)]/[2a sin²(θ/2)] = −1/(4a sin⁴(θ/2)). Deep points: (1) the trap avoided — (d²y/dθ²)/(d²x/dθ²) = (−a cos θ)/(a sin θ) = −cot θ is wrong; the correct answer divides by dx/dθ twice via the operator route; (2) the second derivative is negative for 0 < θ < 2π ⟹ the cycloid's arch is concave down throughout — the brachistochrone curve's descent is always flattening.
  • NEB-style (5m): If x² + xy + y² = 7, find dy/dx by implicit differentiation and the equation of the tangent at (1, 2). — Differentiate through in x (y is y(x); the product term needs the product rule): 2x + [y + x·y′] + 2y·y′ = 0. Collect y′ terms: (x + 2y)y′ = −(2x + y) ⟹ dy/dx = −(2x + y)/(x + 2y). At (1, 2): dy/dx = −(2 + 2)/(1 + 4) = −4/5. Tangent line: y − 2 = (−4/5)(x − 1) ⟹ 4x + 5y = 14. Check the point lies on the curve: 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 ✓. Deep points: (1) implicit differentiation is the chain rule applied to every y — each y-term contributes a y′ factor, turning the equation into a linear equation in y′ that is solved algebraically; (2) the derivative formula −(2x+y)/(x+2y) works at every curve point at once — one differentiation prices the whole family of tangents, which is why implicit methods beat solving for y explicitly.
  • NEB-style (5m): If y = A cos(mx) + B sin(mx), show that d²y/dx² + m²y = 0. — First derivative (linearity + chain): y′ = A(−m sin mx) + B(m cos mx) = −Am sin mx + Bm cos mx. Second derivative: y′′ = −Am(m cos mx) + Bm(−m sin mx) = −m²(A cos mx + B sin mx) = −m²y. Rearrange: y′′ + m²y = 0 ∎. Deep conceptual reading: sinusoidal functions are the natural eigenfunctions of the operator d²/dx² — differentiating twice returns the function multiplied by −m². This is the differential equation of simple harmonic motion (spring–mass, pendulum small-angle): position y, acceleration y′′, and the equation says acceleration is proportional to displacement with a restoring sign. Every "show that y satisfies y′′ + m²y = 0" question is a two-line verification, but its physics is the entire theory of oscillation — the derivative table's most consequential corollary.
  • NEB-style (6m): If y = (sin x)^{cos x}, show that dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)), and find dy/dx at x = π/4. — Logarithmic differentiation: ln y = cos x·ln(sin x). Differentiate (product rule on the right; y′/y on the left): y′/y = (−sin x)·ln(sin x) + cos x·(cos x/sin x) = −sin x·ln(sin x) + cos x·cot x. Multiply by y: dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)) ∎ — the required form. At x = π/4: sin x = cos x = 1/√2; cot x = 1; y = (1/√2)^{1/√2}. Compute the bracket: cos x·cot x = (1/√2)(1) = 1/√2; sin x·ln(sin x) = (1/√2)·ln(1/√2) = (1/√2)(−½ ln 2) = −(ln 2)/(2√2). Bracket = 1/√2 + (ln 2)/(2√2) = (2 + ln 2)/(2√2). Answer: dy/dx = (1/√2)^{1/√2}·(2 + ln 2)/(2√2). Deep points: (1) keeping y unexpanded matches the target form — substituting (sin x)^{cos x} back is optional; (2) evaluating at a point tests whether the symbolic answer is computable — the bracket is a clean closed form, confirming the differentiation.

Summary

The differentiation toolkit turns the table into universal machinery. Product rule (fg)′ = f′g + fg′ (sin x·cos x → cos²x − sin²x = cos 2x, NEB 2023) and quotient rule (f′g − fg′)/g² are the workhorses; the chain rule dy/dx = dy/du·du/dx is the master — outer table, inner derivative, multiply. Parametric curves differentiate as (dy/dt)/(dx/dt), with the second derivative requiring a second division by dx/dt (the classic trap). Implicit equations differentiate through with y = y(x), then solve linearly for y′. And logarithmic differentiation is the power-tool for u(x)^{v(x)} — ln y = v ln u, then y′ = y(v′ ln u + vu′/u): xˣ → xˣ(ln x + 1), (1+x)^{1/x}, and NEB 2023's show-that (sin x)^{cos x} → y(cos x·cot x − sin x·ln(sin x)). Higher orders repeat the operation, with Leibniz generalising the product rule binomially. Triage the situation, pick the rule, execute.

  • Product rule: (fg)' = f'g + fg'. Quotient rule: (f/g)' = (f'g - fg')/g^2. Chain rule: (f o g)' = f'(g(x)) g'(x), i.e. dy/dx = dy/du du/dx - the master rule. Parametric: x = x(t), y = y(t) => dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt); d^2y/dx^2 = [d/dt(dy/dx)]/(dx/dt) - divide by dx/dt again. Implicit: differentiate F(x, y) = 0 in x with y = y(x), solve for dy/dx. Logarithmic differentiation for u(x)^v(x): ln y = v ln u => y' = y(v' ln u + vu'/u) - handles x^x, (sin x)^cos x, (1+x)^(1/x). Higher orders: f'', f'''...; Leibniz: (fg)^(n) = sum C(n,k) f^(n-k) g^(k).
Important Concepts
  • The chain rule is the master rule: outer table derivative times inner derivative - product, quotient and every composite reduce to it.
Examples
  • The x^x litmus test - neither power nor exponential rule applies when both base and exponent vary: ln y = x ln x is the universal escape hatch.
Universal Facts
  • Gear trains multiply rates like the chain rule: output speed = gear ratio (outer) times input speed (inner) - differentiation's mechanics is mechanics.

Practice Questions
  • NEB 2023 (verified PYQ): Find the derivative of y = sin x · cos x using the product rule. (3m) — Product rule: y′ = (sin x)′·cos x + sin x·(cos x)′ = cos x·cos x + sin x·(−sin x) = cos²x − sin²x. Simplify: by the double-angle identity, cos²x − sin²x = cos 2x. Cross-check: sin x·cos x = ½·sin 2x, so differentiating directly gives ½·2cos 2x = cos 2x ✓ — the two routes agree, and the product rule has delivered a double angle.
  • NEB 2022 (verified PYQ): Differentiate with respect to x: (i) y = xˣ, (ii) y = sin²x · eˣ, (iii) y = (1+x)^{1/x}. (3m) — (i) Logarithmic differentiation (both base and exponent variable — neither single rule applies): ln y = x ln x ⟹ y′/y = 1·ln x + x·(1/x) = ln x + 1 ⟹ y′ = xˣ(ln x + 1). (ii) Product + chain: y′ = [d/dx(sin²x)]·eˣ + sin²x·(eˣ)′ = (2 sin x cos x)·eˣ + sin²x·eˣ = eˣ(sin 2x + sin²x) (using 2 sin x cos x = sin 2x). (iii) Logarithmic: ln y = (1/x)·ln(1+x); differentiate the product: y′/y = (−1/x²)·ln(1+x) + (1/x)·(1/(1+x)) ⟹ y′ = (1+x)^{1/x}·[x/(1+x) − ln(1+x)]/x². Three different rule-combinations, one page — the toolkit triage in a single question.
  • NEB 2023 (verified PYQ): If y = (sin x)^{cos x}, show that dy/dx = y(cos x · cot x − sin x · log_e(sin x)). (3m) — Step 1 — take logs (u^v with both parts variable): ln y = cos x · ln(sin x). Step 2 — differentiate both sides in x (product rule on the right; chain rule inserts y′/y on the left): y′/y = (−sin x)·ln(sin x) + cos x·[cos x/sin x] = −sin x·ln(sin x) + cos x·cot x. Step 3 — solve for y′: multiply by y: dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)) ∎ — matching the required form exactly, with y retained (the target keeps y unexpanded; substituting (sin x)^{cos x} back is optional but the shown form is the mark).
  • NEB-style (6m): For the cycloid x = a(θ − sin θ), y = a(1 − cos θ), find dy/dx and d²y/dx². — First derivative (parametric rule): dx/dθ = a(1 − cos θ); dy/dθ = a sin θ ⟹ dy/dx = a sin θ/[a(1 − cos θ)] = sin θ/(1 − cos θ). Simplify with half-angles: sin θ = 2 sin(θ/2)cos(θ/2) and 1 − cos θ = 2sin²(θ/2) ⟹ dy/dx = cot(θ/2). Second derivative (the correct route): d²y/dx² = [d/dθ(dy/dx)]/(dx/dθ). Differentiate cot(θ/2) in θ: d/dθ[cot(θ/2)] = −cosec²(θ/2)·(1/2). Divide by dx/dθ = a(1 − cos θ) = 2a sin²(θ/2): d²y/dx² = [−½cosec²(θ/2)]/[2a sin²(θ/2)] = −1/(4a sin⁴(θ/2)). Deep points: (1) the trap avoided — (d²y/dθ²)/(d²x/dθ²) = (−a cos θ)/(a sin θ) = −cot θ is wrong; the correct answer divides by dx/dθ twice via the operator route; (2) the second derivative is negative for 0 < θ < 2π ⟹ the cycloid's arch is concave down throughout — the brachistochrone curve's descent is always flattening.
  • NEB-style (5m): If x² + xy + y² = 7, find dy/dx by implicit differentiation and the equation of the tangent at (1, 2). — Differentiate through in x (y is y(x); the product term needs the product rule): 2x + [y + x·y′] + 2y·y′ = 0. Collect y′ terms: (x + 2y)y′ = −(2x + y) ⟹ dy/dx = −(2x + y)/(x + 2y). At (1, 2): dy/dx = −(2 + 2)/(1 + 4) = −4/5. Tangent line: y − 2 = (−4/5)(x − 1) ⟹ 4x + 5y = 14. Check the point lies on the curve: 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 ✓. Deep points: (1) implicit differentiation is the chain rule applied to every y — each y-term contributes a y′ factor, turning the equation into a linear equation in y′ that is solved algebraically; (2) the derivative formula −(2x+y)/(x+2y) works at every curve point at once — one differentiation prices the whole family of tangents, which is why implicit methods beat solving for y explicitly.
  • NEB-style (5m): If y = A cos(mx) + B sin(mx), show that d²y/dx² + m²y = 0. — First derivative (linearity + chain): y′ = A(−m sin mx) + B(m cos mx) = −Am sin mx + Bm cos mx. Second derivative: y′′ = −Am(m cos mx) + Bm(−m sin mx) = −m²(A cos mx + B sin mx) = −m²y. Rearrange: y′′ + m²y = 0 ∎. Deep conceptual reading: sinusoidal functions are the natural eigenfunctions of the operator d²/dx² — differentiating twice returns the function multiplied by −m². This is the differential equation of simple harmonic motion (spring–mass, pendulum small-angle): position y, acceleration y′′, and the equation says acceleration is proportional to displacement with a restoring sign. Every "show that y satisfies y′′ + m²y = 0" question is a two-line verification, but its physics is the entire theory of oscillation — the derivative table's most consequential corollary.
  • NEB-style (6m): If y = (sin x)^{cos x}, show that dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)), and find dy/dx at x = π/4. — Logarithmic differentiation: ln y = cos x·ln(sin x). Differentiate (product rule on the right; y′/y on the left): y′/y = (−sin x)·ln(sin x) + cos x·(cos x/sin x) = −sin x·ln(sin x) + cos x·cot x. Multiply by y: dy/dx = y(cos x·cot x − sin x·log_e(sin x)) ∎ — the required form. At x = π/4: sin x = cos x = 1/√2; cot x = 1; y = (1/√2)^{1/√2}. Compute the bracket: cos x·cot x = (1/√2)(1) = 1/√2; sin x·ln(sin x) = (1/√2)·ln(1/√2) = (1/√2)(−½ ln 2) = −(ln 2)/(2√2). Bracket = 1/√2 + (ln 2)/(2√2) = (2 + ln 2)/(2√2). Answer: dy/dx = (1/√2)^{1/√2}·(2 + ln 2)/(2√2). Deep points: (1) keeping y unexpanded matches the target form — substituting (sin x)^{cos x} back is optional; (2) evaluating at a point tests whether the symbolic answer is computable — the bracket is a clean closed form, confirming the differentiation.
