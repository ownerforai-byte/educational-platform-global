Notes

  • Electric field intensity is the force on a small positive test charge divided by that charge. Its unit is the newton per coulomb. The test charge must be small enough not to disturb the sources.
  • A point charge Q produces E = k Q / r^2, outward if Q is positive. Two point charges need a vector sum. On the perpendicular bisector of an equal and opposite pair, the fields point the same way along the bisector.
  • A long straight line of charge uses Gauss's law. The field is radial and E = λ / (2 π ε0 r), so it falls as 1/r rather than 1/r^2. λ is the charge per unit length.
  • An infinite non-conducting sheet with surface charge density σ produces a field σ / (2 ε0) on each side, pointing away from the sheet if σ is positive. The field does not fall with distance in this ideal infinite case.
  • A conducting sheet with the same charge density on one outer surface has E = σ / ε0 just outside, because the conductor forces the field lines to leave from one side in the standard result for a thin charged conductor.
  • Field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge. They do not cross. Closer lines mean a stronger field. The force on any charge q placed in the field is q E.

Universal Facts

  • Coulomb: F = (1/4πε₀)·q₁q₂/r². Electric field E = F/q = kq/r²; for a line/dipole use superposition.
  • Gauss's law: ∮E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀ — powerful for spherical/cylindrical/planar symmetry.
  • Potential V = kq/r; ΔV = W/q; U of a charge in potential = qV. Equipotential surfaces are ⟂ to field lines.

Summary

Field intensity is force per unit positive charge. A point charge gives kQ/r^2, a long line gives a field that falls as 1/r, and an infinite insulating sheet gives the constant field σ/(2ε0). Just outside a conductor the field is σ/ε0.
