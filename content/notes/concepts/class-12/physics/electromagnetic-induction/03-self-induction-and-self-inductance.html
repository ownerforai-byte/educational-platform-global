Notes

  • Self-induction is the emf induced in a coil by a change of its own current. The changing current changes the coil's own flux, and Faraday's law gives an emf.
  • The self-inductance L is defined by NΦ = L I, so L is the flux linkage per unit current. The induced emf is −L dI/dt. The minus sign is Lenz's law: the emf opposes the change in current.
  • When the current is increasing, the induced emf opposes the battery. When the current is decreasing, the induced emf tries to keep the current going. A spark at a switch is that attempt.
  • L increases with more turns, a larger area, and an iron core. A long solenoid has L = μ0 n^2 A l, with n turns per unit length, if the field is the ideal solenoid field.
  • The henry is the inductance that induces 1 V when the current changes at 1 A/s. Energy stored is (1/2) L I^2.
  • Two coils on one core have self-inductance each and mutual inductance as well. This page is only the self part.

Key Points

  • A coil's own changing current induces an emf.
  • L is the flux linkage per unit current.
  • The emf opposes the change.
  • L depends on turns, area and the core.

Summary

Self-inductance L links a coil's flux to its own current, and the induced emf is −L dI/dt. It is zero for a steady current and it opposes any change. The stored energy is (1/2) L I^2.

Exercises

1.

A coil of inductance 0.50 H carries a current that falls at 4.0 A/s. Find the magnitude of the induced emf.

Steps
  • Magnitude of emf = L |dI/dt|.
  • emf = 0.50 × 4.0 = 2.0 V.
  • The current is falling, so the induced emf tries to maintain the current.
  • A steady current would have given zero.
  • The energy in the field is decreasing and is the source of this emf.
Answer

2.0 V, opposing the fall of current.
