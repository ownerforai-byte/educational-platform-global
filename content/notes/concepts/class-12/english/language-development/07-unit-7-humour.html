Notes

  • The hardest thing to do in a second language

    Humour is the speed test of fluency: a pun lands only if you hear the seam in the word, irony only if you catch the daylight between said and meant. That is why a language course needs this unit — and why the exam keeps setting it. What follows names the species of the funny, gives you the analyst's method (why is this funny — the actual question), and then the writer's craft, because the best way to understand a joke is to try to build one that works.

  • The species of the funny — named and tamed

    Start with the engine: most humour is incongruity — two frames colliding in one sentence, one image, one banana peel — resolved safely by a punchline, with the relief doing the rest. The priest slips on ice: dignity and gravity (both senses) disagree. Once you see the two frames, you can see them in every species:

    • Slapstick — the body as punchline; needs no translation, which is why it crosses borders wordplay cannot.
    • Pun — one sound, two meanings: "Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana."
    • Irony — meaning the opposite of what you say; the audience must hold the truth to get the joke.
    • Sarcasm — irony with a bite and a target: funny to allies, a slap to the victim.
    • Satire — irony organised against institutions, aiming to reform: the cartoon that mocks corruption is satire; mocking a classmate is just meanness with better grammar.
    • Parody — a style imitated until it laughs at itself: a school assembly delivered in solemn news-anchor voice.
    • Deadpan — the flatter the face, the bigger the laugh; the gap does all the work.
    • Self-deprecation — the joke aimed at yourself: the safest humour in any room, and the most likeable.

  • Reading humour — the analyst's three questions

    For any set passage, ask in order: What is the target? (a person, a vice, or — most honourable — the narrator himself). What is the device? (exaggeration, understatement, pun, irony). Where is the turn? — the exact words where the second frame arrives. An answer that names device, quotes the turn and identifies the target is analysis; "it is very funny" is a fan letter, and examiners do not award marks for fandom.

    The devices worth carrying into every answer: exaggeration ("I was so nervous I could have buttered the ceiling"), understatement (after the storm: "the weather has turned slightly breezy"), the rule of three ("he packed his books, his pens, and his fear of mathematics"), anticlimax ("he fought bravely for justice, honour and the last piece of cake"), and the clash of registers — formal words in undignified scenes, which is why "the mortuary-cold water of the bucket bath" is funnier than "the freezing water".

    And hear the tone: is the writer laughing with the fools or at them? The same stumble can be written fondly or cruelly, and the tell is in the small words — poor man, our heroic fool, bless him. NEB's tone questions are this distinction wearing an examiner's suit.

  • Writing humour — craft, not chance

    The comic sentence is architecture: setup in plain language, turn in as few words as possible, and the surprise word at the very end — anything after a punchline is a stumble. "I finally got eight hours of sleep. Four at a time." Twelve words, two frames, one laugh.

    Exaggerate the true, not the invented: the invigilator patrolling like a border guard, the mother who detects an untidy room through a closed door, the queue for water at break. Shared recognition — we all do that — earns the laugh; invented absurdity earns nothing, because the reader has nothing to recognise.

    And keep a spine under the jokes: the story of the disastrous picnic is also about optimism, or family, or rain. Write the spine first, then dress it. An essay that is funny and about something is literature; an essay that is only funny is a party trick — and party tricks get sober marks.

  • How the exam asks it

    "What makes the passage humorous?" (4 marks) — two devices, each with a quoted phrase and one line on the turn; never retell the joke, analyse it. "Distinguish irony and sarcasm" (2 marks) — the target test in one sentence. The humorous narrative ("A funny incident I cannot forget", 6–8 marks) — setup-turn structure per paragraph, one true detail exaggerated one step past sense, the spine stated in the last line. The short answer ("Why is humour called a social lubricant?") — it eases tension, bonds strangers, and lets criticism be heard where direct words would be refused: the court jester's licence, still valid.

    Hunt first for the opposites — grand words for small things, praise that cannot be sincere, formal dress at a food fight. One quoted phrase per device is evidence enough; quoting whole paragraphs shows everything and proves nothing.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Name the humour device precisely: irony, pun, hyperbole, situational comedy — precision of device + the quoted instance is the full-mark pair.
  • Trick 2: 'Why is this funny?' answers must explain the INCONGRUITY (what we expected vs what happened) — expectation violation is the theory to cite.

Practice Questions

  • Q. What makes the situation in the text humorous? (2 marks) — the gap between expectation and outcome; cite the moment.
  • Q. Identify one example of irony in the text. (2 marks) — quote the line and explain the intended vs actual meaning.

Key Points

  • Identify humour types: situational, verbal, irony — name the type then explain.
  • Humour often carries social criticism; state what is being mocked.
