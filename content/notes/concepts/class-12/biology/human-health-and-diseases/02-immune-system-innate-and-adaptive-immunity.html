    Immune system — innate and adaptive immunity

    Fill order per concept: definition → structure → process → significance (§3.2). Step-by-step numbered points, Pioneer style.

  • The immune system is the body's defence network, arranged in layers. Innate immunity is inborn, non-specific and immediate — barriers, phagocytes, inflammation. Adaptive (acquired) immunity is specific to each antigen, learns, remembers, and is carried by lymphocytes (B and T cells). Cell-mediated immunity (T cells) kills infected cells; humoral immunity (B cells → antibodies) neutralises free pathogens.

  • Worked example. A splinter enters the skin. Barriers fail (step 1). Neutrophils and macrophages arrive within hours; the area reddens and swells — inflammation (step 2). Macrophages present antigen to helper T cells, which activate B cells; antibodies appear after ~5–7 days (step 3). Cytotoxic T cells clear any infected cells (step 4). Memory cells persist — a second splinter with the same bacteria is cleared in 1–2 days (step 5).
