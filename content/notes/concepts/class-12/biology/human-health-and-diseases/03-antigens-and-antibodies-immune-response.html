  • An antigen is any substance that the immune system recognises as foreign and reacts against (toxin, protein on a pathogen surface, pollen). An antibody (immunoglobulin) is the Y-shaped protein a plasma B cell secretes to bind that antigen specifically. Antibodies never kill directly — they tag: neutralise toxins, coat pathogens for phagocytes (opsonisation), clump them (agglutination), or trigger complement.

  • Worked example — reading an antibody titre. A patient's blood shows high IgM against a virus but almost no IgG: the infection is new (primary response). A second sample two weeks later shows IgM falling and IgG rising steeply: the patient is clearing the infection and has gained memory. If the first sample already had high IgG with no IgM, the infection was old or the patient was vaccinated — IgG tells history, IgM tells news.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Antigen = foreign molecule binding antibody/MHC; antibody = Y-shaped Ig (2 heavy + 2 light chains, disulphide bonds) with variable antigen-binding sites — draw the Y.
  • Trick 2: Ig classes: IgG (most abundant, crosses placenta), IgA (secretions), IgM (first response, pentamer), IgE (allergy), IgD — the five classes with one role each.
  • Trick 3: Primary vs secondary response: secondary is faster, stronger (memory cells) — the graph sketch earns marks.
  • Trick 4: Antibody actions: neutralisation, opsonisation, agglutination, complement lysis — four verbs, four marks.
