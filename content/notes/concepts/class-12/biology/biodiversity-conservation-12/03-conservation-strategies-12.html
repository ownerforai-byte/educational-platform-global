Relevance

Notes

  • A conservation strategy chooses where to act and which tool to use. The hotspot approach spends effort first on places that still hold many endemic species and have already lost most of their habitat, because that is where the next extinctions are concentrated.
  • The on-site tools are national parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas and buffer zones. Strict cores limit extraction. Buffer land allows managed use so that people who live beside the park are not pushed into the core.
  • The off-site tools are zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, field gene banks and cryopreservation. They keep options open for species and crop varieties that are already critically reduced.
  • Community forestry and conservation areas in Nepal put local users into the management. People who receive fodder, tourism income or a legal fuelwood quota have a reason to report poaching and to limit new clearing.
  • International agreements support the national tools. CITES regulates trade in listed species and their parts. The Convention on Biological Diversity asks countries to conserve diversity, use it sustainably and share benefits from genetic resources.
  • A strategy fails if it picks only one tool. Hotspot maps choose the place. In-situ protection holds the place. Ex-situ stores are the insurance. Laws and local institutions make the rules stick.

Confusion

  • A hotspot map is a priority tool, not a park boundary. Declaring a hotspot does not by itself hire rangers.
  • Community forestry is not the opposite of conservation. In Nepal it is one of the ways forest cover and local species are kept.
  • CITES does not ban all use of wild species. It controls international trade according to how threatened the species is.
  • Ex-situ storage is not the first choice for a species that still has a working habitat. It is the reserve plan.

Key Points

  • Prioritise hotspots: high endemism plus heavy habitat loss.
  • On-site tools: parks, reserves, conservation areas, buffers.
  • Off-site tools: zoos, gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation.
  • Community forestry gives local people a stake in protection.
  • CITES and the Convention on Biological Diversity are the trade and national-duty agreements.

Important Statements

  • Conservation strategy is a combination of priority setting, habitat protection, gene storage and rules that people will follow.
  • Benefit sharing makes protection durable. People who gain nothing from a park often undermine it.
  • International trade controls matter when the threat is a market for horn, skin, timber or pets.

Examples

  • Annapurna Conservation Area is managed with local institutions rather than as a park that excludes all use.
  • A seed bank of native rice is the off-site half of a strategy whose on-site half is keeping farmers growing those varieties.
  • A CITES listing is the relevant tool when rhinoceros horn is smuggled across a border. A ranger post is the relevant tool when the hunter is still in the park.

Universal Facts

  • Hotspot priority uses two numbers together: at least 1,500 endemic vascular plants, and loss of more than 70 percent of original habitat.
  • Nepal combines strict national parks with conservation areas and community forests, so the strategy is not one legal category.

Formulas

  • Strategy = priority (hotspot) + in-situ sites + ex-situ stores + local institutions + trade rules.
  • Hotspot tests: endemic vascular plants >= 1500 and habitat loss > 70 percent.

Practice

  • List four tools and say whether each is on-site or off-site.
  • Explain one way community forestry supports conservation.
  • Say what CITES regulates and what it does not replace.

Practice Questions

  • Why does a hotspot approach not protect every species equally?
  • How is a buffer zone different from the core of a park?
  • A medicinal plant is being exported unsustainably. Which mix of tools fits?

Exam Notes

  • Structure the answer as priority, on-site, off-site, community, international. Five short paragraphs beat one vague paragraph.
  • Name one convention, CITES or the Convention on Biological Diversity, and one Nepali institution or park.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Place, then protect the place, then store a backup, then share the benefit.
  • CITES is about trade across borders. A park is about land.

Special Notes

  • This note is the strategy view. The paired note on in-situ and ex-situ is the definition view. Use both names of the tools, and here add priority and law.
  • Benefit sharing is easy to forget and is often worth a sentence.

Important Notes

  • Quote the two hotspot numbers when the question mentions hotspots as a strategy.
  • Give community forestry as the Nepal-specific social tool.

Exercises

1.

A valley has many plants found nowhere else, has lost most of its forest, and a rare orchid is being smuggled abroad. Outline the strategy in order.

Steps
  • The place meets the hotspot idea: high endemism and heavy habitat loss, so it is a priority.
  • Protect what forest remains on site, with a core and a buffer, and involve the people who use the valley.
  • Keep orchid plants or seed off site so a bad year does not erase the species.
  • Use a trade rule such as CITES against the smuggling, because the threat crosses a border.
Answer

Prioritise the valley, protect remaining habitat with local users, store the orchid ex situ, and control the international trade.

Summary

Conservation strategy stacks tools. Hotspots decide where to spend effort. Parks, reserves and buffer zones protect habitat. Seed banks and zoos insure the genes. Community forestry and laws, including CITES and the Convention on Biological Diversity, make the protection last.
