  • Gregor Mendel (1822–1884), a monk in Brno, bred garden peas (Pisum sativum) from 1856–1863 and published in 1866. Peas were the right organism: true-breeding varieties, seven clearly contrasting traits, short life cycle, large numbers of offspring, and easy self- plus cross-pollination. He counted thousands of offspring and used mathematics — the first quantitative study of heredity. His work was ignored until 1900, when Correns, de Vries and Tschermak rediscovered it.

  • Worked example. A tall pea plant of unknown genotype is test-crossed with a dwarf plant (tt). The offspring are 58 tall and 54 dwarf. Because dwarf offspring (tt) can only receive a t from each parent, the unknown parent must have supplied t gametes — so it was Tt (heterozygous), not TT. A 1:1 test-cross result is the standard proof of heterozygosity. If all offspring had been tall, the parent would have been homozygous TT.

  • 8. Summary and self-check

    5–10 line chapter summary, self-check questions and the common-confusions list (block VIII) — completed in the unit fill pass.

  • Summary. Mendel's factors (genes) occur in pairs, separate cleanly during gamete formation (Law of Segregation — 3:1, 1:2:1), and pairs for different traits assort independently (Law of Independent Assortment — 9:3:3:1). Dominance hides recessive alleles in the phenotype but never destroys them. Self-check: can you reproduce the 2×2 and 4×4 grids from memory, and explain why the test cross is the single most useful cross in genetics?

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Monohybrid cross scaffold: TT × tt → all Tt (F1) → F2 3:1 (phenotype), 1:2:1 (genotype) — draw the Punnett square; the ratios ARE the marks.
  • Trick 2: Dihybrid: TtYy × TtYy → 9:3:3:1 — quote the law of independent assortment for the ratio; test cross with a double recessive gives 1:1:1:1.
  • Trick 3: Three laws in one line each: dominance (one allele masks), segregation (alleles separate in gametes), independent assortment (genes on different chromosomes assort freely).
  • Trick 4: Test cross identifies an unknown genotype: with a homozygous recessive, all-dominant offspring → parent homozygous; 1:1 → heterozygous.
