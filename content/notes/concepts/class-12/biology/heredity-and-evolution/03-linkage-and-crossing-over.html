  • Mendel's second law assumes genes assort independently — but genes live on chromosomes, and a chromosome carries hundreds of them. Genes on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together: this is linkage, and it was proved by T. H. Morgan's work on Drosophila (1910–1915). During prophase I of meiosis, homologous chromosomes swap segments at chiasmata — crossing over — which breaks linkage apart in proportion to the distance between the genes. Recombination frequency became the ruler of the genetic map.

  • Worked example. A test cross of a female Drosophila heterozygous for b (black body) and vg (vestigial wings) gives: b+vg+ 860, b vg 880, b+ vg 45, b vg+ 40. The two large classes are the parentals; the two small classes are recombinants. Recombination frequency = (45 + 40) / 1825 ≈ 4.7%, so b and vg are about 4.7 map units apart. The farther apart two genes are, the more often a chiasma falls between them — up to a maximum of 50 cM, where the genes behave as if unlinked.

  • Summary. Genes on the same chromosome are linked; crossing over in prophase I breaks the linkage in proportion to distance, and the recombination percentage is the map distance in centimorgans. Linkage is the great exception to independent assortment — and the tool that let Sturtevant draw the first chromosome map. Self-check: from a test-cross table, can you spot the parentals, count the recombinants and compute the cM distance?

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: Linkage: genes on the SAME chromosome tend to inherit together, violating independent assortment; coupling phase (AB/ab) vs repulsion (Ab/aB).
  • Trick 2: Crossing over in pachytene of prophase-I produces recombinants; recombination frequency ∝ distance between genes (1 map unit = 1% recombination).
  • Trick 3: Strongly linked genes (close together) show FEWER recombinants; a test cross ratio near 1:1:1:1 means unlinked genes — read the data this way.
  • Trick 4: Significance: crossing over creates variation for evolution; linkage preserves favourable gene combinations.
