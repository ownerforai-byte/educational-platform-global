Notes

  • In-situ conservation protects a species inside the ecosystem where it already lives. The population keeps its predators, food, climate and the chance to keep evolving. National parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas, buffer zones and sacred groves are in-situ tools.
  • Ex-situ conservation removes individuals, seeds or genes from that habitat and keeps them under human care. Zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks, pollen banks and cryopreservation are ex-situ tools. It is the backup when the wild population is already too small.
  • In-situ is preferred whenever the habitat can still be saved, because a captive population loses ecological relationships and can lose genetic variety. Ex-situ cannot replace a forest, but it can stop an immediate extinction and supply stock for a later release.
  • Nepal's in-situ system dates from Chitwan National Park (1973), the country's first national park, and Sagarmatha National Park (1976), later listed as a World Heritage Site. Conservation areas such as Annapurna add community management beside strict protection.
  • A protected area fails if it is an island. Animals that need a large range suffer inbreeding and edge effects when the reserve is small and cut off. Corridors, buffer zones and control of poaching are part of the strategy, not extras.
  • Sacred groves and community forests protect patches of habitat because local rules limit cutting and hunting. They are in-situ even when they are not named as national parks.

Confusion

  • Ex-situ does not mean 'better protection'. It means protection away from the natural habitat. A zoo is ex-situ. A national park is in-situ.
  • A seed bank saves plant genetic diversity. It does not save the insects, fungi and animals that lived with that plant.
  • Buffer zones are not the core protected area. They are the surrounding land where limited use is allowed so that pressure on the core is reduced.
  • Releasing zoo-bred animals succeeds only if the habitat they are returned to is still safe. Captive breeding without habitat protection does not restore the species.

Key Points

  • In-situ: protect the species in its own habitat (parks, reserves, sacred groves).
  • Ex-situ: protect it off-site (zoos, botanical gardens, seed and gene banks, cryopreservation).
  • In-situ keeps ecological interactions and ongoing evolution.
  • Ex-situ is the rescue option for critically endangered species and for crop varieties.
  • Chitwan (1973) and Sagarmatha (1976) are landmark Nepali in-situ sites.

Important Statements

  • In-situ conservation is the primary strategy because it protects the whole community, not one species in isolation.
  • Ex-situ conservation is complementary: it preserves genes and individuals when the wild habitat is failing.
  • A reserve that is too small or too isolated loses species even if hunting is banned.

Examples

  • Chitwan National Park protects the greater one-horned rhinoceros in its floodplain habitat. That is in-situ.
  • A seed bank holding local rice landraces is ex-situ conservation of crop diversity.
  • The Central Zoo holding a breeding pair of a rare mammal is ex-situ. Returning their offspring to a guarded habitat joins the two strategies.

Universal Facts

  • Nepal's protected-area system, including conservation areas and buffer zones, covers more than a fifth of the country's land.
  • Cryopreservation stores semen, eggs, embryos or tissues at very low temperature, commonly in liquid nitrogen, so the cells remain viable for years.

Formulas

  • In-situ sites: national parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas, buffer zones, sacred groves.
  • Ex-situ sites: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks, cryopreservation.

Exam Notes

  • A two-column answer, in-situ versus ex-situ, with two examples each, is the standard written question.
  • Use a Nepal example (Chitwan, Sagarmatha, Annapurna, or a sacred grove) rather than only a foreign park.

Exam Short Tricks

  • In-situ = in the site. Ex-situ = exit the site.
  • Parks protect the play. Zoos and seed banks protect the actors off-stage.

Special Notes

  • Sacred groves count as in-situ. Students often leave them off the list.
  • Gene banks store genetic material. They are ex-situ even when the species still has a wild population.

Important Notes

  • Always state the limitation: ex-situ does not preserve the ecosystem.
  • Mention corridors or buffer zones when the question asks how to make a park actually work.

Exercises

1.

A rhino population remains only in one small park, and poaching has removed the surrounding forest. Which strategy is primary, and what ex-situ step is still worth taking?

Steps
  • The animals still have a wild habitat, so the primary move is in-situ: protect and reconnect that habitat and stop poaching.
  • The park is small, so add a buffer and corridors if any neighbouring forest remains.
  • Because the population is already tiny, also keep a captive breeding or gamete store as insurance.
  • The captive stock is useful only if animals can later return to a safe habitat.
Answer

Primary strategy is in-situ protection of the park and its connections. Ex-situ breeding or a gamete store is the backup, not a substitute.

Summary

In-situ conservation guards species inside living ecosystems, through parks, reserves, buffer zones and sacred groves. Ex-situ conservation, in zoos, gardens and seed or gene banks, is the rescue layer when wild numbers collapse. Nepal's system, beginning with Chitwan in 1973, works only when reserves stay connected and local use around them is managed.
