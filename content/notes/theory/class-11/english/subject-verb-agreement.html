Subject-Verb Agreement — Tricky Cases

Class 11English

Notes

  • Subject-Verb Agreement is one of the most tested grammar topics in NEB English. The basic rule is: singular subjects take singular verbs, plural subjects take plural verbs. However, many nouns break this rule — they look plural but take singular verbs, or look singular but take plural verbs.

  • 🔹 Looks Plural, Takes a Singular Verb

  • These nouns end in -s but are actually singular and take is/was:

  • 📘 School Subjects / Science

    • Physics is my favorite subject.
    • Mathematics is not difficult if you practice.
    • Economics is a popular choice for commerce students.
    • Genetics is a branch of biology.
    • Robotics is an emerging field.

  • 🩺 Diseases

    • Measles is a contagious childhood disease.
    • Mumps is caused by a virus.
    • Diabetes is a chronic condition.
    • Rabies is fatal if not treated.

  • 🎲 Games / Sports

    • Darts is a popular pub game.
    • Billiards is played on a large table.
    • Dominoes is a game of tiles.
    • Gymnastics requires great flexibility.

  • 🌍 Countries / Places

    • The United States is a large country.
    • The Netherlands is known for tulips.
    • The Philippines is an archipelago.
    • Headquarters is located in the city center.

  • 💰 Measurement Units (Time / Money / Distance)

    • Ten dollars is too much for a coffee.
    • Five miles is a long distance to run.
    • Three hours is a long flight.

  • 📰 Other Words

    • News is broadcast at 6 PM.
    • Series is my favorite TV genre.
    • Species is a biological classification.
    • Crossroads is a busy intersection.

  • Rule: Words ending in -s that are names of academic subjects, diseases, games, countries, measurements, or abstract concepts often take singular verbs.

  • 🔹 Looks Singular, Takes a Plural Verb

  • These nouns have no -s but are actually plural and take are/were:

  • 👥 Groups of People

    • Police are investigating the crime.
    • People are waiting outside.
    • Clergy were present at the ceremony.
    • Gentry are known for their generosity.
    • Folk are traditional and colorful.

  • 🐄 Animals / Livestock

    • Cattle are grazing in the field.
    • Poultry are kept for eggs and meat.
    • Vermin are pests that destroy crops.

  • 💼 Work Groups (when focusing on members)

    • The staff are having a meeting.
    • The crew are working hard.

  • Rule: Collective nouns that refer to groups of individuals often take plural verbs when emphasizing the members.

  • 🔹 Compound Subjects Joined by 'And'

  • 🍽️ One Single Meal / Dish → Singular Verb

  • These foods are served and eaten together as one dish:
    • Bread and butter is my daily breakfast.
    • Rice and curry is a popular meal in Asia.
    • Fish and chips is a famous British dish.
    • Bacon and eggs is a heavy breakfast.
    • Macaroni and cheese is comfort food.
    • Strawberries and cream is served at tennis matches.

  • 🛒 Separate Food Items / Groceries → Plural Verb

  • Same-sounding pairs, but here they're two separate items:
    • Bread and butter are both on the shopping list.
    • Milk and cookies are in the kitchen.
    • Apples and oranges grow on trees.

  • 🤝 One Concept or Idiom → Singular Verb

    • Law and order is important for a safe city.
    • Peace and quiet is all I need.
    • Trial and error is a great way to learn.
    • Slow and steady wins the race.
    • All work and no play makes Jack a dull boy.

  • 👫 Distinct People or Roles → Plural Verb

    • My mom and dad are coming.
    • The teacher and the student were talking.
    • Fire and water do not mix.

  • 🏹 Two Objects Working as One Unit → Singular Verb

    • A horse and carriage was outside.
    • A needle and thread is needed.
    • A bow and arrow was used.

  • 🎒 Different Objects / Tools → Plural Verb

    • A pen and a pencil are on the desk.
    • The hammer and the wrench are in the box.
    • Shoes and socks are in the closet.

  • 🔍 How to Tell the Difference Quickly

  • Look at the little word "the" or "a" in the sentence — it's a major clue:
    • One article = Singular idea: "The chef and owner is here." (one person, two jobs)
    • Two articles = Plural people: "The chef and the owner are here." (two different people)

  • Example: _Rice and curry is my favorite dish._ (one meal) vs. _Rice and curry are both sold at the market._ (two items)

  • 🔹 Plural-Only Nouns: Clothing & Tools

  • These items have no singular form — they are always plural:

  • 👖 Clothing and Underwear

    • Pants — The pants are on the bed.
    • Jeans — Blue jeans look great.
    • Shorts — Running shorts are comfortable.
    • Trousers — Black trousers match any shirt.
    • Pajamas — My pajamas feel soft.
    • Leggings — Yoga leggings stretch a lot.
    • Tights — Warm tights keep you cozy.
    • Briefs / Boxers / Underpants — These boxers are new.
    • Overalls — Denim overalls are trendy.
    • Slacks — Dress slacks are required for work.
    • Suspenders — Clip-on suspenders hold up pants.

  • ✂️ Tools and Instruments

    • Scissors — Large scissors cut thick paper.
    • Pliers — Needle-nose pliers grip wires tightly.
    • Tweezers — Metal tweezers pick up small items.
    • Tongs — Ice tongs are in the bucket.
    • Shears — Hedge shears trim the bushes.
    • Clippers — Nail clippers are sharp.
    • Forceps — Medical forceps hold the bandages.
    • Handcuffs — Steel handcuffs lock tight.
    • Nutcrackers — Wooden nutcrackers crack walnuts easily.
    • Bellows — Fireplace bellows blow air onto logs.

  • 🕶️ Gear for Eyes and Ears

    • Glasses / Spectacles — Reading glasses help you see.
    • Sunglasses — Dark sunglasses block the sun.
    • Goggles — Safety goggles protect your eyes.
    • Binoculars — Heavy binoculars make birds look close.
    • Headphones / Earphones — Wireless headphones play music clearly.

  • Rule: Two-part items that are always plural — no singular form exists. Use are/were, never is/was.

  • 🔹 Indefinite Pronouns — The Agreement Rules

  • 👤 SINGULAR (take singular verbs)

    • Each, every, either, neither → singular: 'Each of the students is present.'
    • Someone, someone else, anyone, anyone else, no one, nobody, everyone, everybody → singular: 'Everyone is here.'
    • Something, anything, nothing, everything → singular: 'Nothing is impossible.'
    • One → singular: 'One must follow the rules.'

  • 👥 PLURAL (take plural verbs)

    • Both, few, several, many → plural: 'Both of them are coming.'
    • All → depends on what follows: 'All of the cake is gone.' (singular) vs. 'All of the students are gone.' (plural)

  • Rule: Words like each, every, anyone, everyone, nobody, somebody ALWAYS take singular verbs — even when followed by 'of the students/people'.

  • 🔹 'Of' Phrases — Finding the Real Subject

  • In sentences like 'The list of items is long,' the subject is NOT 'items' — it's 'list.' The phrase 'of items' is just additional information. Common patterns:
    • The number of students is large. (The number = singular; a specific quantity)
    • A number of students are absent. (A number = plural; many students)
    • The majority of the work is done. (The majority = singular when referring to a whole)
    • Half of the pie is eaten. / Half of the students are absent.
    • One third of the population lives in cities. / One third of the voters support the plan.
    • None of the money has been spent. / None of the students have arrived.

  • Rule: In 'The [noun] of [plural noun]' constructions, the FIRST noun (not the one after 'of') is the subject. 'The number of' = singular. 'A number of' = plural.

  • 🔹 Headings, Titles, and Quotations as Subjects

    • The title of the book is 'Harry Potter.' (singular — title = subject)
    • 'Himalaya' is a mountain range. (singular — the word itself)
    • 'Les Misérables' is a famous novel. (singular — one title)
    • 'The Rajiv Gandhi Award' was established in 1991. (singular)
    • 'War and Peace' is Tolstoy's longest novel. (singular — one book)
    • 'Tom and Jerry' is a classic cartoon. (singular — one show)

  • Rule: Titles, names of books/movies, and single entities take singular verbs — even if they contain 'and' or look plural.

  • 🔹 Words That Are Always Singular

    • Mathematics, physics, economics, ethics, measles, mumps, tennis, billiards → singular
    • The poor, the rich, the elderly, the young, the deaf, the blind → plural (referring to groups): 'The poor need help.'
    • Dias, weeks, months, years, miles (as measurement) → singular: 'Ten miles is a long walk.'
    • Measles, diabetes, malaria, gymnastics → singular: 'Malaria is a serious disease.'

  • 🔹 Collective Nouns — When Singular, When Plural

  • Collective nouns like 'team', 'family', 'committee', 'government', 'class', 'board' can take EITHER singular or plural verbs depending on whether you mean the GROUP as a unit or the INDIVIDUAL MEMBERS:
    • Singular (unit): 'The team wins the championship.' (the team as one entity)
    • Plural (members): 'The team are arguing among themselves.' (individual members disagreeing)
    • Singular: 'The government has announced a new policy.'
    • Plural: 'The government are divided on this issue.'
    • Singular: 'The class is going to the museum.'
    • Plural: 'The class are wearing their uniforms.'

  • Rule: If the group acts as ONE UNIT → singular verb. If the members act INDIVIDUALLY → plural verb. Look for clues: 'themselves' often signals plural.

  • 🔹 Inversion and Questions — Finding the Subject

  • In inverted sentences (where the verb comes before the subject), find the REAL subject to get agreement right:
    • There is a book on the table. / There are books on the table. (subject follows the verb)
    • Here comes the bus. / Here come the buses. (subject follows the verb)
    • Where is the manager? / Where are the managers?
    • Under the tree sits a old man. / Under the tree sit three old men.
    • On the wall hangs a painting. / On the wall hang three paintings.

  • Rule: In 'There is/are' and 'Here comes/come' constructions, the subject comes AFTER the verb. Match the verb to the subject that follows.

  • 🔹 'And' vs. 'Or' / 'Nor' / 'Either…or' / 'Neither…nor'

  • AND — always plural (unless one idea)

    • Ram and Shyam are coming. (plural — two people)
    • Bread and butter is my breakfast. (singular — one dish)

  • OR / NOR / EITHER…OR / NEITHER…NOR — verb agrees with NEAREST subject

    • Neither the teacher nor the students are present. (nearest = 'students' → plural)
    • Neither the students nor the teacher is present. (nearest = 'teacher' → singular)
    • Either the boys or the girl has won. (nearest = 'girl' → singular)
    • Either the girl or the boys have won. (nearest = 'boys' → plural)
    • Neither the captain nor the players were happy. (nearest = 'players' → plural)

  • Rule: With 'or/nor/either…or/neither…nor', the verb agrees with the subject CLOSEST to it. Always check which subject is nearest.

  • 🔹 Quantifiers and Agreement

    • Many a + singular noun + singular verb: 'Many a student has failed.' (literary/formal)
    • A number of + plural noun + plural verb: 'A number of students are absent.'
    • The number of + plural noun + singular verb: 'The number of students is increasing.'
    • Some, most, all, half + of + singular/plural noun → verb matches the noun: 'Some of the water is dirty.' / 'Some of the students are late.'
    • None of + singular/plural → both acceptable, but singular is more formal: 'None of the answers is correct.' / 'None of the answers are correct.'
