Conditions

Text

Newton's law of cooling works for all temperature differences — It is valid only for ∣T−T0​∣<30∘C (approx). For large differences, radiative cooling dominates and follows T4−T04​, giving faster cooling than the linear law predicts. · The cooling constant k is always the same — k=hA/(mc) depends on heat transfer coefficient h which varies with temperature, air velocity, and surface conditions. k is only approximately constant within the small ΔT range.

Key points

Text

The law fails at large temperature differences: radiation then dominates and the body's temperature follows the T⁴ Stefan law, not a straight line. · It also fails when the surroundings are not at a fixed temperature, when cooling is forced (fan, wind), or when the body changes phase while cooling.

13.
Label

Universal facts

Text

Historical: Isaac Newton described his law of cooling in Principia Mathematica (1701): 'The rates of cooling are proportional to the excess temperature above ambient.' He observed candle flames cooling and noted the exponential relationship, without calculus — he used geometric methods. · The law is an empirical fit, not a fundamental law of physics — Unlike Newton's laws of motion or universal gravitation, this is an engineering correlation. The underlying physics is heat transfer: conduction, convection, radiation, each with its own T-dependence.

18.
Label

Study notes

Text

Newton's law of cooling states: dtdT​=−k(T−T0​), where T is body temperature, T0​ is surrounding temperature, and k is a positive constant. This law has several critical limitations that restrict its applicability. · Limitation 1 — Small temperature difference: The law holds accurately only when ∣T−T0​∣<30∘C (roughly). For larger differences, radiative heat loss dominates, which follows the Stefan-Boltzmann law: P=eσA(T4−T04​), not linear in ΔT. · The linear approximation: T4−T04​=(T−T0​)(T+T0​)(T2+T02​)≈4T03​(T−T0​) for small ΔT. The approximation breaks down when ΔT is large — radiative cooling is much faster than predicted.

19.
Label

More ideas

Text

Concept 5: Connection between Newton's law of cooling and other topics · A large body cools the same way as a small body — For large bodies, Biot number Bi=hLc​/K determines if lumped capacitance applies. Large Bi (metals, large solids) → significant temperature gradient inside body → Newton's law fails. · Surrounding temperature T0​ can change during cooling — T0​ must be constant. If surroundings heat up (sun, nearby heater), or if the body itself warms the air around it, k is not constant and the exponential solution T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt is invalid.

20.
Label

More ideas

Text

The formula T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt works for any body — This solution holds only when k is constant (small ΔT) and no phase change/internal heat. For radiation-dominated cooling, T(t) follows a different integral. · Evaporation doesn't affect cooling in a covered calorimeter — Evaporation removes heat (latent heat ≈2260 kJ/kg) and changes liquid composition (concentration). Even in covered calorimeters, some evaporation occurs. For volatile liquids, error is significant. · Newton's law applies to all states of matter — Valid for liquids and gases where convection homogenizes temperature. For solids without internal convection, the Fourier heat equation applies, not Newton's law.

21.
Label

More ideas

Text

If two bodies have the same mass and same c, they cool identically — Surface area A is in k. Two spheres of same mass but different radii have different A/m ratio → different k → different cooling rates. Shape matters. · The lumped capacitance assumption (uniform body temperature) is always valid for liquids — For liquids in still air, convection currents maintain uniformity. But in a viscous liquid cooling by conduction alone, internal gradients exist and Newton's law over-simplifies. · The law fails only at very high temperatures — The failure depends on ΔT relative to T0​, not absolute T. A body at 100∘C cooling in 25∘C air (ΔT=75∘C) fails the linear approximation even though absolute T is not 'high'.

22.
Label

More ideas

Text

Water equivalent W is the mass of water in the calorimeter — W is a calculated mass-equivalent, not actual water present. For a dry copper calorimeter of mass 50 g, W≈4.7 g. No water is inside. · Water equivalent and heat capacity are the same thing — Heat capacity C=mc has units J/°C. Water equivalent W=mc/cw​ has units of mass (g or kg). They are related by C=W×cw​, but numerically different in SI. · The water equivalent of a calorimeter is constant — W=mcal​ccal​/cw​. Since cw​ is nearly constant, W is constant for a given calorimeter. But cw​ itself varies with temperature (4.186→4.219 J/g⋅K), so W varies slightly.

23.
Label

More ideas

Text

A heavier calorimeter always has larger W — Not always. W∝m×ccal​. Aluminum (c≈900) has higher specific heat per unit mass than copper (c≈390). So an aluminum calorimeter has higher W per unit mass than copper. · Water equivalent is used only in method of cooling — W appears in all calorimetry calculations: method of mixtures, method of cooling, bomb calorimetry, and steam calorimetry. Any time a calorimeter absorbs heat, W must be included. · The formula W=mc/cw​ means the body behaves like mc/cw​ grams of water — Yes, this is exactly the physical meaning. A 100 g copper block (c=390) has W=100×390/4186=9.3 g — it behaves thermally like 9.3 g of water.
