A material resists three kinds of deformation with three moduli, each stress-over-strain: Young's modulus Y=FL/(AΔL) for stretching, bulk modulus K=−ΔP/(ΔV/V) for uniform compression (minus sign because pressure up means volume down; compressibility = 1/K), and shear modulus η=(F/A)/θ for twisting, with the strain measured as an angle in radians.…

3.
Label

Conditions

Text

Material State Determines Available Moduli: Solids have all three moduli (E,B,G). Fluids (liquids/gases) have only bulk modulus B — they cannot sustain shear stress (G=0) and have no defined Young's modulus for stretching. · Isotropic Material Relations: For isotropic solids, the three moduli are linked via Poisson's ratio σ: E=2G(1+σ)=3B(1−2σ). These relations do not hold for anisotropic materials (crystals, composites) where moduli depend on direction.

Special cases

Text

Choose the modulus by the type of deformation: stretch → E, squeeze → B, twist → G. · The sign in B = −P/(ΔV/V) makes the modulus positive since ΔV is negative under compression.

More ideas

Text

Geometry Separation: E is an intensive material property (Pa), while spring constant k=EA/L is extensive. Changing a wire's length or cross-section changes k but not E — this distinction is essential for connecting spring problems to material science. · Three moduli, one idea: each is stress ÷ strain for a different deformation type — stretch (Y), squeeze (K), twist (η). · Liquids have K only — they resist compression but not shape change.
