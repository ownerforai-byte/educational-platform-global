Concept

Text

Black body: An idealized physical body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence. The term "black" refers to its absorption property, not its visual color — a black body in thermal equilibrium emits radiation across a continuous spectrum.

Derivation

Text

Absorptivity and emissivity: Absorptivity α is the fraction of incident radiation absorbed (α=1 for a black body). Emissivity e is the fraction of radiation emitted compared to a black body (e=1 for a black body). Kirchhoff's law states α=e at thermal equilibrium for each wavelength. · Planck's radiation law: The spectral radiance of a black body at temperature T is B(λ,T)=λ52hc2​ehc/(λkB​T)−11​, where h is Planck's constant, c is the speed of light, kB​ is Boltzmann's constant, and λ is wavelength. This formula accurately describes the entire spectrum.

Shortcut

Stars approximate black bodies, but their atmospheres cause absorption lines (Fraunhofer lines) that deviate from a pure black body spectrum. By modeling these deviations, astronomers determine stellar composition, magnetic fields, and rotation. · The concept of a black body is essential to understanding the cosmic infrared background and the extragalactic background light — both are measured to be close to modified black body spectra with different temperatures for different redshift ranges.

A black body at temperature T emits power P. If the temperature is doubled to 2T, by what factor does the power increase? By the Stefan-Boltzmann law: P∝T4, so at 2T: P′=(2T)4/T4×P=16P. The power increases by a factor of 16. Similarly, the peak wavelength halves. · At what temperature does a black body peak at 1 μm (near-infrared)? T=b/λmax​=2.898×10−3/(1×10−6)=2898 K. This is about the temperature of a heating element or a red-hot piece of iron.

Universal facts

In engineering, selective surfaces are coatings engineered to have high absorptivity at one wavelength and high emissivity at another — for example, solar absorbers (high absorption in visible, low emission in IR) and radiative cooling surfaces (high emission in atmospheric transparency window 8-13 μm).

Study notes

Text

Wien's displacement law: λmax​T=b=2.898×10−3m⋅K. The wavelength of peak emission is inversely proportional to temperature. Hot objects peak at shorter wavelengths: the Sun (~5778 K) peaks at ~500 nm (visible), while a room-temperature object (~300 K) peaks at ~10 μm (infrared). · Wien's approximation: At short wavelengths (high frequencies), Planck's law simplifies to B(λ,T)≈λ52hc2​e−hc/(λkB​T). This is valid when hc/(λkB​T)≫1, i.e., for the short-wavelength tail of the spectrum. · Rayleigh-Jeans law: At long wavelengths (low frequencies), Planck's law simplifies to B(λ,T)≈λ42ckB​T​. This classical result agrees with experiments at long wavelengths but fails catastrophically at short wavelengths — the "ultraviolet catastrophe" where it predicts infinite energy density.

E=σT4=5.67×10−8×1012=5.67×104 W/m² = 56,700 W/m².… · Explain why the Rayleigh-Jeans law fails at short wavelengths. The classical theory treats each electromagnetic mode as having average energy kB​T. At short wavelengths (high frequencies), there are infinitely many modes, each carrying kB​T energy, giving infinite total energy — the ultraviolet catastrophe.… · Stefan-Boltzmann law (total emission): The total power radiated per unit area by a black body is E=σT4, where σ=5.67×10−8W/m2K4 is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. This is obtained by integrating Planck's law over all wavelengths and solid angles.

More ideas

Text

Spectral distribution shape: The black body spectrum has a characteristic single-peaked curve. At a given temperature, the intensity rises steeply from zero to the peak, then falls off more gradually. The area under the curve (total emission) increases as T4. · Temperature dependence of peak wavelength: As temperature doubles, the peak wavelength halves. For example, at 3000 K, λmax​=966 nm (near-IR); at 6000 K, λmax​=483 nm (blue-green). This shift explains why metals glow progressively from red to white to blue-white as they heat up. · Experimental verification: Lummer and Pringsheim (1899) experimentally confirmed the shape of the black body spectrum. Their measurements showed the characteristic peak and the shift of the peak with temperature, providing strong evidence against the Rayleigh-Jeans classical prediction.

Application — radiation thermometry: Optical pyrometers measure temperature by comparing the brightness of an object to a calibrated reference. By filtering to a single wavelength and applying Planck's law, temperature can be determined without contact. · Application — climate science: Earth's energy balance depends on the balance between absorbed solar radiation (peak ~0.5 μm) and emitted terrestrial radiation (peak ~10 μm).
