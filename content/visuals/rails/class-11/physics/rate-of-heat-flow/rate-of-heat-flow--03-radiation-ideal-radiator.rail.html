Concept

Text

Radiation: Heat transfer by electromagnetic waves (mainly infrared, but also visible and ultraviolet for hot objects). Unlike conduction and convection, radiation does NOT require a material medium — it can travel through vacuum at the speed of light, c=3×108m/s.

Conditions

Text

❌ Good reflectors are also good emitters. ✅ Good REFLECTORS are POOR emitters (and poor absorbers). By Kirchhoff's law, what is absorbed equals what is emitted. A highly reflective surface absorbs little, so it emits little. · ❌ Radiation requires a medium. ✅ Radiation does NOT require any medium — it propagates perfectly through vacuum. This is how energy from the Sun reaches Earth across ~150 million km of empty space.

Special cases

Text

The concept of black body radiation was crucial in the development of quantum mechanics. Classical physics predicted the "ultraviolet catastrophe" — infinite radiation at short wavelengths. Max Planck resolved this in 1900 by quantizing energy: E=nhf. · The cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation is a nearly perfect black body spectrum at T = 2.725 K. Discovered by Penzias and Wilson in 1965, it is the remnant radiation from the Big Bang and provides strong evidence for the Big Bang model.

Solved

Text

A black body at 1000 K radiates energy. Find the peak wavelength. λmax​=b/T=2.898×10−3/1000=2.898×10−6 m = 2.9 μm. This is in the near-infrared — the object glows with a dull red color visible to the dark-adapted eye. · A spherical black body of radius 0.1 m at 500 K radiates. Find the total power emitted. Surface area A=4πr2=4π(0.01)=0.126 m². E=σT4=5.67×10−8×5004=5.67×10−8×6.25×1010=3544 W/m². Total power = 3544×0.126=446 W.

Limitation

Text

❌ Dark-colored objects are always better absorbers of all radiation. ✅ A dark object is a good absorber in the visible range, but its absorption in the infrared depends on surface finish, not color. White paint can have higher IR emissivity than black paint. · ❌ A perfect black body exists in nature. ✅ A perfect black body is an IDEALIZATION. The closest real approximations are cavity radiators (hole in a large enclosed heated chamber) and lamp-black surfaces.

7.
Label

Derivation

Text

Mechanism of radiation: All objects above absolute zero emit electromagnetic radiation due to the thermal motion of their charged particles (electrons and nuclei). The radiation arises from accelerating charges: oscillating dipoles in the material emit photons. · Electromagnetic spectrum of thermal radiation: At room temperature (~300 K), peak emission is in the far-infrared (~10 μm). At 1000 K, it shifts to near-infrared (~3 μm). At 6000 K (Sun's surface), the peak is in the visible range (~0.5 μm). Wien's law governs this shift.

The human body at 37°C radiates predominantly at wavelengths around 9-10 μm (mid-infrared). This is the basis of thermal imaging and infrared thermography used in medicine, security, and building inspection. · In astronomy, the color index (B-V) of a star — the difference in brightness through blue and visual filters — is a direct measure of its surface temperature and departure from a perfect black body spectrum.

11.
Label

Universal facts

Text

The Stefan-Boltzmann law was empirically discovered by Josef Stefan in 1879 and theoretically derived by Ludwig Boltzmann five years later. Boltzmann's derivation used thermodynamics and the radiation pressure of electromagnetic waves.

12.
Label

Study notes

Text

Black body (ideal radiator): An idealized object that absorbs ALL incident radiation at every wavelength (absorptivity α=1). It is also the PERFECT emitter at every wavelength. Since it absorbs everything, it appears perfectly black when cold. · Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation: At thermal equilibrium, the emissivity of a body equals its absorptivity at every wavelength: eλ​=αλ​. This means a good absorber is necessarily a good emitter, and a poor absorber is a poor emitter. · Perfectly black objects: Lamp-black (carbon soot) has emissivity ~0.97 — nearly a perfect black body. A cavity with a small hole is the best practical approximation: radiation entering the hole undergoes multiple internal reflections and is almost entirely absorbed.

13.
Label

More ideas

Text

The Sun's surface temperature is about 5778 K. Find the peak wavelength of solar radiation. λmax​=2.898×10−3/5778=5.01×10−7 m = 501 nm. This is in the green portion of the visible spectrum, which is why sunlight appears white (mixture of all visible wavelengths). · Explain why a thermos flask uses a silvered surface. The silvered walls have very low emissivity (~0.02), so they radiate very little heat. Combined with vacuum conduction prevention and low-conductivity stoppers, the silvered surfaces minimize ALL three heat transfer mechanisms. · Shiny vs. dull surfaces: Shiny, polished metals have LOW emissivity (0.05-0.2) and LOW absorptivity. Dull, dark surfaces have HIGH emissivity (0.8-0.95). This is why shiny surfaces keep food hot longer (less emission) and why dark clothes absorb more sunlight.

14.
Label

More ideas

Text

Moon and space: The Moon's surface has emissivity ~0.95. Without an atmosphere, it cannot convect or conduct heat. Surface temperatures range from ~127°C (day) to ~-173°C (night) — the extreme variation is due to radiative heating and cooling only. · The greenhouse effect: Greenhouse gases (CO₂, H₂O, CH₄) are transparent to visible sunlight but absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth's surface. They re-radiate this energy in all directions, including back toward the surface, warming the planet. · Thermal radiation in astrophysics: Stars are approximately perfect black bodies. By measuring their color (peak wavelength), astronomers can determine surface temperature using Wien's law: λmax​T=2.898×10−3m⋅K. Blue stars are hotter than red stars.

15.
Label

More ideas

Text

Application — thermography: Infrared cameras detect thermal radiation from objects and create images based on temperature differences. Used in medical diagnostics (inflammation detection), building inspection (heat leaks), and military (night vision). · Application — solar panels: Solar panels absorb visible radiation from the Sun (peak ~0.5 μm) and convert it to electricity. The panel surface is designed with high absorptivity in the visible range but LOW emissivity in the infrared — to minimize radiative heat loss. · Application — low-emissivity (Low-E) windows: A thin metallic coating on window glass has low emissivity (~0.05) and low absorptivity in the infrared. It reflects thermal radiation back into the room, reducing heat loss by radiation in winter.

Label

More ideas

Text

Application — cooling satellites: Satellite surfaces use coatings with carefully chosen emissivity to control temperature. Surfaces facing the Sun have low absorptivity; surfaces facing deep space have high emissivity to radiate heat away efficiently. · NEB exam focus: Define black body and ideal radiator. State Kirchhoff's law. Explain why dark surfaces are good absorbers and emitters. Apply Wien's displacement law to determine peak wavelengths for different temperatures. · Spectral emissivity: Real objects have wavelength-dependent emissivity e(λ). A surface that is white to visible light (reflects visible light) may still have high emissivity in the infrared. This distinction between visual appearance and thermal emissivity is crucial in engineering.

Label

More ideas

Text

Stefan-Boltzmann law (preview): The total power radiated per unit area by a black body is E=σT4. For real bodies, E=eσT4. This law quantifies the total radiative output and shows the dramatic T4 dependence.
