Potential gradient (uniform field): E=−dxdV​ Vector relation: E=−∇V Magnitude: ∣E∣=∣∇V∣

3.
Label

Conditions

Text

Omitting the negative sign in E = -dV/dx: The minus sign indicates that the field points toward decreasing potential. Writing E = dV/dx gives the wrong direction whenever V decreases along the chosen axis. · Assuming the potential gradient is a vector while differentiating only with respect to one variable: For a general three-dimensional field the gradient is a vector; the scalar expression dV/dx is valid only along a chosen axis where the field is known to be uniform or one-dimensional.

4.
Label

Special cases

Text

The relation E = -∇V holds only for electrostatic fields. In a time-varying magnetic field, the generalized relation is E = -∇V - dA/dt, so potential alone no longer determines the field (Faraday's law). · Wherever the potential is constant, the potential gradient and hence the electric field are exactly zero. This is why the interior of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is field-free even though its potential is finite.

5.
Label

Solved

Text

High-voltage transmission lines: Insulators are designed so the potential gradient along their surface stays below the corona-discharge threshold, preventing leakage currents and audible corona noise at transmission voltages of hundreds of kilovolts. · Resistivity measurement in the laboratory: Measuring the potential gradient along a current-carrying wire of known length and area yields its resistivity, the standard four-probe technique used in semiconductor doping analysis.

6.
Label

Limitation

Text

Confusing the potential gradient with the gradient of a scalar function in general mathematics: In electrostatics the potential gradient carries a minus sign and is identical in magnitude to the electric field, but a generic mathematical gradient is always positive in magnitude and has no such sign convention.

8.
Label

Shortcut

Text

Field recovery trick: differentiate the known V(r) and put a minus sign to obtain E(r). E.g. V = kQ/r gives E = kQ/r², and V = -(λ/2πε₀)ln r gives E = λ/(2πε₀r). · Unit trick: 1 V/m = 1 (J/C)/m = 1 N/C = 1 (kg·m/s²)/C, so volt per metre and newton per coulomb are interchangeable.

Key points

Text

Potential gradient is the rate of change of potential with distance, measured in V/m (identical to N/C). · Electric field equals the negative of the potential gradient: E = -dV/dx. · The field always points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential. · Differentiating V = kQ/r reproduces the inverse-square law E = kQ/r² exactly.
