Label

Conditions

Text

Homogeneity Constraint: Only quantities with the same dimensional formula can be added, subtracted, or equated in an equation. Term-by-term analysis is mandatory. · Function Argument Constraint: The arguments of trigonometric (e.g., sin θ, cos θ), exponential (e.g., ex), and logarithmic functions must be dimensionless. This is a critical check for determining the dimensions of unknown constants in an equation.

Limitation

Text

❌ 'If an equation is dimensionally correct, it is physically correct.' ✅ Homogeneity is a necessary but not sufficient test — T=l/g​ and T=3l/g​ are both dimensionally correct; only one is true (the constant 2π is invisible to dimensions). · ❌ 'Dimensional formula and dimensional equation are the same.' ✅ The formula is [F]=[MLT−2]; the equation is F=[MLT−2] — the equation shows the quantity equated to its dimensions.

Quick concepts

Text

Concept 1: Base quantities (M, L, T, I, Θ, J, N) and derived quantities expressed as dimensional formulas. · Concept 2: Principle of homogeneity — the fundamental test of dimensional validity of any equation. · Concept 3: Method of powers for deriving relations: assume product form, equate exponents per base dimension. · Concept 4: Unit conversion via dimensional formula: n₂ = n₁ × (scale factors)^(exponents).

12.
Label

Key points

Text

Dimensional homogeneity is a necessary test of correctness — it can reject an equation but can never fully prove one. · Dimensionless constants (½, 2π, e) are invisible to dimensional analysis. · Quantities with identical dimensions can still be physically different (work vs torque; energy vs moment). · The powers method can only solve for as many unknown exponents as there are independent base dimensions (usually 3: M, L, T).

13.
Label

Universal facts

Text

Dimensional analysis was pioneered by Fourier (1822) and developed into a systematic tool by Rayleigh; the Buckingham π theorem is its formal generalisation used throughout engineering. · The 2019 SI redefinition fixed h (Planck constant), c, e, kB​, NA​ etc. exactly — every unit is now dimensionally anchored to fundamental constants.

Study notes

Text

Dimensional formula expresses a physical quantity in terms of the seven base quantities using symbols M (mass), L (length), T (time), I (current), Θ (temperature), J (luminous intensity) and N (amount of substance). Examples: velocity [M0L1T−1], force [M1L1T−2], energy [M1L2T−2], pressure [M1L−1T−2]. · Principle of homogeneity: a physically correct equation must have identical dimensions in every term. You can add 3 apples to 4 apples but not 3 apples to 4 metres — likewise v=u+at works because every term is a velocity [LT−1]. · Use 1 — checking equations: replace every quantity by its dimensional formula and verify the two sides match. If they don't, the equation is certainly wrong. If they do match, the equation is probably right (never proof — a factor of ½ is dimensionless and invisible).

Dimensionless Constant Constraint: Dimensional analysis inherently cannot identify or verify dimensionless constants (e.g., 2π, 0.5). These factors must be determined via independent theoretical derivation or experimental calibration. · Angle (radian) and steradian are supplementary, dimensionless [M0L0T0] units. · Any term inside sin, cos, exp, log must be dimensionless.

Label

More ideas

Text

❌ 'Angle has units but no dimensions, so it is not a physical quantity.' ✅ Angle is a dimensionless [M0L0T0] SUPPLEMENTARY quantity — it has a unit (radian) but zero exponents in M, L, T. · ❌ 'Trigonometric, exponential and log functions can be applied to dimensional quantities.' ✅ Their arguments MUST be dimensionless — sin(ωt) is valid only because ωt is an angle (dimensionless). · ❌ '1 newton = 1 dyn.' ✅ 1 extN=105 extdyn — dimensional conversion ([MLT−2]: 1000 g × 100 cm) gives the factor 105.
