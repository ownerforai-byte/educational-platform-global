Study notes

Text

Definition: the scalar product (or dot product) of two vectors a and b is a⋅b=∣a∣∣b∣cosθ, where θ is the angle between them (0°≤θ≤180°). The result is a scalar quantity. · Geometric interpretation: a⋅b=a(bcosθ)=b(acosθ) — the product of one vector's magnitude and the projection of the other onto it. If θ<90°, the projection is positive; if θ>90°, the projection is negative. · Physical significance: the dot product of force and displacement gives work: W=F⋅d=Fdcosθ. Only the component of force along the displacement does work; perpendicular components contribute nothing.

A larger dot product means the vectors are more parallel — Yes, the dot product increases as θ decreases. Maximum at θ=0° (parallel), minimum at θ=180° (anti-parallel, negative dot product), zero at θ=90°. · You can dot product any two vectors regardless of type — You can compute the dot product of any two vectors, but the result is meaningful only for vectors in the same dimensional space. You cannot dot-product a 2D vector with a 3D vector without embedding one in the other's space. · A⋅A is the same as A⋅∣A∣ — A⋅A=∣A∣2 (magnitude squared), not the product of magnitude and magnitude. The dot product of a vector with itself is always a non-negative scalar.

A unit vector has zero magnitude — A unit vector has magnitude EXACTLY 1, not 0. The zero vector 0 has magnitude 0 and indeterminate direction; a unit vector has magnitude 1 and a specific direction. · Every vector is a unit vector — Only vectors with magnitude 1 are unit vectors. A general vector A=3i^ has magnitude 3, not 1. To get a unit vector, you must divide by the magnitude: A^=A/∣A∣. · There is only one unit vector in space — There are infinitely many unit vectors, one for each direction in space. In 3D, unit vectors form a unit sphere (all vectors of magnitude 1).

Unit vectors add to give a vector with the sum of magnitudes — Unit vectors add via the parallelogram law, considering their direction. i^+j^​ has magnitude 2​, not 2. · The unit vector is just the direction — The unit vector encodes ONLY the direction, but it is itself a vector (with magnitude 1). Saying 'just the direction' is informal — formally, the unit vector is a vector in 3D space with magnitude 1. · The unit vector A^ equals A — A^ equals A only if ∣A∣=1. For a general vector, A^=A/∣A∣, which is the unit vector in the same direction.
