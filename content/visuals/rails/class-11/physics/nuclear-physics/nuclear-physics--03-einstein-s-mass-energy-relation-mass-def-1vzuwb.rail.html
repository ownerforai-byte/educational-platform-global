Conditions

Text

E=mc² and Binding Energy: Einstein's relation E=mc2 underpins nuclear binding energy. The mass defect Δm corresponds to binding energy BE=Δm⋅c2. In nuclear reactions, the total mass-energy (rest mass + kinetic) is conserved. · Packing Fraction Significance: Packing fraction f=(M−A)/A measures how much the actual atomic mass M deviates from the mass number A. Negative f means the nucleus is bound (mass less than sum of parts). Most stable nuclei have the most negative f (e.g., 56Fe).

Study notes

Text

Atomic mass unit (u): 1, extu=1.660539 imes10−27, extkg. Defined as exactly 1/12 the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state. All nuclear masses are expressed in u for convenience. · Exact mass vs mass number: The mass number A is the integer count of nucleons. The exact mass in u differs slightly from A due to mass defect. For example, 12 extC has exact mass 12, extu by definition, but 1 extH has exact mass 1.007825, extu, not 1, extu. · Isotopes: atoms of the same element (Z same) with different mass numbers (A different). They have identical chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different nuclear masses, stabilities, and decay modes. Example: 35 extCl (75.78) and 37 extCl (24.22) are chlorine isotopes.

More ideas

Text

The mass defect is NOT the binding energy — it is the mass DEFICIT that, when multiplied by c², gives the binding energy. Δm = BE/c², not BE = Δm. BE = Δm × c². · Mass defect arises because binding energy is negative potential energy. When nucleons come together, potential energy decreases (becomes more negative), so mass decreases (since energy decreased). The system is more stable at lower mass-energy. · Binding energy per nucleon is NOT the same as binding energy. BE/A peaks at iron. Even though a heavy nucleus has a large total BE (it is very bound), BE/A can be low. This is why heavy nuclei can undergo fission to release energy.

More ideas

Text

The formula E = Δmc² does NOT mean you can convert any arbitrary mass m into energy. Only the mass associated with the binding energy (the 'defect') can be released in nuclear reactions. The rest mass of a stable nucleus cannot be converted to energy without destroying the nucleus. · Chemical energy does NOT involve mass-energy equivalence — chemical reactions involve rearrangement of electrons, and the mass changes are too tiny to measure. Nuclear physics deals with changes in the nucleus itself. · Note 3: Practice numerical problems regularly for mastery of Einstein's mass-energy relation; mass defect, packing fraction, binding energy per nucleon
