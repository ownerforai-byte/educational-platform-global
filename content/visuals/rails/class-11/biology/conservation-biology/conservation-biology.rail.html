1.

Concept

Text

Conservation biology is triage with a ledger. Biodiversity counts at three levels — genetic variety inside species, species number in an area, ecosystem variety across a region. Conservation then runs in two directions, in-situ (protect the habitat) and ex-situ (keep a backup outside), while the IUCN Red List records how close each species is to the exit: Extinct → Extinct in the Wild → Critically Endangered → Endangered → Vulnerable → Near Threatened → Least Concern.

formula

Text

Biodiversity = genetic + species + ecosystem diversity · IUCN ladder (high → low risk): EX → EW → CR → EN → VU → NT → LC · Nepal: 10 national parks + 6 conservation areas + 1 hunting reserve + Ramsar wetlands · Threats: H-O-P-I-C (Habitat loss, Overexploitation/poaching, Pollution, Invasive species, Climate change)

3.
Label

Conditions

Text

An IUCN category is an assessment, not a label — it follows criteria on population size, rate of decline, range area and fragmentation, and it is global, so a species can be safer worldwide than it is locally in Nepal. Protected-area categories also differ by law: a national park enforces strict protection with no settlement or resource use, while a conservation area is managed with the people living in it. Ramsar listing requires a wetland of international importance.

4.
Label

Special cases

Text

Chitwan (1973) was Nepal's first national park and is UNESCO World Heritage — Bengal tiger plus one-horned rhino. Sagarmatha (1976) is the Everest park, also UNESCO. Nepal's key threatened species: Bengal tiger (Endangered), Asian elephant (Endangered), one-horned rhino (Vulnerable), snow leopard (Vulnerable), red panda (Vulnerable). Ramsar wetlands include Koshi Tappu, Rara and Chitwan. Nepal sits in the Himalaya biodiversity hotspot, and community forestry — 22,000+ groups managing ~36 % of forest land — is the country's globally cited success.

5.
Label

Solved

Text

Use the ladder like a decision tree. 'Population crashed by more than half, range shrinking fast' → CR or EN depending on numbers (tiger → EN). 'Numbers falling but range still large' → VU (rhino, snow leopard). 'No living individual anywhere' → EX. Then match species to protection: rhino and tiger → Chitwan/Bardia; snow leopard → Sagarmatha, Annapurna, Kanchenjunga; red panda → Langtang and Kanchenjunga. Name the tool for each threat: poaching → anti-poaching units + CITES; habitat loss → parks and corridors; local conflict → community-based conservation.

6.
Label

Limitation

Text

The numbers move and the sources disagree — Nepal's park count is commonly written as 10 but newer additions push it to 11–14, and IUCN statuses change with every assessment. A park on paper is not protection on the ground: poaching, human–wildlife conflict and unmanaged tourism continue inside boundaries. Ex-situ conservation saves individuals but loses genetic flow, and hotspot maps are qualitative, not measurable targets.

7.
Label

Derivation

Text

Derive the whole unit from small-population genetics: habitat loss fragments a population → numbers fall → genetic diversity drops → inbreeding depression and disease vulnerability rise → lower reproduction → still fewer individuals — the extinction vortex. Every strategy is aimed at one link: in-situ protection keeps the population large, corridors restore gene flow, anti-poaching and CITES cut the harvest, ex-situ is insurance once the population is too small to survive alone, and community management works because the vortex starts on land people use.

8.
Label

Shortcut

Text

Three strings answer most of the paper: biodiversity = G-S-E; threats = H-O-P-I-C; IUCN = EX-EW-CR-EN-VU-NT-LC. Nepal facts to keep ready: 10 NP + 6 CA + 1 HR + Ramsar; Chitwan 1973 (tiger + rhino, UNESCO); Sagarmatha 1976 (Everest, UNESCO); tiger EN, elephant EN, rhino VU, snow leopard VU, red panda VU. One-line rule: 'in-situ = in the habitat, ex-situ = outside it'.
