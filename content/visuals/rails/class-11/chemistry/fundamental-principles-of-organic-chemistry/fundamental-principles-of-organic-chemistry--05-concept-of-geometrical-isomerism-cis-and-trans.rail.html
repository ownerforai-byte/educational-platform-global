Resonance and isomerism — same atoms, different molecules

Stat Key

Key Points

  • Geometric isomerism arises from restricted rotation around double bonds (cis/trans) or chirality (d/l).
  • Chain isomerism involves different skeletal arrangements (straight vs branched chains).
  • Position isomerism occurs when functional groups are placed at different carbon positions.
  • Functional isomerism differs in the type of functional group present.
  • Metamerism involves different alkyl groups attached to a central atom (e.g., ethers, ketones).
  • Optical isomerism requires a chiral center with four different substituents.
  • Resonance stabilization delocalizes π electrons across multiple contributing structures.
  • Lassaigne's test detects nitrogen, sulfur, and halogens via sodium fusion.
  • Homolytic cleavage produces radicals; heterolytic cleavage produces ions (electrophile/nucleophile).
  • Inductive (+I/-I) effects transmit electron density through sigma bonds.
  • Resonance (+R/-R) effects involve electron movement through pi systems.

Important Statements

  • Same molecular formula, different arrangement = isomers: structural (skeleton/position/group) vs stereo (cis-trans/chiral).
  • Resonance delocalizes π electrons, giving the real molecule greater stability than any single contributor.
  • Geometric constraints (rigid double bonds) prevent free rotation, enabling cis/trans isomerism.
  • Chiral centers with four different groups give rise to enantiomers (d and l forms).
  • Lassaigne's test fuses compound with Na to convert N→CN⁻, S→S²⁻, X→X⁻ for qualitative detection.
  • Inductive effects (+I = electron-donating, -I = electron-withdrawing) influence reactivity and stability.
  • Resonance energy of benzene (~150 kJ/mol) explains its exceptional stability compared to localized double bonds.

Important Notes

  • Tautomerism (keto↔enol) is a dynamic form of structural isomerism involving proton migration.
  • Benzene's equal C-C bond lengths (1.39 Å) confirm delocalized π bonding.
  • Butan-2-ol (CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃) is named butan-2-ol per IUPAC rules (lowest locant for OH group).
  • Lassaigne's test fails for volatile compounds that escape fusion or for mixtures with competing elements.
  • N-detection requires selective steps (FeSO₄/FeCl₃ discrimination) to distinguish from S presence.

Examples

  • 2-butene: cis-2-butene (both methyl groups on same side of double bond) vs trans-2-butene (opposite sides).
  • Butan-2-ol: CH₃-CH(OH)-CH₂-CH₃, demonstrating position isomerism (OH on C2 vs C3).
  • Ozone: O=O⁺–O⁻ ↔ O⁻–O⁺=O (bent, equal bonds) illustrates resonance in ozone.
  • Benzene: Two Kekulé structures ↔ one 6π-delocalized hexagon (equal C-C bonds).
  • Lassaigne test: Na + C + N → NaCN (Prussian blue test) confirms nitrogen presence.

Universal Facts

  • Isomers have identical molecular formulas but different structural arrangements.
  • Geometric isomerism requires restricted rotation (typically around C=C double bonds).
  • Optical isomerism requires a chiral center (asymmetric carbon with four different groups).
  • Resonance stabilization lowers the energy of the molecule compared to localized structures.
  • IUPAC nomenclature prioritizes the principal functional group for numbering.

Confusion

  • Confusing geometric (cis/trans) isomerism with optical (d/l) isomerism – both arise from restricted rotation but involve different symmetry elements.
  • Thinking that all isomers have different molecular weights – isomers have identical molecular formulas.
  • Assuming all double bonds produce geometric isomers – only alkenes with ≥2 different substituents on each carbon can.
  • Mixing up Lassaigne's test for nitrogen with other qualitative tests for halogens or sulfur.

Summary

  • Geometrical isomerism (cis/trans) results from restricted rotation around double bonds, while optical isomerism (d/l) arises from chiral centers. Both are crucial for understanding molecular shape and reactivity in organic chemistry.
  • Resonance delocalization stabilizes molecules like benzene, lowering their energy relative to localized structures.
  • Lassaigne's test and qualitative analysis methods enable detection of functional groups in organic compounds.
  • Understanding isomerism is foundational for predicting physical properties, biological activity, and reaction outcomes in organic synthesis.
