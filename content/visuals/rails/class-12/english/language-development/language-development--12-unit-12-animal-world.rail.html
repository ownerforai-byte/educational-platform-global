The debates, held honestly "Zoos: prisons or sanctuaries?" Concede the cage's indictment — pacing predators, concrete enclosures. Rebut with the modern zoo's changed job: breeding programmes for the near-extinct, veterinary care, the child who sees a tiger and grows up to fund its forest.… · ## Grammar and style: comparison and habit Animals invite comparison, and the grammar must keep up: "A cheetah runs faster than a deer"; "The rhino is the most heavily built of them all"; "A cat sleeps as much as sixteen hours a day". Watch the double comparatives ("more faster" — the classic slip) and the irregulars (good/better/best, bad/worse/worst) that examiners salt into every paper.…

How the exam asks it The descriptive essay ("The animal I like most", 6–8 marks) wants one animal, seen precisely: appearance in naturalist detail, habitat and habits in the present simple, one reason for your choice that is not "it is beautiful". The argumentative essay ("Zoos should be banned", "Save wildlife") wants the frames above with one Nepal anchor — the tiger census, the community forests.…

The words that carry the theme Precision begins with the collective nouns the exam loves: a pack of wolves, a herd of elephants, a flock of birds, a swarm of bees, a pride of lions, a school of fish. Then the family words: cub, calf, kitten, chick, foal, puppy; and the gender pairs the paper tests — lion/lioness, tiger/tigress, bull/cow, ram/ewe, stallion/mare.… · ## Reading the theme like a writer — the devices animals carry Personification is the theme's oldest device — animals in literature think, plot and judge because it lets writers say human things at a safe distance: the crow's persistence in the fable is really a lecture on patience.…

Text

Trick 1: Animal texts often argue ethics — organise answers as welfare facts (sentience, suffering) + human duty + one practice to change. · Trick 2: Comprehension questions target behaviour details (habitat, diet, instinct) — underline them during reading.
