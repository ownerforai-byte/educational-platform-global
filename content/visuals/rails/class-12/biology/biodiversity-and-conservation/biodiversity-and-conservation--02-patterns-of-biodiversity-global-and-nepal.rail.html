Text

Biodiversity is richest toward the equator because of time, stability and energy. The number of species grows with area as S = c A^z, more slowly than area itself. Nepal's richness comes from a huge altitude range and from lying in the Himalaya and Indo-Burma hotspots, each of which must combine high endemism with heavy habitat loss.

2.

Formula

Kind

formula

Text

S = c A^z, where S is species number, A is area, c is a constant and z is the slope. log S = log c + z log A. Hotspot thresholds: endemic vascular plants >= 1500, and original habitat lost > 70 percent.

3.
Label

Conditions

Text

A hotspot is not simply a place with many species. It also requires many endemics and severe habitat loss. A well-preserved forest with few unique species is not a hotspot. · The species-area curve does not say that every extra hectare adds the same number of species. The rise flattens: the first hectares add many species, later hectares add fewer.

4.
Label

Special cases

Text

Quote z as a range, not a single number: about 0.1-0.2 locally, about 0.6-1.2 for very large areas. · Do not call every protected forest a hotspot. Protected status and hotspot status are different ideas.

Solved

Text

Amazon rainforest and Indo-Pacific coral reefs illustrate high equatorial richness. · Chitwan's lowland forest and the Annapurna mid-hills hold different communities along Nepal's altitude gradient.

Limitation

Text

Equatorial richness is not caused only by heat. Time, climatic stability and productivity act together. A hot desert can be species-poor. · Endemic means the species is found nowhere else, not merely that it is rare inside Nepal. A widespread species that is uncommon in one valley is not an endemic.

Key points

Text

Latitudinal gradient: species richness generally increases toward the equator. · Species-area law: S = c A^z, and log S = log c + z log A. · z is shallow (about 0.1-0.2) for local samples and steeper for continents and islands. · Nepal lies in two hotspots, the Himalaya and Indo-Burma.

Universal facts

Text

There are 36 recognised global biodiversity hotspots. Two of them, Himalaya and Indo-Burma, include parts of Nepal. · More than half of the world's species live in tropical regions, which cover a much smaller share of the land surface.

17.

Study notes

Text

Species richness is not spread evenly. The strongest global pattern is the latitudinal gradient: the number of species of most groups rises from the poles toward the equator, so tropical forests and coral reefs hold far more species than tundra or boreal forest. · Two ideas explain that gradient. The tropics have had a longer uninterrupted time for speciation, and a more stable climate, so extinction rates stay lower. Higher solar energy also supports higher productivity and, through that, more niches. · The species-area relationship says that larger areas hold more species. Plotted as species against area it is a rectangular hyperbola, S = c A^z. On a log-log graph it becomes a straight line, log S = log c + z log A.
