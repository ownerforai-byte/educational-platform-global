Questions

1.

Describe the structure of a bacterial cell and state the function of any three parts.

ID

bio-mic-2025-1

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

A bacterial cell has a cell envelope of capsule (in some), cell wall of peptidoglycan and plasma membrane; inside lie the nucleoid — a single circular DNA without histones — 70S ribosomes, plasmids, inclusion bodies and storage granules. Many bear flagella for movement, pili for attachment and conjugation, and some form endospores. Functions: the capsule protects against drying and phagocytosis and helps adhesion; the cell wall gives shape and resists osmotic lysis; flagella provide motility; pili transfer DNA during conjugation; ribosomes carry out protein synthesis.

2.

Explain the modes of nutrition in bacteria and describe the bacterial growth curve.

ID

bio-mic-2025-2

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Autotrophic bacteria make their own food — photoautotrophs use light (cyanobacteria) and chemoautotrophs oxidise inorganic compounds (Nitrosomonas). Heterotrophic bacteria depend on organic matter: saprophytes decompose dead material, parasites live on hosts and pathogens cause disease. In a closed culture the growth curve has four phases: lag (cells adapt and enzymes are induced, no division), log or exponential (fastest doubling under ample food), stationary (growth equals death as nutrients fall and waste accumulates) and decline or death phase.

3.

Give the characteristic features of cyanobacteria and their economic importance.

ID

bio-mic-2025-3

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Cyanobacteria are prokaryotic, photosynthetic and mostly blue-green; they contain chlorophyll a with phycocyanin and phycoerythrin, have no flagella, and store food as cyanophycean starch. Some form filaments with heterocysts for nitrogen fixation, and many secrete mucilage. Importance: they fix atmospheric nitrogen (Anabaena, Nostoc) and are used as biofertiliser in paddy fields, add organic matter and improve soil fertility, serve as protein-rich food (Spirulina) and are used in sewage treatment. On the other hand, heavy growth in nutrient-rich water causes blooms that deoxygenate the water and release toxins.

4.

Draw the general structure of a virus and explain why viruses are regarded as a link between living and non-living things.

ID

bio-mic-2025-4

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

A virus has a nucleic acid core — either DNA or RNA, never both — enclosed in a protein coat, the capsid, built of subunits called capsomeres; some also have an envelope derived from the host membrane. They behave as non-living outside a host: they have no cytoplasm, ribosomes or metabolism, do not respire, cannot reproduce independently and can be crystallised and stored like a chemical (tobacco mosaic virus). Yet inside a suitable host they replicate, mutate and evolve and show host specificity, so they sit on the border between living and non-living.

5.

Describe the lytic cycle of a bacteriophage.

ID

bio-mic-2025-5

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution
  1. Adsorption: the phage tail fibres recognise and attach to specific receptors on the bacterial wall.
  2. Penetration: the tail sheath contracts and injects the phage DNA into the bacterium, leaving the capsid outside.
  3. Biosynthesis: host DNA is degraded and the phage DNA directs the host machinery to make phage DNA and proteins.
  4. Maturation: heads, tails and DNA are assembled into new virions.
  5. Lysis: phage lysozyme bursts the cell, releasing many progeny phages that infect new bacteria.

In the lysogenic cycle the phage DNA instead integrates into the bacterial chromosome as a prophage and is copied with it until induction triggers the lytic cycle.

6.

State four impacts of biotechnology in the field of microbiology.

ID

bio-mic-2025-6

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution
  1. Recombinant bacteria such as E. coli produce human insulin, growth hormone and interferon, giving pure products in bulk.
  2. Fermentation industries use microbes to make antibiotics (penicillin from Penicillium), alcohol, citric acid, vitamins and enzymes.
  3. Gene cloning, PCR and DNA probes let pathogens be detected quickly and vaccines and gene therapy developed.
  4. Engineered microbes carry out bioremediation of oil spills and industrial waste and produce biogas, while microbial genomics helps trace outbreaks.

Risks such as escape of engineered strains and antibiotic-resistance genes mean such work needs strict containment.
