Questions

1.

Define biodiversity and describe its three levels with an example of each.

ID

bio-bdc-2025-1

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

easy

Solution

Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms at all levels, together with the ecological complexes they form. Genetic diversity: variation of genes within a species, as among the many landraces of rice in Nepal. Species diversity: the number and abundance of species in a region — the hundreds of tree species in a tropical forest against a pine plantation. Ecosystem diversity: the variety of habitats and communities — forests, grasslands, wetlands, rivers and alpine zones in one landscape. Diversity is measured by species richness (number of species) and evenness (relative abundance), combined in indices such as Simpson's or Shannon–Wiener.

2.

Explain the latitudinal gradient and the species–area relationship.

ID

bio-bdc-2025-2

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Latitudinal gradient: species richness is greatest near the equator and falls towards the poles. Tropical regions have a long evolutionary history without glaciation, a warm and stable climate, high and reliable rainfall, greater productivity and more complex habitats, so more niches and hence more species can coexist. Species–area relationship: the number of species increases with the area sampled, following S = C A^z (or log S = log C + z log A), where z is about 0.1–0.2 for small areas and 0.6–1.2 for very large continents. The relationship is why small habitat fragments lose species quickly and why large protected areas conserve more diversity.

3.

Give the four main causes of biodiversity loss with an explanation of each.

ID

bio-bdc-2025-3

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Habitat loss and fragmentation: the most important cause — forests cleared for farms, settlements and roads, wetlands drained, and remaining habitat broken into patches too small for large animals. Over-exploitation: hunting, poaching, fishing and logging beyond the rate of replacement, as with rhino horn, tiger bone and some medicinal plants. Invasive alien species: introduced plants and animals such as Mikania, water hyacinth, Lantana and tilapia outcompete or prey on natives. Co-extinction and secondary effects: when a host or partner species disappears its dependants follow, and pollution plus climate change add further pressure; small populations also suffer inbreeding and loss of genetic variation.

4.

Describe in-situ conservation and the three zones of a biosphere reserve.

ID

bio-bdc-2025-4

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

In-situ conservation protects species in their natural habitat, so the whole community and its interactions are preserved and populations keep evolving. It includes national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, conservation areas, community forests, sacred groves and Ramsar wetlands. A biosphere reserve has three zones: a core zone, legally protected and undisturbed, where no human activity is allowed except research; a buffer zone, where research, education and limited traditional use are permitted around the core; and a transition or manipulation zone, where settlements, farming, forestry and recreation are allowed with sustainable management. The design separates strict protection from the livelihoods of local people.

5.

Explain ex-situ conservation with four methods and one limitation.

ID

bio-bdc-2025-5

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Ex-situ conservation keeps species outside their natural habitat. Methods: botanical and zoological gardens, where living plants and animals are maintained and bred; seed and gene banks, storing seeds at low temperature and humidity for decades; cryopreservation of embryos, sperm, ova and tissue in liquid nitrogen; and tissue culture, micropropagation and DNA banks for species difficult to store as seed. Limitation: only a small sample of individuals is kept, so genetic diversity is narrow, and behaviour, migration and ecological relationships needed for life in the wild cannot be preserved — hence it supplements but cannot replace in-situ conservation.

6.

What is the Red Data Book? Explain the IUCN categories with examples of Nepali species.

ID

bio-bdc-2025-6

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

The Red Data Book (IUCN Red List) is the global record of the conservation status of species, based on quantitative criteria of population size, decline and range. Categories: Extinct, Extinct in the wild, Critically endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near threatened, Least concern and Data deficient; the first three of the threatened group are the highest risk. Nepali examples: the greater one-horned rhinoceros and red panda are endangered, the snow leopard and tiger are vulnerable or endangered depending on assessment, the gharial is critically endangered, and the spiny babbler is near threatened. The list guides legal protection and conservation priority in Nepal's National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act.

7.

What is a biodiversity hotspot? Name the hotspot that includes Nepal and state its importance.

ID

bio-bdc-2025-7

Type

mcq

Marks

2

Difficulty

medium

Solution

A biodiversity hotspot is a region with exceptionally high species richness and endemism that has already lost at least 70% of its original vegetation; 36 such hotspots hold a large share of the world's threatened species in a small area. Nepal lies within the Himalaya hotspot, which also covers Bhutan, northern India, and parts of Pakistan, China and Myanmar. Its importance lies in the steep altitudinal range that packs tropical to alpine life into short distances, many endemic and medicinal plants, and the headwaters of rivers on which hundreds of millions of people depend.
