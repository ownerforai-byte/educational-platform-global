Questions

1.

Explain classical and photochemical smog, and give three control measures for air pollution.

ID

bio-env-2025-1

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Classical (London-type) smog forms in cold, humid air when smoke from coal burning mixes with fog, trapping sulphur dioxide and particulates; it irritates the lungs and cuts visibility. Photochemical (Los Angeles-type) smog forms in strong sunlight when nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons from vehicles react to produce ozone, peroxyacetyl nitrate and aldehydes, which irritate eyes and damage crops and rubber. Control: use cleaner fuel and renewable energy, fit catalytic converters and flue-gas scrubbers, expand public transport, control industrial emissions, avoid open burning and plant green belts. Air quality indices now track particulate matter, SO2, NO2, CO and ozone so that warnings and restrictions can be issued.

2.

What is eutrophication? Explain how sewage and fertiliser runoff cause it, and give BOD as a measure of water pollution.

ID

bio-env-2025-2

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Eutrophication is the nutrient enrichment of a water body, mainly by nitrate and phosphate, that leads to excessive growth of algae and plants. Sewage and fertiliser runoff add these nutrients; an algal bloom shades the water and then dies, and its decomposition by bacteria consumes dissolved oxygen, so oxygen levels fall sharply, fish die and the water turns foul, with bad odour and toxins from cyanobacteria. The lake slowly fills with organic sediment. Biological oxygen demand (BOD) measures the oxygen used by microbes to oxidise the organic matter in a water sample over five days; clean water has a low BOD and polluted sewage-loaded water a high BOD, so BOD is a standard index of organic pollution.

3.

Describe solid waste management with its main methods and their problems.

ID

bio-env-2025-3

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Solid waste — household, commercial, industrial, hospital and construction waste — is managed by the hierarchy of reduce, reuse, recycle and recover. Methods: segregation at source into biodegradable and non-biodegradable fractions; composting and vermicomposting of organic waste; recycling of paper, glass, metal and plastic; sanitary landfill with a clay or plastic liner, leachate collection and gas venting; and incineration with energy recovery for suitable waste, with hospital waste incinerated separately. Problems: open dumping breeds vectors and leaches leachate into groundwater, methane from landfills adds to the greenhouse effect, burning plastics releases dioxins, and recycling is limited by mixed waste and lack of markets — hence segregation and reduction are the priorities.

4.

Explain the hazards of nuclear radiation and the safeguards against them.

ID

bio-env-2025-4

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Nuclear radiation comes from reactor accidents, waste from power plants and research, weapons testing and natural radon. Ionising radiation damages DNA, causing burns, radiation sickness, cancers, leukaemia, genetic defects and, at high dose, death; long-lived isotopes such as strontium-90 and caesium-137 enter the food chain and concentrate in bone and muscle, and accidents such as Chernobyl and Fukushima spread contamination over whole regions. Safeguards: strict design with multiple containment barriers, monitoring and dosimetry for workers, shielding and remote handling, safe storage of high-level waste for long periods in stable geological sites, and international safeguards on nuclear material; medical and industrial sources must be licensed and disposed of properly.

5.

Explain the greenhouse effect, its causes and its consequences, and state two mitigation measures.

ID

bio-env-2025-5

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Greenhouse gases — carbon dioxide from fossil fuels and deforestation, methane from livestock and paddy fields, nitrous oxide from fertiliser, CFCs and water vapour — are transparent to incoming sunlight but absorb outgoing infrared radiation, warming the lower atmosphere. Consequences: rising average temperature, melting glaciers and polar ice, sea-level rise and coastal flooding, more frequent droughts, floods and heat waves, shifting crop belts, coral bleaching, spread of vector-borne disease and loss of biodiversity; Nepal sees retreating glaciers, expanding glacial lakes and more erratic monsoon. Mitigation: cut fossil-fuel use and shift to solar, hydro and wind; plant and protect forests; improve efficiency and public transport; and adopt international commitments such as the Paris Agreement.

6.

Explain ozone layer depletion — its causes, effects and control measures.

ID

bio-env-2025-6

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Ozone in the stratosphere absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation. Chlorofluorocarbons released from refrigerators, air conditioners and aerosol sprays rise to the stratosphere, where ultraviolet light breaks them down and chlorine atoms catalytically destroy ozone — one chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone molecules; halons and nitrogen oxides add to the loss, producing the Antarctic ozone hole. Effects: more UV-B reaching the ground causes skin cancer, cataract, sunburn, weakened immunity, damage to phytoplankton, crops and materials. Control: the Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs and halons and they were replaced by HCFCs and HFCs; recovery of the ozone layer is now being observed, though HFCs are potent greenhouse gases and are being phased down under the Kigali Amendment.

7.

What is rainwater harvesting? Describe its methods and its importance in Nepal.

ID

bio-env-2025-7

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Rainwater harvesting is the collection and storage of rain from roofs or catchments for later use and for recharging groundwater. Methods: roof catchment with gutters, first-flush diversion, storage tanks or jars; surface runoff collection in ponds, check dams, farm ponds and recharge pits; and recharge wells or trenches that send water into the aquifer. Importance in Nepal: hill settlements and the Terai face drying springs and falling water tables in the dry season, so harvesting supplies drinking water, irrigation and livestock water, reduces flooding and soil erosion, recharges springs and reduces dependence on distant sources; traditional dhunge dhara and pokhari systems are examples of the same idea.

8.

What is wasteland development? Describe its causes and methods of reclamation.

ID

bio-env-2025-8

Type

short

Marks

4

Difficulty

medium

Solution

Wasteland is degraded land that has lost its productivity — eroded slopes, gullied and ravine land, waterlogged and saline soil, mined and industrial spoil, and overgrazed pasture. Wasteland development restores such land to productive and ecological use. Causes: deforestation, overgrazing, unsuitable cultivation on steep slopes, faulty irrigation causing salinity and waterlogging, mining and industrial waste, and shifting cultivation. Methods: check dams, contour bunds, terracing and gully plugging to stop erosion; planting of hardy grasses and nitrogen-fixing trees, agroforestry and social forestry; drainage and gypsum treatment of saline-sodic soil; and legal protection with community participation, so that the land returns to fuel, fodder, food and groundwater recharge.
