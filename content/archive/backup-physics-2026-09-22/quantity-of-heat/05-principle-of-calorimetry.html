Principle of Calorimetry (Method of Mixtures)

Relevance

Notes

  • Statement: When two or more bodies at different temperatures are brought into thermal contact in an isolated system, heat flows from hotter to colder bodies until they reach a common final temperature (thermal equilibrium).
  • Core principle: In the absence of heat loss to the surroundings: Heat lost by hot bodies = Heat gained by cold bodies.
  • Mathematical form: For two bodies: m1​c1​(θ1​−θeq​)=m2​c2​(θeq​−θ2​), where θ1​>θeq​>θ2​.
  • Including the calorimeter: In real experiments, the calorimeter vessel also absorbs/releases heat. The full equation is: mh​ch​(θh​−θeq​)=(mc​cc​+Ccal′​)(θeq​−θc​).
  • Water equivalent form: If the calorimeter's water equivalent W is known: mh​ch​(θh​−θeq​)=(mc​cc​+Wcw​)(θeq​−θc​).
  • Derivation from energy conservation: The total energy of the isolated system is constant: ΔUtotal​=0. Since ΔU=mcΔθ (no phase change), ∑mcθfinal​−∑mcθinitial​=0, which rearranges to the method of mixtures equation.
  • Finding an unknown specific heat: If you know mh​, ch​, θh​, mc​, θc​, and measure θeq​, you can solve for the unknown cc​: cc​=mc​(θeq​−θc​)mh​ch​(θh​−θeq​)​.
  • Finding an unknown mass: Similarly, if you know the specific heats but not the mass of one body, you can find the mass from the equilibrium temperature.
  • Finding the equilibrium temperature: θeq​=m1​c1​+m2​c2​m1​c1​θ1​+m2​c2​θ2​​. This is a weighted average of the initial temperatures, weighted by heat capacities.
  • Calorimeter setup: A typical calorimeter is a copper vessel (good conductor, low c) nested inside an insulating jacket (wood, cork, or air gap) to minimize heat loss. A stirrer ensures uniform temperature, and a thermometer measures the temperature.
  • Assumptions in the method: (1) No heat is lost to the surroundings (adiabatic). (2) Heat transfer is complete - all bodies reach the same temperature. (3) No phase change occurs. (4) The calorimeter is in thermal equilibrium with its contents at each reading.
  • Error sources: Heat loss to surroundings (always occurs to some degree) makes θeq​ slightly lower than ideal, leading to overestimation of c for the cold body. Radiation, convection, and conduction through supports all contribute.
  • Newton's cooling correction: To minimize heat-loss error, the experiment is often arranged so that the initial temperature excess above room temperature equals the final temperature deficit below room temperature. The gains and losses then approximately cancel.
  • NEB exam focus: Method of mixtures problems are extremely common. You MUST include the calorimeter's heat capacity (or water equivalent) in your equation. Forgetting it is the most common error.
  • Quick check: After solving for θeq​, verify that θh​>θeq​>θc​. If this is violated, you made an error in sign conventions.

Confusion

  • ❌ Heat lost + heat gained = 0, so m1​c1​Δθ1​+m2​c2​Δθ2​=0 means both Δθ are positive. ✅ Use signed convention: Δθ1​=θeq​−θh​<0 (negative, heat lost) and Δθ2​=θeq​−θc​>0 (positive, heat gained). The sum is zero.
  • ❌ You can ignore the calorimeter's heat capacity. ✅ The calorimeter is in thermal contact with both substances and reaches the same θeq​. Its heat capacity Ccal′​ can be significant - especially for thick or non-copper calorimeters.
  • ❌ The equilibrium temperature is the arithmetic mean of the two initial temperatures. ✅ Only true if m1​c1​=m2​c2​ (equal heat capacities). Generally: θeq​=m1​c1​+m2​c2​m1​c1​θ1​+m2​c2​θ2​​ - a WEIGHTED average.
  • ❌ Heat lost by hot body equals heat gained by cold body even if heat escapes to surroundings. ✅ This equality holds ONLY for an isolated (adiabatic) system. In practice, some heat is always lost, making the equation an approximation.
  • ❌ You need to know the specific heat of both substances to find the equilibrium temperature. ✅ You need m and c for both, OR you can work with heat capacities directly if they're given. If one c is unknown, you can find it using a known reference substance.
  • ❌ The method of mixtures works for gases. ✅ The method is designed for solids and liquids. Gases expand and do work, so cp​eqcv​ and the analysis is more complex. The method works best for incompressible substances.
  • ❌ Phase changes can be included in the method of mixtures using the same formula. ✅ Phase changes require H=mL instead of H=mcΔθ. If a phase change occurs, you must include the latent heat term separately.

Practice

  • A 0.2 kg copper calorimeter (c=390) contains 0.15 kg of water at 15°C. A 0.08 kg metal at 90°C is dropped in. Final temperature is 20°C. Find the metal's specific heat. Heat lost by metal: 0.08×cm​×70=5.6cm​. Heat gained: (0.15×4200+0.2×390)×5=(630+78)×5=3540. cm​=3540/5.6=632 J/kg⋅K (close to iron).
  • Find the equilibrium temperature when 0.5 kg of aluminum (c=900) at 100°C is mixed with 0.3 kg of water (c=4200) at 20°C, ignoring the calorimeter. θeq​=0.5×900+0.3×42000.5×900×100+0.3×4200×20​=450+126045000+25200​=171070200​=41.05°C.
  • A 0.1 kg block of lead (c=130) at 100°C is dropped into a 0.2 kg copper calorimeter containing 0.25 kg of water at 10°C. The calorimeter's water equivalent is 8 g. Find θeq​. Heat lost: 0.1×130×(100−T)=13(100−T). Heat gained: (0.25×4200+0.008×4200)×(T−10)=1092(T−10). 1300−13T=1092T−10920. 1105T=12220⇒T≈11.06°C.
  • 0.05 kg of a liquid at 60°C is mixed with 0.1 kg of water at 20°C in a calorimeter of water equivalent 5 g. The equilibrium is 30°C. Find the liquid's specific heat. Heat lost by liquid: 0.05×cl​×30=1.5cl​. Heat gained by water+cal: (0.1×4200+0.005×4200)×10=4410 J. cl​=4410/1.5=2940 J/kg⋅K (close to ethanol).
  • Why must the calorimeter be polished and placed in a lagging jacket? Polishing minimizes radiation heat loss (shiny surfaces reflect IR). Lagging (cork/wood) minimizes conduction/convection. Together they approximate an adiabatic system, making the method of mixtures accurate.
  • Two liquids of equal mass but different specific heats c1​ and c2​ are at temperatures θ1​ and θ2​. Show that the equilibrium temperature is θeq​=c1​+c2​c1​θ1​+c2​θ2​​. Since m1​=m2​=m: mc1​(θ1​−θeq​)=mc2​(θeq​−θ2​). Cancel m: c1​θ1​−c1​θeq​=c2​θeq​−c2​θ2​. θeq​(c1​+c2​)=c1​θ1​+c2​θ2​. ✓

Universal Facts

  • The calorimeter was invented by Joseph Black in the 1760s. He used an ice calorimeter - measuring heat by the amount of ice melted - which was more accurate than temperature-based methods because ice melts at a fixed temperature.
  • The principle of calorimetry is a special case of the conservation of energy. It was one of the first applications of energy conservation to thermal phenomena, predating the formal statement of the first law of thermodynamics by decades.
  • Modern calorimeters can measure heat changes as small as 10−6 J (microcalorimetry). These are used in biochemistry to study enzyme reactions, protein folding, and drug binding - processes that release or absorb tiny amounts of heat.
  • The "jump method" for measuring specific heat uses the method of mixtures: heat a small sample in an oil bath to a known temperature, then quickly transfer it to the calorimeter. The speed of transfer minimizes heat loss during the transfer.
  • Bomb calorimeters (used for food energy and fuel values) operate at constant volume, so the heat measured is ΔU, not ΔH. The water equivalent of a bomb calorimeter is typically 500-2000 g of water.
