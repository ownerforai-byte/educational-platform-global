Heat Capacity (Thermal Capacity, C')

Notes

  • Definition: Heat capacity (also called thermal capacity), denoted C′ or simply C, is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of an entire object (not a unit mass) by 1 K (or 1°C).
  • Formula: C′=m⋅c, where m is the mass of the object and c is the specific heat capacity of the material. Alternatively, C′=H/Δθ - heat required per degree of temperature rise.
  • Heat exchanged using C': H=C′⋅Δθ. This is simpler than H=mcΔθ when you already know the heat capacity of the object.
  • SI unit: J/K (joule per kelvin) or J/°C. Since a kelvin and a degree Celsius have the same size, J/K=J/°C numerically.
  • CGS unit: cal/°C (calorie per degree Celsius). Water's heat capacity is 1 cal/°C per gram, or 4.186 J/°C per gram.
  • Key distinction - C' vs c: c (specific heat) is an INTENSIVE property - it depends only on the MATERIAL (e.g., copper always has c=390 J/kg⋅K). C′ (heat capacity) is an EXTENSIVE property - it depends on both the material AND the mass of the object. A 2 kg copper block has C′=2×390=780 J/K.
  • Dimensional formula: [C′]=[M]⋅[L2T−2Θ−1]=[ML2T−2Θ−1]. Unlike c, the mass dimension [M] does NOT cancel.
  • Water equivalent connection: Heat capacity is directly related to water equivalent: C′=W⋅cw​, where W is the water equivalent mass and cw​ is water's specific heat. So C′=W×4186 J/K.
  • Calorimeter heat capacity: A calorimeter has its own heat capacity Ccal′​=mcal​⋅ccal​. In calorimetry problems, this must be included: Hgained​=(mliquid​cliquid​+Ccal′​)Δθliquid​.
  • Practical examples: A 500 g aluminum pot (c=900): C′=0.5×900=450 J/K. A 2 kg iron pan (c=450): C′=2×450=900 J/K. The iron pan needs more heat for the same temperature rise - despite iron's lower c, its larger mass wins.
  • Why it matters in experiments: When measuring specific heat using the method of mixtures, you must account for the heat capacity of the container. Neglecting Ccal′​ is a common source of error.
  • Thermal mass: In building physics
  • thermal mass" is the aggregate heat capacity of a building's materials (walls, floors, furniture). High thermal mass means the building heats and cools slowly - beneficial for passive solar design.
  • Comparison table: Substance | Mass | c (J/kg·K) | C' (J/K) Water (1 L) | 1 kg | 4200 | 4200 Aluminum pot (0.5 kg) | 0.5 kg | 900 | 450 Iron pan (2 kg) | 2 kg | 450 | 900 Lead weight (1 kg) | 1 kg | 130 | 130
  • NEB exam tip: Distinguish clearly between c (specific heat, per kg) and C′ (heat capacity, for the whole object). Many exam questions give C′ directly and ask you to find c, or vice versa. Remember: C′=mc and c=C′/m.
  • Relationship to Newton's law of cooling: In Newton's law, the cooling constant K is often written as K=hA/C′, where h is the surface heat transfer coefficient and A is the surface area. A larger C′ means slower cooling.

Confusion

  • ❌ Heat capacity and specific heat are the same thing. ✅ Heat capacity (C′) depends on the object's mass; specific heat (c) is a property of the material alone. C′=mc - they are related but not identical.
  • ❌ A larger object always has a larger heat capacity. ✅ For the SAME material, yes. But a small lead weight (c=130) may have a smaller C′ than a large aluminum block (c=900) even if the lead is more massive.
  • ❌ Heat capacity has no units. ✅ Heat capacity has units: J/K (SI) or cal/°C (CGS). Specific heat has units J/kg·K or cal/g·°C. The units are different by a factor of mass.
  • ❌ Heat capacity is the same for all objects of the same material. ✅ Heat capacity depends on BOTH material and mass. Two copper objects have the same c but different C′ if their masses differ.
  • ❌ The dimensional formula of heat capacity is [M0L2T−2Θ−1]. ✅ Heat capacity includes mass: [C′]=[ML2T−2Θ−1]. The [M] does NOT cancel because C′ depends on the amount of substance.
  • ❌ You can use C′ in place of c in all formulas. ✅ In H=mcΔθ, replace mc with C′: H=C′Δθ. But if you need c itself (e.g., to identify the material), you must divide: c=C′/m.
  • ❌ Water equivalent has units of J/K. ✅ Water equivalent (W) has units of MASS (kg or g). Heat capacity has units of J/K. They are related by C′=W⋅cw​, where cw​=4186 J/kg⋅K.

Practice

  • A 2 kg iron block (c=450 J/kg⋅K) is heated from 20°C to 80°C. Find its heat capacity and the heat required. C′=2×450=900 J/K. H=900×60=54,000 J=54 kJ.
  • An object has heat capacity 840 J/K. How much heat raises its temperature by 15°C? H=C′Δθ=840×15=12,600 J=12.6 kJ.
  • A calorimeter has mass 0.05 kg and c=400 J/kg⋅K. Find its heat capacity and water equivalent. C′=0.05×400=20 J/K. W=C′/cw​=20/4186=0.00478 kg=4.78 g.
  • Two objects A and B have the same heat capacity. Object A is 0.5 kg of aluminum (c=900). Object B is 1.0 kg of an unknown material. Find B's specific heat. CA′​=0.5×900=450 J/K. Since CB′​=CA′​: 1.0×cB​=450⇒cB​=450 J/kg⋅K (iron).
  • A copper calorimeter (m=0.1 kg, c=390) contains 0.2 kg of water. Find the total heat capacity of the system. Ccal′​=0.1×390=39 J/K. Cwater′​=0.2×4200=840 J/K. Ctotal′​=39+840=879 J/K.
  • An object's temperature rises by 10 K when 2000 J of heat is supplied. Find its heat capacity, water equivalent, and the mass if it's made of copper (c=390). C′=2000/10=200 J/K. W=200/4186=0.0478 kg=47.8 g. m=C′/c=200/390=0.513 kg.

Universal Facts

  • Heat capacity is sometimes called "thermal mass" in engineering. A brick wall has high thermal mass - it absorbs heat during the day and releases it at night, moderating indoor temperatures.
  • The concept of heat capacity was developed by Joseph Black in the 1760s. He realized that "heat content
  • (what he called
  • quantity of heat
  • ) is different from
  • temperature" - two objects at the same temperature can have different heat capacities.
  • In chemistry, the molar heat capacity (Cm​) is more useful than mass heat capacity. For gases, Cp​ (constant pressure) and Cv​ (constant volume) are both molar heat capacities and differ by R=8.314 J/mol⋅K.
  • Phase changes involve enormous heat capacities in a sense - during melting, temperature doesn't change despite continuous heat input, so C′=H/Δθ→∞. This is why the concept of heat capacity breaks down at phase transitions.
  • The heat capacity of the Earth's oceans (∼1.4×1021 J/K) is so large that absorbing 1 W per square meter of ocean surface raises the ocean temperature by only about 0.02 K per century - yet this small change drives global sea level rise through thermal expansion.
