Water Equivalent (W) — Definition & Derivation

Notes

  • Definition of water equivalent W: The mass of water that would require the same amount of heat as a given body to raise its temperature by 1∘C (or 1 K). It is the thermal capacity of the body expressed in mass units of water.
  • Thermal capacity (heat capacity): C=mc — the heat required to raise the temperature of a body by 1∘C. Water equivalent W is defined so that W×cw​=m×c, therefore W=cw​m⋅c​, where cw​=4200 J/kg⋅K or 1 cal/g⋅°C.
  • Physical meaning: A calorimeter with water equivalent W=20 g absorbs as much heat when warming by 1∘C as 20 g of water. The calorimeter's own mass mcal​ and its specific heat ccal​ contribute: W=cw​mcal​ccal​​.
  • Derivation: For a body of mass m, specific heat c, raising temperature by ΔT: heat gained H=mcΔT. For W grams of water gaining same heat: H=W⋅cw​⋅ΔT. Equating: mcΔT=Wcw​ΔT⇒W=cw​mc​.
  • Units: Since c has units J/kg⋅K or cal/g⋅°C, and m has kg or g, W has units of kg or g (mass). Note: W is not a heat capacity itself — it is a mass equivalent.
  • For calorimeter: Wcal​=cw​mcal​ccal​​. For thin copper calorimeter: cCu​≈390 J/kg⋅K, cw​=4186 J/kg⋅K, so WCu​≈0.093mCu​. A 50 g copper calorimeter has W≈4.7 g.
  • Why water as reference? Water has the highest specific heat of common liquids (cw​=4186 J/kg⋅K at 20∘C). Using water as the reference makes W directly comparable to actual water mass — physical meaning is intuitive.
  • Effective heat capacity in calorimetry: When a calorimeter contains liquid of mass ml​, specific heat cl​, and water equivalent W, the total effective heat capacity is Ceff​=ml​cl​+Wcw​. This is used in both method of mixtures and method of cooling.
  • In method of cooling: Formula (m1​c1​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​c2​+Wcw​)/t2​ accounts for calorimeter heat capacity. If W is neglected, calculated c2​ has systematic error.
  • Finding W experimentally: (1) Cool known mass of water from T1​ to T2​, measure time t1​. (2) Cool same calorimeter with different mass of water, measure t2​. (3) Solve two equations: (m1​cw​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​cw​+Wcw​)/t2​. This eliminates k and gives W.
  • Dimensional analysis: [W]=[M]. [mc/cw​]=[Q][Θ]−1[M]⋅[Q][Θ]−1​=[M]. Dimensionally consistent.
  • NEB exam focus: Define W, derive W=mc/cw​, calculate W for given calorimeter, include W in calorimetry equations, distinguish between water equivalent and actual mass of calorimeter.
  • Connection to heat capacity: Heat capacity C=mc. Since cw​=1 cal/g⋅°C in CGS, W=mc numerically equals the heat capacity in calories per degree. For any body in SI: C=W×4.2 J/°C.
  • Applications: Calorimetry (all types),热水器 design (W concept used in thermal mass calculations), building thermal mass (W of walls/furniture affects room temperature stability), spacecraft thermal control.
  • Special cases: For ideal gas in bomb calorimeter (constant volume): CV​ used instead of c. For phase change calorimetry: effective W includes latent heat contributions. For food calorimetry: W of bomb calorimeter includes ignition wire, crucible.

Confusion

  • Water equivalent W is the mass of water in the calorimeter — W is a calculated mass-equivalent, not actual water present. For a dry copper calorimeter of mass 50 g, W≈4.7 g. No water is inside.
  • Water equivalent and heat capacity are the same thing — Heat capacity C=mc has units J/°C. Water equivalent W=mc/cw​ has units of mass (g or kg). They are related by C=W×cw​, but numerically different in SI.
  • The water equivalent of a calorimeter is constant — W=mcal​ccal​/cw​. Since cw​ is nearly constant, W is constant for a given calorimeter. But cw​ itself varies with temperature (4.186→4.219 J/g⋅K), so W varies slightly.
  • A heavier calorimeter always has larger W — Not always. W∝m×ccal​. Aluminum (c≈900) has higher specific heat per unit mass than copper (c≈390). So an aluminum calorimeter has higher W per unit mass than copper.
  • Water equivalent is used only in method of cooling — W appears in all calorimetry calculations: method of mixtures, method of cooling, bomb calorimetry, and steam calorimetry. Any time a calorimeter absorbs heat, W must be included.
  • The formula W=mc/cw​ means the body behaves like mc/cw​ grams of water — Yes, this is exactly the physical meaning. A 100 g copper block (c=390) has W=100×390/4186=9.3 g — it behaves thermally like 9.3 g of water.
  • Water equivalent can be negative — W is always positive since m>0, c>0, cw​>0. However, in some contexts (endothermic reactions in calorimeter) the effective W from calibration includes correction factors.
  • All calorimeters have negligible W — Thin copper calorimeters have small W≈0.1m. But thick brass calorimeters, massive aluminum blocks, or calorimeters with large stirrers can have W comparable to liquid mass (∼20%–50% of liquid mass).
  • Water equivalent is independent of temperature — cw​ varies by ∼1% from 0∘C to 100∘C. For precise work, use cw​ at the average temperature of the experiment. In school physics, 4.2 J/g⋅K is standard.
  • Water equivalent is measured in liters — Since density of water ≈1 g/mL, 1000 g=1 L. But W is defined in mass units, not volume. A W=50 g means thermal capacity equivalent to 50 mL of water.

Practice

  • A copper calorimeter weighs 60 g. Specific heat of copper =390 J/kg⋅K. Find its water equivalent. If filled with 150 g of water, what is total effective thermal capacity? [Solution: Wcal​=mcal​ccal​/cw​=0.06×390/4186=0.00559 kg=5.59 g. Total Ceff​=mw​cw​+Wcw​=(150+5.59)×4.186=651.3 J/°C or Ceff​=ml​cl​+Wcal​cw​=150×4.186+5.59×4.186=651.3 J/°C.]
  • A calorimeter of water equivalent 12 g contains 80 g of water at 20∘C. A hot metal piece (mass 50 g, c=400 J/kg⋅K) at 100∘C is dropped in. Find final equilibrium temperature. [Solution: Heat lost by metal = heat gained by water + calorimeter. 50×0.4×(100−T)=(80+12)×4.2×(T−20). 20(100−T)=92×4.2×(T−20). 2000−20T=386.4T−7728. 406.4T=9728⇒T=23.94∘C.]
  • Two calorimeters A and B have water equivalents WA​=8 g and WB​=15 g. Both contain 100 g of water at 25∘C. Equal masses (50 g) of a hot substance (c=2000 J/kg⋅K) at 100∘C are added to each. Will final temperatures differ? [Solution: A: effective mass =100+8=108 g. B: 100+15=115 g. 50×2(100−TA​)=108×4.2×(TA​−25)⇒TA​=36.5∘C. B: 100−TB​=115×2.1×(TB​−25)/50⇒TB​=35.8∘∘C. Yes, B has slightly lower final T because larger W absorbs more heat.]
  • How would you determine the water equivalent of a calorimeter experimentally using the method of cooling? [Solution: (1) Fill calorimeter with mass m1​ of water, cool from T1​ to T2​, record time t1​. (2) Repeat with mass m2​ of water, same T1​→T2​, record t2​. (3) Method of cooling: (m1​cw​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​cw​+Wcw​)/t2​. (4) Solve: W=cw​(m2​t1​−m1​t2​)/(t1​−t2​).]
  • A calorimeter of mass 100 g and c=900 J/kg⋅K is used. Calculate W. Compare with a copper calorimeter of same mass (c=390 J/kg⋅K). [Solution: Aluminum: WAl​=100×900/4186=21.5 g. Copper: WCu​=100×390/4186=9.3 g. Same mass, aluminum has W 2.3× larger because cAl​>cCu​.]
  • In a method of cooling experiment, the formula t1​(m1​+W)cw​​=t2​(m2​+W)cw​​ is used. If m1​=100 g, m2​=120 g, t1​=5 min, t2​=4 min, find W. [Solution: (100+W)/5=(120+W)/4⇒4(100+W)=5(120+W)⇒400+4W=600+5W⇒W=−200 g. Negative W is physically impossible — indicates experimental error, non-Newtonian cooling, or wrong assumptions. Recheck measurements.]
  • Steam at 100∘C is passed into a calorimeter of water equivalent 15 g containing 200 g of water at 20∘C. The mass of condensed steam is 5 g. Find final temperature. (Latent heat of steam L=540 cal/g.) [Solution: Heat from condensing steam: 5×540=2700 cal. Heat from cooling condensed water (100→T): 5×(100−T) cal. Heat gained by water + calorimeter: (200+15)(T−20) cal. 2700+5(100−T)=215(T−20)⇒2700+500−5T=215T−4300⇒220T=7500⇒T=34.1∘C.]
  • A calorimeter with stirrer (W=10 g) contains 150 g of water at 15∘C. When 100 g of a substance at 80∘C (c=800 J/kg⋅K) is added, equilibrium is 25∘C. Find W again using this data. [Solution: Heat lost by substance: 0.1×800×55=4400 J. Heat gained by water+calorimeter: (0.15×4200+W×4200)(10)=(630+4200W)×10=6300+42000W J. 4400=6300+42000W⇒W=−0.0452 kg=−45.2 g. Negative! Suggests heat loss to surroundings, incomplete mixing, or wrong c.]
  • State the physical meaning of water equivalent. A calorimeter of mass 80 g has water equivalent 7.5 g. Find specific heat of calorimeter material. [Solution: Physical meaning: The mass of water that would absorb the same heat as the calorimeter for the same temperature rise. W=mcal​ccal​/cw​⇒7.5=80×ccal​/4.2⇒ccal​=7.5×4.2/80=0.394 J/g⋅K=394 J/kg⋅K. Material is likely copper or brass.]
  • Two calorimeters of water equivalents W1​=10 g and W2​=20 g contain equal masses of water. The same hot solid is added to each. Show that the temperature rise is inversely proportional to (m+W). [Solution: Heat lost by solid Q=ms​cs​(Ts​−T). Heat gained: Q=(mw​+W)cw​(T−Ti​). T−Ti​=cw​(mw​+W)ms​cs​​(Ts​−T). Since ms​,cs​,Ts​,T are same for both, (T−Ti​)(mw​+W)=constant. Thus (T−Ti​)∝1/(mw​+W). Larger W → smaller ΔT.]

Universal Facts

  • Historical: The concept of water equivalent was introduced by Joseph Black (1760s), who coined 'latent heat' and made precise calorimetry measurements. Black's water equivalent was the basis for the calorie definition — 1 calorie = heat to raise 1 g of water by 1∘C.
  • The calorie (cal) is defined relative to water: 1 cal = heat to raise 1 g of water by 1∘C at 14.5∘C. This makes cw​=1 cal/g⋅°C by definition. In SI, 1 cal=4.184 J (thermochemical), 4.1868 J (international).
  • NEB exam tip: Many calorimetry problems in NEB give W directly. If not given and calorimeter is mentioned, assume W is negligible unless specified otherwise, or estimate W≈0.1mcal​ for thin copper calorimeter.
  • Real-world: Solar thermal systems use water equivalent to size thermal storage tanks. The thermal mass of a building (walls, furniture, water) is expressed as 'liters of water equivalent' — how many liters of water would provide the same temperature stability.
  • Connection to specific heat: Materials with high specific heat (water, concrete, brick) have large W per unit mass. This is why coastal climates are milder — water's high c moderates temperature. Desert sand has low c≈800 J/kg⋅K, causing extreme day/night swings.
  • Calorimeter materials: Copper (c≈390) has low W per unit mass — ideal for sensitive calorimetry. Aluminum (c≈900) has moderate W. Glass (c≈800) has higher W. Stainless steel (c≈500) is common for lab calorimeters.
  • Advanced: Mixing calorimeters use the method of mixtures with W correction. Bomb calorimeters (constant volume) use CV​ instead of cp​. Adiabatic calorimeters minimize heat loss (effectively W of surroundings = 0). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measures W and phase changes precisely.
  • NEB practical tip: In the calorimetry practical, always tabulate: mass of empty calorimeter (m1​), mass with water (m2​), mass of water (mw​=m2​−m1​), mass of unknown solid/liquid, initial temperatures, final temperature. Calculate heat gained and lost, then c.
