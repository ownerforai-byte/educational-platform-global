Measuring Cooling Rate in the Lab (Time-Based 'Method of Cooling')

Relevance

Notes

  • The method of cooling experiment measures specific heat capacity by comparing the time taken for two liquids to cool through the same temperature range under identical conditions.
  • Newton's law: dtdT​=−k(T−T0​). For a body of mass m and specific heat c, heat loss rate is dtdQ​=mcdtdT​=−mck(T−T0​).
  • In the lab, we cannot measure dT/dt continuously with a stopwatch. Instead, we measure the time t for temperature to fall through a fixed interval ΔT (e.g., 60∘C→50∘C). Average cooling rate ≈tΔT​.
  • For two liquids in the same calorimeter (same k, same surface area, same surroundings), the heat loss rates are equal at the same temperature: m1​c1​t1​ΔT​=m2​c2​t2​ΔT​.
  • Since ΔT is identical, it cancels out, giving the practical formula: t1​m1​c1​​=t2​m2​c2​​ or c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​.
  • Apparatus: Calorimeter (thin copper cup) with stirrer and lid, thermometer (0ext−−100∘C, least count 0.5∘C or 0.1∘C), stopwatch, water (reference liquid), unknown liquid, burner/heater, stand.
  • Procedure: (1) Weigh empty calorimeter →mcal​. (2) Fill with known volume of water → find mass mw​. (3) Heat to ∼70∘C. (4) Place in constant-temperature enclosure (or still air). (5) Stir gently, record temperature every 30 s or 1 min. (6) Note time tw​ for 60∘C→50∘C. (7) Repeat with same volume of unknown liquid →ml​, tl​. (8) Calculate cl​.
  • Why same volume? Same volume → same liquid height → same surface area exposed → same k. If masses differ due to density, use m1​, m2​ in formula.
  • Stirring: Essential to maintain uniform temperature throughout liquid. Without stirring, surface cools faster than bulk → non-uniform T → Newton's law invalid.
  • Surroundings: Must be constant temperature. Best done in a double-walled vessel (water jacket) or a large room with stable temperature. Drafts cause forced convection → k changes.
  • Temperature range: Choose range where T−T0​<30∘C (e.g., 60→50, 50→40 if T0​≈25∘C). Ensures linear approximation of radiation holds.
  • Water equivalent of calorimeter: If calorimeter absorbs significant heat, effective thermal capacity is (ml​cl​+Wcw​). Formula becomes tw​mw​cw​+Wcw​​=tl​ml​cl​+Wcw​​. W found by separate experiment (cooling empty calorimeter + water).
  • Graphical analysis: Plot T vs t for both liquids. Draw tangents at same T to find (dT/dt)1​, (dT/dt)2​. Or plot ln(T−T0​) vs t — slope =−k. Verify k matches for both runs.
  • Sources of error: (1) Heat loss by radiation not purely Newtonian, (2) Convection currents in room, (3) Thermometer lag, (4) Incomplete stirring, (5) Evaporation (especially volatile liquids), (6) Calorimeter W not accounted, (7) Temperature range too large.
  • Accuracy: Typically ±5ext−−10%. For better accuracy: use oil bath for constant T0​, electrical heating for precise initial T, digital thermometer with data logging.
  • NEB practical exam: Common questions: 'Describe the method of cooling experiment.' 'Why same calorimeter?' 'Why stir?' 'Why small temperature range?' 'How to find water equivalent?' 'Plot cooling curve and find k.'
  • Alternative: Continuous cooling curve. Record T(t) for both liquids. Fit T(t)=T0​+(Ti​−T0​)e−kt. Extract k and Ti​. If k identical, ratio of initial slopes gives c ratio.

Confusion

  • We measure cooling rate dT/dt directly with a stopwatch — We measure time t for a fixed ΔT, then average rate =ΔT/t. Instantaneous dT/dt requires continuous recording or tangent method.
  • The formula m1​c1​/t1​=m2​c2​/t2​ works for any temperature range — Only valid for small ΔT where Newton's law holds (T−T0​<30∘C). Large range → k not constant → formula fails.
  • Stirring is optional — Stirring is mandatory. Without it, temperature is not uniform, T in Newton's law is undefined (surface vs bulk), and cooling is non-exponential.
  • Water equivalent W is negligible for all calorimeters — For thin copper cups, W≈0.1mcal​ may be small. For thick brass/aluminum, W is significant. Always check if given in problem.
  • The method works for any two liquids — Liquids must not react with calorimeter, not evaporate significantly, and have similar viscosity (affects convection). Mercury, acids, volatile organics are problematic.
  • Initial temperature must be exactly the same for both runs — Temperature range (e.g., 60→50) must be same. Initial Ti​ can differ slightly if T0​ constant, but same Ti​ ensures identical k(T−T0​) profile.
  • Cooling time t is inversely proportional to specific heat c — From c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​, if m1​=m2​, c2​∝t2​. But this is for fixed ΔT, not total cooling time to room temperature.
  • The calorimeter's water equivalent is the same as its mass — W=mcal​ccal​/cw​. For copper (ccal​≈390 J/kg⋅K), W≈0.093mcal​. For aluminum (ccal​≈900), W≈0.21mcal​.

Practice

  • In a method of cooling experiment, 100 g of water in a calorimeter (water equivalent 10 g) takes 4 minutes to cool from 60∘C to 50∘C. When replaced with 120 g of an unknown liquid, the time is 3 minutes. Find specific heat of liquid. [Solution: (mw​cw​+Wcw​)/tw​=(ml​cl​+Wcw​)/tl​. (100×4.2+10×4.2)/4=(120×cl​+10×4.2)/3⇒115.5=40cl​+14⇒cl​=2.5375 J/g⋅K=2538 J/kg⋅K]
  • A student performs the method of cooling experiment. Water (mass 80 g, cw​=4.2 J/g⋅K) cools from 65∘C to 55∘C in 180 s. The same calorimeter with 100 g of oil cools through the same range in 120 s. Water equivalent of calorimeter is 5 g. Find c of oil. [Solution: (80×4.2+5×4.2)/180=(100×c+5×4.2)/120⇒1.9833=(100c+21)/120⇒238=100c+21⇒c=2.17 J/g⋅K=2170 J/kg⋅K]
  • Equal volumes (100 mL) of water and an unknown liquid are used. Density of water =1 g/mL, density of liquid =0.8 g/mL. Water cools 60→50∘C in 5 min; liquid in 3 min. Water equivalent of calorimeter =8 g. Find c of liquid. [Solution: mw​=100 g, ml​=80 g. (100×4.2+8×4.2)/5=(80×c+8×4.2)/3⇒90.72=(80c+33.6)/3⇒272.16=80c+33.6⇒c=2.982 J/g⋅K=2982 J/kg⋅K]
  • Why must the calorimeter be the same for both liquids? [Solution: Cooling constant k=hA/(mc) depends on surface area A, heat transfer coefficient h, and total thermal capacity mc. Same calorimeter → same A, same h (same surface), same surroundings → same k. Different calorimeters → different k → formula invalid.]
  • A student forgets to stir the liquid during cooling. How does this affect the result? [Solution: Without stirring, temperature gradient develops — surface cools faster than bulk. Measured T (thermometer at center) lags behind surface T. Cooling appears slower → measured t larger → calculated c larger than true value. Also, cooling curve deviates from exponential.]
  • In an experiment, the cooling curve (T vs t) for water is plotted. The tangent at 55∘C gives slope −0.5∘C/min. For the same calorimeter with unknown liquid, tangent at 55∘C gives slope −0.8∘C/min. Masses: water 100 g, liquid 120 g. Water equivalent 5 g. Find c of liquid. [Solution: At same T, (mw​cw​+Wcw​)(dT/dt)w​=(ml​cl​+Wcw​)(dT/dt)l​. (100×4.2+5×4.2)(0.5)=(120cl​+5×4.2)(0.8)⇒220.5=96cl​+16.8⇒cl​=2.122 J/g⋅K]
  • Explain how to determine the water equivalent W of a calorimeter experimentally. [Solution: (1) Cool calorimeter with known mass of water m1​ through range ΔT, measure t1​. (2) Cool calorimeter with different mass of water m2​ through same ΔT, measure t2​. (3) (m1​cw​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​cw​+Wcw​)/t2​. Solve for W. Or: cool empty calorimeter (mass mcal​, known ccal​) — not practical as ccal​ may be unknown.]
  • The temperature of surroundings T0​=25∘C. A liquid cools from 70∘C to 60∘C in 2 min. Is Newton's law applicable? [Solution: Average T−T0​=65−25=40∘C>30∘C. Newton's law not strictly applicable. Radiation ∝T4−T04​ is non-linear. Error ∼10ext−−15%. Better range: 55→45∘C (avg T−T0​=25∘C).]
  • Two liquids A and B have cooling times tA​=4 min, tB​=3 min for same ΔT. Same masses, same calorimeter. If cA​=3000 J/kg⋅K, find cB​. [Solution: mcA​/tA​=mcB​/tB​⇒cB​=cA​(tB​/tA​)=3000×(3/4)=2250 J/kg⋅K]
  • Derive the formula for specific heat by method of cooling starting from dQ/dt=−kA(T−T0​) and dQ=mcdT. [Solution: mcdtdT​=−kA(T−T0​). For same calorimeter, kA same. At same T: m1​c1​(dT/dt)1​=m2​c2​(dT/dt)2​. Average over ΔT: m1​c1​t1​ΔT​=m2​c2​t2​ΔT​⇒c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​.]

Universal Facts

  • Historical: The method of cooling was first used by Joseph Black (1728–1799) in the 1760s to measure specific heats of liquids. Black also discovered latent heat of fusion and vaporization, founding quantitative thermal physics.
  • The method is a null method in principle — it compares ratios without measuring absolute heat. This cancels systematic errors in thermometer calibration, heat losses to surroundings (if identical), and timer calibration.
  • NEB practical exam: This experiment appears in the Physics Practical syllabus for Class 11. Typical marks distribution: Procedure (3), Observation table (4), Calculation (3), Graph (2), Precautions (2), Viva (1). Know the precautions by heart.
  • Connection to thermal conductivity: The cooling constant k=mcdKA​ for conduction through calorimeter wall (thickness d, conductivity K), plus convection/radiation terms. Same calorimeter → same k is the core assumption.
  • Real-world application: Food industry uses cooling curve analysis to determine thermal properties of liquid foods (soups, sauces, dairy). Pharmaceuticals use it for quality control of syrups. Forensic science uses body cooling (algor mortis) with similar principles.
  • Exam tip: If water equivalent W is not given in a numerical problem, assume W=0 and state it. If W is given, always include it in both sides of the equation. Missing W is the most common error.
  • Advanced technique: Regnault-Pfaundler method uses three cooling curves — empty calorimeter, calorimeter+water, calorimeter+liquid — to simultaneously eliminate k and W without needing to know either. Used in precision thermometry.
  • Limitation: Method fails for liquids that evaporate rapidly (alcohol, ether) — evaporative cooling adds non-Newtonian heat loss. Also fails for viscous liquids (glycerin) where internal convection is suppressed, altering k.
