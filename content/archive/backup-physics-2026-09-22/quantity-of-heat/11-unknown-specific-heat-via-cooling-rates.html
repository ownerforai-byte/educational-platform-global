Finding an Unknown Specific Heat Capacity by Comparing Cooling Rates

Notes

  • Newton's law of cooling states dtdT​=−k(T−T0​), where k is a constant depending on surface area, nature of surface, and surrounding medium. For two bodies of different materials but identical shape, size, and surface conditions cooling in the same surroundings, the cooling constant k is the same.
  • Rate of heat loss: dtdQ​=−mcdtdT​. Under identical cooling conditions (same T, same T0​, same k), the cooling rates are proportional to mc: m1​c1​(dtdT​)1​=m2​c2​(dtdT​)2​.
  • This eliminates the need to measure heat energy in joules — we only compare cooling rates (temperature drop per unit time). The unknown specific heat c2​ is found using a reference liquid of known c1​ (usually water, cw​=4200 J/kg⋅K or 1 cal/g⋅°C).
  • Formula: c2​=m2​m1​c1​​×(dT/dt)2​(dT/dt)1​​=m2​m1​c1​​×t1​t2​​, where t1​ and t2​ are times for the same temperature drop ΔT.
  • Key assumptions: (1) Same calorimeter/cup used for both liquids, (2) Same volume of liquid (same mass if densities differ, account for it), (3) Same initial temperature and surroundings, (4) Temperature range small enough for Newton's law to hold (<30∘C difference), (5) No phase change occurs.
  • In practice, cooling rate is measured as time for temperature to fall through a fixed range (e.g., 60∘C→50∘C). Since ΔT is constant, dtdT​∝t1​. So c2​=m2​t1​m1​c1​t2​​.
  • Water as reference: Water's specific heat is exceptionally well-known (cw​=4186 J/kg⋅K at 20∘C), making it ideal. For oils or organic liquids, c typically ranges 1500ext−−2500 J/kg⋅K.
  • Solid samples: For solids, the method uses a calorimeter with the solid immersed in a known mass of water. The solid's cooling curve is compared with water's. More commonly, method of mixtures is used for solids; method of cooling is primarily for liquids.
  • Dimensional check: [c]=L2T−2Θ−1. RHS: [M][M]⋅L2T−2Θ−1​=L2T−2Θ−1. Dimensionally consistent.
  • Limitations: Accuracy depends on how closely 'identical conditions' are met. Slight differences in surface emissivity, convection currents, or calorimeter heat capacity introduce error. Typically ±5ext−−10% error.
  • Calorimeter water equivalent: If the calorimeter cup absorbs significant heat, include its water equivalent W: (m1​c1​+Wcw​)/t1​=(m2​c2​+Wcw​)/t2​. For thin copper calorimeters, W is often negligible.
  • Graphical method: Plot ln(T−T0​) vs t for both liquids. Slope =−k. If slopes match, k is identical. Then compare intercepts or use area under curve for more precision.
  • NEB exam focus: Numerical problems give m1​, c1​, m2​, t1​, t2​ → find c2​. Also conceptual: 'Why must temperature range be small?' 'Why same calorimeter?' 'Why same volume?'
  • Connection to Stefan-Boltzmann: Newton's law is the linear approximation of radiative cooling P=eσA(T4−T04​) for small ΔT: T4−T04​≈4T03​(T−T0​), giving k∝T03​.
  • Real-world application: Quality control in food industry — comparing cooling rates of oils to detect adulteration. Engine coolant testing. Forensic estimation of time of death (body cooling).

Confusion

  • Cooling rate (dT/dt) is the same as cooling constant (k) — Cooling rate changes with temperature; k is constant for a given setup. The law says dT/dt=−k(T−T0​), so rate depends on instantaneous temperature difference.
  • Any two liquids can be compared directly — They must be in the same calorimeter, same volume, same initial temperature, same surroundings. Different containers change surface area and k.
  • The method works for large temperature differences — Newton's law is valid only for small ΔT (typically <30∘C). For large ΔT, radiation dominates and cooling is not exponential.
  • Specific heat of water is exactly 1 cal/g⋅°C at all temperatures — cw​ varies slightly with temperature (4.186 J/g⋅K at 20∘C, 4.219 at 0∘C). For school physics, 4.2 J/g⋅K or 1 cal/g⋅°C is standard.
  • Time t is directly proportional to specific heat c — From c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​, if m1​=m2​ and c1​ known, c2​∝t2​. But t is the time for a fixed ΔT, not total cooling time.
  • Water equivalent W is the same as mass of calorimeter — W=mcal​ccal​/cw​. For copper (ccal​≈390 J/kg⋅K), W≈0.093×mcal​. Not equal to mass.
  • Method of cooling replaces method of mixtures — Method of mixtures is more accurate for solids. Method of cooling is specifically for liquids where mixing would cause confusion or reaction.
  • The formula m1​c1​/t1​=m2​c2​/t2​ is exact — It assumes identical k, negligible calorimeter heat capacity, constant c over the range, and no heat loss other than through the calorimeter walls. Real experiments have ∼5% error.

Practice

  • 50 g of water in a copper calorimeter (water equivalent 10 g) cools from 60∘C to 50∘C in 3 min. The same calorimeter with 50 g of an unknown liquid cools through the same range in 2 min. Find c of the liquid. [Solution: (mw​cw​+Wcw​)/tw​=(ml​cl​+Wcw​)/tl​. (50×1+10×1)/3=(50×cl​+10×1)/2⇒60/3=(50cl​+10)/2⇒20=25cl​+5⇒cl​=0.6 cal/g⋅°C=2510 J/kg⋅K]
  • 100 g of water cools from 70∘C to 60∘C in 4 minutes. 120 g of an unknown liquid in the same vessel cools through the same range in 3 minutes. If water equivalent of vessel is 8 g, find specific heat of liquid. [Solution: (100×4.2+8×4.2)/4=(120×c+8×4.2)/3⇒113.4=40c+11.2⇒c=2.555 J/g⋅K=2555 J/kg⋅K]
  • Equal masses of water and an unknown liquid are cooled in identical calorimeters under identical conditions. The water takes 5 minutes to cool from 60∘C to 50∘C; the liquid takes 3 minutes. Find c of liquid. [Solution: mcw​/tw​=mcl​/tl​⇒cl​=cw​(tl​/tw​)=4200×(3/5)=2520 J/kg⋅K]
  • In a method of cooling experiment, a student plots ln(T−T0​) vs t for water and an unknown liquid. The slopes are −0.025 min−1 and −0.024 min−1. The intercepts correspond to initial excess temperatures of 40∘C and 38∘C. Are conditions identical? What does the slope difference imply? [Solution: Slopes differ by 4% → cooling constant k differs. Conditions not perfectly identical — likely different surface emissivity or convection. Cannot reliably find c without correction.]
  • Why does the method of cooling require the same calorimeter for both liquids? [Solution: The cooling constant k depends on surface area, nature of surface, and surrounding medium. Using the same calorimeter ensures identical k, so the ratio of cooling rates directly gives the ratio of thermal capacities mc. Different calorimeters → different k → formula invalid.]
  • A liquid of mass 80 g cools from 65∘C to 55∘C in 150 s. Water of mass 100 g in the same calorimeter (water equivalent 5 g) cools through the same range in 180 s. Find specific heat of liquid in J/kg⋅K. [Solution: (mw​cw​+Wcw​)/tw​=(ml​cl​+Wcw​)/tl​. (100×4200+5×4200)/180=(80×cl​+5×4200)/150⇒2450=(80cl​+21000)/150⇒367500=80cl​+21000⇒cl​=4331 J/kg⋅K]
  • Explain why the method of cooling is unsuitable for finding specific heat of a metal solid. [Solution: Metals have high thermal conductivity — internal temperature gradients vanish quickly, but the calorimeter surface area and emissivity differ when a solid block replaces liquid. Also, method of mixtures is far more accurate for solids. Method of cooling assumes liquid convection inside calorimeter.]
  • In an experiment, water and an unknown liquid cool from 60∘C to 50∘C. Water takes 4 min, liquid takes 3 min. Masses are equal. The student forgets the calorimeter has water equivalent 12 g. If cw​=4200 J/kg⋅K, what % error in cl​? [Solution: True: (mcw​+12cw​)/4=(mcl​+12cw​)/3. Approx (ignoring W): mcw​/4=mcl′​/3⇒cl′​=0.75cw​=3150. True: (m+12)cw​/4=(mcl​+12cw​)/3. For m=100 g: 112×4200/4=117600=(100cl​+50400)/3⇒cl​=3024. Error = (3150−3024)/3024×100=4.17%]
  • Derive the formula c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​ starting from Newton's law dtdT​=−k(T−T0​). [Solution: dtdQ​=mcdtdT​. For two bodies: m1​c1​dtdT​=m2​c2​dtdT​ (same k, same T−T0​). Integrate over same ΔT: m1​c1​t1​ΔT​=m2​c2​t2​ΔT​⇒c2​=m2​m1​c1​​t1​t2​​.]
  • A calorimeter of water equivalent 15 g contains 200 g of water. It cools from 60∘C to 50∘C in 6 min. When filled with 180 g of an unknown liquid, it cools through the same range in 4 min. Find c of liquid. [Solution: (200×4.2+15×4.2)/6=(180×c+15×4.2)/4⇒151.2=45c+15.75⇒c=3.01 J/g⋅K=3010 J/kg⋅K]

Universal Facts

  • Historical: Newton's law of cooling (1701) was originally an empirical observation. The method of cooling for specific heat measurement was developed by Joseph Black (1760s), who also discovered latent heat. Black used this method to measure specific heats of various liquids.
  • The method of cooling is a comparative method — it does not measure absolute heat energy, only ratios. This makes it robust against systematic errors in temperature measurement (same thermometer used for both).
  • NEB exam tip: Always check if water equivalent W is given. If not mentioned, assume W=0. If given, use (mc+Wcw​) in numerator. Forgetting W is the #1 error in numerical problems.
  • Connection to thermal physics: The cooling constant k=mchA​ where h is heat transfer coefficient, A surface area. This shows why same m, same A, same h → same k. The method exploits this proportionality.
  • Real-world: Food safety — cooling curves of soups/sauces are monitored to ensure safe cooling rates (prevent bacterial growth). The method of cooling principles apply to designing cooling systems for electronics (heat sinks).
  • Exam pattern: NEB often asks 'State the assumptions of Newton's law of cooling' or 'Why must the temperature difference be small?' Answer: Linear approximation of T4−T04​; convection coefficient constant; no phase change; uniform temperature throughout body.
  • Comparison: Method of mixtures (calorimetry) measures heat exchange directly → more accurate (±1%). Method of cooling measures time ratios → less accurate (±5ext−−10%) but simpler for liquids that might react or mix.
  • Advanced: For precise work, the Regnault-Pfaundler method uses cooling curves of the empty calorimeter, calorimeter+water, and calorimeter+liquid to eliminate k and W simultaneously. This is beyond NEB but used in research.
