Notes

  • Convection: Heat transfer by the bulk movement of a fluid (liquid or gas). Unlike conduction (molecular collisions), convection involves the physical transport of fluid molecules from one location to another, carrying thermal energy with them.
  • Mechanism of convection: When a fluid is heated, it expands, becomes less dense, and rises. The cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace it, creating a circulation pattern called a convection cell. This continuous circulation transfers heat efficiently.
  • Natural (free) convection: Fluid motion is driven solely by buoyancy forces arising from density differences due to temperature gradients. Examples: warm air rising from a radiator, sea breezes, magma circulation in the Earth's mantle.
  • Forced convection: Fluid is driven by an external agent — a pump, fan, blower, or wind — rather than buoyancy alone. Forced convection rates are typically 5-20 times higher than natural convection for the same geometry because the flow velocity is much greater.
  • Newton's law of cooling (convection form): H=hAΔT, where h is the convective heat transfer coefficient (W/(m²·K)), A is the surface area, and ΔT is the temperature difference between the surface and the fluid. Typical values: natural convection air ~5-25, forced convection air ~10-200, water ~50-10,000.
  • Convection currents in the atmosphere: Solar heating of the Earth's surface creates convection currents in the troposphere. Warm air rises at the equator, cools at the poles, and sinks back — creating global circulation patterns that drive weather systems.
  • Why radiators are placed near the floor: Hot air from the radiator rises (because it is less dense), drawing cooler air in from below. This creates a continuous convection current that circulates warm air throughout the room. Placing radiators high up would trap hot air at the ceiling.
  • Land and sea breezes: During the day, land heats faster than water. Warm air over land rises, and cooler maritime air flows in (sea breeze). At night, land cools faster than water. Warm air from the sea moves inland (land breeze).
  • Boiling and convection: When water is heated in a pot, natural convection currents distribute heat until boiling begins. Once boiling starts, the process shifts primarily to phase-change heat transfer (much more efficient), which is why boiling water maintains a uniform temperature.
  • Application — cooling towers: Power plants use cooling towers where warm water is sprayed into the air. Natural convection carries the warm, moist air upward, and evaporation cools the water. The cooled water is recirculated back to the plant.
  • Application — computer cooling: CPU heatsinks use forced convection (fans) to move air across finned aluminum surfaces. The high surface area of fins combined with forced airflow provides efficient heat removal. Some systems use liquid cooling (water blocks) for even better forced convection.
  • Application — HVAC: Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems use forced convection to distribute conditioned air throughout buildings. Supply vents direct warm/cool air, and return vents bring it back for reconditioning.
  • Application — cooking: Convection ovens use a fan to force hot air around food, providing uniform and faster cooking than conventional ovens. Natural convection in pots and pans distributes heat from the bottom to the top of the liquid.
  • NEB exam focus: Differentiate between natural and forced convection. Apply Newton's law of cooling. Explain convection in everyday situations (radiator placement, sea breezes, boiling). Compare convective heat transfer coefficients for different fluids.
  • Boussinesq approximation: In natural convection analysis, density variations are neglected except where they appear in the buoyancy term: ρ≈ρ0​[1−β(T−T0​)], where β is the thermal expansion coefficient. This simplifies the Navier-Stokes equations while preserving the driving mechanism.
  • Grashof number: Gr=ν2gβΔTL3​, where g is gravitational acceleration, β is thermal expansion coefficient, ΔT is temperature difference, L is characteristic length, and ν is kinematic viscosity. The Grashof number compares buoyancy to viscous forces — it determines whether convection is laminar or turbulent.

Confusion

  • ❌ Convection can occur in solids. ✅ Convection ONLY occurs in fluids (liquids and gases) because only fluids can flow. Solids cannot have bulk motion — they only conduct heat.
  • ❌ Convection is the same as conduction. ✅ Conduction transfers heat through molecular collisions without bulk motion. Convection transfers heat through the PHYSICAL MOVEMENT of the fluid itself. They are fundamentally different mechanisms.
  • ❌ A fan works by convection. ✅ A fan creates FORCED CONVECTION by moving air. The heat transfer mechanism is convection, but the fan provides the driving force. Without a temperature difference, a fan does not transfer heat.
  • ❌ Hot air rises because it is lighter. ✅ Hot air rises because it is LESS DENSE than the surrounding cooler air. Buoyancy (Archimedes' principle) pushes the less dense warm air upward. Weight is unchanged — density decreases.
  • ❌ Newton's law of cooling says cooling rate is proportional to temperature. ✅ Newton's law says cooling rate is proportional to TEMPERATURE DIFFERENCE (ΔT), not absolute temperature. A hot object in a hot room cools slower than the same object in a cold room.
  • ❌ Forced convection always gives turbulent flow. ✅ Forced convection can be laminar or turbulent depending on the Reynolds number (Re=νvL​). Low velocities and small characteristic lengths give laminar flow even with forced convection.
  • ❌ Convection requires a fan or pump. ✅ Natural convection requires NO external device — buoyancy forces drive the fluid motion naturally. Forced convection uses fans/pumps, but natural convection occurs spontaneously.

Practice

  • A radiator has a surface area of 2 m² at 60°C in a room at 20°C. If the convective coefficient is 8 W/(m²·K), find the heat loss rate. H=hAΔT=8×2×(60−20)=640 W. The radiator loses 640 J/s to the room air.
  • A pot of water (0.5 kg) at 20°C is heated on a stove. If the convective heat transfer coefficient at the pot bottom is 500 W/(m²·K), the bottom area is 0.03 m², and the temperature difference between the flame and water is 200°C, find the heat transfer rate. H=500×0.03×200=3000 W. Time to boil: Q=mcΔT=0.5×4200×80=168,000 J. t=168,000/3000=56 s (ignoring losses).
  • Compare natural and forced convection for air over a flat plate. Natural convection: hnat​≈5 W/(m²·K). Forced convection at 2 m/s: hforced​≈25 W/(m²·K). The ratio is 5:1. Forced convection transfers heat 5 times faster than natural convection for the same geometry and temperature difference.
  • Explain why ceiling fans in summer create a cooling sensation even though they don't cool the air. The fan increases the air velocity over the skin, increasing the convective heat transfer coefficient h. More heat is carried away from the body, creating a cooling effect. The air temperature doesn't change — the body loses heat faster.
  • A cooling tower cools 1000 kg of water per hour from 50°C to 30°C. Find the rate of heat removal. H=tmcΔT​=36001000×4200×20​=2333 W. The cooling tower must remove 2333 J/s using natural convection and evaporation of a small amount of water.
  • A vertical plate 0.5 m tall is maintained at 80°C in quiescent air at 20°C. Estimate the convective heat loss per unit width. Natural convection coefficient for vertical plate: h≈1.32(ΔT/L)1/4 (simplified correlation). h≈1.32(60/0.5)1/4=1.32×1200.25≈1.32×3.31=4.37 W/(m²·K). Per unit width: H=4.37×0.5×60=131 W/m width.

Universal Facts

  • Convection is responsible for approximately 90% of heat transfer in the Earth's troposphere. The atmospheric circulation — wind patterns, storms, and jet streams — is driven entirely by convective processes.
  • The Rayleigh number (Ra=Gr⋅Pr) determines whether natural convection is laminar (Ra<109) or turbulent (Ra>109). Most engineering applications operate in the turbulent regime.
  • Magma convection drives plate tectonics on Earth. The mantle (though solid rock) behaves as a viscous fluid over geological timescales, with convection cells that move the tectonic plates at rates of a few cm per year.
  • In spacecraft design, convection cannot occur in the vacuum of space. Heat can only be removed by radiation, which is why spacecraft radiators are large, specially coated panels.
  • Benard cells (convection cells) form in thin layers of silicone oil heated from below. These hexagonal cell patterns were first studied by Henri Bénard in 1900 and later explained theoretically by Lord Rayleigh.
