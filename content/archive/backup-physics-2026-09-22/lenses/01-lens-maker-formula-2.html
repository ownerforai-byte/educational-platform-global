Lens Maker's Formula

Notes

  • Lens maker's formula: f1​=(n−1)(R1​1​−R2​1​), where n is the refractive index of the lens material relative to the surrounding medium, R1​ is the radius of curvature of the first surface (light-entering), and R2​ is the radius of curvature of the second surface. This is the fundamental equation that lens designers use to create lenses of specific focal length.
  • Sign convention for radii: By the New Cartesian sign convention, the radius of a surface is positive if the center of curvature is on the side of the outgoing light (transmitted side), and negative if on the side of the incoming light (incident side). For a biconvex lens, R1​>0 and R2​<0.
  • Derivation: The lens maker's formula comes from applying the refraction at a single spherical surface twice — once for the first surface, then for the second surface. The thin lens approximation assumes the lens thickness is negligible compared to the object and image distances, so the two refractions can be combined into a single effective refraction at the central plane.
  • Biconvex lens: Both surfaces are convex (bulging outward). R1​>0, R2​<0. The lens is converging. Example: R1​=+20 cm, R2​=−20 cm, n=1.5: 1/f=0.5(1/0.2+1/0.2)=5 m⁻¹, so f=20 cm.
  • Biconcave lens: Both surfaces are concave (curving inward). R1​<0, R2​>0. The lens is diverging. Example: R1​=−20 cm, R2​=+20 cm: 1/f=0.5(−1/0.2−1/0.2)=−5 m⁻¹, so f=−20 cm.
  • Plano-convex lens: One flat surface (R=∞) and one convex surface. 1/f=(n−1)/R where R is the curved surface. Used in applications requiring minimal spherical aberration when parallel light enters the curved side.
  • Plano-concave lens: One flat surface and one concave surface. 1/f=−(n−1)/R. A diverging lens used as a beam expander or to compensate for aberrations.
  • Meniscus lens: One convex and one concave surface. The lens is converging if the convex surface has smaller radius, otherwise diverging. Used in eyeglass prescriptions for fine-tuning focal length.
  • Why it matters: The formula shows that the focal length depends on TWO factors: the refractive index n (the material) and the surface curvatures R1​, R2​ (the geometry). Modern spectacle lenses use high-index materials (n=1.67 to 1.74) to make thinner, lighter lenses with the same focal length — particularly useful for strong prescriptions.
  • Lens in a medium: When the lens is immersed in a medium of refractive index nm​ (not air), the formula becomes f1​=(nm​nlens​​−1)(R1​1​−R2​1​). The relative refractive index determines the bending of light. In water (nm​=1.33), a glass lens has much weaker power than in air.
  • Limitation — thin lens assumption: The formula assumes the lens thickness is much smaller than the radii of curvature. For thick lenses, the position of principal planes must be considered, and the formula gives the effective focal length measured from the principal planes (not the lens center).
  • Limitation — paraxial approximation: The formula is exact only for paraxial rays (rays close to the optical axis making small angles). For wide beams, spherical aberration causes rays far from the axis to focus at different points, reducing image quality.
  • Connection to lens formula: The lens maker's formula gives the focal length f of the lens, which is then used in the lens formula 1/f=1/v−1/u to find image position. The two formulas together solve all lens problems.
  • Connection to power: Since P=1/f, the lens maker's formula can be written as P=(n−1)(1/R1​−1/R2​). This shows that increasing n or decreasing the radii of curvature (more curved surfaces) increases the power.
  • NEB exam focus: State the lens maker's formula with proper sign convention. Calculate the focal length for a given lens geometry and material. Explain why a glass lens has weaker power in water than in air. Solve problems combining lens maker's formula with the lens formula.
  • Achromatic doublet design: Lens designers combine glasses of different refractive indices and dispersions to create achromatic doublets. Two lenses in contact with different n and different dispersions can correct for chromatic aberration while maintaining net positive power.
  • Design example — a 50 cm focal length biconvex glass lens: With n=1.5, choose R1​=+50 cm and R2​=−50 cm. Then 1/f=0.5(1/0.5−1/(−0.5))=0.5×4=2 m⁻¹, so f=50 cm. Note: a biconvex lens with equal magnitudes of radii is symmetric. For a more general shape, you can have R1​=∣R2​∣.

Confusion

  • ❌ The formula 1/f=(n−1)(1/R1​−1/R2​) is the lens formula. ✅ This is the LENS MAKER'S FORMULA — it calculates f from the lens geometry. The LENS FORMULA is 1/f=1/v−1/u, which relates object and image distances. They are different equations for different purposes.
  • ❌ R1​ and R2​ are both positive for a biconvex lens. ✅ For a biconvex lens, R1​ is positive (center of curvature on the outgoing side) and R2​ is negative (center of curvature on the incoming side). The signs are opposite, giving a positive 1/f and positive f (converging lens).
  • ❌ The lens maker's formula applies to thick lenses. ✅ The simple lens maker's formula applies only to THIN lenses. For thick lenses, the concept of principal planes is required, and the formula gives the effective focal length measured from these principal planes.
  • ❌ A lens in water has the same focal length as in air. ✅ The focal length in water is LONGER (weaker lens) because the relative index nglass​/nwater​≈1.125 is much smaller than nglass​/nair​=1.5. The lens becomes much weaker. A glass lens in water might even become diverging if the geometry allows it.
  • ❌ A flat surface has radius R=0. ✅ A flat surface has radius R=∞ (infinite radius of curvature). The 1/R term vanishes, so flat surfaces contribute nothing to the focal length. They do not disappear; they just have no curvature.
  • ❌ Higher refractive index means lower focal length always. ✅ For a given lens geometry, higher n means shorter f (more powerful lens). But the lens maker's formula shows that f also depends on the radii. A high-n flat lens has f=∞ — flat surfaces don't focus regardless of n.
  • ❌ Sign convention doesn't matter if the answer is positive. ✅ Sign convention ALWAYS matters. Without it, you cannot determine whether a lens is converging or diverging, or whether a focal length is positive or negative. The sign is essential information, not a bookkeeping detail.

Practice

  • A biconvex glass lens (n=1.5) has radii of curvature 25 cm and 30 cm. Find the focal length in air. 1/f=(1.5−1)(1/0.25−1/(−0.30))=0.5(4+3.33)=0.5×7.33=3.67 m⁻¹. f=0.273 m = 27.3 cm. Positive — converging lens.
  • The same lens is immersed in water (nwater​=1.33). Find the new focal length. nrel​=1.5/1.33=1.128. 1/f=(1.128−1)(1/0.25−1/(−0.30))=0.128×7.33=0.938 m⁻¹. f=1.066 m ≈107 cm. The lens is much weaker in water.
  • A plano-convex lens (n=1.6) has a curved surface with R=30 cm. Find the focal length. The flat side has R=∞. 1/f=(1.6−1)(1/∞−1/(−0.30))=0.6×1/0.30=2 m⁻¹. f=0.5 m = 50 cm. Converging lens with 50 cm focal length.
  • An equiconvex lens (R1​=−R2​=15 cm) made of glass (n=1.5) is placed in water (n=1.33). Calculate the focal length in both air and water. Air: 1/f=0.5(1/0.15+1/0.15)=6.67 m⁻¹, f=15 cm. Water: 1/f=0.128(1/0.15+1/0.15)=1.71 m⁻¹, f=58.5 cm. The lens is 4× weaker in water.
  • A converging meniscus lens has R1​=20 cm, R2​=30 cm, with n=1.5. Find the focal length. 1/f=0.5(1/0.20−1/0.30)=0.5(5−3.33)=0.5×1.67=0.833 m⁻¹. f=1.20 m = 120 cm. Converging meniscus with long focal length.
  • A biconcave lens has R1​=−25 cm, R2​=+25 cm, n=1.5. Find the focal length and power. 1/f=0.5(1/(−0.25)−1/0.25)=0.5(−4−4)=−4 m⁻¹. f=−25 cm. P=1/(−0.25)=−4 D. Diverging lens of 25 cm focal length and -4 dioptre power.

Universal Facts

  • The lens maker's formula was first derived geometrically by Descartes in 1637, building on earlier work by Kepler. Newton's formulation introduced the connection to the lens's refractive power and laid the groundwork for modern lens design.
  • High-index glass (n=1.7 to 1.9) is used in modern eyeglass lenses to reduce thickness, especially for strong prescriptions. A -10 D lens in standard glass (n=1.5) might be 5 mm thick at the edge, but only 2-3 mm thick in high-index glass.
  • Aspheric lenses (with non-spherical surfaces) cannot be fully described by the simple lens maker's formula, which assumes spherical surfaces. Aspheric designs correct spherical aberration and allow thinner, lighter lenses.
  • Fresnel lenses achieve the same optical power as conventional lenses by dividing the surface into concentric annular zones, each at the appropriate angle. The lens can be much thinner but produces images of lower quality. Used in lighthouses, traffic lights, and overhead projectors.
  • In photography, lens designers optimize multiple parameters: refractive index, surface curvatures, thicknesses, and glass types. Modern zoom lenses contain 12-20 individual lens elements to correct for chromatic and spherical aberrations while maintaining a constant focal length.
