Dark Matter, Black Holes and Gravitational Waves

Notes

  • Dark matter: A form of matter that does not emit, absorb, or reflect electromagnetic radiation — it is completely invisible to all telescopes. Its presence is inferred solely from gravitational effects on visible matter, light, and the large-scale structure of the universe. Dark matter comprises approximately 27% of the universe's total energy density.
  • Evidence for dark matter — galaxy rotation curves: Stars in spiral galaxies orbit the galactic center. Newtonian mechanics predicts orbital velocity should decrease with distance from the center (v∝1/r​). Instead, rotation curves remain FLAT at large radii — stars maintain the same orbital speed far from the visible disk. This requires additional invisible mass (dark matter halo) extending far beyond the visible galaxy.
  • Evidence — gravitational lensing: Dark matter bends light from background galaxies through gravitational lensing. The pattern and strength of lensing arcs around galaxy clusters reveal the total mass distribution, which is consistently much larger than the visible mass. The Bullet Cluster (two colliding galaxy clusters) provides the most direct evidence: the hot gas (visible) has been separated from the dark matter (detected via lensing).
  • Evidence — large-scale structure and CMB: Dark matter was essential in the early universe: its gravity pulled matter together to form the first stars and galaxies. Without dark matter, the universe would look very different. CMB measurements precisely constrain the dark matter density to 26.8% of the critical density.
  • Candidates for dark matter: (1) WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles) — hypothetical particles that interact only through gravity and the weak force. (2) Axions — very light particles proposed to solve the strong CP problem. (3) Primordial black holes — black holes formed in the early universe. All remain undetected as of 2026.
  • Black hole definition: A region of spacetime where gravity is so overwhelmingly strong that nothing — not even light or any other form of information — can escape once it crosses the event horizon. Black holes are solutions to Einstein's field equations of general relativity.
  • Schwarzschild radius (event horizon): The radius of the event horizon for a non-rotating, uncharged black hole: Rs​=c22GM​. For a one solar mass black hole: Rs​≈3 km. For a billion-solar-mass supermassive black hole: Rs​≈3×109 km (~20 AU).
  • Types of black holes: (1) Stellar-mass black holes (3−100M⊙​) formed from collapsed massive stars. (2) Intermediate-mass black holes (100−105M⊙​) — formation mechanism uncertain. (3) Supermassive black holes (106−1010M⊙​) found at the centers of most galaxies, including the Milky Way's Sagittarius A* (4×106M⊙​).
  • Hawking radiation: Stephen Hawking predicted in 1974 that black holes emit thermal radiation due to quantum effects near the event horizon. The temperature is T=8πGMkB​ℏc3​ — extremely tiny for astrophysical black holes. For a solar-mass black hole: T≈6×10−8 K. Black holes lose mass over time and eventually evaporate.
  • Gravitational waves (gravitational radiation): Ripples in the fabric of spacetime caused by accelerating masses, propagating at the speed of light. Predicted by Einstein in 1916 as a consequence of general relativity. Unlike electromagnetic waves, gravitational waves are quadrupolar and interact extremely weakly with matter — they pass through everything essentially unimpeded.
  • Gravitational wave detection — LIGO: The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory detected its first gravitational wave (GW150914) on September 14, 2015 from two black holes (~36 and 29 solar masses) merging 1.3 billion light-years away. The signal lasted 0.2 seconds, with peak strain h≈10−21.
  • LIGO interferometer principle: Each LIGO detector is an L-shaped Michelson interferometer with 4 km arms. A passing gravitational wave stretches one arm and compresses the other alternately, changing the arm length difference by ~10⁻¹⁸ m (one-thousandth the diameter of a proton). Laser interferometry detects this tiny change.
  • Gravitational wave sources: (1) Binary black hole mergers — most massive, detectable to billions of light-years. (2) Binary neutron star mergers — also produce optical counterparts (kilonovae), observed in GW170817. (3) Supernovae — asymmetric explosions produce detectable bursts. (4) Pulsars — rapidly rotating neutron stars with tiny asymmetries. (5) Primordial gravitational waves — from cosmic inflation, detectable in the CMB B-mode polarization.
  • Multi-messenger astronomy: GW170817 (August 2017) was detected by LIGO and Virgo, and 1.7 seconds later, the Fermi GBM detected a gamma-ray burst from the same location. This confirmed that neutron star mergers produce short gamma-ray bursts and heavy elements (gold, platinum) through r-process nucleosynthesis.
  • NEB exam focus: State the evidence for dark matter. Calculate the Schwarzschild radius. Explain gravitational wave generation and detection. Distinguish between dark matter, dark energy, and black holes. Solve problems using Rs​=2GM/c2.
  • Black hole information paradox: In quantum mechanics, information cannot be destroyed. But Hawking radiation causes black holes to evaporate, seemingly destroying information. This paradox — whether information is lost or preserved — remains one of the deepest unsolved problems in theoretical physics.
  • Event horizon telescope: In 2019, the EHT collaboration released the first image of a black hole — the supermassive black hole in galaxy M87 (6.5 billion solar masses) — as a silhouette against surrounding hot gas. The image confirmed predictions of general relativity about the shadow's size and shape.

Confusion

  • ❌ Dark matter is the same as dark energy. ✅ Dark matter (27%) is a form of mass that exerts gravitational attraction — it clumps and forms structure. Dark energy (68%) is a property of space itself that causes accelerated expansion and does not clump. They are fundamentally different.
  • ❌ Black holes suck in everything around them. ✅ Outside the event horizon, black holes behave like any other object of the same mass. A black hole's gravity at the distance of Earth's orbit is identical to the Sun's. Black holes are dangerous only if you get very close.
  • ❌ Nothing can escape from inside a black hole, not even light. ✅ This is the DEFINITION of an event horizon. Nothing, not even light, can escape once inside Rs​. This is why they are called black holes.
  • ❌ Gravitational waves are made of matter. ✅ Gravitational waves are ripples in SPACETIME ITSELF — not made of any material substance. They propagate through empty space at the speed of light, carrying energy.
  • ❌ Dark matter is composed of familiar particles we just haven't detected yet. ✅ Most dark matter cannot be composed of Standard Model particles (electrons, protons, neutrons, neutrinos). Known neutrinos are too light and too abundant in the wrong way. Dark matter must be a new type of particle or form of matter beyond the Standard Model.
  • ❌ Black holes are infinitely dense singularities. ✅ The singularity at the center is a breakdown of general relativity where density becomes infinite — but this is likely a mathematical artifact. In quantum gravity (not yet fully developed), the singularity would be replaced by something finite.
  • ❌ Gravitational waves are easy to detect because they are so energetic. ✅ Gravitational waves interact extremely weakly with matter. The strain is h≈10−21 — LIGO measures changes in arm length of one part in 1021. They pass through the Earth without any interaction, which makes detection both fascinating and extremely challenging.

Practice

  • Calculate the Schwarzschild radius for (a) a 10 solar mass black hole, (b) the Earth. For (a): Rs​=2GM/c2=2(6.67×10−11)(2×1031)/(9×1016)≈30 km. For (b): M=6×1024 kg, Rs​=2×6.67×10−11×6×1024/(9×1016)=8.9 mm. The Earth would become a black hole if compressed to the size of a pea!
  • A galaxy cluster has a total mass of 1014M⊙​ but visible mass of only 3×1013M⊙​. What fraction is dark matter? Dark matter fraction = (1014−3×1013)/1014=70%. Dark matter is 7 times the visible mass — this is typical for galaxy clusters.
  • Two black holes, each of mass M, merge to form a black hole. What is the mass of the final black hole? The final mass is LESS than 2M. During merger, energy is radiated away as gravitational waves. For a non-rotating head-on merger, ΔM/M≈5.7%. This energy can be detected as gravitational waves billions of light-years away.
  • The LIGO detector has arm length 4 km. If a gravitational wave of strain h=10−21 passes, how much does each arm change in length? ΔL=h×L=10−21×4000=4×10−18 m = 0.000004 femtometers. This is 1/1000 the diameter of a proton. Advanced LIGO achieves this by measuring laser phase differences over hundreds of reflections.
  • The supermassive black hole at the center of M87 has mass 6.5×109M⊙​. Calculate its Schwarzschild radius. Rs​=2GM/c2. Using Rs,⊙​=3 km: Rs​=3×6.5×109=1.95×1010 km ≈130 AU ≈ the size of Pluto's orbit. The event horizon is enormous.
  • A dark matter particle has mass m. Estimate its characteristic size if it is a Compton wavelength-sized primordial black hole. The Compton wavelength is λC​=h/(mc). If Rs​=2Gm/c2=λC​/2, then m=hc/(2G)​≈mP​/2​, where mP​=ℏc/G​≈21.7 μg is the Planck mass. This gives m≈15 μg — suggesting Planck-mass dark matter candidates.
  • A neutron star has mass 2M⊙​ and radius 10 km. Calculate its surface gravity. g=GM/R2=6.67×10−11×4×1030/(105)2=2.67×1012 m/s² ≈2.7×1011g. The escape velocity from the surface is v=2GM/R​≈0.6c — almost the speed of light!

Universal Facts

  • The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish, and Kip Thorne for LIGO's detection of gravitational waves — confirming a prediction Einstein made in 1916 and opening a completely new window on the universe.
  • The Event Horizon Telescope (2019) imaged the black hole shadow in M87, showing a ring of radio emission surrounding a dark center. The image matched general relativistic predictions to high precision, confirming black hole properties predicted by Einstein's theory.
  • The Higgs boson discovery at CERN in 2012 confirmed that mass arises from interaction with the Higgs field. However, the mass of the Higgs (125 GeV) is far below the natural scale suggested by quantum corrections — the 'hierarchy problem', potentially solved by supersymmetry or other BSM physics.
  • The LIGO-Virgo collaboration has detected over 100 gravitational wave events from binary black hole and neutron star mergers since 2015. Each detection opens a new channel for studying the universe — gravitational wave astronomy is now a mature field.
  • Dark matter detection experiments (XENON, LUX, PandaX) use ultra-sensitive underground detectors looking for WIMP-nucleus scattering. As of 2026, no dark matter particle has been directly detected, ruling out much of the WIMP parameter space and motivating alternative candidates.
