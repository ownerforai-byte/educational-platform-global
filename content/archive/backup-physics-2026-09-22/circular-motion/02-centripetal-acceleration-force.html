Centripetal Acceleration and Force

Notes

  • Centripetal acceleration (a_c): the acceleration directed toward the centre of a circular path that keeps a body moving in a circle. Magnitude: a_c = v²/r = rω². Direction: always toward the centre (hence 'centripetal' = 'centre-seeking'). It changes the direction of velocity, not its magnitude.
  • Centripetal force (F_c): the net force required to produce centripetal acceleration: F_c = mv²/r = mrω². It is NOT a new force — it is the RESULTANT of real forces (tension, gravity, friction, normal) pointing toward the centre.
  • Why centripetal force is not a separate force: the centripetal force is always provided by one or more real forces. For a car turning on a flat road, friction provides F_c. For a planet orbiting the Sun, gravity provides F_c. For a ball on a string, tension provides F_c.
  • Derivation of a_c = v²/r: consider a body moving at constant speed v in a circle of radius r. In a small time Δt, it moves from A to B. The change in velocity Δv points toward the centre. From similar triangles: Δv/v = Δs/r → Δv = vΔs/r. Acceleration a = Δv/Δt = v²/r (since Δs/Δt = v).
  • Worked example — car turning: a 1000 kg car turns a flat curve of radius 50 m at 20 m/s. Required friction: F_c = mv²/r = 1000 × 400/50 = 8000 N. If μ_s = 0.6, max friction = 0.6 × 1000 × 9.8 = 5880 N — the car skids (8000 > 5880).
  • Worked example — ball on a string: a 0.5 kg ball on a 1.5 m string rotates at 4 m/s. Tension = mv²/r = 0.5 × 16/1.5 = 5.33 N. If the string breaks at 10 N, max speed = √(10 × 1.5/0.5) = 5.48 m/s.
  • Worked example — planet orbit: Earth orbits the Sun at 30 km/s at a distance of 1.5 × 10¹¹ m. Centripetal acceleration = v²/r = (3×10⁴)²/(1.5×10¹¹) = 6 × 10⁻³ m/s². This is provided by the Sun's gravity.
  • NEB exam focus: calculating centripetal force/acceleration; identifying which real force provides F_c; maximum speed problems (string breaking, car skidding); linking v, ω, r, and a_c.
  • Centripetal force is not a force — it is a role: the term 'centripetal force' describes the NET force pointing toward the center. It is always provided by some real force or combination of forces: tension (string), gravity (planets), friction (car on road), normal force (banked road), magnetic force (charged particle). Always draw an FBD and identify WHICH real force plays this role.
  • Centrifugal force in rotating frames: in a reference frame attached to the rotating body (e.g., a car turning), passengers feel pushed outward. This is called centrifugal force. It is a pseudo-force — it arises from the inertia of the body wanting to move in a straight line. In an inertial (non-accelerating) frame, there is NO outward force — only the inward centripetal force.
  • Derivation via vector diagram: consider a particle moving at constant speed v around a circle of radius r. After a small time Δt, it moves through angle Δθ = vΔt/r. The velocity vectors change direction by Δθ. From the similar triangles formed by position vectors and velocity vectors: |Δv|/v = |Δr|/r = Δs/r = vΔt/r. Therefore a = |Δv|/Δt = v²/r.
  • Worked example — satellite orbit: a satellite orbits Earth at altitude 400 km (r = 6771 km) with speed 7.67 km/s. Centripetal acceleration: a_c = v²/r = (7670)²/6771000 = 8.69 m/s². This equals the local gravitational acceleration at that altitude, confirming that gravity provides the centripetal force.
  • Common NEB trap — direction of acceleration: students often confuse the direction of centripetal acceleration (toward center) with the direction of velocity (tangential). Remember: a_c changes the DIRECTION of v, not its magnitude. If speed is changing too, there is also a tangential component a_t.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'Centrifugal force pulls the body outward.' ✅ Centrifugal force is a pseudo-force that appears only in a rotating reference frame. In an inertial frame, there is only centripetal force toward the centre.
  • ❌ 'Centripetal force is a new type of force.' ✅ Centripetal force is the RESULTANT of real forces (tension, gravity, friction). It is not a separate force you add to the FBD.
  • ❌ 'The body in circular motion is in equilibrium.' ✅ The body accelerates toward the centre (a_c = v²/r). It is NOT in equilibrium — the net force is mv²/r toward the centre.
  • ❌ 'Centripetal acceleration changes the speed.' ✅ Centripetal acceleration changes only the DIRECTION of velocity. Tangential acceleration (if present) changes the speed.
  • ❌ 'Centripetal force acts outward.' ✅ Centripetal force acts INWARD, toward the centre. The tendency to move outward is inertia, not a force.
  • ❌ 'If the string breaks, the body moves radially outward.' ✅ If the string breaks, the body moves TANGENT to the circle (in the direction of its velocity at that instant), not radially.

Universal Facts

  • Centripetal force is not a new force — it is the resultant of real forces pointing toward the centre.
  • The direction of centripetal acceleration is always toward the centre — it changes velocity direction, not magnitude.
  • Centrifugal force is a pseudo-force that appears only in rotating reference frames.
  • If the centripetal force is removed, the body moves tangent to the circle (Newton's first law).
  • Centripetal force is proportional to v² — doubling the speed quadruples the required force.

Examples

  • Car turning: A 1200 kg car turns a flat curve of radius 60 m at 15 m/s. F_c = 1200 × 225/60 = 4500 N (provided by friction).
  • Ball on string: A 0.3 kg ball on a 2 m string rotates at 6 m/s. Tension = 0.3 × 36/2 = 5.4 N.
  • Planet orbit: A satellite orbits Earth at 8 km/s at a radius of 7000 km. a_c = (8000)²/(7×10⁶) = 9.14 m/s².
  • Maximum speed: A 0.4 kg ball on a 1.5 m string (max tension 15 N). v_max = √(15 × 1.5/0.4) = 7.5 m/s.
  • Conical pendulum: A 0.2 kg bob rotates at 3 m/s in a horizontal circle of radius 0.8 m. F_c = 0.2 × 9/0.8 = 2.25 N.

Practice Questions

  • A 1500 kg car turns a flat curve of radius 80 m at 25 m/s. Find the minimum coefficient of friction needed to prevent skidding.
  • A 0.6 kg ball on a 2 m string rotates at 5 m/s. Find the tension. If the string breaks at 12 N, find the maximum speed.
  • A satellite orbits Earth at 7 km/s at a radius of 6800 km. Find the centripetal acceleration.
  • A cyclist turns a curve of radius 30 m at 12 m/s. At what angle must the cyclist lean to avoid falling?
  • Explain why passengers feel pushed outward when a car takes a sharp turn, and identify the real forces involved.

Summary

Centripetal acceleration (a_c = v²/r = rω²) is directed toward the centre of a circular path. The centripetal force (F_c = mv²/r) is the resultant of real forces (tension, gravity, friction) — not a new force. Maximum-speed problems (string breaking, car skidding) and identifying which real force provides F_c are the key NEB topics.

Special Notes

  • Always identify which real force provides the centripetal force — never add 'centripetal force' as a separate force in the FBD.
  • Centrifugal force is a pseudo-force — it appears only in rotating reference frames, not in inertial frames.
  • If the centripetal force is removed, the body moves tangent to the circle (Newton's first law).
  • Maximum-speed problems: set F_c = mv²/r equal to the maximum available force (tension, friction) and solve for v.
  • NEB frequently asks: 'Find the maximum speed' — use v_max = √(μ_s rg) for curves, v_max = √(T_max r/m) for strings.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Force identification trick: always ask 'which real force points toward the centre?' — that's your F_c.
  • Maximum speed shortcut: v_max = √(μ_s rg) for flat curves, v_max = √(T_max r/m) for strings.
  • Cyclist lean shortcut: tan θ = v²/(rg) — the lean angle depends on v²/r, not on mass.
  • Centripetal acceleration shortcut: a_c = rω² — if you know ω, this is faster than v²/r.
  • Breaking condition: the string breaks when mv²/r > T_max — solve for v_max.
