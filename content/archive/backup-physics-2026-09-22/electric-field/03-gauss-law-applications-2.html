Notes

  • Field of a uniformly charged sphere (insulating): outside (r>R): E=kQ/r2 as if all charge at the centre. Inside (r<R): E=kQr/R3 (linear in r). At the surface (r=R): E=kQ/R2 (both formulas give the same value).
  • Field of a charged conducting sphere: all charge resides on the surface. Outside: E=kQ/r2. Inside: E=0 everywhere (within the conducting material). At the surface: E=kQ/R2.
  • Field of an infinite line of charge: E=2πϵ0​rλ​=r2kλ​. Decreases as 1/r, not 1/r2. Direction is radial (perpendicular to the line).
  • Field of an infinite plane of charge: E=2ϵ0​σ​. Independent of distance from the plane. Direction is perpendicular to the plane (outward for positive σ).
  • Field between two parallel planes with opposite charge: inside: E=σ/ϵ0​ (twice the single-plane value). Outside: E=0 (fields cancel). This is the configuration of a parallel plate capacitor.
  • Field of a coaxial cable (cylindrical conductor with charge on outer surface): outside (r>Router​): E=λ/(2πϵ0​r). Inside the inner conductor: E=0. Between the conductors: depends on where the charge is on each conductor.
  • Field at the surface of a conductor: E=σ/ϵ0​, directed perpendicular to the surface. This is a special case of the field of a charged plane, but here only one side has field (the other is inside the conductor where E=0).
  • Charge density on a curved conductor: σ is highest where the surface curvature is highest. At a sharp point (small radius of curvature), the surface charge density is very large, leading to high field and discharge (corona).
  • Field of a uniformly charged cylinder (long): outside (r>R): E=λ/(2πϵ0​r). Inside the cylinder with volume charge density ρ (r<R): E=ρr/(2ϵ0​).
  • Field of a uniformly charged slab (thick infinite plane): inside the slab at distance d from the centre plane: E=ρd/ϵ0​. Outside the slab: E=ρt/(2ϵ0​) where t is the slab thickness.
  • Field of a charged ring (along its axis): E=(z2+R2)3/2kQz​, where z is the axial distance from the centre and R is the ring radius. At the centre (z=0), E=0 by symmetry. Far away (z≫R): E≈kQ/z2.
  • Cavity in a charged conductor: if a cavity has no charge inside, the field in the cavity is zero (regardless of the external field). This is a Faraday cage effect.
  • Solid angle approach: for any closed surface and any internal charge, the flux is q/ϵ0​. This works for surfaces of any shape, even irregular ones, as long as they enclose the charge.

Confusion

  • The field inside a uniformly charged solid insulating sphere is NOT zero — it is E=kQr/R3, which increases with r. Only for conducting spheres (or inside a cavity with no charge) is the field zero.
  • For an infinite plane of charge, the field is E=σ/(2ϵ0​), not σ/ϵ0​. The latter is the field just outside a conductor, which is twice as large because the conductor has charge on one side only.
  • Between two oppositely charged parallel plates, the field is E=σ/ϵ0​. Outside the plates, the field is zero (the contributions from each plate cancel). This is not the same as two isolated planes.
  • The field of a long line of charge is E∝1/r, while the field of a point charge is E∝1/r2. The difference arises because the line charge has an effectively infinite extent.
  • Gauss's law gives the magnitude of E when symmetry allows E to be constant over the surface. Without symmetry, you can still use the law but cannot extract E from the integral.

  • A long straight wire has linear charge density λ=2μC/m. Find the electric field at a point 0.5 m from the wire. Solution: E=2kλ/r=2×9×109×2×10−6/0.5=7.2×104 N/C, directed radially outward.
  • A spherical conducting shell of inner radius 5 cm and outer radius 7 cm has a charge of +3μC on the outer surface. A point charge +1μC is at the centre. Find the field at (a) 3 cm from centre, (b) at the inner surface (5 cm), (c) at the outer surface (7 cm), (d) at 10 cm. Solution: (a) Inside cavity: E=kq/r2=9×109×10−6/0.032=107 N/C. (b) At inner surface: E=kq/Rin2​=9×109×10−6/0.0025=3.6×106 N/C. (c) Inside conductor: E=0. (d) Outside: total enclosed charge is +4μC, E=k(4μC)/0.12=3.6×106 N/C.
  • Two infinite parallel planes are 5 cm apart, one with σ=+10μC/m² and the other with σ=−10μC/m². Find the field at the midpoint between them, and 10 cm beyond the positive plate. Solution: At the midpoint, both fields point from positive to negative, so they add: E=σ/ϵ0​=10×10−6/8.85×10−12=1.13×106 N/C. 10 cm beyond the positive plate: the field from the positive plate is outward (in +x), from the negative plate is inward (toward it, in −x direction, so net is in −x); magnitudes: E1​=σ/(2ϵ0​)=5.65×105 N/C, E2​=σ/(2ϵ0​)=5.65×105 N/C, so net = 0.
  • A long cylinder of radius 0.05 m has a uniform volume charge density ρ=2μC/m³. Find the field at (a) 0.02 m from the axis, (b) 0.1 m from the axis. Solution: (a) Inside: E=ρr/(2ϵ0​)=2×10−6×0.02/(2×8.85×10−12)=2.26×105 N/C. (b) Outside: linear charge density λ=ρπR2=2×10−6×π×0.0025=1.57×10−8 C/m. E=λ/(2πϵ0​r)=1.57×10−8/(2π×8.85×10−12×0.1)=2.82×103 N/C.
  • A non-conducting sphere of radius 0.1 m has uniform charge density ρ=5μC/m³. Find the field at 0.05 m and 0.2 m from the centre. Solution: Total charge Q=ρ⋅34​πR3=5×10−6×4.19×10−3=2.09×10−8 C. (a) At 0.05 m: Qenc​=ρ⋅34​πr3=5×10−6×34​π(0.05)3=2.62×10−9 C. E=kQenc​/r2=9×109×2.62×10−9/0.0025=9.42×103 N/C. (b) At 0.2 m: E=kQ/r2=9×109×2.09×10−8/0.04=4.7×103 N/C.

Universal Facts

  • Gauss's law is most useful when the field has high symmetry — spherical, cylindrical, or planar. For irregular shapes, computational methods are needed.
  • The field just outside any conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is σ/ϵ0​, regardless of the conductor's shape. This is why the charge density can be very high at sharp points.
  • The Earth itself has an electric field of about 100-150 V/m near the surface, due to the charge on the ground and in the atmosphere. The total charge of the Earth is about −5.1×105 C.
  • Lightning rods work on the principle of high field at sharp points. The strong field ionizes the air, providing a conducting path for lightning to follow safely.
  • The electric field in a thundercloud can reach 100 kV/m or more. When this exceeds the breakdown field of air (∼3×106 V/m), a lightning discharge occurs.
