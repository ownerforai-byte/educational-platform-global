Notes

  • Electric flux ΦE​: the measure of electric field passing through a surface. ΦE​=∫E⋅dA. Units: N·m²/C or V·m. It is a scalar, though it can be positive or negative depending on the angle between E and dA.
  • Gauss's law: the total electric flux through a closed surface is ΦE​=∮E⋅dA=ϵ0​Qenc​​, where Qenc​ is the total charge enclosed by the surface. This is a fundamental law of electromagnetism, equivalent to Coulomb's law.
  • Sign convention: dA points outward from the closed surface. Flux is positive where E points outward (net positive charge inside) and negative where E points inward (net negative charge inside).
  • Choice of Gaussian surface: must be chosen to take advantage of symmetry. For spherical symmetry, use a sphere. For cylindrical, use a cylinder. For planar, use a pillbox.
  • Derivation from Coulomb's law: Gauss's law can be derived by considering the flux through a sphere surrounding a point charge. Since E=kq/r2 and A=4πr2, the flux ΦE​=E⋅A=kq/r2⋅4πr2=q/ϵ0​. This is independent of the sphere's radius.
  • Net flux is zero for no enclosed charge: if no charge is enclosed, ∮E⋅dA=0. This does not mean the field is zero everywhere on the surface; it means as much field enters as leaves.
  • Faraday cage: a closed conducting shell. The electric field inside is exactly zero regardless of external fields. Any excess charge on the conductor resides on the outer surface.
  • Solid angle: flux through a small surface element is dΦ=E⋅dA⋅cosθ=(q/(4πϵ0​))⋅dΩ, where dΩ is the solid angle subtended. The total solid angle around a closed surface is 4π, giving Φ=q/ϵ0​.
  • Differential form of Gauss's law: ∇⋅E=ρ/ϵ0​. This is one of Maxwell's four equations and connects the divergence of the electric field to the local charge density.
  • Gauss's law with dielectrics: ∮D⋅dA=Qfree​, where D=ϵE is the electric displacement. This form separates the free charge (which we can control) from the bound charge in the dielectric.
  • Surface charge distribution: for a conductor with surface charge density σ, the field just outside the surface is E=σ/ϵ0​. The field just inside is zero. The discontinuity Eout​−Ein​=σ/ϵ0​.
  • Limitations: Gauss's law is always true (it is a fundamental law), but it is most useful when the symmetry allows us to factor E out of the integral. For non-symmetric distributions, the integral cannot be evaluated directly.

Confusion

  • Gauss's law does not mean the flux through any surface depends only on enclosed charge — it means the NET flux through a CLOSED surface depends only on enclosed charge. The flux through an open surface depends on the field everywhere.
  • The direction of dA is always outward (by convention) for a closed surface. Choosing inward would flip the sign of the answer.
  • Gauss's law tells us the total flux, not the field at each point. To find the field, you need to use symmetry to argue that E is constant over the surface, then divide by area.
  • Inside a charged conductor, E=0, but there is charge on the surface. The net enclosed charge for a Gaussian surface inside the conductor is zero (charges are on the surface, not strictly inside).
  • The unit vector n^ in E⋅n^ is normal to the surface, not to the field line. These two are different when the field is not perpendicular to the surface.

Practice

  • A point charge q=+5μC is placed at the centre of a sphere of radius 0.1 m. Find the electric flux through the sphere. Solution: ΦE​=q/ϵ0​=5×10−6/8.85×10−12=5.65×105 N·m²/C. This is independent of the sphere's radius.
  • A charge of +2μC is at the centre of a cube of side 10 cm. Find the total flux through the cube and through one face. Solution: Total flux Φ=q/ϵ0​=2×10−6/8.85×10−12=2.26×105 N·m²/C. By symmetry, each face carries equal flux: Φface​=Φ/6=3.77×104 N·m²/C.
  • A long cylinder of radius 0.05 m has a uniform charge density λ=4μC/m on its surface. Find the electric field at a distance 0.1 m from the axis. Solution: Use a coaxial Gaussian cylinder of radius r=0.1 m and length L. Φ=E⋅2πrL=λL/ϵ0​, so E=λ/(2πϵ0​r)=2kλ/r=2×9×109×4×10−6/0.1=7.2×105 N/C, directed radially outward.
  • A conducting sphere of radius 0.1 m carries a total charge of +1μC. Find the electric field at (a) the surface, (b) 0.05 m from the centre, (c) 0.2 m from the centre. Solution: (a) At the surface: E=kQ/R2=9×109×10−6/0.01=9×105 N/C. (b) Inside the conductor: E=0. (c) Outside: E=kQ/r2=9×109×10−6/0.04=2.25×105 N/C.
  • A solid sphere of radius 0.1 m has a uniform charge density ρ=10μC/m³. Find the field at a point 0.05 m from the centre. Solution: Charge enclosed within radius r=0.05 m: Qenc​=ρ⋅34​πr3=10−5×34​π×(0.05)3=5.24×10−9 C. By Gauss's law: E⋅4πr2=Qenc​/ϵ0​, so E=kQenc​/r2=9×109×5.24×10−9/0.0025=1.89×104 N/C, directed radially outward.

Universal Facts

  • Gauss's law was formulated by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1835 and is one of Maxwell's four equations of electromagnetism.
  • The total electric flux through a closed surface depends only on the enclosed charge, not on the field or the location of the charge.
  • In a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the field at the surface equals σ/ϵ0​. This is why sharp points on conductors (high σ) produce strong fields and tend to leak charge (corona discharge, lightning rods).
  • Faraday's ice-pail experiment (1843) demonstrated that charges reside on the outside of a conductor, confirming a key consequence of Gauss's law.
  • Gauss's law is more general than Coulomb's law. Coulomb's law assumes a static inverse-square force, but Gauss's law holds even for moving charges and changing fields, when the full set of Maxwell's equations is used.
