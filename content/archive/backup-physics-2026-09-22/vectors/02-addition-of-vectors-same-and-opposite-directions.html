Addition of Vectors — Same and Opposite Directions

Notes

  • Case 1 — same direction: when two vectors P and Q​ point in exactly the same direction (θ=0°), their resultant has maximum magnitude: R=P+Q​ with R=P+Q. The direction is the same as both original vectors.
  • Case 2 — opposite direction: when P and Q​ point in exactly opposite directions (θ=180°), the resultant is R=P−Q​ (assuming P>Q) with magnitude R=∣P−Q∣, acting along the direction of the larger vector.
  • General formula (parallelogram/triangle law): for any angle θ between P and Q​: R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​ and the direction α from P is α=tan−1(P+QcosθQsinθ​).
  • Graphical method (head-to-tail): place the tail of Q​ at the head of P. The resultant R is the vector from the tail of P to the head of Q​. This works for any angle, not just 0° and 180°.
  • Collinear vectors: vectors lying along the same line (same or opposite direction) are called collinear. Their addition reduces to ordinary algebraic addition with appropriate signs: R=±P±Q.
  • Component method for general addition: resolve each vector into x and y components, add components separately: Rx​=Px​+Qx​, Ry​=Py​+Qy​, then R=Rx2​+Ry2​​ and α=tan−1(Ry​/Rx​).
  • Three or more vectors: vector addition is associative: P+(Q​+S)=(P+Q​)+S. The order of addition does not affect the resultant. Use the polygon method for 3+ vectors.
  • Special angles summary: (i) θ=0°: Rmax​=P+Q, α=0°. (ii) θ=90°: R=P2+Q2​, α=tan−1(Q/P). (iii) θ=180°: Rmin​=∣P−Q∣, direction of larger vector. (iv) θ=60°: R=P2+Q2+PQ​. (v) θ=120° with P=Q: R=P=Q.
  • Equilibrium condition: if the resultant of multiple forces is zero (R=0), the system is in equilibrium. For two forces, this means they must be equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
  • The resultant of two equal vectors P=Q inclined at angle θ bisects the angle between them: R=2Pcos(θ/2) and α=θ/2. This is a frequently used shortcut in NEB problems.
  • Triangle inequality: for any two vectors, ∣P−Q∣≤R≤P+Q. The resultant can never exceed the sum of magnitudes nor be less than their difference.
  • Physical example: two people pulling a crate — if both pull east with 50 N each, the resultant is 100 N east. If one pulls east with 50 N and the other west with 30 N, the resultant is 20 N east.
  • Vector subtraction P−Q​ is defined as P+(−Q​), where −Q​ has the same magnitude as Q​ but opposite direction. Graphically, it is the vector from the head of Q​ to the head of P when both tails are at the same point.
  • Order doesn't matter (commutative law): P+Q​=Q​+P. The resultant is the same regardless of which vector you place first in the head-to-tail construction.
  • When θ=90°, the Pythagorean theorem gives R2=P2+Q2 — this is the most common special case in NEB examinations. Students should memorize this form instantly.
  • Vector subtraction from addition: P−Q​=P+(−Q​). The magnitude of the difference is ∣P−Q​∣=P2+Q2−2PQcosθ​. Note the minus sign — it is the same formula with θ replaced by 180°−θ.
  • Resultant of three vectors in a line: for vectors along the same line, assign positive/negative signs based on direction, then add algebraically: R=±P±Q±S. The sign convention is arbitrary but must be consistent.
  • Geometric interpretation of triangle inequality: for any two vectors, the straight-line distance between two points is the shortest path. Thus the magnitude of the resultant (straight line) is always less than or equal to the sum of magnitudes (two sides of triangle): ∣A+B∣≤∣A∣+∣B∣.
  • Adding vectors with unknown angle: if the resultant of two vectors of magnitudes P and Q is R, the angle between them is θ=cos−1(2PQR2−P2−Q2​). This inverse form is frequently tested.
  • Resultant when one vector is doubled: if Q​ is doubled to 2Q​, the new resultant is R′=P+2Q​ with magnitude R′=P2+4Q2+4PQcosθ​. This changes both magnitude and direction of the resultant.
  • Relative velocity is an application of vector subtraction: vA/B​=vA​−vB​. If two cars move in the same direction at 60 km/h and 80 km/h, the relative speed is 20 km/h; if opposite directions, it is 140 km/h.

Confusion

  • Adding vectors is the same as adding numbers — Vectors have direction, so you cannot simply add magnitudes unless they point in the same direction. Two 5 N forces at 120° give a resultant of only 5 N, not 10 N.
  • The resultant is always larger than either component — The resultant can be smaller than one or both components. For opposite directions, R=∣P−Q∣; if P=Q, the resultant is zero.
  • You can add vectors pointing in different directions by just adding their magnitudes — You must use the parallelogram law, triangle law, or component method. The magnitude of the resultant depends critically on the angle between the vectors.
  • If the resultant is zero, the vectors must be zero — Two non-zero vectors can have a zero resultant if they are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction (P+(−P)=0).
  • The angle in the formula is the angle between the tails of the vectors — In the formula R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​, θ is the angle between the vectors when placed tail-to-tail. If given the exterior angle, use 180°−angle.
  • Vector addition is not commutative — Vector addition IS commutative: P+Q​=Q​+P. What is non-commutative is vector multiplication (cross product): A×B=B×A.
  • A zero resultant means no forces are acting — A zero resultant means all forces balance. Multiple forces can act simultaneously with a net effect of zero (equilibrium).
  • The direction of the resultant is always between the two vectors — This is true only for 0°<θ<180°. In the special case of opposite vectors (θ=180°), the resultant lies along the larger vector, not 'between' them.
  • The difference of two vectors has a larger magnitude than their sum — No. For 0°<θ<90°, ∣P−Q​∣<∣P+Q​∣ because cosθ>0. The difference is only larger when θ>90°.
  • You can cancel vectors by just writing them with opposite signs — You must be careful about what 'cancels' what. P+(−P)=0 only if they act at the same point. If they act at different points (non-concurrent forces), they produce a couple, not cancellation.

Practice

  • Two forces of 8 N and 6 N act at a point. Find the resultant when (a) they are in the same direction, (b) they are opposite, (c) they are perpendicular. [Solution: (a) R=8+6=14N, same direction. (b) R=8−6=2N, along the 8 N force. (c) R=64+36​=100​=10N, at tan−1(6/8)=36.9° from the 8 N force.]
  • Two equal forces of 10 N each have a resultant of 10 N. Find the angle between them. [Solution: R=2Pcos(θ/2), so 10=2(10)cos(θ/2), giving cos(θ/2)=1/2, so θ/2=60° and θ=120°.]
  • A force of 20 N acts due east and a force of 15 N acts due north. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant. [Solution: R=202+152​=400+225​=625​=25N. Direction: α=tan−1(15/20)=tan−1(3/4)≈36.9° north of east.]
  • Three forces of 3 N, 4 N, and 5 N act along the sides of a right triangle taken in order. Find their resultant. [Solution: Since 32+42=52, these forces form a closed triangle. By the triangle law, the resultant is R=0. The system is in equilibrium.]
  • Two vectors A=3i^+4j^​ and B=5i^−2j^​ are added. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant. [Solution: R=A+B=8i^+2j^​. R=64+4​=68​≈8.25. α=tan−1(2/8)=tan−1(0.25)≈14.0° from the positive x-axis.]
  • A boat heads north at 12 km/h in a river flowing east at 5 km/h. Find the boat's actual speed and direction relative to the ground. [Solution: vactual​=12j^​+5i^. Speed =144+25​=13km/h. Direction =tan−1(12/5)≈67.4° east of north (or 22.6° north of east).]
  • Find the resultant of two forces P and Q when the angle between them is θ. If θ increases from 0° to 180°, describe qualitatively how R changes. [Solution: R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​. As θ increases, cosθ decreases from 1 to -1, so R decreases from P+Q to ∣P−Q∣. The resultant is maximum when vectors are parallel and minimum when anti-parallel.]
  • Two forces act at a point: F1​=10N at 30° north of east, and F2​=15N at 60° north of west. Find the resultant using component method. [Solution: F1x​=10cos30°=8.66, F1y​=10sin30°=5.00. F2x​=−15cos60°=−7.50, F2y​=15sin60°=12.99. Rx​=1.16, Ry​=17.99. R=1.35+323.64​≈18.0N, α=tan−1(17.99/1.16)≈86.3° north of east.]
  • Three forces of 4 N, 5 N, and 6 N act along the three sides of a triangle taken in order. Find the magnitude and direction of their resultant. [Solution: If taken head-to-tail around a closed triangle, the resultant is R=0. The forces form a closed polygon, so the system is in equilibrium.]
  • A force of 30 N acts at 0°, another at 90°, and a third at 180°. Find the resultant. [Solution: F1​=30i^, F2​=30j^​, F3​=−30i^. R=30i^+30j^​−30i^=30j^​. The resultant is 30 N in the +y direction.]

Universal Facts

  • The parallelogram law of vector addition was first clearly stated by Simon Stevin (1548–1620) in his treatise on statics. He showed that two forces acting at a point are equivalent to a single force represented by the diagonal of the parallelogram.
  • NEB exam priority: addition of collinear vectors (same/opposite direction) is a guaranteed question type. Master the sign convention: assign one direction as positive, the opposite as negative, then add algebraically.
  • The component method of vector addition works in any number of dimensions — 2D, 3D, or even higher-dimensional spaces. This makes it the most versatile method for finding resultants.
  • In navigation and aviation, vector addition is essential. A pilot must account for wind velocity (a vector) to maintain the desired ground track. This is called wind correction angle calculation.
  • The concept of resultant force is central to Newton's second law: Fnet​=ma. The acceleration of an object is determined by the vector sum of all forces acting on it, not by any single force.
  • Force polygons are used in engineering to check equilibrium. If the force vectors drawn head-to-tail form a closed polygon, the resultant is zero and the system is in equilibrium.
  • When resolving vectors for addition, always draw a clear diagram first. Label all angles, choose convenient axes (often along and perpendicular to an incline), and be consistent with sign conventions.
  • For NEB: the formula R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​ applies to ANY two vectors at angle θ. Remember that cos0°=1 gives maximum, cos90°=0 gives Pythagorean, and cos180°=−1 gives minimum.
  • The equilibrant is the vector that balances a system — it equals −R (same magnitude as resultant, opposite direction). For equilibrium, the equilibrant must be applied.
  • In relative motion, vector subtraction is ubiquitous: vA/B​=vA​−vB​ gives the velocity of A as seen from B. This is a direct application of vector subtraction in the same direction (1D) or at angles (2D).
