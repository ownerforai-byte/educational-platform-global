Polygon Law of Vectors

Relevance

Notes

  • Statement: If a number of vectors are represented in magnitude and direction by the sides of a polygon taken in the same order (head-to-tail), their resultant is represented in magnitude and direction by the closing side of the polygon taken in the opposite order.
  • Mathematical form: for n vectors A1​​,A2​​,…,An​​ placed head-to-tail: R=A1​​+A2​​+A3​​+⋯+An​​. The closing side (from the tail of the first to the head of the last) represents R.
  • Derivation from triangle law: applying the triangle law successively: OB=A1​​+A2​​, then OC=OB+A3​​=A1​​+A2​​+A3​​, continuing until R=A1​​+A2​​+⋯+An​​. Each step uses the triangle law.
  • Closed polygon (R=0): if the polygon closes (the last head meets the first tail), the resultant is zero and the system is in equilibrium. This is the polygon of forces condition for equilibrium.
  • For three vectors in equilibrium: the vectors form a closed triangle. This is the triangle of forces theorem: if three forces keep a particle in equilibrium, they can be represented by the sides of a triangle taken in order.
  • Lami's theorem (special case for three forces): if three forces F1​​,F2​​,F3​​ act at a point in equilibrium: sinαF1​​=sinβF2​​=sinγF3​​, where α,β,γ are the angles opposite to each force respectively.
  • Any number of vectors: the polygon law generalizes to any number of coplanar vectors. For 4 vectors, you get a quadrilateral; for 5, a pentagon; and so on. The closing side is always the resultant.
  • Component method alternative: for many vectors, it is more efficient to resolve each into components: Rx​=∑Aix​ and Ry​=∑Aiy​, then R=Rx2​+Ry2​​ and α=tan−1(Ry​/Rx​).
  • Graphical construction: (1) Draw the first vector to scale. (2) From its head, draw the second vector to scale at the correct angle. (3) Continue for all vectors. (4) The closing side from the start to the end is the resultant. (5) Measure its length and angle.
  • Order independence: vector addition is commutative and associative, so the polygon can be drawn in any order — the resultant is the same. A+B+C=B+A+C=C+B+A.
  • Equilibrium condition: a system of coplanar forces is in equilibrium if and only if their vector polygon closes (forms a closed polygon). This means ∑F=0.
  • Resolution method for polygon: resolve all vectors along two perpendicular axes. For equilibrium: ∑Fx​=0 and ∑Fy​=0. These two equations allow solving for two unknowns.
  • The polygon law is essential in structural analysis of trusses and frames. Each joint is treated as a point where multiple force vectors meet, and the polygon must close for static equilibrium.
  • Real-world application: surveyors use the polygon method (called the 'traverse method') to check the accuracy of measured angles and distances. Any closure error indicates measurement mistakes.
  • For a regular polygon with n equal forces acting along its sides, the resultant is zero if the forces are arranged symmetrically. This is because the vectors are equally spaced in direction around the circle.
  • Special case — three equal forces: three equal forces in equilibrium must be at 120° to each other. This follows from the force triangle being equilateral, which requires all angles to be 60° (exterior angles of 120°).
  • The polygon law applies to non-coplanar vectors as well — in 3D, the polygon becomes a spatial polygon. The component method (Rx​,Ry​,Rz​) is preferred for 3D problems.
  • In electrostatics, the polygon law is used to find the net electric field at a point due to multiple charges. Each field vector is drawn head-to-tail, and the resultant gives the total field.
  • When the polygon does not close, the closing side (from endpoint back to starting point) represents the equilibrant — the force needed to bring the system into equilibrium. The equilibrant is equal in magnitude but opposite in direction to the resultant.
  • Equilibrant vs resultant: the resultant R is the single vector that replaces all vectors; the equilibrant E=−R is the vector that balances them to produce equilibrium. They have equal magnitude but opposite direction.
  • Sequential application: for n vectors, apply the triangle law successively: R2​=A1​+A2​, then R3​=R2​+A3​, and so on. The final Rn​ is the resultant of all vectors.
  • Polygon for non-coplanar vectors: in 3D, the polygon law still holds but the polygon is now a spatial (non-planar) polygon. The component method is more practical for 3D: Rx​=∑Aix​, Ry​=∑Aiy​, Rz​=∑Aiz​.
  • Regular polygon equilibrium: n equal forces acting along the sides of a regular n-gon in order have a zero resultant. The vectors are equally spaced by angle 2π/n around the circle, and their sum cancels by symmetry.
  • Polygon of velocities: just as force polygons check equilibrium, velocity polygons check relative motion. A swimmer crossing a river: vground​=vswimmer​+vriver​ forms a velocity triangle.

Confusion

  • The polygon law is a different law from the triangle and parallelogram laws — It is the generalization of both. The triangle law is the polygon law for 2 vectors; the polygon law extends this to any number of vectors.
  • If the polygon doesn't close, the system is in equilibrium — A closed polygon means equilibrium (resultant = 0). An open polygon means the resultant is the closing side. Only a closed polygon indicates equilibrium.
  • The order of vectors in the polygon matters — No. Vector addition is commutative: A+B+C gives the same resultant regardless of order. You can rearrange the polygon sides freely.
  • Lami's theorem applies to any number of forces — Lami's theorem applies ONLY to three forces in equilibrium. For more than three forces, use the polygon law or component resolution.
  • The closing side is the resultant — The closing side TAKEN FROM the tail of the first to the head of the last is the resultant. The closing side TAKEN FROM the head back to the tail is the equilibrant (opposite to the resultant).
  • Polygon law only works for forces — It works for all vectors: displacements, velocities, accelerations, electric fields, magnetic fields. Any vector quantity obeys the polygon law.
  • A regular polygon of forces always means equilibrium — Only if the forces are equal in magnitude and equally spaced in direction. Unequal forces along the sides of a regular polygon generally do not produce equilibrium.
  • You must draw the polygon to scale to get the answer — No. The polygon law is a mathematical principle. You can solve problems analytically using components without any drawing. Graphical methods are for verification and visualization.

Practice

  • Four forces of 10 N, 20 N, 25 N, and 15 N act along the sides of a quadrilateral taken in order. If the quadrilateral closes, show the system is in equilibrium. [Solution: A closed quadrilateral means the vector polygon closes, so R=0. The system is in equilibrium by the polygon law.]
  • Three forces of 5 N, 7 N, and 8 N act at a point and keep it in equilibrium. Find the angles between the forces. [Solution: The forces form a triangle with sides 5, 7, 8. Using cosine rule: cosA=(49+64−25)/(2⋅7⋅8)=88/112=0.7857, so angle opposite 5 N is ≈38.2°. Similarly, angle opposite 7 N ≈61.0°, angle opposite 8 N ≈80.8°. The angles BETWEEN the forces are the supplements: 180°−38.2°=141.8°, 180°−61.0°=119.0°, 180°−80.8°=99.2°.]
  • A body is acted upon by three forces: 3 N east, 4 N north, and 5 N at 53° south of west. Show whether the body is in equilibrium. [Solution: Resolve: Fx​=3+0+5cos(180°−53°)=3−5(0.6018)=3−3.009≈0. Fy​=0+4+5sin(180°−53°)(−1)=4−5(0.7986)=4−3.993≈0. Both components ≈0, so the body is in equilibrium.]
  • Five equal forces act at a point with consecutive angles of 60° between them. Find the resultant. [Solution: The forces are at 0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°. Rx​=F(cos0°+cos60°+cos120°+cos180°+cos240°)=F(1+0.5−0.5−1−0.5)=−0.5F. Ry​=F(sin0°+sin60°+sin120°+sin180°+sin240°)=F(0+0.866+0.866+0−0.866)=0.866F. R=F0.25+0.75​=F. The resultant has magnitude F.]
  • Using Lami's theorem, find the tension in two strings supporting a 100 N weight, if the strings make angles of 30° and 60° with the vertical. [Solution: The angles between forces: between T1 and T2 is 180°−30°−60°=90°, between T1 and W is 180°−60°=120°, between T2 and W is 180°−30°=150°. By Lami: T1​/sin120°=T2​/sin150°=100/sin90°. T1​=100⋅sin120°/1=86.6N. T2​=100⋅sin150°/1=50N.]
  • Three concurrent forces are in equilibrium. The angles between them are 150°, 120°, and 90°. If the smallest force is 20 N, find the other two. [Solution: Smallest force is opposite the largest angle (150°). By Lami's theorem: F1​/sin150°=F2​/sin120°=F3​/sin90°. With F1​=20: 20/0.5=40. So F2​=40sin120°=34.64N, F3​=40sin90°=40N.]
  • A force polygon has sides representing forces of 5 N, 12 N, and 13 N. Show that these forces can be in equilibrium and find the angles between them. [Solution: 52+122=25+144=169=132. This is a right triangle. For equilibrium, the angles between forces are the supplements of the triangle angles: 90°→90°, tan−1(12/5)≈67.4°→112.6°, tan−1(5/12)≈22.6°→157.4°.]
  • Four forces of magnitudes 1 N, 2 N, 3 N, and 4 N act along the sides of a rectangle taken in order. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant. [Solution: Along the rectangle sides (say, 2 units by 1 unit): F1​=1i^, F2​=2j^​, F3​=−3i^, F4​=−4j^​. R=(1−3)i^+(2−4)j^​=−2i^−2j^​. R=4+4​=8​=22​≈2.83N. Direction: 225° from positive x-axis (southwest).]
  • Five forces of 1 N, 2 N, 3 N, 4 N, and 5 N act along the five sides of a regular pentagon taken in order. Find the resultant. [Solution: For a regular pentagon, the exterior angle is 72°. Taking the first side along the x-axis, the five forces are at angles 0°, 72°, 144°, 216°, 288°. Rx​=1+2cos72°+3cos144°+4cos216°+5cos288°=1+0.618−2.427−3.236+1.545=−2.5. Ry​=0+2sin72°+3sin144°+4sin216°+5sin288°=0+1.902+1.754−2.351−4.755=−3.45. R=6.25+11.9​≈4.26N. Direction: tan−1(3.45/2.5)≈54.1° below negative x-axis, or 234.1° from positive x-axis.]
  • Three forces of 3 N, 4 N, and 5 N act at a point. The angle between the 3 N and 4 N forces is 90°. Find the angle between the 5 N force and the resultant of the other two. [Solution: Resultant of 3 N and 4 N at 90° is R34​=5N. Since the third force is also 5 N and the three are in equilibrium, the 5 N force must be opposite to the resultant of the other two. Therefore the angle between the 5 N force and the resultant of 3 N and 4 N is 180° — they are anti-parallel.]

Universal Facts

  • The polygon law of forces is the basis of graphical statics, a method developed in the 19th century by engineers like James Clerk Maxwell and Karl Culmann to analyze structures before the advent of computer-aided design.
  • NEB exam tip: questions on the polygon law often appear as 'show that these forces are in equilibrium' problems. The key is to check if the vector polygon closes (sum of components in both directions equals zero).
  • The concept of a force polygon is used in the graphical analysis of trusses (bridge frameworks). Each joint's forces form a closed polygon, allowing engineers to determine internal forces in each member visually.
  • In navigation, the dead reckoning method uses the polygon law: a ship's position is found by adding successive displacement vectors (course and distance segments) head-to-tail.
  • The closure error in a surveying traverse is a direct application of the polygon law. If the measured sides and angles don't form a closed polygon, the error is distributed among the measurements.
  • Lami's theorem (a corollary of the polygon law for three forces) is named after Bernard Lami (1640-1715), a French mathematician and engineer. It provides a quick shortcut for three-force equilibrium problems.
  • The polygon law extends to three dimensions — spatial force polygons are used in 3D truss analysis. The component method (∑Fx​=0, ∑Fy​=0, ∑Fz​=0) is the algebraic version of the 3D polygon law.
  • When forces are in equilibrium, the polygon closes — this means the work done by the forces in any closed path is also zero (for conservative forces). This connects the polygon law to the concept of conservative force fields.
  • The polygon law is the foundation of finite element analysis (FEA). In FEA, complex structures are broken into simple elements (triangles), and equilibrium at each node is checked using polygon laws.
  • A vector loop in kinematics (e.g., four-bar linkages) uses the polygon law: the sum of displacement vectors around a closed loop equals zero. This is fundamental in mechanism analysis.
