Notes

  • Definition: The cross product (vector product) of two vectors A and B is a vector C=A×B whose magnitude is ABsinθ and whose direction is perpendicular to the plane containing A and B.
  • Magnitude: ∣A×B∣=ABsinθ, where θ is the angle between the two vectors (0°≤θ≤180°).
  • Direction — right-hand rule: Point the fingers of your right hand in the direction of A, then curl them toward B. Your thumb points in the direction of A×B. The result is a vector perpendicular to both A and B.
  • Unit vector form: A×B=ABsinθn^, where n^ is the unit vector perpendicular to the plane of A and B, given by the right-hand rule.
  • Case 1 — Parallel vectors (θ=0° or 180°): A×B=0. Parallel (or anti-parallel) vectors have zero cross product. This is the condition for parallelism: A×B=0⟺A∥B.
  • Case 2 — Perpendicular vectors (θ=90°): ∣A×B∣=AB (maximum possible magnitude). Perpendicular vectors produce the largest cross product.
  • Non-commutative property: A×B=−(B×A). Reversing the order reverses the direction. The cross product is ANTI-COMMUTATIVE, unlike the dot product which is commutative.
  • Cross products of unit vectors: i^×i^=j^​×j^​=k^×k^=0. Cyclic: i^×j^​=k^, j^​×k^=i^, k^×i^=j^​. Anti-cyclic: j^​×i^=−k^, k^×j^​=−i^, i^×k^=−j^​.
  • Distributive property: A×(B+C)=A×B+A×C. The cross product distributes over vector addition.
  • Physical applications: (1) Torque: τ=r×F. (2) Magnetic force: F=qv×B. (3) Angular momentum: L=r×p​. (4) Linear velocity: v=ω×r.
  • NEB exam focus: Define the cross product. State the right-hand rule. Calculate cross products of given vectors. Distinguish between dot product (scalar result) and cross product (vector result). Solve torque and magnetic force problems.
  • Comparison with dot product: Dot product: A⋅B=ABcosθ (scalar, commutative). Cross product: A×B=ABsinθn^ (vector, anti-commutative). The dot product measures "sameness" (projection); the cross product measures "perpendicularity" (area).
  • Geometric interpretation: ∣A×B∣ equals the AREA of the parallelogram formed by A and B. The direction of the cross product is normal to this parallelogram.
  • Cross product and motion: If v is the velocity and r is the position vector, then r×v is twice the areal velocity (area swept per unit time). This is the basis of Kepler's second law.
  • Scalar triple product: A⋅(B×C) gives the volume of the parallelepiped formed by the three vectors. It is cyclic: A⋅(B×C)=B⋅(C×A)=C⋅(A×B).

Confusion

  • ❌ The cross product gives a scalar. ✅ The cross product gives a VECTOR. The result is perpendicular to both input vectors. The dot product gives a scalar; the cross product gives a vector.
  • ❌ A×B=B×A. ✅ The cross product is ANTI-COMMUTATIVE: A×B=−(B×A). Reversing the order flips the direction by 180°.
  • ❌ The cross product is defined for any number of vectors. ✅ The cross product is defined only for EXACTLY TWO vectors in 3D space. You cannot take A×B×C without parentheses — it is not associative.
  • ❌ If A×B=0, then both A and B must be zero vectors. ✅ If A×B=0, then either A=0, B=0, OR A and B are PARALLEL. Any one of these conditions makes the cross product zero.
  • ❌ The right-hand rule is arbitrary and any convention works. ✅ The right-hand rule is a CONVENTION, but it must be consistent throughout physics. Using the left-hand rule would flip the sign of torque, angular momentum, and magnetic force, causing contradictions.
  • ❌ A×(B×C)=(A×B)×C. ✅ The cross product is NOT ASSOCIATIVE. In general, A×(B×C)=(A×B)×C. The vector triple product identity is: A×(B×C)=(A⋅C)B−(A⋅B)C.
  • ❌ The cross product exists in 2D. ✅ The cross product is defined in 3D (and 7D with special structure). In 2D, you can compute a "pseudo-cross product" that gives a scalar (Ax​By​−Ay​Bx​), but this is not a true vector cross product.

Universal Facts

  • The cross product was independently developed by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside in the 1880s as part of their effort to simplify vector calculus for physics applications.
  • The cross product is unique to three dimensions (and seven dimensions with a special construction). In 3D, it is essential for describing rotation, angular momentum, and magnetic forces.
  • In computer graphics, the cross product is used to compute surface normals for lighting calculations. The normal vector determines how light reflects off a 3D surface, creating the illusion of depth and texture.
  • The scalar triple product A⋅(B×C) is used to determine if three vectors are coplanar — if the volume they enclose is zero, the vectors lie in the same plane.
  • In quantum mechanics, the commutation relation [x,px​]=iℏ is analogous to the cross product's anti-commutative property — order matters, and swapping changes the sign.
