Law of Triangle of Vectors — Statement and Derivation

Notes

  • Statement: When two non-zero vectors are represented by two sides of a triangle taken in the same order (head-to-tail), their resultant is represented by the third side of the triangle taken in the opposite order.
  • Setup for derivation: let P and Q​ act at point A, represented by sides AB=P and BC=Q​ of triangle ABC. The resultant is AC=R (the third side taken from A to C, opposite to the order B to C). Let θ be the angle between P and Q​ when placed tail-to-tail.
  • Geometric construction: from C drop a perpendicular CD to the line AB extended. In right triangle BCD: CD=BCsinθ=Qsinθ and BD=BCcosθ=Qcosθ.
  • Applying Pythagoras to triangle ACD: AC2=AD2+CD2=(AB+BD)2+CD2=(P+Qcosθ)2+(Qsinθ)2.
  • Expanding: R2=P2+2PQcosθ+Q2cos2θ+Q2sin2θ=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ(cos2θ+sin2θ).
  • Using sin2θ+cos2θ=1: we get the fundamental result R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​.
  • Direction of resultant: in right triangle ADC, tanα=ADCD​=P+QcosθQsinθ​, so α=tan−1(P+QcosθQsinθ​), where α is the angle between R and P.
  • Special case θ=0° (same direction): R=P2+Q2+2PQ​=P+Q, α=0°. The vectors add algebraically — maximum resultant.
  • Special case θ=90° (perpendicular): R=P2+Q2​, tanα=Q/P. This is the Pythagorean result — the most tested case in NEB.
  • Special case θ=180° (opposite direction): R=P2+Q2−2PQ​=∣P−Q∣, α=0° or 180°. The resultant acts along the larger vector — minimum resultant.
  • Special case P=Q: R=2P2(1+cosθ)​=2Pcos(θ/2) and α=θ/2. The resultant bisects the angle between two equal vectors.
  • Minimum and maximum resultant: Rmax​=P+Q (when θ=0°) and Rmin​=∣P−Q∣ (when θ=180°). For any angle, ∣P−Q∣≤R≤P+Q.
  • Lami's theorem connection: for three forces in equilibrium, each force is proportional to the sine of the angle between the other two: sinαF1​​=sinβF2​​=sinγF3​​. This follows from the triangle law applied to the force triangle.
  • The triangle law is equivalent to the parallelogram law — the diagonal of the parallelogram is the third side of the triangle formed by completing half the parallelogram. Both yield identical results.
  • Graphical construction steps: (1) Draw P to scale from a point. (2) From the head of P, draw Q​ at angle θ. (3) The resultant R is the line from the tail of P to the head of Q​. (4) Measure length and angle.
  • Application to equilibrium: if three vectors form a closed triangle when placed head-to-tail, their resultant is zero and the system is in equilibrium. This is the triangle of forces condition.
  • The triangle law works for ANY vector quantities — forces, velocities, displacements, accelerations, electric fields. The mathematical structure is identical regardless of the physical nature of the vectors.
  • When θ=120° and P=Q, the resultant equals each component: R=P2+P2+2P2(−1/2)​=P2​=P. This is a neat result often tested in NEB exams.
  • The derivation assumes a Euclidean (flat) plane. In curved spaces (general relativity), vector addition follows different rules, but for all Class 11 physics, the flat-plane derivation is exact.
  • Law of sines for vectors: from the triangle formed by P, Q​, R, the sine rule gives sinβP​=sinαQ​=sinθR​ where α and β are the angles opposite Q​ and P respectively.
  • Finding the unknown angle: if three sides of the triangle are known (magnitudes of P, Q​, R), the angle between P and Q​ is θ=cos−1(2PQR2−P2−Q2​).
  • Application to navigation: a ship sailing 30 km on a bearing of 60° then 40 km on a bearing of 150° — the resultant displacement is found using the triangle law with the exterior angle being the change in bearing.
  • Three concurrent forces in equilibrium: if three forces F1​, F2​, F3​ act at a point in equilibrium, they form a closed triangle when drawn head-to-tail. Each side represents the magnitude, and each angle inside the triangle relates to the angle between the corresponding force directions.
  • Minimum force to maintain equilibrium: when a weight W is suspended by two strings making angles α and β with the horizontal, the tensions can be found from the force triangle. The tension is minimum when the strings are perpendicular to each other.

Confusion

  • The angle θ in the formula is the angle inside the triangle — θ is the angle between the two vectors when placed tail-to-tail, NOT the interior angle of the triangle. If the interior angle is ϕ, then θ=180°−ϕ.
  • The resultant always points between the two vectors — This is only true for 0°<θ<180°. When vectors are opposite (θ=180°), the resultant lies along the larger vector, not between them.
  • Triangle law only works for forces — Triangle law applies to ALL vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration, electric field, magnetic field, momentum. Any quantity that is a vector obeys this law.
  • If the triangle doesn't close, the vectors are wrong — An open triangle means the resultant is the closing side. The triangle law says the resultant IS the third side taken in the opposite order — the triangle is open by design.
  • The direction formula tanα=Qsinθ/(P+Qcosθ) is only for the triangle law — This formula is actually derived from the triangle law but is identical to the one from the parallelogram law. Both methods give the same α.
  • A zero resultant means the triangle has no area — A zero resultant means the two vectors are equal and opposite (P=Q, θ=180°), forming a degenerate triangle (a line segment), not a triangle with zero area in the usual sense.
  • You can use the triangle law for more than two vectors directly — The triangle law is specifically for two vectors. For three or more, you apply it successively or use the polygon law. A+B+C requires two applications of the triangle law.
  • The order of vectors in the triangle matters for the resultant's direction — The triangle law is commutative: P+Q​=Q​+P. Swapping the order just reflects the triangle but gives the same resultant vector.
  • The interior angles of the force triangle are the same as the angles between the force vectors — No. The interior angle of the triangle at a vertex equals 180° minus the angle between the two forces meeting at that point (when drawn head-to-tail).
  • If three vectors form a triangle, they are automatically in equilibrium — Only if they are drawn head-to-tail AND the polygon closes (head of last meets tail of first). If they simply form a geometric triangle but are all pointing in the same rotational direction around the triangle, their resultant is not zero.

Practice

  • Two forces of 5 N and 12 N act at a point with an angle of 90° between them. Find the resultant and its direction. [Solution: R=25+144​=169​=13N. tanα=12/5, so α=tan−1(2.4)≈67.4° from the 5 N force.]
  • Two forces of 10 N each act at a point with an angle of 60° between them. Find the resultant. [Solution: R=100+100+200cos60°​=200+100​=300​=103​≈17.32N. α=tan−1(10sin60°/(10+10cos60°))=tan−1(8.66/15)≈30°. The resultant bisects the angle.]
  • Find the resultant of two forces 8 N and 6 N acting at an angle of 120°. [Solution: R=64+36+96cos120°​=100−48​=52​≈7.21N. tanα=8+6cos120°6sin120°​=55.20​≈1.04, so α≈46.1°.]
  • Three forces of 3 N, 4 N, and 5 N act at a point. If the 3 N and 4 N forces are perpendicular, show that the system is in equilibrium when the 5 N force is opposite to their resultant. [Solution: Resultant of 3 N and 4 N at 90°: R=9+16​=5N. The 5 N force acting in the opposite direction gives Rtotal​=5−5=0. Equilibrium confirmed.]
  • Two vectors A and B have magnitudes 7 N and 9 N. What is the range of possible resultant magnitudes? [Solution: Rmin​=∣9−7∣=2N (when opposite), Rmax​=9+7=16N (when same direction). Range: 2N≤R≤16N.]
  • Two equal forces F each have a resultant of F3​. Find the angle between them. [Solution: R=2Fcos(θ/2), so F3​=2Fcos(θ/2), giving cos(θ/2)=3​/2, so θ/2=30° and θ=60°.]
  • A displacement of 3 km is made east, then 4 km at 60° north of east. Find the resultant displacement using the triangle law. [Solution: R2=9+16+24cos60°=25+12=37, so R=37​≈6.08km. tanα=3+4cos60°4sin60°​=53.464​≈0.693, α≈34.7° north of east.]
  • If the resultant of two vectors P and Q​ is perpendicular to P, show that Qcosθ=−P where θ is the angle between them. [Solution: If R⊥P, then α=90° and tanα is undefined. From tanα=Qsinθ/(P+Qcosθ), the denominator must be zero: P+Qcosθ=0, giving cosθ=−P/Q. This requires Q>P.]
  • Two forces of 15 N and 20 N act at a point. The resultant is 25 N. Find the angle between the forces. [Solution: R2=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ. 625=225+400+600cosθ. 625=625+600cosθ, so cosθ=0 and θ=90°. The forces are perpendicular.]
  • A force of 10 N is resolved into two components along directions that make angles of 30° and 60° with the force. Find the magnitudes of the components. [Solution: Using the law of sines in the force triangle: sin90°F​=sin60°F1​​=sin30°F2​​. So F1​=10sin60°=8.66N and F2​=10sin30°=5N.]

Universal Facts

  • The triangle law of vectors is one of the oldest results in mechanics. It appears in Euclid's Elements (Book I, Proposition 26) in geometric form, and was adapted to physics by Stevin in 1586 for analyzing forces on inclined planes.
  • NEB exam pattern: triangle law derivation + numerical resultant problems account for approximately 8-12 marks in the physics paper. Students who can derive R=P2+Q2+2PQcosθ​ from first principles typically score full marks.
  • The law of cosines in trigonometry (c2=a2+b2−2abcosC) is the mathematical backbone of the triangle law. The only difference is the sign: in the triangle law, the interior angle is 180°−θ, and cos(180°−θ)=−cosθ, flipping the sign.
  • In structural engineering, triangle law is used to analyze trusses — frameworks of triangles that distribute loads. Each joint is in equilibrium, so the forces form closed triangles.
  • The triangle law is directly related to Lami's theorem, which states: if three forces keep a particle in equilibrium, each force is proportional to the sine of the angle between the other two. This is derived by applying the sine rule to the force triangle.
  • When two vectors are perpendicular (θ=90°), the triangle law reduces to the Pythagorean theorem. This special case is so common in NEB problems that students should recognize it instantly.
  • Vector addition via the triangle law is how GPS systems calculate your position: combining the satellite signal vectors (displacement from each satellite) gives your position vector relative to Earth's center.
  • The triangle law fails only when vectors are not free vectors (bound vectors tied to specific points) or in non-Euclidean geometries. For all Class 11 physics problems in flat space, it is universally valid.
  • The sine rule for any triangle: sinAa​=sinBb​=sinCc​=2Rcircum​, where Rcircum​ is the circumradius. In vector triangles, this relates force magnitudes to the angles between them.
  • In statics of rigid bodies, the triangle law combined with Lami's theorem provides a complete solution for any three-force system in equilibrium, eliminating the need for component resolution in simple cases.
