Multiplication of Vectors — Scalar (Dot) Product

Notes

  • Definition: the scalar product (or dot product) of two vectors a and b is a⋅b=∣a∣∣b∣cosθ, where θ is the angle between them (0°≤θ≤180°). The result is a scalar quantity.
  • Geometric interpretation: a⋅b=a(bcosθ)=b(acosθ) — the product of one vector's magnitude and the projection of the other onto it. If θ<90°, the projection is positive; if θ>90°, the projection is negative.
  • Physical significance: the dot product of force and displacement gives work: W=F⋅d=Fdcosθ. Only the component of force along the displacement does work; perpendicular components contribute nothing.
  • Commutative property: a⋅b=b⋅a. The order of vectors in a dot product does not matter. This contrasts with the cross product, which is anti-commutative.
  • Distributive property: a⋅(b+c)=a⋅b+a⋅c. This is essential when working with vectors expressed as sums.
  • Case 1 — perpendicular vectors: if θ=90°, a⋅b=abcos90°=0. This is the condition for two vectors to be perpendicular (orthogonal). It is a powerful test: a⋅b=0 implies perpendicularity (for non-zero vectors).
  • Case 2 — parallel vectors: if θ=0°, a⋅b=abcos0°=ab. Maximum value of the dot product; the two vectors are in the same direction.
  • Case 3 — anti-parallel vectors: if θ=180°, a⋅b=abcos180°=−ab. Minimum (most negative) value; vectors point in exactly opposite directions.
  • Dot products of standard unit vectors: i^⋅i^=j^​⋅j^​=k^⋅k^=1 (parallel to themselves), i^⋅j^​=j^​⋅k^=k^⋅i^=0 (mutually perpendicular).
  • Component-form formula: for A=A1​i^+A2​j^​+A3​k^ and B=B1​i^+B2​j^​+B3​k^, expanding gives A⋅B=A1​B1​+A2​B2​+A3​B3​ (the sum of products of corresponding components).
  • Derivation of component form: A⋅B=(A1​i^+A2​j^​+A3​k^)⋅(B1​i^+B2​j^​+B3​k^)=A1​B1​(i^⋅i^)+A1​B2​(i^⋅j^​)+⋯=A1​B1​+A2​B2​+A3​B3​. The cross terms vanish because of orthogonality.
  • Finding angle from dot product: cosθ=∣A∣∣B∣A⋅B​=A12​+A22​+A32​​B12​+B22​+B32​​A1​B1​+A2​B2​+A3​B3​​. This is a standard application.
  • Properties summary: (1) a⋅b=b⋅a (commutative). (2) a⋅(b+c)=a⋅b+a⋅c (distributive). (3) a⋅a=∣a∣2≥0 (positive semi-definite). (4) a⋅b=0⟺a⊥b (orthogonality test, for non-zero vectors).
  • Work as a dot product: W=F⋅d. If F is perpendicular to d (e.g., centripetal force on circular motion), W=0. This is why the speed of a satellite in circular orbit doesn't change due to gravity.
  • Power as a dot product: instantaneous power P=F⋅v. For a constant force, P=Fvcosθ, where θ is the angle between F and v.
  • Magnetic flux: the flux of magnetic field through an area is Φ=B⋅A=BAcosθ, where θ is the angle between B and the area vector A (perpendicular to the surface).
  • Finding the component of A along B: AB​=A⋅B^=A⋅B/∣B∣. The signed magnitude of the projection of A on the direction of B.
  • Cauchy-Schwarz inequality: ∣a⋅b∣≤∣a∣∣b∣. This is a direct consequence of ∣cosθ∣≤1. Equality holds when a and b are parallel.
  • Triangle inequality for dot product: (a+b)⋅(a+b)=∣a∣2+∣b∣2+2a⋅b, leading to ∣a+b∣2=∣a∣2+∣b∣2+2∣a∣∣b∣cosθ, which is the cosine rule for the resultant of two vectors.
  • Sign of dot product: a⋅b>0 if θ<90° (acute angle), =0 if θ=90°, <0 if θ>90° (obtuse angle). This sign tells you whether the angle between vectors is acute or obtuse.

Confusion

  • Dot product is just multiplication of magnitudes — Dot product is ∣a∣∣b∣cosθ, NOT just ∣a∣∣b∣. The cosine factor accounts for direction. If a⊥b, the dot product is 0 even though both are non-zero.
  • Dot product is commutative only sometimes — Dot product is ALWAYS commutative: a⋅b=b⋅a. There are no exceptions for non-zero vectors.
  • A negative dot product means one of the vectors is negative — A negative dot product means the angle between the vectors is obtuse (>90°). Both vectors can be entirely positive (e.g., 5 N and 10 N at 120° give −25).
  • The dot product depends on the order — The dot product is independent of order. A⋅B=B⋅A. Only the cross product has order dependence.
  • The dot product formula a⋅b=abcosθ works for parallel vectors too — Yes, for θ=0°, cos0°=1 and a⋅b=ab. The formula is general for all angles.
  • A larger dot product means the vectors are more parallel — Yes, the dot product increases as θ decreases. Maximum at θ=0° (parallel), minimum at θ=180° (anti-parallel, negative dot product), zero at θ=90°.
  • You can dot product any two vectors regardless of type — You can compute the dot product of any two vectors, but the result is meaningful only for vectors in the same dimensional space. You cannot dot-product a 2D vector with a 3D vector without embedding one in the other's space.
  • A⋅A is the same as A⋅∣A∣ — A⋅A=∣A∣2 (magnitude squared), not the product of magnitude and magnitude. The dot product of a vector with itself is always a non-negative scalar.

Universal Facts

  • The dot product was introduced by Hermann Grassmann in 1844 and independently by William Kingdon Clifford in 1878. It is the foundation of the inner product in linear algebra, generalizing to higher dimensions.
  • Work-energy theorem in its vector form: W=F⋅d=ΔK. The dot product of force and displacement gives the work, which equals the change in kinetic energy.
  • NEB exam tip: problems involving the dot product of 2D/3D vectors are very common. The fastest method is the component form A⋅B=Ax​Bx​+Ay​By​+Az​Bz​. Then find the angle using cosθ=A⋅B/(∣A∣∣B∣).
  • In electromagnetism, the dot product appears everywhere: electric potential V=E⋅d for a uniform field, magnetic flux Φ=B⋅A, and power delivered P=F⋅v.
  • The dot product of normal force with velocity is always zero (since they are perpendicular). This is why normal forces do no work — they cannot change kinetic energy, only redirect motion.
  • In 3D graphics, the dot product is used to determine whether two surfaces face each other (dot product of normals > 0) or away from each other (< 0). This is critical for lighting calculations (Lambert's cosine law).
  • The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (∣a⋅b∣≤∣a∣∣b∣) is one of the most powerful inequalities in mathematics. It follows directly from ∣cosθ∣≤1 in the dot product formula.
  • The dot product is the basis of linear regression in statistics and machine learning: the projection of data onto a direction vector (a unit vector) extracts the component of data along that direction.
