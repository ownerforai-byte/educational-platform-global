Types of Vectors

Notes

  • Like (parallel) vectors: vectors having the same direction, regardless of their magnitudes. They can be written as A=aB where a is a positive scalar. Like vectors add by ordinary arithmetic: their resultant is also parallel to them.
  • Unlike (anti-parallel) vectors: vectors having exactly opposite directions. If A and B are anti-parallel, A=−aB where a is a positive scalar. The dot product of two anti-parallel vectors is −∣A∣∣B∣.
  • Co-planar vectors: vectors that lie in the same plane. Any two vectors are always coplanar. Three vectors are coplanar if their vector sum or the scalar triple product A⋅(B×C)=0.
  • Non-coplanar (skew) vectors: vectors that do not lie in the same plane. Three vectors are non-coplanar if they span 3D space. The minimum number of non-coplanar vectors needed to span 3D is three.
  • Null (zero) vector 0: a vector with zero magnitude and indeterminate direction. It is the additive identity: A+0=A. The zero vector is parallel to every vector, since 0A=0.
  • Unit vector: a vector of magnitude 1 in a specified direction, denoted with a hat/circumflex: n^, A^. For any vector A, the unit vector along it is A^=A/∣A∣.
  • Standard unit vectors i^,j^​,k^: unit vectors along the x, y, and z axes of a Cartesian coordinate system. They are mutually perpendicular: i^⋅j^​=j^​⋅k^=k^⋅i^=0 and i^×j^​=k^ (right-hand rule).
  • Position vector: the vector from the origin to a point, denoted r. If the point is (x,y,z), then r=xi^+yj^​+zk^. It specifies the location of a point relative to the origin.
  • Equal vectors: two vectors A and B are equal if they have the same magnitude AND the same direction. They can be drawn from different points but are physically equivalent: A=B implies ∣A∣=∣B∣ and parallel with same orientation.
  • Negative vector: −A is the vector with the same magnitude as A but opposite direction. A+(−A)=0. Useful in problems like relative velocity and force balance.
  • Collinear vectors: vectors lying along the same line, regardless of direction. They are either parallel or anti-parallel. Collinear vectors add algebraically with appropriate signs.
  • Axial vector (pseudovector): vectors representing quantities like angular velocity, torque, and magnetic field. They change sign under reflection (mirror inversion). In contrast, polar vectors (force, velocity) follow normal vector addition.
  • Free vector: a vector that can be translated parallel to itself without changing its physical meaning. Examples: velocity, force. In contrast, a bound (localized) vector is tied to a specific point (e.g., moment of a force about a point).
  • Basis vectors: a set of linearly independent vectors that span the entire vector space. For 3D, {i^,j^​,k^} is the standard basis. Any vector in 3D can be written as a linear combination of basis vectors.
  • Orthogonal vectors: vectors that are perpendicular to each other. The dot product of orthogonal vectors is zero: A⋅B=0 for perpendicular A,B.
  • Mutually perpendicular vectors: a set of vectors where each is perpendicular to every other. {i^,j^​,k^} is the canonical example in 3D. The magnitude of any vector is the square root of the sum of squares of components along such a basis.
  • Solved example: two vectors A and B are equal if A=B. But 'parallel' only requires same direction, allowing different magnitudes. So parallel does not imply equal, but equal implies parallel.
  • Collinear three-vector property: if A,B,C are collinear, then C=aA+bB for some scalars a,b (and conversely, three collinear vectors are linearly dependent).
  • Vector notation distinction: bold A and arrow A both denote vectors. Magnitude is ∣A∣, ∣A∣, or simply A. Unit vectors are a^ or u^ (with hat).
  • Practical applications: in physics, identifying the type of vector helps in selecting the correct operation. Force is a polar vector (adds via parallelogram law), torque is an axial vector (different rules for reflection).
  • Contravariant vs covariant vectors: in advanced vector analysis, vectors are classified by how their components transform under coordinate changes. Contravariant components transform opposite to basis vectors; covariant components transform the same way. This distinction is crucial in tensor analysis and general relativity.
  • Basis-independent definition: a vector is fundamentally defined as an object that transforms in a specific way under coordinate rotations. The component representation A=Ax​i^+Ay​j^​+Az​k^ is basis-dependent, but the vector itself is a geometric object independent of coordinates.
  • Linear dependence and independence: a set of vectors is linearly independent if no vector in the set can be written as a linear combination of the others. In 3D, at most 3 vectors can be linearly independent. Four or more vectors in 3D are always linearly dependent.
  • Zero vector and linear dependence: the zero vector is always linearly dependent with any set of vectors (since 0⋅A+0⋅B+1⋅0=0). Any set containing the zero vector is linearly dependent.
  • Coplanarity test for three vectors: three vectors A, B, C are coplanar if and only if their scalar triple product is zero: A⋅(B×C)=0. This is equivalent to the volume of the parallelepiped they form being zero.

Confusion

  • Like vectors are equal — Like vectors are parallel (same direction), but their magnitudes can differ. Equal vectors require both same direction and same magnitude. A=2i^ and B=5i^ are like vectors but not equal.
  • The null vector has no direction at all — The null vector has indeterminate direction, meaning we cannot assign a unique direction to it. By convention, it is treated as parallel to every vector.
  • All vectors in 3D are coplanar — Any TWO vectors are coplanar, but three vectors can be non-coplanar (skew). Four or more vectors can always be reduced to a set in 3D, with at most 3 linearly independent members.
  • Unit vectors are unique for each direction — There are infinitely many unit vectors for the same direction (any vector of magnitude 1 in that direction). However, for a given direction, the unit vector is unique up to sign.
  • Co-planar and parallel mean the same thing — Parallel vectors must lie along the same line (collinear). Coplanar vectors only need to be in the same plane; they can point in different directions within that plane.
  • Anti-parallel vectors are like vectors with a sign — Anti-parallel vectors point in OPPOSITE directions. Like vectors point in the SAME direction. They are fundamentally different categories.
  • Equal vectors must start at the same point — No. Free vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and direction, regardless of position. The position of the vector's tail or head does not affect its equality.
  • Position vector and displacement vector are the same — Position vector r is from origin to a point. Displacement vector Δr is the change in position from one point to another. They are related but not identical.
  • The negative of a vector changes its magnitude — No. The negative vector −A has the SAME magnitude as A but the OPPOSITE direction. Only the direction changes.
  • All unit vectors are the same — No. Each unit vector points in a different direction. i^, j^​, and k^ are all unit vectors but point in different (mutually perpendicular) directions. There are infinitely many unit vectors, one for each direction.

  • State which of the following pairs of vectors are like, unlike, coplanar, or equal: (a) A=3i^, B=7i^. (b) A=4j^​, B=−6j^​. (c) A=2i^+3j^​, B=2i^+3j^​. [Solution: (a) like, coplanar, not equal (different magnitudes). (b) unlike, coplanar, not equal. (c) equal (and therefore like, coplanar, parallel).]
  • Find the unit vector along A=3i^+4j^​. [Solution: ∣A∣=9+16​=5. A^=A/∣A∣=(3/5)i^+(4/5)j^​.]
  • Are the vectors A=2i^+3j^​+4k^, B=i^−2j^​+k^, C=3i^+j^​+5k^ coplanar? [Solution: Compute the scalar triple product A⋅(B×C). First, B×C=i^((−2)(5)−(1)(1))−j^​((1)(5)−(1)(3))+k^((1)(1)−(−2)(3))=−11i^−2j^​+7k^. Then A⋅(B×C)=2(−11)+3(−2)+4(7)=−22−6+28=0. Since the scalar triple product is 0, the vectors are coplanar.]
  • Show that the zero vector 0 is parallel to every vector. [Solution: For any A, the zero vector can be written as 0=0⋅A. The scalar multiplier is 0, which is a non-negative real number. So 0 is parallel to A by the definition of parallelism. Also, 0 has no specific direction, so the convention treats it as parallel to all directions.]
  • If A and B are unit vectors making 60° with each other, find A+B. [Solution: ∣A+B∣=1+1+2cos60°​=2+1​=3​. Direction: bisects the angle, so 30° from each. A+B=3​n^, where n^ is the unit vector at 30° from both.]
  • Determine whether the vectors A=3i^−2j^​+k^, B=i^−3j^​+5k^, C=2i^−j^​+3k^ are coplanar. [Solution: B×C=i^((−3)(3)−(5)(−1))−j^​((1)(3)−(5)(2))+k^((1)(−1)−(−3)(2))=−4i^+7j^​+5k^. A⋅(B×C)=3(−4)+(−2)(7)+1(5)=−12−14+5=−21=0. Not coplanar.]
  • Express the vector F=6i^+8j^​ in terms of its magnitude and a unit vector along it. [Solution: ∣F∣=36+64​=10. F^=(6/10)i^+(8/10)j^​=0.6i^+0.8j^​. F=10(0.6i^+0.8j^​)=10F^.]
  • Given A=2i^−3j^​ and B=−4i^+6j^​, show that the vectors are anti-parallel. [Solution: B=−2(2i^−3j^​)=−2A. The negative scalar indicates opposite direction. So A and B are anti-parallel. Verify: B/A=∣−4i^+6j^​∣/∣2i^−3j^​∣=52​/13​=2. The ratio is negative, confirming anti-parallel.]
  • Find three unit vectors that are mutually perpendicular to each other. [Solution: The standard unit vectors i^, j^​, k^ are mutually perpendicular: i^⋅j^​=0, j^​⋅k^=0, k^⋅i^=0. Any rotation of this set also gives three mutually perpendicular unit vectors.]
  • Show that the vectors A=i^+j^​, B=−i^+j^​+k^, and C=B−A=−2i^+k^ form a triangle. Find its type and area. [Solution: A+C=i^+j^​−2i^+k^=−i^+j^​+k^=B, so the triangle closes. Magnitudes: ∣A∣=2​, ∣B∣=3​, ∣C∣=5​. Check: 2+3=5, so ∣A∣2+∣B∣2=∣C∣2 — it is a right-angled triangle with the right angle between A and B. Verify: A⋅B=−1+1+0=0. Area =21​∣A∣∣B∣=21​2​3​=26​​≈1.225.]
  • If A and B are unit vectors with A⋅B=1/2, find the angle between them and the magnitude of A+B. [Solution: A⋅B=∣A∣∣B∣cosθ=cosθ=1/2, so θ=60°. ∣A+B∣=1+1+2cos60°​=2+1​=3​.]
  • Three mutually perpendicular unit vectors u^1​, u^2​, u^3​ form a basis for 3D space. Express an arbitrary vector A=5i^+12j^​ in terms of this basis. [Solution: The standard basis i^,j^​,k^ IS a set of three mutually perpendicular unit vectors. A is already expressed in this basis: Ax​=5, Ay​=12, Az​=0. The magnitude is ∣A∣=25+144​=13.]

Universal Facts

  • The classification of vectors into types (like/unlike, coplanar/non-coplanar, polar/axial) is essential for choosing the right operation. Like vectors add algebraically; non-coplanar vectors require 3D analysis.
  • NEB exam pattern: 'types of vectors' questions are usually short answer or fill-in-the-blank format. Common question: 'define and give an example of unit vector/like vector/co-planar vector'.
  • The right-hand rule for unit vectors i^,j^​,k^ is essential for cross products: i^×j^​=k^, j^​×k^=i^, k^×i^=j^​. This cyclic order defines the orientation of 3D space.
  • Polarity of vectors (polar vs axial) matters in electromagnetism. Electric field E is a polar vector, magnetic field B is axial. The Lorentz force F=qv×B produces a polar force from the cross product of two vectors of different polarities.
  • The zero vector is unique in that it is the only vector with indeterminate direction. It is the additive identity, and it is parallel to every vector (since 0=0⋅A for any A).
  • In linear algebra, the concept of basis vectors generalizes i^,j^​,k^ to any set of linearly independent vectors. The 3D space we work in has dimension 3, requiring exactly 3 linearly independent basis vectors.
  • The displacement vector Δr=rf​−ri​ is a fundamental concept in kinematics. It depends only on initial and final positions, not on the path taken — this is why the work-energy theorem works.
  • Co-planar vectors simplify problems enormously: any problem with three or more vectors that are coplanar can be analyzed in 2D using x and y components. Non-coplanar vectors require full 3D analysis.
  • The concept of linear independence is fundamental: three non-coplanar vectors form a basis for 3D space. Any vector in 3D can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of three linearly independent vectors.
  • Vector spaces in physics: the set of all displacement vectors in 3D space forms a vector space. Similarly, the set of all force vectors forms a vector space. Understanding vector types helps identify which vector space a physical quantity belongs to.
