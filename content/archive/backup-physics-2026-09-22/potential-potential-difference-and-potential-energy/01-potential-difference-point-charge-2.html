Potential Difference and Point Charge

Notes

  • Potential difference: the work done per unit positive charge in moving a test charge between two points: ΔV=VB​−VA​=WAB​/q. Units: volt (V) = J/C.
  • Potential due to a point charge: V=kQ/r=4πϵ0​1​rQ​. This is a scalar, so for multiple charges the potentials add algebraically: Vtotal​=∑ri​kQi​​. By convention, V=0 at infinity.
  • Potential energy of two point charges: U=krq1​q2​​. Positive U means repulsive (like charges); negative U means attractive (opposite charges). This energy is stored in the field configuration.
  • Potential energy of a system of charges: sum over all unique pairs: U=∑i<j​rij​kqi​qj​​. Work done to assemble the charges from infinity to their positions.
  • Relation between electric field and potential: E=−∇V=−drdV​r^ (radial). The field points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential. Magnitude: E=−dV/dr.
  • Potential difference in terms of field: VB​−VA​=−∫AB​E⋅dl. The negative sign means moving against the field increases potential.
  • Equipotential surfaces: surfaces where V is constant. No work is done moving a charge along an equipotential. Electric field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.
  • Electron volt (eV): the kinetic energy gained by an electron (charge e=1.602×10−19 C) when accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V. 1eV=1.602×10−19J. Useful unit for atomic-scale energies.
  • Potential of a charged conducting sphere: outside (r≥R): V=kQ/r. At the surface: V=kQ/R. Inside (r<R): V=kQ/R (constant, same as surface). The entire conductor is an equipotential.
  • Potential of an electric dipole: V=r2kpcosθ​ for r≫d, where p=qd is the dipole moment. Along the axis (heta=0): V=kp/r2. In the equatorial plane (heta=90°): V=0.
  • Work done by electric field: W=−ΔU=q(VA​−VB​). The field does positive work when a positive charge moves to lower potential (or a negative charge moves to higher potential).
  • Superposition for potential: since potential is a scalar, Vtotal​=∑Vi​. This makes potential easier to calculate than field (vector) for complex charge distributions.
  • Potential of continuous distributions: V=∫rkdq​. For a line charge: V=∫rkλdl​. For a surface: V=∫rkσdA​. For a volume: V=∫rkρdV​.
  • Zero of potential: the choice of V=0 at infinity is conventional. Only potential differences are physically meaningful. You can add a constant to all potentials without changing the physics.
  • Potential energy of a charge in an external field: U=qV, where V is the potential due to all other charges (not including the charge itself). For a dipole in uniform field: U=−p​⋅E=−pEcosθ.
  • Electron volt in practice: ionisation energy of hydrogen = 13.6 eV. Work function of metals ∼ 2-5 eV. Thermal energy at room temperature kB​T≈0.025 eV. Energy gap in silicon = 1.1 eV.

Confusion

  • Potential is a scalar, field is a vector. You add potentials as numbers, but add fields as vectors. This makes potential calculations much simpler for multiple charges.
  • Potential energy U=qV is the energy of a test charge q in the potential V created by OTHER charges. Do not include the self-energy of q in its own potential.
  • The electron volt is a unit of ENERGY, not voltage. 1eV=1.602×10−19 J. It is the energy an electron gains moving through 1 V potential difference.
  • Potential difference ΔV=VB​−VA​=−∫AB​E⋅dl. The negative sign means the field points from higher to lower potential. A positive charge accelerates toward lower potential.
  • Inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the potential is CONSTANT, not zero. The entire conductor (including any cavities) is an equipotential volume.
  • For a point charge, V=kQ/r and E=kQ/r2. Note E=−dV/dr, so V falls off as 1/r while E falls off as 1/r2. They are not independent — knowing one determines the other.
  • The work done BY the electric field is W=−ΔU=q(VA​−VB​). The work done BY AN EXTERNAL AGENT against the field is Wext​=+ΔU=q(VB​−VA​). These have opposite signs.
  • Potential of a dipole falls off as 1/r2, faster than a point charge (1/r). At large distances, the dipole potential dominates only if the net charge is zero.
  • In a circuit, the potential difference across a battery is its EMF (when no current flows) or terminal voltage V=E−Ir (when current flows). This is different from the electrostatic potential of isolated charges.

Practice

  • Two charges q1​=+3μC at (0,0) and q2​=−3μC at (0.1m,0) form a dipole. Find the potential at (0.05m,0.05m). Solution: r1​=r2​=0.052+0.052​=0.0707 m. V1​=9×109×3×10−6/0.0707=3.82×105 V. V2​=−3.82×105 V. Total V=0 V (point is on equatorial plane of dipole).
  • A point charge q=+2μC is at the origin. How much work is needed to bring a charge q0​=+1μC from infinity to a point 0.1 m away? Solution: W=q0​V=q0​⋅kq/r=10−6×9×109×2×10−6/0.1=0.18 J. This equals the potential energy of the pair.
  • An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of 500 V. Find its kinetic energy in joules and eV, and its final speed. Solution: K=500eV=500×1.602×10−19=8.01×10−17 J. K=21​mv2, so v=2K/m​=2×8.01×10−17/9.11×10−31​=1.33×107 m/s.
  • Four charges +q are placed at the corners of a square of side a. Find the potential energy of the system. Solution: Pairs: 4 edges with separation a, 2 diagonals with separation a2​. U=4×akq2​+2×a2​kq2​=akq2​(4+2​). For q=1μC, a=0.1 m: U=9×109×10−12/0.1×5.414=0.487 J.
  • A proton and an electron are separated by 0.53 Å (Bohr radius). Find the potential energy of the system in eV. Solution: U=−ke2/r=−9×109×(1.602×10−19)2/(0.53×10−10)=−4.36×10−18 J = −27.2 eV. This is the ground state energy of hydrogen atom.
  • A parallel plate capacitor has plates of area 0.01 m2 separated by 2 mm. It is charged to 100 V. Find the energy stored and the energy density. Solution: C=ϵ0​A/d=8.85×10−12×0.01/0.002=4.425×10−11 F. U=21​CV2=0.5×4.425×10−11×10000=2.21×10−7 J. E=V/d=5×104 V/m. u=21​ϵ0​E2=0.5×8.85×10−12×25×108=1.11×10−2 J/m3.
  • A charge of +5μC is moved from a point at 10 V to a point at 50 V. Find the work done by the electric field and by an external agent. Solution: ΔV=40 V. Wfield​=q(VA​−VB​)=5×10−6×(−40)=−0.2 mJ. Wext​=+0.2 mJ. The field does negative work (the charge moves to higher potential).
  • Find the potential at the centre of a uniformly charged ring of radius R=0.1 m and total charge Q=1μC. Solution: All charge elements are at distance R from centre. V=kQ/R=9×109×10−6/0.1=9×104 V. Note: the field at the centre is zero by symmetry, but potential is not zero.

Universal Facts

  • The electron volt is the standard unit for atomic, nuclear, and particle physics energies. 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The proton rest energy is 938 MeV; electron rest energy is 511 keV.
  • The potential of Earth is conventionally taken as zero. A 12 V car battery means its positive terminal is 12 V higher than its negative terminal, which is connected to the car chassis (ground).
  • In a thundercloud, the potential difference between cloud and ground can reach 100 million volts. The electric field before a lightning strike can exceed 3 × 10⁶ V/m (breakdown field of air).
  • The electric potential inside a living cell membrane is about -70 mV relative to the outside. This membrane potential is maintained by ion pumps and is crucial for nerve impulses and muscle contraction.
  • A Van de Graaff generator can produce potentials of several million volts. The maximum potential is limited by corona discharge — when the field at the surface exceeds the breakdown strength of air (~3 MV/m), charge leaks into the air.
  • In a CRT (old TV/monitor), electrons are accelerated through 10-30 kV. Their kinetic energy is 10-30 keV, corresponding to speeds ~0.1-0.3c. This is a classic application of the electron volt.
  • The work function of metals (energy to eject an electron) is typically 2-5 eV. This is why UV light (photons > 3 eV) can cause photoelectric emission but visible light (1.8-3 eV) usually cannot.
  • For NEB exams: remember V=kQ/r, U=kq1​q2​/r, E=−dV/dr, and 1eV=1.6×10−19 J. Practice converting between eV and J, and calculating potential for multiple charges using scalar addition.
