Notes

  • Coulomb's law (scalar form): the magnitude of the force between two point charges is F=4πϵ0​1​⋅r2∣q1​q2​∣​. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs, repulsive if they have the same sign.
  • Coulomb's law (vector form): F12​=4πϵ0​1​⋅r2q1​q2​​r^12​, where r^12​ is the unit vector from charge 1 to charge 2. The negative of this is the force on charge 1 due to charge 2 (Newton's third law).
  • Permittivity constant: k=1/(4πϵ0​)=8.99×109N⋅m2/C2. This is also called Coulomb's constant. ϵ0​=8.854×10−12C2/(N⋅m2).
  • Inverse-square dependence: the force falls off as 1/r2. Doubling the distance reduces the force to one-quarter. This is the same dependence as Newton's law of gravitation.
  • Linear superposition of forces: for N charges, the total force on charge i is Fi​=∑j=i​Fij​. Each pair interaction is independent, and the vector sum gives the net force.
  • Direction of force: F12​ points along the line connecting the two charges, away from charge 2 if both have the same sign (repulsion) and toward charge 2 if they have opposite signs (attraction).
  • Dielectric effect: in a uniform dielectric medium with relative permittivity κ (also called dielectric constant), the force is reduced: F=4πκϵ0​1​⋅r2∣q1​q2​∣​. This is because the medium polarizes and partially shields the charges.
  • Coulomb vs Newton: the electric force is 1036 times stronger than the gravitational force between two protons. Yet gravity dominates on cosmic scales because matter is electrically neutral.
  • Action at a distance vs field concept: Coulomb's law was originally stated as action at a distance. Modern physics interprets it as the interaction mediated by the electromagnetic field. A change in one charge propagates to the other at the speed of light, not instantaneously.
  • Limitation of Coulomb's law: applies only to point charges or spherically symmetric charge distributions. For non-spherical distributions, the calculation requires integration over the charge distribution.
  • Couple vs force: two equal and opposite forces on a body form a couple if their lines of action are different. The couple produces a torque but no net force.
  • For three charges: place the third charge, calculate the force from each of the other two independently, then add them as vectors. Always draw a clear free-body diagram with all forces labelled.
  • Stability of three charges: the only stable configuration of three point charges is when all three are in a line, with the largest charge in the middle and the smaller charges symmetrically placed (Earnshaw's theorem says stable equilibrium under electrostatic forces alone is impossible).

Confusion

  • Coulomb's law F∝1/r2 is for the inverse-square force between two charges in vacuum. Do not confuse with the potential energy U∝1/r — the force is the gradient of potential energy, so different distance dependence.
  • The direction of force is determined by the signs of both charges. A positive test charge near a positive source charge is repelled (force outward); near a negative source, it's attracted (force inward).
  • Adding more charges does not change the pairwise force between any two. Each pair experiences the same force as if the others were absent — the net force is just the vector sum.
  • In a medium with dielectric constant κ, the force is reduced by a factor of κ. This is not a 'shielding' in the usual sense — it's the medium's polarization creating an opposing field.
  • The unit vector r^ in Coulomb's law is from the source charge toward the point where the field is being calculated. Getting the direction wrong is the most common error in vector force calculations.

Practice

  • Two charges q1​=+4μC and q2​=+6μC are placed at vertices of an equilateral triangle of side 10 cm. Find the magnitude of the force on a third charge q3​=−2μC at the third vertex. Solution: Force from q1​ on q3​: F1​=9×109×4×10−6×2×10−6/0.12=7.2 N (attractive). Force from q2​ on q3​: F2​=9×109×6×10−6×2×10−6/0.12=10.8 N (attractive). Angle between them = 60°. Net force: F=F12​+F22​+2F1​F2​cos60°​=51.84+116.64+77.76​=246.24​=15.7 N.
  • Three charges of +1μC, −2μC, and +3μC are placed on a line at positions x=0, x=0.5 m, and x=1 m. Find the net force on the middle charge. Solution: Force from +1μC (at x=0) on −2μC (at x=0.5): F1​=9×109×1×10−6×2×10−6/0.52=0.072 N (attractive, points toward +1μC, i.e., in −x direction). Force from +3μC (at x=1) on −2μC: F2​=9×109×2×10−6×3×10−6/0.52=0.216 N (attractive, points toward +3μC, i.e., in +x direction). Net force = 0.216−0.072=0.144 N in +x direction.
  • Two small identical conducting spheres of mass 10 g each are hung from the same point by insulating strings of length 1 m. Equal charge is given to each, and they repel so that each string makes an angle of 30° with the vertical. Find the charge on each sphere. Solution: At equilibrium, tan30°=Fe​/(mg), so Fe​=mgtan30°=0.01×9.8×0.577=0.0566 N. Distance between spheres: r=2Lsin30°=1 m. Fe​=kq2/r2=0.0566 gives q2=0.0566×1/(9×109)=6.29×10−12, so q=7.93×10−6C≈8μC.
  • Four equal charges q are placed at the corners of a square of side a. Find the magnitude and direction of the force on a charge q at one corner due to the other three. Solution: The two adjacent charges each give force kq2/a2 along the adjacent sides. The diagonal charge gives kq2/(2a2) along the diagonal. Net force along diagonal: F=2⋅kq2/a2⋅cos45°+kq2/(2a2)=(2​+1/2)kq2/a2≈1.91kq2/a2, directed along the diagonal outward.
  • A charge q1​=2μC is at the origin, q2​=−2μC is at (d,0) where d=0.1 m. Find the point on the x-axis where the net electric field is zero. Solution: On the x-axis between the charges, the fields due to q1​ and q2​ point in opposite directions. At point x: E1​=kq1​/x2 (in +x direction), E2​=kq2​/(d−x)2 (in −x direction since q2​ is negative). Setting them equal: q1​/x2=q2​/(d−x)2, but since ∣q1​∣=∣q2​∣, we need x=d−x, so x=d/2=0.05 m. (The midpoint is the only point on the x-axis where the net field is zero.)

Universal Facts

  • Coulomb's law is one of the most precisely verified laws in physics. Experiments have confirmed the 1/r2 dependence to better than 1 part in 1016.
  • The electric force and gravitational force both follow inverse-square laws, but they are fundamentally different. Gravity is always attractive; the electric force can be attractive or repulsive.
  • The superposition principle holds exactly for electric forces in classical electromagnetism. In quantum electrodynamics, it holds as a probability amplitude, not as a force.
  • The relative strength of the electric and gravitational forces between two protons is Fe​/Fg​≈1.24×1036. This dimensionless ratio is one of the great unexplained numbers in physics.
  • Coulomb's law is named after Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, who measured the force between charges in 1785 using a torsion balance.
