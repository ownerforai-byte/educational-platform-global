Enriched Content

Notes
  • The problem-solving framework (the 'FBD method'): (1) isolate the body, (2) draw ALL forces acting ON it (weight, normal, friction, tension, applied), (3) choose axes (one along acceleration), (4) write ΣF=ma for each axis, (5) solve. Every single NEB numerical follows this pattern.
  • Elevator weight paradox: in an accelerating upward elevator, the normal force N=m(g+a) — you feel heavier. Accelerating downward: N=m(g−a) — you feel lighter. Free fall (a=g): N=0 (weightlessness). The weight mg never changes; what changes is the apparent weight (the normal force).
  • Atwood's machine (two masses on a pulley): acceleration a=m1​+m2​(m1​−m2​)g​; tension T=m1​+m2​2m1​m2​g​. The string is massless and inextensible; the pulley is frictionless — if not, extra terms appear.
  • Connected bodies (push and pull): a single force F applied to a system of masses (m₁, m₂, m₃) gives a=m1​+m2​+m3​F​. The contact force between m1​ and m2​ is the force needed to accelerate the trailing masses: N=(m2​+m3​)×a.
  • Inclined plane: weight components: mg sin θ down the slope, mg cos θ into the plane. Normal N = mg cos θ. With friction: net force down slope = mgsinθ−μmgcosθ. The body slides when sinθ>μcosθ, i.e., tanθ>μ — the critical angle depends only on μ, NOT on mass.
  • Non-inertial frames and pseudo-forces: When analyzing motion from an accelerating frame (e.g., inside an accelerating car), Newton's laws require the addition of a 'pseudo-force' Fp​=−maframe​ to account for the frame's acceleration. This force is not real (no agent), but it makes the math work.
  • Static vs. Kinetic Friction: Static friction fs​≤μs​N acts when there is no relative motion; kinetic friction fk​=μk​N acts when sliding occurs. Always \mu_s > \mu_k.
  • Worked example: a 5 kg block on a 30° rough slope (μ = 0.3) — does it slide? tan 30° = 0.577 > 0.3 → yes. Acceleration: a = g(sin 30° − 0.3 cos 30°) = 9.8(0.5 − 0.26) = 2.35 m/s² down the slope.
  • Apparent weight in fluids: an object submerged in a liquid appears lighter by the buoyant force (Archimedes): apparent weight = mg − ρVg. This is still a Newton's second law application: ΣF = mg − ρVg = ma.
  • NEB exam focus: elevator problems, Atwood's machine, connected bodies on horizontal surfaces, inclined plane with/without friction, apparent weight in accelerating systems — all use the same FBD → ΣF = ma framework.
Confusion
  • ❌ 'The normal force is always mg.' ✅ N = mg only on a horizontal surface with no vertical acceleration. On a slope N = mg cos θ; in an accelerating elevator N = m(g ± a).
  • ❌ 'Friction always opposes motion.' ✅ Friction opposes RELATIVE motion (or its tendency). Static friction can act in the direction of motion — e.g., the static friction on your feet pushes you forward when walking.
  • ❌ 'The tension in a string equals the weight of the hanging mass.' ✅ Only if the system is in equilibrium. In an accelerating Atwood's machine, T ≠ mg — use ΣF = ma on each mass to find T.
  • ❌ 'Action and reaction forces cancel each other.' ✅ They act on DIFFERENT bodies, so they never cancel in a single FBD. Include only forces acting ON the body you are analyzing.
  • ❌ 'Heavier bodies slide slower down a slope.' ✅ a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ) has no mass term — all bodies slide at the same rate (if μ is the same), just like free fall.
  • ❌ 'A body at rest has no forces acting on it.' ✅ Static equilibrium means ΣF = 0, not zero forces — weight and normal (and possibly friction and tension) all act, they just balance.
Practice
  • Draw free-body diagrams for 10 different scenarios: block on slope, elevator, Atwood's machine, connected bodies, object in fluid.
  • Solve 5 elevator problems (upward acceleration, downward acceleration, free fall) computing apparent weight.
  • Solve 5 Atwood's machine problems for acceleration and tension.
  • Solve 5 inclined-plane problems (smooth and rough) for acceleration and critical angle.
  • Solve 5 connected-body problems (push and pull configurations) finding both acceleration and contact forces.
  • Analyze a 3-block system connected by strings on a horizontal surface with a pulling force.
Universal Facts
  • Newton's second law (ΣF = ma) is the master equation of mechanics — it connects forces to motion.
  • Every mechanics problem in the NEB exam can be solved by drawing a correct FBD and applying ΣF = ma.
  • The apparent weight of a body depends on the acceleration of its support — the basis of weightlessness in orbit.
  • In the absence of friction, all bodies slide down a slope at the same rate regardless of mass — a parallel to Galileo's free-fall result.
  • The tension in a string transmits force between bodies — it is the mechanism by which connected bodies influence each other's motion.
  • Pseudo-forces are a mathematical convenience for analyzing motion in non-inertial reference frames.
Examples
  • Elevator: A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2 m/s². Scale reading = m(g + a) = 70(9.8 + 2) = 826 N (apparent mass ≈ 84.3 kg).
  • Atwood: m₁ = 6 kg, m₂ = 4 kg. a = (6−4)×9.8/(6+4) = 1.96 m/s²; T = 2×6×4×9.8/10 = 47.04 N.
  • Incline: A 10 kg block on a 37° smooth slope — a = g sin 37° = 9.8 × 0.6 = 5.88 m/s² down the slope.
  • Connected: F = 30 N pushes m₁ = 2 kg, m₂ = 3 kg, m₃ = 5 kg. a = 30/10 = 3 m/s²; contact force between m₁ and m₂ = (3+5)×3 = 24 N.
  • Rough incline: A 5 kg block on a 30° slope with μ = 0.2. a = 9.8(sin30° − 0.2cos30°) = 9.8(0.5 − 0.173) = 3.20 m/s².
Practice Questions
  • A 60 kg woman stands on a scale in a lift moving upward at 3 m/s². What does the scale read? What if the lift cable breaks?
  • Two masses 5 kg and 3 kg hang over a frictionless pulley. Find the acceleration and tension.
  • A block slides down a smooth 45° incline. Find the time to travel 4 m from rest.
  • A 40 N force pushes two blocks (3 kg and 7 kg) on a smooth horizontal surface. Find the contact force between them.
  • A 2 kg block rests on a 30° rough slope (μ = 0.4). Does it slide? If so, find the acceleration.
Formulas
  • Newton's second law (vector): ΣF = ma → ΣF_x = ma_x, ΣF_y = ma_y
  • Elevator apparent weight: N = m(g ± a) where + is upward acceleration, − is downward
  • Atwood's machine: a = (m₁ − m₂)g/(m₁ + m₂); T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁ + m₂)
  • Smooth inclined plane: a = g sin θ down the slope; N = mg cos θ
  • Rough inclined plane: a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ); slides when tan θ > μ
  • Pseudo-force: F_p = -ma_{frame}
Key Points
  • The FBD method is universal: isolate → draw forces → choose axes → ΣF = ma → solve.
  • The normal force adapts to the situation — it is NOT always mg.
  • Tension in an accelerating string differs from the weight of the attached mass.
  • Action-reaction pairs act on different bodies — never cancel in a single FBD.
  • On a frictionless incline, all bodies slide at the same rate (independent of mass).
  • Pseudo-forces are necessary in non-inertial frames.
Summary

Newton's second law (ΣF = ma) applied through free-body diagrams solves every mechanics problem: elevators (apparent weight), Atwood's machine, connected bodies, inclined planes (smooth and rough), and objects in fluids. The key is correct isolation of the body and accurate force identification. Pseudo-forces allow analysis in non-inertial frames.

Special Notes
  • Always draw the FBD BEFORE writing equations — a wrong diagram guarantees a wrong answer.
  • Choose one axis along the direction of acceleration — this makes ma appear on only one axis.
  • Check units: forces in newtons, masses in kg, accelerations in m/s².
  • Static equilibrium (a = 0) is just ΣF = ma with a = 0 → ΣF = 0 — the same framework.
  • NEB frequently asks: 'Draw the free body diagram and find the acceleration' — the FBD itself carries marks.
Important Statements
  • Newton's second law: the net force on a body equals the rate of change of its momentum (ΣF = dp/dt); for constant mass, ΣF = ma.
  • Every force has an identifiable agent — if you cannot name the agent, the force does not exist (no 'force of motion').
  • Static equilibrium (a = 0) requires ΣF = 0 — forces exist but balance.
  • The apparent weight of a body equals the normal force exerted by its support — it changes with acceleration.
  • In an accelerating reference frame, pseudo-forces appear — but Newton's laws in their standard form hold only in inertial frames.
Important Notes
  • The FBD is the single most important tool in mechanics — invest time in drawing it correctly.
  • Always check: does the body accelerate? If yes, ΣF = ma; if no, ΣF = 0 — same law, different cases.
  • The normal force is a constraint force — it adjusts to prevent penetration, and equals mg only in the simplest case.
  • Tension in a massless string is uniform throughout; in a massive string, it varies along the length.
  • NEB marks are awarded for the FBD, the equations, and the final answer — skip none of these.
Exam Short Tricks
  • Elevator shortcut: apparent weight = m(g ± a) — just add or subtract ma from the real weight.
  • Atwood shortcut: a = (m₁ − m₂)g/(m₁ + m₂) — the net driving force is the weight difference; total inertia is the total mass.
  • Incline shortcut: a = g sin θ (smooth) — the acceleration depends only on g and θ, not on mass.
  • Connected bodies shortcut: first find a = F/m_total, then use a to find internal contact forces.
  • Equilibrium shortcut: if a body is at rest OR moving at constant velocity, ΣF = 0 — same condition.
Exam Notes
  • The FBD carries marks — draw it clearly, label all forces, and show the coordinate axes.
  • Elevator and Atwood problems appear almost every year — master both.
  • Inclined plane problems (smooth and rough) are standard — know the mg sin θ and mg cos θ decomposition.
  • Connected-body problems test both system thinking (find a) and isolation thinking (find contact forces).
  • Apparent weight in fluids and elevators uses the same ΣF = ma framework — recognize the pattern.
