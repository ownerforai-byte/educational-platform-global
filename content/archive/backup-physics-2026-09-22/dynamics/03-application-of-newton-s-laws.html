Application of Newton's Laws

Notes

  • Newton's First Law (Inertia): a body remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted on by an external force. Inertia is the resistance to change in motion, quantified by mass. Mass is the measure of inertia — more massive objects are harder to accelerate.
  • Newton's Second Law: Fnet​=ma=dtdp​​. The acceleration of a body is directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to its mass. In the form F=dtdp​​, it is valid even when mass changes (e.g., rockets).
  • Newton's Third Law: for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. FAB​=−FBA​. Action and reaction forces act on DIFFERENT bodies, so they never cancel each other in a free-body diagram of a single body.
  • Free-body diagrams: the key tool for applying Newton's laws. Draw the body of interest and show ALL forces acting ON it (not forces it exerts on others). Common forces: weight mg, normal reaction N, tension T, friction f.
  • Tension in a string: for a massless, inextensible string, tension is the same throughout. For a massive string, tension varies along its length. At a pulley, the tension is the same on both sides if the pulley is massless and frictionless.
  • Normal reaction: the force perpendicular to a surface. On an inclined plane at angle θ, N=mgcosθ (if no other vertical forces act). The normal force adjusts to prevent penetration of the surface.
  • Pseudo force: in a non-inertial frame accelerating with a, a fictitious force −ma appears on every mass. For example, in an accelerating car, passengers feel pushed backward — this is the pseudo force. It has no reaction pair.
  • Connected masses: when two bodies are connected by a string over a pulley (Atwood machine), they share the same acceleration magnitude. Write F=ma for each body separately and solve the system of equations.
  • Friction in Newton's laws: static friction fs​≤μs​N adjusts to prevent slipping; kinetic friction fk​=μk​N opposes motion. Always include friction as a force in the free-body diagram when surfaces are rough.
  • Apparent weight: the normal force experienced by a person in an accelerating elevator. When accelerating upward, apparent weight =m(g+a)>mg. When accelerating downward, apparent weight =m(g−a)<mg. In free fall (a=g), apparent weight is zero — weightlessness.
  • Atwood machine: two masses m1​ and m2​ (m2​>m1​) connected by a string over a frictionless pulley. Acceleration: a=m1​+m2​(m2​−m1​)g​. Tension: T=m1​+m2​2m1​m2​g​. This is a standard problem type in NEB exams.
  • Inclined plane: a block of mass m on a plane inclined at angle θ. Resolve weight into components: mgsinθ down the plane and mgcosθ perpendicular to the plane. Normal reaction N=mgcosθ.
  • System approach: for connected bodies, you can sometimes treat the system as a whole. The net external force on the system equals total mass times acceleration: Fnet,ext​=Mtotal​⋅a. Internal forces (tension between connected bodies) cancel.
  • Limiting friction: the maximum static friction just before motion begins: fs,max​=μs​N. If the applied force exceeds this, the object starts moving and kinetic friction takes over: fk​=μk​N<fs,max​.
  • Friction on an incline: a block on an inclined plane begins to slide when mgsinθ>μs​mgcosθ, i.e., when tanθ>μs​. The critical angle is θc​=tan−1μs​, called the angle of repose.
  • Pulley systems: in a simple pulley, the tension is the same on both sides. In a block-and-tackle system with n supporting strands, the mechanical advantage is n — the force needed is n1​ of the weight (neglecting friction and pulley mass).
  • Contact force between blocks: when two blocks are pushed together on a frictionless surface, the contact force depends on how the external force is applied. If force F pushes block m1​ which contacts m2​, the contact force on m2​ is F⋅m1​+m2​m2​​.
  • Newton's laws in everyday life: walking (friction pushes you forward), swimming (water pushes you forward as you push water backward), driving (friction between tyres and road provides forward force), jumping (ground pushes you up). All illustrate Newton's third law.
  • Weightlessness in orbit: astronauts in orbit are not weightless because gravity is absent — they are in continuous free fall around Earth. The normal force from the spacecraft floor is zero, giving the sensation of weightlessness.
  • Non-inertial frames: in an accelerating frame, Newton's second law must be modified: Fnet​+Fpseudo​=marel​. The pseudo force is −maframe​, where aframe​ is the acceleration of the reference frame.

Confusion

  • Action-reaction pairs NEVER act on the same body — they act on two different bodies. A common mistake is to say "the book pushes down on the table and the table pushes up on the book, so they cancel" — these forces act on different bodies and never cancel for a single body's analysis.
  • Newton's second law is Fnet​=ma, not F=ma for any single force. You must sum ALL forces acting on the body before setting them equal to ma.
  • The normal reaction is NOT always equal to mg. On an inclined plane, N=mgcosθ. If an additional vertical force acts, N changes. Always determine N from the specific situation.
  • Tension is NOT a force that "saves" energy — it does no work on a system of connected masses because the displacements at both ends are equal (for an inextensible string), so the net work by tension is zero.
  • In an accelerating reference frame, pseudo forces are NOT real forces — they have no reaction pair and do not arise from any physical interaction. They are introduced only to make Newton's second law work in non-inertial frames.
  • A body moving at constant velocity has zero net force on it, but it can still have forces acting on it. The forces simply balance. A car moving at constant speed on a level road has engine force balancing friction and air resistance.
  • The tension in a string is NOT always equal to the weight of a hanging mass. If the mass is accelerating, T=m(g±a). Only when the mass is at rest or moving at constant velocity does T=mg.
  • When two bodies of different masses are dropped from the same height, they fall with the same acceleration g (neglecting air resistance). The gravitational force is larger on the heavier body, but its greater inertia exactly compensates.

Practice

  • A 5 kg block rests on a smooth horizontal table. A horizontal force of 20 N is applied. Find the acceleration and the tension in a string connecting it to a 3 kg hanging mass over a frictionless pulley. Solution: For the system: Fnet​=(5+3)a, so 20=8a, a=2.5 m/s2. For the 5 kg block: T=5×2.5=12.5 N. For the 3 kg mass: 3g−T=3×2.5, so T=30−7.5=22.5 N. Wait — recheck: the applied force is 20 N on the 5 kg block, and the hanging mass provides additional tension. Let me solve the system properly: 20+3g−Tinternal​=8a... Actually, with the 20 N force on the 5 kg block and the 3 kg hanging: equations are (1) 20−T=5a and (2) T−3g=3a — no, the hanging mass pulls down: 3g−T=3a is wrong if 20 N pulls the other way. Correct: 20−T=5a and T−3g=3a (if upward is positive for hanging mass). Adding: 20−3g=8a, 20−29.4=8a, a=−1.175 m/s2. The system accelerates with the 3 kg mass falling, so a=1.175 m/s2 downward for the 3 kg mass. Tension: T=20−5(1.175)=14.125 N.
  • A 10 kg body is pulled up a smooth incline of angle 30° by a force of 80 N parallel to the incline. Find the acceleration. Solution: Forces along the incline: F−mgsinθ=ma. 80−10×9.8×sin30°=10a. 80−49=10a, so a=3.1 m/s2 up the incline.
  • An elevator of mass 500 kg is suspended by a cable. Find the tension when the elevator (a) accelerates upward at 2 m/s2, (b) moves at constant velocity, (c) accelerates downward at 2 m/s2. Solution: (a) T−mg=ma, T=m(g+a)=500(9.8+2)=5900 N. (b) a=0, T=mg=500×9.8=4900 N. (c) mg−T=ma, T=m(g−a)=500(9.8−2)=3900 N.
  • Two masses m1​=4 kg and m2​=6 kg are connected by a string over a frictionless pulley (Atwood machine). Find the acceleration and tension. Solution: a=m1​+m2​(m2​−m1​)g​=10(6−4)×9.8​=1.96 m/s2. T=m1​+m2​2m1​m2​g​=102×4×6×9.8​=47.04 N.
  • A 2 kg block rests on a rough horizontal surface (μs​=0.5, μk​=0.4). A horizontal force of 8 N is applied. Find the friction force and acceleration. Solution: Maximum static friction fs,max​=μs​N=0.5×2×9.8=9.8 N. Since applied force (8 N) < fs,max​ (9.8 N), the block does not move. Friction force = 8 N (equal and opposite to the applied force). Acceleration = 0.
  • A 4 kg block is pulled up a rough incline of angle 20° by a force of 30 N parallel to the incline. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2. Find the acceleration. Solution: Normal force N=mgcos20°=4×9.8×0.9397=36.85 N. Friction fk​=0.2×36.85=7.37 N. Net force up the incline: F−mgsin20°−fk​=30−4×9.8×0.3420−7.37=30−13.42−7.37=9.21 N. Acceleration: a=49.21​=2.30 m/s2.
  • A 10 kg box is pushed across a horizontal floor with a force of 50 N at an angle of 30° below the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.3. Find the acceleration. Solution: Vertical forces: N=mg+Fsin30°=10×9.8+50×0.5=98+25=123 N. Friction: fk​=0.3×123=36.9 N. Horizontal force: Fcos30°=50×0.866=43.3 N. Net force: 43.3−36.9=6.4 N. Acceleration: a=106.4​=0.64 m/s2.
  • Three blocks of masses 2 kg, 3 kg, and 5 kg are connected in a line on a frictionless surface. A force of 20 N pulls the 5 kg block. Find the tension between each pair. Solution: Total mass = 10 kg, acceleration a=1020​=2 m/s2. Tension between 2 kg and 3 kg: T1​=2×2=4 N. Tension between 3 kg and 5 kg: T2​=(2+3)×2=10 N. Check: T2​−T1​=10−4=6 N on the 3 kg block, giving a=6/3=2 m/s2. ✓

Universal Facts

  • Newton's laws were published in his Principia Mathematica in 1687 and remain the foundation of classical mechanics.
  • Newton's second law in the form F=dtdp​​ is more general than F=ma — it correctly describes systems with changing mass, such as rockets.
  • The concept of pseudo force is essential for solving problems in accelerating frames like elevators, rotating platforms, and accelerating vehicles.
  • Newton's third law implies conservation of momentum for an isolated system — the two laws are deeply connected.
  • In real-world applications, Newton's laws are supplemented by empirical force laws: friction (f=μN), spring force (F=−kx), and drag force (F∝v or v2).
  • The normal force is a constraint force — it adjusts to whatever value is needed to prevent interpenetration of surfaces. It is not a fixed value but responds to the specific situation.
  • In a frictionless Atwood machine, the acceleration is always less than g (unless one mass is zero). The tension is always between the two weights: m1​g<T<m2​g (assuming m2​>m1​).
  • A person standing on a scale in an elevator reads different values depending on the elevator's acceleration. This reading is the apparent weight, equal to the normal force from the scale.
