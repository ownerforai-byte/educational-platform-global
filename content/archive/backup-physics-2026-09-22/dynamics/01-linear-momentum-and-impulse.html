Enriched Content

Notes
  • Type 2 — alternate treatment: this version emphasises the momentum form of Newton's second law and the impulse–momentum theorem with force–time graphs — the framing NEB theory questions use.
  • Impulsive Force: A force that acts for a very short time but produces a finite change in momentum. Examples: hammer hitting a nail, bat hitting a ball, foot kicking a football. The force is extremely large, often thousands of Newtons, but the duration is milliseconds. The impulse is the area under the force-time curve, J=∫Fdt.
  • Linear momentum: p​=mv — mass in motion, SI unit kg·m/s. It is a VECTOR along the velocity. A 1 kg ball at 10 m/s and a 10 kg cart at 1 m/s carry equal momentum but very different kinetic energies (21​mv2: 50 J vs 5 J). Momentum is a measure of the 'difficulty' to stop an object. It is the 'inertia of motion'.
  • Physical Significance: Momentum is fundamentally linked to the translational symmetry of space (Noether's theorem). In quantum mechanics, momentum is related to the wave vector k by p=ℏk.
  • Conservation: Momentum is conserved in any system where the net external force is zero. This is a universal law, holding from subatomic particles to galactic collisions.
  • Newton's second law in momentum form: F=dtdp​​=dtd(mv)​. For constant mass this reduces to F=ma — but the momentum form is the GENERAL law and is the version that stays valid for variable-mass systems (rockets).
  • Impulse: J=FΔt for a constant force, and J=∫Fdt in general — the AREA under a force–time graph. Impulse has units N·s = kg·m/s, the same as momentum — by design.
  • Impulse–momentum theorem: J=Δp​=mvf​−mvi​. Any desired momentum change can be had with a LARGE force for a short time or a SMALL force for a long time — the product is what matters.
  • Why airbags/crumple zones work: to stop a passenger, Δp is fixed by the crash; extending the collision time Δt cuts the average force Favg​=Δp/Δt. Bending your knees on landing, catching a cricket ball with draw-back hands, and jumping with flexed legs are all the same physics.
  • Worked example: a 0.15 kg cricket ball at 30 m/s is stopped in 0.1 s. Δp=0.15×30=4.5 kg·m/s → Favg​=45 N. If caught in 0.02 s: 225 N — five times the force from the same momentum change.
  • Variable force: when F(t) is a curve, impulse is the area under it; for F=F0​e−t/τ-style impacts, J=F0​τ. NEB usually gives triangular or rectangular force–time pulses — count the areas.
  • NEB exam focus: define momentum and impulse with units; state and use F=dp/dt; compute impulse from force–time graphs; apply J=Δp to catches, kicks and landings; explain safety devices via the force–time argument.
Confusion
  • ❌ 'Momentum and kinetic energy are interchangeable measures of motion.' ✅ Momentum mv is a vector conserved in ALL collisions; kinetic energy 21​mv2 is a scalar conserved only in elastic ones. Both grow with speed but differently.
  • ❌ 'Impulse equals force.' ✅ Impulse = force × time (or the area under F–t). A small force acting long can deliver a huge impulse.
  • ❌ 'The ball exerts no force on the bat while being hit because the bat is harder.' ✅ Newton's third law: contact forces are equal and opposite during the collision — the ball's velocity change comes from the contact force acting on IT.
  • ❌ 'A heavy object always has more momentum.' ✅ Momentum = mass × VELOCITY — a light fast bullet can out-momentum a heavy slow trolley; direction matters too.
  • ❌ Using F=ma for a rocket ejecting fuel. ✅ Mass changes with time, so F=ma fails; the momentum form F=dp/dt handles it and yields the rocket equation.
  • ❌ Impulse has units of newtons. ✅ N·s (= kg·m/s) — momentum units. Dimensionally [MLT−1].
Practice
  • A 60 kg runner at 8 m/s is stopped in 0.5 s. Find the impulse and the average force. — J=480 kg·m/s; F=960 N.
  • A 0.5 kg ball hits a wall at 12 m/s and rebounds at 8 m/s. Find the impulse. — Δp=0.5(8+12)=10 kg·m/s BACKWARD (note the sign reversal!).
  • From a triangular F–t pulse peaking at 400 N over 0.02 s, find the impulse delivered to a 0.2 kg ball at rest. — Area =21​(0.02)(400)=4 N·s → v=20 m/s.
  • A 1000 kg car at 20 m/s crashes into a barrier that stops it in 0.4 s. Find the average force, and compare with a crumple-zone stop in 1.0 s. — 50 kN vs 20 kN.
  • A machine-gun fires 30 g bullets at 400 m/s, 10 per second. Find the average recoil force. — F=nmv=10×12=120 N.
Universal Facts
  • Airbags, crumple zones, helmets and safety nets are all impulse engineering: stretch the stopping time, cut the peak force, keep Δp fixed.
  • The impulse–momentum theorem explains why follow-through matters in sports — prolonging contact time increases delivered impulse and exit speed.
  • Rocketry is continuous impulse: each second, expelling mass dm at exhaust speed ve​ gives thrust ve​dm/dt — momentum conservation making spaceflight possible.
  • Karate masters break boards by maximising Δp in minimum Δt: peak contact forces of thousands of newtons in a few milliseconds.
  • The concept of 'impulse' is crucial in collision theory, where the duration of contact is often too short to measure the force directly, but the change in momentum is easily observed.
  • In relativistic mechanics, momentum is defined as p​=γmv, where γ=1/1−v2/c2​. At low speeds, γ≈1, and it reduces to the classical mv.
  • Fluid Impulse: A jet of water hitting a wall exerts a force F=m˙v, where m˙ is the mass flow rate (kg/s). This is the principle behind Pelton wheels and jet engines.
  • Impulse-Momentum Theorem: J=Δp​. This theorem is the foundation for analyzing collisions, explosions, and recoil. It allows us to relate the force-time history of a collision to the change in velocity of the objects involved.
Examples
  • Example 1 (recoil): a 5 kg rifle fires a 20 g bullet at 300 m/s — recoil speed =50.02×300​=1.2 m/s backward.
  • Example 2 (kicking): a footballer gives a 0.45 kg ball at rest a speed of 20 m/s in 0.05 s — impulse 9 N·s, average force 180 N.
  • Example 3 (graph): a rectangular F–t pulse of 250 N lasting 0.04 s delivers J=10 N·s — a 2 kg cart leaves at 5 m/s.
  • Example 4 (safety): the same 4000 kg·m/s car momentum change stopped in 0.1 s (rigid wall) means 40 kN; in 0.5 s (crumple zone) only 8 kN — five times gentler.
  • Example 5 (jump landing): a 60 kg jumper hitting the ground at 6 m/s stops in 0.3 s with stiff legs (1200 N extra) vs 0.9 s with bent knees (400 N extra).
Practice Questions
  • Q1. Define linear momentum and impulse, and state the relation between them.
  • Q2. Show that Newton's second law can be written as F=dp/dt and state when this reduces to F=ma.
  • Q3. A 0.3 kg ball moving at 15 m/s is hit back along the same path at 25 m/s. Find the impulse delivered.
  • Q4. Explain, using the impulse–momentum theorem, why a crumple zone reduces injury in a crash.
  • Q5. From a given force–time graph (triangle), determine the final velocity of the struck body.
  • Q6. A 2 kg object is subjected to a force F=10t N. Find the change in momentum in 3 seconds.
  • Q7. Compare the impulse delivered by a constant force of 10 N for 5 s and a force F=2t for 5 s.
Formulas
  • Momentum: p​=mv [kg·m/s]
  • Second law (general): F=dtdp​​; constant mass: F=ma
  • Impulse (constant force): J=FΔt; general: J=∫Fdt (area under F–t)
  • Impulse–momentum theorem: J=Δp​=mvf​−mvi​
  • Average force: Favg​=ΔtΔp​
Key Points
  • Momentum is a vector — always assign signs/directions before computing Δp.
  • Impulse and momentum share units (N·s = kg·m/s); impulse is the area under the force–time graph.
  • For a FIXED momentum change, force and time trade off: Favg​=Δp/Δt.
  • F=dp/dt is the general second law — essential for variable-mass (rocket) problems.
  • Momentum conservation holds in all collisions; kinetic energy only in elastic ones.
  • Recoil problems are momentum bookkeeping: MV+mv=0 from rest.
Summary

Linear momentum p​=mv measures 'quantity of motion'. Newton's second law in its general form is F=dp​/dt, and integrating it over a collision interval gives the impulse–momentum theorem J=∫Fdt=Δp​ — impulse is the area under the force–time graph. Because a given momentum change can come from a big force briefly or a small force slowly, safety design (airbags, crumple zones, bent knees) is the art of stretching Δt. Recoil, ball games and rocket thrust are all direct applications.

Special Notes
  • Note 1: In rebound problems the velocity CHANGES SIGN — Δp=m(vf​+vi​), not the difference. The commonest numerical error.
  • Note 2: Quote impulse in N·s and momentum in kg·m/s — numerically identical, and examiners check units.
  • Note 3: For force–time graphs, rectangular pulses are FΔt; triangular pulses are 21​Fmax​Δt.
  • Note 4: When a question says 'average force', it means Δp/Δt — the constant force delivering the same impulse.
Important Statements
  • Statement 1: The linear momentum of a body is the product of its mass and velocity — a vector along the velocity.
  • Statement 2: The rate of change of momentum of a body equals the net force acting on it (general form of Newton's second law).
  • Statement 3: Impulse of a force is the product of the force and the time for which it acts, equal to the change in momentum produced.
  • Statement 4: For a fixed momentum change, the average force is inversely proportional to the collision time.
Important Notes
  • Note 1: Impulse units (N·s) and momentum units (kg·m/s) are identical — that is the theorem speaking.
  • Note 2: Momentum is directional: a rebound doubles the magnitude change versus a stop at the same speeds.
  • Note 3: The momentum form F=dp/dt remains valid when mass varies (rockets, leaking trucks) where F=ma fails.
  • Note 4: Force–time graph areas: rectangle = FΔt; triangle = 21​Fmax​Δt.
Exam Short Tricks
  • Trick 1: Rebounds: add the speeds in Δp=m(vf​+vi​) — sign flip doubles the effect.
  • Trick 2: 'Soft landing' MCQs: longer Δt ⇒ smaller F ⇒ pick the option that stretches the time.
  • Trick 3: Recoil from rest: V=mv/M instantly, with V opposite the bullet.
  • Trick 4: Machine-gun/stream problems: average force = mass flow rate × exit speed (nmv).
  • Trick 5: If a force–time curve has no nice shape, the answer is still its AREA — estimate with boxes if needed.
  • Trick 6: Impulsive force problems: always look for the 'short time' keyword — it implies Favg​=Δp/Δt is the intended approach.
Exam Notes
  • Exam Tip 1: Theory answers should state BOTH J=FΔt and J=Δp and connect them — one-line answers lose marks.
  • Exam Tip 2: In numericals, convert grams to kg and quote directions ('opposite to initial motion') with magnitudes.
  • Exam Tip 3: Graph-reading impulse questions: label the base and height you used to compute the area.
  • Exam Tip 4: Safety-device explanations score full marks only with the chain: fixed Δp → longer Δt → smaller Favg​.
