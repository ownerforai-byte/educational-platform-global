  • <h2>9.1 Historical Introduction</h2>
  • <p>Before the 19th century, scientists believed in the <b>Caloric Theory</b> â€” heat was thought to be a fine, weightless fluid called 'caloric', hiding in the pores of a substance and flowing like a liquid or gas.</p>
  • <p>1) According to this theory, an object contains a fixed amount of caloric; adding caloric raised its temperature, and removing it lowered the temperature.</p>
  • <p>2) Caloric was assumed to be odorless, tasteless, and invisible â€” it was never actually detected experimentally.</p>
  • <div style="border-left:4px solid #ef4444;background:#fef2f2;padding:8px 12px;margin:10px 0;border-radius:4px;"><b>âš ï¸ The Fatal Flaw:</b> The caloric theory could not explain <b>heat generated by friction</b>.   • <p><b>Davy and Joule</b> later established heat as a <b>form of energy</b>, arising from the mechanical motion of particles â€” not a fluid substance at all.</p>
  • <p>The modern concept: <b>Internal energy</b> is the total energy (kinetic + vibrational + rotational) of all the molecules of a body; when this energy flows between bodies due to a temperature difference, it is called <b>heat</b>.</p>
  • <p><i>"Order is manifestly maintained in the universe... governed by the sovereign will of God."</i> â€” James Prescott Joule</p>
  • <h2>9.2 Heat â€” Transfer of Energy due to Temperature Difference</h2>
  • <p><b>Definition:</b> Heat is the form of energy which flows from one body to another due to a temperature difference. Symbol: <b>Q</b>.</p>
  • <p><b>Example:</b> A hot iron ball dropped in water â†’ the temperature of the water increases, and the temperature of the ball decreases, until both reach the same temperature. The 'something' transferred between them is <b>heat</b>.</p>
  • <div style="border-left:4px solid #f59e0b;background:#fffbeb;padding:8px 12px;margin:10px 0;border-radius:4px;"><b>âš ï¸ Common Misconception:</b> We commonly say a body 'contains heat' â€” this is <b>incorrect</b>.   • <h2>9.3 Temperature</h2>
  • <p><b>Definition:</b> Temperature is the thermal condition that determines the direction of flow of heat from one body to another â€” i.e., the degree of hotness or coldness of a body.</p>
  • <p>1) Hot and cold are <b>relative</b> terms (like motion/rest, or left/right).</p>
  • <p>2) <b>Classic demonstration:</b> a hand from hot water and a hand from cold water are both placed in tepid (lukewarm) water â€” the tepid water feels <i>cool</i> to the 'hot-water hand' and <i>warm</i> to the 'cold-water hand', even though it is the same temperature for both. This shows that our sense of hot/cold is a subjective sensation, not a reliable measurement.</p>
  • <p>3) Heat flows spontaneously from a <b>high temperature</b> body to a <b>low temperature</b> body.</p>
  • <p>4) There is <b>no upper limit</b> of temperature; the lower limit is <b>0 Kelvin</b> â€” temperature cannot go below 0 K.</p>
  • <div style="border-left:4px solid #3b82f6;background:#eff6ff;padding:8px 12px;margin:10px 0;border-radius:4px;"><b>ðŸ“š Did You Know?</b> In <b>1757</b>, <b>Joseph Black</b> discovered latent heat and was the first to clearly distinguish temperature from heat.<br><br>A brick at 20Â°C, broken into two unequal pieces, leaves <b>both</b> pieces at 20Â°C (temperature does not depend on size) â€” but the <b>larger</b> piece has <b>more internal energy</b>.   • <h2>9.4 Molecular Concept: Thermal Energy, Heat Energy, Temperature</h2>
  • <p>The molecules of matter are in constant motion (translational, rotational, vibrational) â†’ this gives them <b>kinetic energy</b>. Intermolecular forces between them give rise to <b>potential energy</b>.</p>
  • <p>1) <b>Thermal energy (internal energy)</b> = sum of the kinetic and potential energies of all the molecules of a body.</p>
  • <p>2) <b>Temperature</b> = the effect or measure of the thermal energy in a body â€” higher thermal energy corresponds to higher temperature.</p>
  • <p>3) <b>Heat energy</b> = thermal energy that is <i>transferred</i> from one body to another due to a temperature difference â€” in short, <b>"heat is thermal energy in transit."</b></p>
  • <h2>9.5 Thermal Equilibrium and the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics</h2>
  • <p><b>Thermal equilibrium:</b> a number of bodies are said to be in thermal equilibrium if they have the same temperature, so there is <b>no net exchange</b> of heat between them.</p>
  • <div style="border-left:4px solid #f59e0b;background:#fffbeb;padding:8px 12px;margin:10px 0;border-radius:4px;"><b>âš ï¸ Common Misconception:</b> Saying there is 'no flow of heat' at thermal equilibrium is <b>wrong</b>. Heat always flows both ways between bodies, even at equilibrium â€” but the <b>net</b> flow is zero, since equal amounts flow in each direction.</div>
  • <p><b>Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics:</b> If two systems (A and B) are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system (C), then A and B are also in thermal equilibrium with each other.</p>
  • <p><b>Two bodies</b> are said to be in thermal equilibrium if there is no net flow of heat between them when placed in contact. If heat flows from body A to body B, then the temperature of A is <b>greater</b> than that of B (heat flows from the hotter to the colder body, from higher to lower temperature).</p>
  • <p><b>Three-body case:</b> Consider bodies A, B, and C. If A and C are in thermal equilibrium, and B and C are also in thermal equilibrium, then A and B must also be in thermal equilibrium with each other. This is exactly the Zeroth Law stated above.</p>
  • <p><i>Fig. 9.2:</i> A and B are directly linked; Aâ€“C and Bâ€“C are linked via dashed lines representing separate equilibrium relationships, concluding A â‰¡ B.</p>
  • <p><b>History:</b> Propounded by <b>R. H. Fowler</b>, this law came to light only in <b>1930</b> â€” much later than the 1st and 2nd Laws of Thermodynamics â€” yet it is fundamental to both, which is why it is placed 'before' them and called the <b>Zeroth Law</b>.</p>
  • <p><b>Significance of the Zeroth Law:</b></p>
  • <p>(i) It defines the temperature of a body.</p>
  • <p>(ii) It establishes temperature as a valid, fundamental concept underlying the 1st and 2nd Laws of Thermodynamics.</p>
  • <p>(iii) It clarifies that heat always flows both ways between bodies, even at equilibrium â€” only the <i>net</i> flow is zero.</p>
  • <h2>9.6 Cause and Direction of Heat Flow</h2>
  • <p>Heat flows from a high-temperature region to a low-temperature region because molecules at a higher temperature have more thermal energy. During collisions, energy transfers from high-thermal-energy molecules to low-thermal-energy ones, until equilibrium is reached â€” at which point no net energy transfer occurs (no force acts through a distance, so no net work is done).</p>
  • <p><b>Key definition:</b> Heat is the <b>spontaneous transfer of thermal energy</b> from a higher to a lower temperature body, in order to attain thermal equilibrium. The <b>cause</b> of heat flow is the <b>temperature difference</b> â€” <b>not</b> the internal energy difference.</p>
  • <p><b>Important example:</b> Consider 200 g of water at 30Â°C placed in contact with 50 kg of water at 25Â°C. Heat flows from the 30Â°C water to the 25Â°C water â€” even though the 50 kg body (despite being at the <i>lower</i> temperature) has far more <b>total internal energy</b> than the small 200 g body. This shows that the <b>direction</b> of heat flow depends on the <b>temperature</b> of the bodies, not on their total internal energy.</p>
  • <h2>9.7 Thermometry</h2>
  • <p><b>Definition:</b> Thermometry is the study of the measurement of the temperature of a body. Its basic principle: two bodies in thermal equilibrium have the same temperature.</p>
  • <p><b>Thermometer:</b> a temperature-measuring device, marked with a scale of temperature.</p>
  • <p><b>History:</b> the earliest thermometer was constructed by <b>Galileo (1593)</b>; <b>Newton</b> later suggested the necessity of using fixed points on the scale.</p>

  • <p><b>Working principle:</b> thermal equilibrium is the working principle of a mercury thermometer â€” heat transfers between the substance and the thermometer until both reach the <b>same</b> temperature (equilibrium); the thermometer's own reading then equals the substance's temperature.</p>
