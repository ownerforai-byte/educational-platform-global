Thermal Equilibrium and Zeroth Law

  1843 Joule     → Mechanical equivalent of heat (Joule's experiment: paddles in water)

  • First Law of Thermodynamics: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another. This is the law of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems.
  • Mathematical statement: ΔU = Q − W, where ΔU is the change in internal energy of the system, Q is the heat supplied TO the system, and W is the work done BY the system on the surroundings.
  • Sign conventions (physics/Chemistry IUPAC): Q > 0 when heat enters the system; W > 0 when work is done BY the system (expansion). Some textbooks use W as work done ON the system (W = -PΔV), giving ΔU = Q + W. Always check the convention used.
  • Internal energy U: A state function depending only on the thermodynamic state (P, V, T). For an ideal gas, U depends only on temperature: U = nCᵥT. For real substances, U depends on both T and V.
  • Heat Q and Work W are path functions: Their values depend on the process path, not just the initial and final states. Only ΔU is path-independent.
  • Cyclic process: The system returns to its initial state, so ΔU = 0 over a complete cycle. Therefore Q = W for any cyclic process — the net heat absorbed equals the net work done.
  • Isothermal process (ideal gas): ΔU = 0 (since U depends only on T), so Q = W. For reversible isothermal expansion of n moles: W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  • Adiabatic process: Q = 0 (no heat exchange). Then ΔU = −W. For ideal gas: TV^(γ−1) = constant, PV^γ = constant, where γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ.
  • Isobaric process: Constant pressure. W = PΔV = nRΔT. Q = nCₚΔT, ΔU = nCᵥΔT.
  • Isochoric (isovolumetric) process: Constant volume. W = 0 (no volume change). Q = ΔU = nCᵥΔT.
  • Free expansion (Joule expansion): Gas expands into vacuum. No external pressure, so W = 0. If insulated, Q = 0, so ΔU = 0. For ideal gas, temperature remains constant. For real gases, temperature may change (Joule-Thomson effect).
  • Work done in a reversible process: W = ∫ P dV. On a P-V diagram, work equals the area under the process curve. Clockwise cycle = positive net work (heat engine); counterclockwise = negative net work (refrigerator/heat pump).
  • Heat capacity: C = δQ/dT (inexact differential). Molar heat capacities: Cᵥ = (∂U/∂T)ᵥ, Cₚ = Cᵥ + R (for ideal gas, Mayer's relation). Ratio γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ = 5/3 (monatomic), 7/5 (diatomic), 4/3 (polyatomic).
  • Enthalpy H = U + pV: Useful for constant-pressure processes. ΔH = Qₚ (heat at constant pressure). For ideal gas, H = nCₚT, also depends only on T.
  • Thermodynamic state variables: Intensive (T, P) vs extensive (U, V, S, H, n). State functions (U, H, S, G) vs path functions (Q, W). The first law connects them: dU = δQ − δW.
  • Practical implications: Perpetual motion machines of the first kind (producing work without energy input) are impossible. Energy conversion efficiency is limited: η = W_out/Q_in ≤ 1.

Confusion

  • Heat (Q) and work (W) are NOT properties of a system — they are energy in transit. A system does not 'contain heat' or 'contain work'; it contains internal energy. Only ΔU is a state function.
  • The sign convention for W varies: in physics, W = work done BY the system (expansion = positive); in some chemistry texts, W = work done ON the system (compression = positive). ΔU = Q − W (physics) vs ΔU = Q + W (chemistry). Always verify which convention your textbook uses.
  • In an adiabatic process, Q = 0 but temperature CAN change (ΔU = −W ≠ 0). Adiabatic ≠ isothermal. Confusing these is a common error.
  • For a cyclic process, ΔU = 0 always (since U is a state function), but Q and W are NOT zero individually — only their net values satisfy Q_net = W_net.
  • The first law (ΔU = Q − W) does not tell you the DIRECTION of spontaneous processes — that is governed by the second law (entropy). A process can satisfy the first law but still be impossible (e.g., heat flowing from cold to hot without work input).

Practice

  • A gas expands isobarically at 2 atm from 3 L to 7 L. 500 J of heat is supplied. Find ΔU. Solution: W = PΔV = 2 × 101325 × (7−3)×10⁻³ = 810.6 J. ΔU = Q − W = 500 − 810.6 = −310.6 J. Internal energy decreases.
  • Two moles of monatomic ideal gas undergo reversible adiabatic compression from 300 K, 1 atm to 10 atm. Find final temperature and work done. Solution: γ = 5/3. T₂ = T₁(P₂/P₁)^((γ−1)/γ) = 300 × 10^(0.4) = 300 × 2.51 = 753 K. W = ΔU = nCᵥΔT = 2 × (3/2)R × (753−300) = 3 × 8.314 × 453 = 11,300 J (work done ON gas).
  • A heat engine operates in a cycle, absorbing 2000 J from a hot reservoir and rejecting 1200 J to a cold reservoir. Find the efficiency and work output per cycle. Solution: W = Q_h − Q_c = 2000 − 1200 = 800 J. η = W/Q_h = 800/2000 = 40%.
  • 500 g of water at 20°C is mixed with 300 g of water at 80°C in an insulated container. Find the final temperature. Solution: Heat lost = heat gained. m₁c(T_f − 20) = m₂c(80 − T_f). 500(T_f − 20) = 300(80 − T_f). 500T_f − 10000 = 24000 − 300T_f. 800T_f = 34000. T_f = 42.5°C.
  • A Carnot engine operates between 500 K and 300 K. If it absorbs 1000 J from the hot reservoir per cycle, how much work does it do and how much heat does it reject? Solution: η_carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h = 1 − 300/500 = 0.4. W = ηQ_h = 0.4 × 1000 = 400 J. Q_c = Q_h − W = 600 J.

Universal Facts

  • The first law is essentially the conservation of energy principle extended to include heat as a form of energy transfer. It was established by Joule, Mayer, and Helmholtz in the 1840s.
  • Internal energy U is a state function — its change depends only on initial and final states, not the path. This makes it possible to define enthalpy H = U + pV and other thermodynamic potentials.
  • For an ideal gas, internal energy depends ONLY on temperature (Joule's law), not on volume or pressure. This is a consequence of no intermolecular forces in the ideal gas model.
  • The efficiency of any heat engine is fundamentally limited by the Carnot efficiency η = 1 − T_c/T_h, a consequence of the second law, not the first. The first law alone would allow 100% efficiency.
  • The first law applies to ALL systems — gases, liquids, solids, electromagnetic fields, even black holes (where mass-energy equivalence E=mc² extends the concept of internal energy).
