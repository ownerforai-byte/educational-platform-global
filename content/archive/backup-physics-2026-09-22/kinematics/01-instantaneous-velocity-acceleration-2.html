Notes

  • Type 2 — alternate treatment: this version complements the original note with the limit-definition approach, calculus forms and graphical slope reasoning for instantaneous velocity and acceleration.
  • Average quantities first: average velocity vavg​=ΔtΔx​ uses the whole interval; average acceleration aavg​=ΔtΔv​ likewise. They smooth out everything that happened in between — a car doing 100 km in 2 h has vavg​=50 km/h regardless of traffic.
  • The limit idea: instantaneous velocity is the average velocity as Δt→0: v=limΔt→0​ΔtΔx​=dtdx​. It answers 'how fast RIGHT NOW' — exactly what a speedometer reads.
  • Instantaneous acceleration: a=limΔt→0​ΔtΔv​=dtdv​=dt2d2x​. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity — the 'slope of the slope'.
  • Graphical meaning: on an x–t graph, velocity = slope of the tangent at the instant (not the chord); on a v–t graph, acceleration = slope of the tangent, and the AREA under the curve between two times gives the displacement.
  • Sign reasoning: v>0 → moving forward; v<0 → moving backward; a>0 with v>0 → speeding up; a>0 with v<0 → slowing down (a negative velocity becoming more negative). 'Slowing down' means v and a have OPPOSITE signs — the classic exam trap.
  • Worked calculus example: x(t)=5t2−3t. Then v=dx/dt=10t−3 and a=dv/dt=10m/s2 (constant). At t=2 s: v=17 m/s; the particle is momentarily at rest when t=0.3 s.
  • Uniform vs non-uniform acceleration: if a is constant the SUVAT equations apply; if a varies with time, position or velocity, only the calculus definitions (or graphs) work. This is why the derivative definition is the more general and more fundamental one.
  • NEB exam focus: define instantaneous velocity/acceleration as limits; differentiate a given x(t); find when a particle is at rest or reverses; convert between x–t, v–t and a–t graphs (slopes and areas).

Confusion

  • ❌ 'Instantaneous velocity equals average velocity.' ✅ Only when motion is uniform. In general vinst​=dx/dt while vavg​=Δx/Δt; they coincide only if the x–t graph is a straight line.
  • ❌ 'Negative acceleration always means slowing down.' ✅ Slowing means v and a have OPPOSITE signs. A ball thrown upward (up = positive) has v>0 with a=−g — it slows going up and speeds up coming down, with the SAME downward acceleration throughout.
  • ❌ 'The area under an x–t graph gives velocity.' ✅ The SLOPE of x–t gives velocity; the AREA under a v–t graph gives displacement. Mixing these up is the classic graph-question error.
  • ❌ 'At the turning point, acceleration is zero.' ✅ At a reversal, v=0 but a is NOT zero (e.g. a ball at the top of its flight has v=0, a=g downward). Zero velocity ≠ zero acceleration.
  • ❌ 'Speed can be negative.' ✅ Speed is ∣v∣, always non-negative; velocity carries the sign (direction).
  • ❌ 'If v=0 at an instant, the particle has stopped permanently.' ✅ v=0 can be a momentary pause (top of a throw) — check the acceleration to know what happens next.

Practice

  • Given x(t)=4t3−2t2+5 (m): find v(t) and a(t); evaluate at t=1 s. — v=12t2−4t=8 m/s; a=24t−4=20 m/s².
  • A particle's velocity is v(t)=6t−3t2 (m/s). When is it instantaneously at rest, and what is its acceleration then? — t=0 or 2 s; at t=2: a=6−6t=−6 m/s².
  • From a v–t graph consisting of a straight line from (0, 4) to (4, 12) m/s, find the acceleration and the displacement in 4 s. — a=2 m/s²; displacement = area =24+12​×4=32 m.
  • A stone is thrown up at 20 m/s (g=10 m/s²). Find its velocity and acceleration at the highest point. — v=0; a=10 m/s² downward — zero velocity, non-zero acceleration.
  • The position of two trains is x1​=20t and x2​=5t2 (m). When are their instantaneous velocities equal, and where? — 20=10t → t=2 s at x=40 m.

Universal Facts

  • Calculus was invented (Newton and Leibniz, 1660s–70s) precisely to handle instantaneous quantities — physics and calculus were born together, and Newton's name for the derivative was the 'fluxion'.
  • Car speedometers show instantaneous speed, while trip computers computing distance ÷ time show average speed — the two can differ enormously in city traffic.
  • Radar guns and GPS speed trackers estimate instantaneous velocity by measuring position over very small time windows — a physical implementation of the limit Δx/Δt with Δt→0.
  • In relativity and quantum mechanics, instantaneous velocity remains fundamental: particle accelerators track beam particles through billions of instantaneous velocity updates per second.

Examples

  • Example 1: x(t)=3t2 m → v=6t m/s; at t=2 s the instantaneous velocity is 12 m/s, while the average velocity over 0–2 s is 212−0​=6 m/s — different because the motion speeds up.
  • Example 2: A metro train's v–t graph: trapezoid of acceleration, cruise, braking. Displacement = area of trapezoid; acceleration = slope in each segment.
  • Example 3: v(t)=8−4t: the particle reverses at t=2 s where v=0 but a=−4 m/s² throughout — illustrating reversal with constant acceleration.
  • Example 4: Two instants with equal speeds but different velocities: v=+10 m/s at t1​ and v=−10 m/s at t2​ — same speed, opposite direction of motion.
  • Example 5: From a=6t and v(0)=2 m/s: integrate to v=3t2+2 and, with x(0)=0, x=t3+2t — showing how derivatives run downward (x→v→a) and integrals run upward.

Formulas

  • Instantaneous velocity: v=limΔt→0​ΔtΔx​=dtdx​
  • Instantaneous acceleration: a=dtdv​=dt2d2x​
  • Displacement from velocity: x2​−x1​=∫t1​t2​​vdt (area under v–t curve)
  • Velocity from acceleration: v2​−v1​=∫t1​t2​​adt
  • Rest condition: v=dtdx​=0; reversal check: sign change of v around that instant

Key Points

  • Instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent of the x–t graph; instantaneous acceleration is the slope of the v–t graph.
  • Displacement is the area under the v–t graph; change in velocity is the area under the a–t graph.
  • v=0 does not imply a=0 — turning points have zero velocity but non-zero acceleration.
  • Speeding up ⟺ v and a share the same sign; slowing down ⟺ opposite signs.
  • Derivatives go down the chain x→v→a; integrals go up a→v→x, using initial conditions.
  • The limit definition is valid for ANY motion — SUVAT equations are special cases of constant acceleration only.

Summary

Instantaneous velocity and acceleration are defined as limits: v=dx/dt and a=dv/dt=d2x/dt2. Graphically, v is the tangent slope of the x–t curve and a the tangent slope of the v–t curve, while areas under v–t and a–t curves give displacement and velocity change. These definitions work for all motion — uniform or not — making them more fundamental than the constant-acceleration equations. Key exam skills: differentiating given x(t), locating rest/reversal instants, and reading slopes and areas correctly with attention to signs.

Special Notes

  • Note 1: Carry units through calculus: if x is in metres and t in seconds, v is m/s and a is m/s² automatically.
  • Note 2: NEB graph questions award marks for BOTH slope reasoning and area reasoning — label which you used.
  • Note 3: When integrating acceleration, always determine integration constants from given initial conditions.
  • Note 4: In 'is the particle speeding up?' questions, compare signs of v and a at that instant — not the sign of a alone.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: Instantaneous velocity is the limit of average velocity as the time interval tends to zero: v=dx/dt.
  • Statement 2: Instantaneous acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time: a=dv/dt.
  • Statement 3: On a position–time graph the slope of the tangent at any instant gives the instantaneous velocity at that instant.
  • Statement 4: The area under a velocity–time curve between two instants equals the displacement in that interval, regardless of whether the acceleration is constant.

Important Notes

  • Note 1: Zero instantaneous velocity does not imply zero acceleration (turning points).
  • Note 2: Instantaneous speed equals the magnitude of instantaneous velocity, but never carries a direction.
  • Note 3: For constant acceleration only, vavg​=(u+v)/2; this shortcut fails when a varies.
  • Note 4: The calculus definitions apply to ALL motion; SUVAT equations are the special case of constant a.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: 'At rest' ⇒ set dx/dt=0 and solve for t; then check the sign of v on both sides to classify pause vs reversal.
  • Trick 2: In graph MCQs, remember: slope of x–t = v; slope of v–t = a; area of v–t = x; area of a–t = v.
  • Trick 3: If x(t) is a polynomial, differentiate term-by-term (d(tn)/dt=ntn−1) — no formula sheet needed.
  • Trick 4: 'Maximum/minimum position' questions ⇒ set v=dx/dt=0, exactly like turning points in calculus.
  • Trick 5: Units self-check: differentiate x in metres → v in m/s → a in m/s²; if your units don't cascade, recheck the algebra.

Exam Notes

  • Exam Tip 1: NEB regularly sets 2-3 mark calculus questions: differentiate x(t), evaluate v and a at a given time — show the derivative step explicitly.
  • Exam Tip 2: In graph-conversion questions (given v–t, sketch x–t), track curvature: constant v → straight x line; linear v → parabolic x.
  • Exam Tip 3: When asked 'when is the particle at rest', also state the acceleration at that instant — examiners expect the follow-through.
  • Exam Tip 4: Define the limit properly in theory questions: shrinking interval, ratio of small changes — vague wording loses marks.

Important Concepts

  • Concept 1: Limit definition — instantaneous quantities are average quantities over a vanishing interval.
  • Concept 2: Derivative chain: xd/dt​vd/dt​a; integrals run the reverse way with initial conditions.
  • Concept 3: Graph duality — slopes give rates (v, a); areas give accumulations (x, v).
  • Concept 4: Sign logic — direction of motion from sign of v; speeding up/slowing down from relative signs of v and a.
  • Concept 5: Generality — the derivative definitions hold for any acceleration; constant-a equations are a special case.
