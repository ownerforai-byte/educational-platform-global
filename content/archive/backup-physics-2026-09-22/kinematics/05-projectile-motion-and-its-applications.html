Notes

  • Type 2 — alternate treatment: this version builds projectile motion from the independence of the two components and derives all standard results step by step — the derivation chain NEB expects.
  • The governing idea: horizontal and vertical motions are INDEPENDENT. Gravity acts only vertically, so the horizontal velocity stays constant (vx​=ucosθ) while the vertical motion is free fall with initial speed usinθ. The path is the combination — a parabola.
  • Component equations (up positive, launch at angle θ with speed u): horizontal: x=(ucosθ)t, vx​=ucosθ; vertical: y=(usinθ)t−21​gt2, vy​=usinθ−gt.
  • Time of flight: set y=0 on landing: T=g2usinθ​ — twice the time to the top (usinθ/g).
  • Maximum height: at the top vy​=0: H=2gu2sin2θ​ — vertical throw with initial speed usinθ.
  • Horizontal range: R=vx​T=gu2sin2θ​. Since sin2θ peaks at 2θ=90°, maximum range at θ=45°, and complementary angles (30° & 60°, 15° & 75°) give the SAME range.
  • Velocity at any instant: v=(ucosθ)2+(usinθ−gt)2​ at angle ϕ=tan−1(ucosθusinθ−gt​) below/above horizontal. On landing, magnitude equals u (symmetry) with direction −θ.
  • Trajectory equation: eliminate t: y=xtanθ−2u2cos2θgx2​ — a parabola. Also useful: R=4Hcotθ connecting range and height.
  • Applications to quote: cannon/artillery ranges, projectile games (javelin, football), water-fountain arcs, bombing-release problems (bomb keeps the plane's horizontal velocity), and projectile on an inclined plane (extension: R=gcos2α2u2cosθsin(θ−α)​ for incline angle α — beyond syllabus but good insight).
  • NEB exam focus: state independence of motions; derive T, H, R; prove max range at 45°; show complementary angles share range; compute velocity (magnitude and direction) at a given time or height; draw the labelled trajectory.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'A projectile's horizontal velocity decreases during flight.' ✅ vx​=ucosθ is CONSTANT (no horizontal force in the ideal model); only vy​ changes.
  • ❌ 'At the top of the trajectory the velocity is zero.' ✅ Only vy​=0; the horizontal component ucosθ persists — speed at the top is ucosθ, the MINIMUM of the flight.
  • ❌ 'The path is part of a circle / straight line decaying.' ✅ The trajectory is a PARABOLA — the graph of y=xtanθ−gx2/(2u2cos2θ).
  • ❌ Using sinθ instead of sin2θ in the range. ✅ R=u2sin2θ/g; and sin2θ is maximal at θ=45°, not 90°.
  • ❌ 'Complementary angles give different ranges.' ✅ sin2(90°−θ)=sin(180°−2θ)=sin2θ — 30° and 60° land at the SAME spot (the 60° shot flies higher).
  • ❌ Doubling u doubles the range. ✅ R∝u2 — doubling launch speed QUADRUPLES range (and quadruples max height too).

Practice

  • A ball is kicked at 20 m/s at 30°. Find T, H, R (g=10). — T=2 s; H=5 m; R=34.6 m.
  • Prove that the speed on landing equals the launch speed, using components at t=T.
  • A projectile has range 100 m and time of flight 4 s. Find its horizontal velocity and launch angle. — vx​=25 m/s; uy​=gt/2=20 m/s → θ=tan−1(20/25)=38.7°.
  • Two projectiles are fired at 30° and 60° with the same speed 40 m/s. Show both have R=141 m, and compare their maximum heights (1 : 3).
  • From a 45 m cliff a stone is thrown horizontally at 10 m/s. When and where does it land? — fall time t=3 s; horizontal landing distance =30 m; impact speed =102+302​≈31.6 m/s.

Universal Facts

  • Galileo's Dialogues (1638) first showed projectile motion is a parabola by superposing uniform horizontal motion with vertical fall — the first known composition of motions.
  • Artillery fire above ~30 km (Paris Gun, WWI) deviates from the parabola: thin air changes drag and g's direction varies — ideal parabolic flight is a near-Earth, no-air model.
  • Long-jump athletes take off near 21°, not 45°, because take-off speed itself drops with steeper angles — the real optimum balances range angle against speed loss.
  • Basketball free throws exploit the high-arc (complementary-angle) region: a steep entry angle enlarges the effective hoop target, even though the flatter shot travels the same distance.

Examples

  • Example 1 (horizontal launch): a stone thrown horizontally at 15 m/s from a 20 m tower: t=2×20/10​=2 s; lands 30 m out — vertical fall sets the TIME, horizontal speed sets the DISTANCE.
  • Example 2 (full trajectory): u=25 m/s at θ=37° (sin37°=0.6, cos37°=0.8): T=3 s, H=11.25 m, R=60 m — the classic 3-4-5 projectile.
  • Example 3 (velocity at top): speed at the top =ucosθ=20 m/s for the previous shot — the projectile is never at rest.
  • Example 4 (45° range): maximum range with u=30 m/s is Rmax​=u2/g=90 m at 45°, with H=R/4=22.5 m — note Rmax​=4H.
  • Example 5 (bomb release): a plane at 500 m flying level at 100 m/s releases a bomb — it lands after t=10 s, 500 m ahead of the release point (it keeps the plane's horizontal velocity).

Practice Questions

  • Q1. Show that the path of a projectile is a parabola by eliminating t from the equations of motion.
  • Q2. Derive R=gu2sin2θ​ and hence prove that the range is maximum for θ=45°.
  • Q3. Show that two angles of projection differing by 90° give the same horizontal range.
  • Q4. A projectile is fired at 50 m/s at 60°. Find (a) time of flight, (b) maximum height, (c) velocity after 2 s.
  • Q5. Show that the relation between range and maximum height is R=4Hcotθ, and verify it for θ=45° where R=4H.

Formulas

  • Components: vx​=ucosθ (constant); vy​=usinθ−gt
  • Time of flight: T=g2usinθ​
  • Maximum height: H=2gu2sin2θ​
  • Range: R=gu2sin2θ​; Rmax​=gu2​ at θ=45°
  • Trajectory: y=xtanθ−2u2cos2θgx2​ (parabola); R=4Hcotθ

Key Points

  • Horizontal and vertical motions are independent; they share only TIME.
  • vx​ is constant; vy​ changes by g every second; the path is a parabola.
  • At the top: vy​=0, speed =ucosθ (minimum), a=g downward.
  • Landing speed equals launch speed; landing angle equals −launch angle (symmetry).
  • R is maximum at 45°; complementary angles give equal ranges but different heights.
  • R∝u2 and H∝u2 — doubling launch speed quadruples both.

Summary

Projectile motion is the superposition of constant horizontal velocity and vertical free fall, giving a parabolic path. The three headline results — T=2usinθ/g, H=u2sin2θ/2g, R=u2sin2θ/g — all follow from the component equations, with maximum range at 45° and equal ranges at complementary angles. Speed at the top is ucosθ (never zero), landing speed equals launch speed, and horizontal-launch problems are handled by letting the vertical fall set the time. These results assume no air resistance and constant g.

Special Notes

  • Note 1: In horizontal-launch problems, get the time from the VERTICAL fall first, then multiply by vx​.
  • Note 2: Memorise sin37°=0.6, cos37°=0.8 and sin2θ values for 30°/45°/60° — NEB numericals use them constantly.
  • Note 3: 'Same range' pairs: compare heights as sin2θ:sin2(90°−θ) — the steeper shot rises 3× higher for the 30°/60° pair.
  • Note 4: State the assumptions (no air resistance, constant g, flat ground) in theory answers — they define the model.

Important Statements

  • Statement 1: A projectile's horizontal and vertical motions are completely independent; gravity acts only on the vertical component.
  • Statement 2: The horizontal velocity of a projectile remains constant throughout the flight (no air resistance).
  • Statement 3: The trajectory of an ideal projectile is a parabola.
  • Statement 4: The horizontal range is maximum at 45° and equal for complementary angles of projection.

Important Notes

  • Note 1: At the highest point the velocity is purely horizontal: v=ucosθ — not zero.
  • Note 2: Landing speed equals launch speed and the landing angle mirrors the launch angle (energy/symmetry).
  • Note 3: R∝u2: small speed increases at launch produce large range gains.
  • Note 4: For horizontal projection from a height, time of flight depends ONLY on the height: t=2h/g​.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Trick 1: R=u2sin2θ/g — for 30° and 60°, sin2θ=sin60°=0.866; write the common range instantly.
  • Trick 2: At 45°, R=4H — if a question gives one, the other is a division away.
  • Trick 3: Horizontal launch from height h: time =2h/g​, landing speed =vx2​+2gh​.
  • Trick 4: Velocity direction at time t: tanϕ=ucosθusinθ−gt​ — positive above the horizontal, negative below.
  • Trick 5: In 'bomb released from plane' questions, the bomb retains the plane's horizontal speed — range = plane speed × fall time.

Exam Notes

  • Exam Tip 1: Derivations of T, H and R from components are standard 4-mark questions — write the two component tables first.
  • Exam Tip 2: Draw the trajectory with vx​ arrows equal everywhere and vy​ arrows shrinking/rising — examiners look for the independence idea.
  • Exam Tip 3: For 'velocity at height h' questions use vy2​=(usinθ)2−2gh — avoids solving a quadratic in t.
  • Exam Tip 4: State the no-air-resistance assumption; if a question mentions drag, the parabola model no longer applies.

Important Concepts

  • Concept 1: Independence of horizontal (uniform) and vertical (accelerated) motions.
  • Concept 2: Component kinematics — vx​ constant, vy​ with −gt; time is the shared variable.
  • Concept 3: Standard results: T=2usinθ/g, H=u2sin2θ/2g, R=u2sin2θ/g.
  • Concept 4: Symmetry — equal speeds at equal heights; landing mirrors launching.
  • Concept 5: Trajectory parabola and the R–H relation R=4Hcotθ.
