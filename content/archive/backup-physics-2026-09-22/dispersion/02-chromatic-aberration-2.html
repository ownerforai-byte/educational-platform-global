Chromatic and Spherical Aberration

Notes

  • Chromatic aberration: a lens fails to focus all colours at the same point because n varies with wavelength. Violet (higher n) focuses closer to the lens than red (lower n), producing coloured fringes around images.
  • Spherical aberration: rays passing through the edge (marginal rays) of a spherical lens focus at a different point from rays passing through the centre (paraxial rays). Marginal rays focus closer to the lens because the surface curvature is steeper at the edges.
  • Why spherical aberration occurs: the lens surface is part of a sphere, but the ideal focusing surface for parallel rays is a paraboloid, not a sphere. A spherical surface is only an approximation.
  • Longitudinal chromatic aberration: the distance between the focal points of the extreme colours (violet and red). For a thin lens, Δf=fv​−fr​≈−f⋅ω, where ω is the dispersive power.
  • Lateral chromatic aberration: the difference in image heights for different colours, significant when the object is off-axis.
  • Reducing spherical aberration: use a diaphragm to block marginal rays (reduces brightness but improves sharpness), or use a compound lens with surfaces chosen so that aberrations of one surface cancel those of the other.
  • Best-form lens: a plano-convex lens has minimum spherical aberration when the curved face is oriented toward the more distant object (collimated light). For finite conjugates, the lens should be oriented so that the refraction is shared more equally between the two surfaces.
  • Aplanatic surfaces: certain combinations of object distance and lens shape eliminate both spherical aberration and coma. A sphere has two aplanatic points used in oil-immersion microscope objectives.

Confusion

  • Chromatic aberration is caused by dispersion (wavelength-dependent n); spherical aberration is caused by the spherical shape of the lens surface. They are fundamentally different phenomena that both degrade image quality.
  • Stopping down (using a diaphragm) reduces spherical aberration by blocking marginal rays, but it does NOT reduce chromatic aberration — all rays, regardless of height, still experience the same dispersion.
  • A plano-convex lens does not eliminate spherical aberration entirely; it only minimizes it for a particular object distance. Only a parabolic surface eliminates it completely for collimated light.
  • Spherical aberration affects all wavelengths equally (monochromatic aberration), while chromatic aberration affects different wavelengths differently (polychromatic aberration).
  • Using a thinner lens does not eliminate either aberration — it only reduces the magnitude. The fundamental cause (spherical shape for spherical aberration, dispersion for chromatic aberration) remains.

Practice

  • A convex lens of focal length 20 cm is made of glass with ω=0.02. Calculate the longitudinal chromatic aberration. Solution: Δf=f⋅ω=20×0.02=0.4 cm =4 mm. The focal length for violet is 0.4 cm shorter than for red.
  • A plano-convex lens of focal length 30 cm is used with the plane face toward the object. Explain why spherical aberration is worse than when the curved face faces the object. Solution: When the plane face faces the object, all refraction occurs at the curved surface, making marginal rays bend sharply. When the curved face faces the object, refraction is shared more equally between the two surfaces, reducing the bending at each surface and thus reducing spherical aberration.
  • A converging lens of focal length 50 cm is combined with a diverging lens to eliminate chromatic aberration. If the dispersive powers are in the ratio 2:3, find the focal length of the diverging lens. Solution: Condition: f1​ω1​​+f2​ω2​​=0. Let ω1​=2ω, ω2​=3ω. Then 502ω​+f2​3ω​=0, giving f2​=−23×50​=−75 cm.
  • Describe two methods to reduce spherical aberration in a lens system. Solution: (1) Use a diaphragm/aperture stop to block the marginal rays that cause the greatest deviation from the paraxial focus. (2) Use an aplanatic lens combination, such as two thin lenses separated by a distance, chosen so that the spherical aberration of one lens cancels that of the other.
  • A telescope objective shows a coloured halo around bright stars. Is this chromatic or spherical aberration? Explain how to distinguish it. Solution: This is chromatic aberration — the halo is coloured because different wavelengths focus at different points. To distinguish from spherical aberration: observe a monochromatic source (e.g., sodium lamp); if the halo disappears, it is chromatic aberration. Spherical aberration persists with monochromatic light, showing as a general blur rather than coloured fringes.

Universal Facts

  • All simple spherical lenses suffer from both chromatic and spherical aberration — no single spherical lens can form a perfect image.
  • The Hubble Space Telescope's initial primary mirror had a spherical aberration of about 2.2 micrometres — a tiny error that produced blurry images because of the mirror's 2.4-metre diameter.
  • Modern camera lenses contain 10–20 elements to correct both aberrations simultaneously, using combinations of crown and flint glass.
  • Aparabolic mirrors are used in telescopes instead of spherical mirrors because a paraboloid focuses all parallel rays to a single point, eliminating spherical aberration for on-axis objects.
  • The human eye has very little chromatic aberration because the cornea and lens have complementary dispersion properties, and the brain processes the colour information to compensate.
