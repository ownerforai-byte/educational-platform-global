Notes

  • Achromatic doublet: a convex crown-glass lens paired with a concave flint-glass lens. The crown lens converges all colours; the flint lens diverges more for violet than for red, cancelling the chromatic spread.
  • Condition for achromatism (thin lenses in contact): f1​ω1​​+f2​ω2​​=0. Since ω1​ and ω2​ are both positive, f1​ and f2​ must have opposite signs: one converging, one diverging.
  • Result: two colours (usually red and blue) are brought to the same focus. Intermediate colours fall close enough that the image appears colour-free to the eye.
  • Power of the combination: the total power is P=P1​+P2​=f1​1​+f2​1​. For an achromatic doublet, P=f1​1​−ω2​f1​ω1​​=f1​1​(1−ω2​ω1​​). The combination remains converging if ω2​>ω1​.
  • For a convex lens of crown glass (ωc​=0.016) and concave lens of flint glass (ωf​=0.031): fc​ff​​=−ωc​ωf​​=−0.0160.031​≈−1.94. The flint lens must have roughly twice the focal length (in magnitude) of the crown lens.
  • Applications: camera lenses, telescope objectives, and microscope objectives all use achromatic doublets or more complex multi-element designs to eliminate chromatic aberration.
  • Apochromatic lenses: advanced designs that bring three colours to the same focus using special glasses with anomalous dispersion (e.g., fluorite). These provide superior colour correction.
  • Achromatic prism pair: two prisms of different materials can be combined to produce dispersion without net deviation, or deviation without net dispersion, by adjusting the prism angles.

Confusion

  • An achromatic doublet brings two wavelengths to the same focus, not all wavelengths — there is still residual (secondary) spectrum for intermediate colours.
  • The condition f1​ω1​​+f2​ω2​​=0 requires that one lens is converging and the other diverging — you cannot make an achromatic doublet from two converging lenses.
  • Achromatism eliminates chromatic aberration but NOT spherical aberration — a real achromatic objective must also correct for spherical aberration separately.
  • The combination must still have net converging power to be useful as a telescope or camera objective — this requires the flint glass to have the larger dispersive power (ωf​>ωc​).
  • For separated lenses (not in contact), the achromatism condition is f1​ω1​​+f2​ω2​​−f1​f2​d(ω1​+ω2​)​=0, where d is the separation. This gives an additional degree of freedom.

Universal Facts

  • The achromatic doublet was invented by Chester Moor Hall around 1733 and independently by John Dollond in 1758, who patented the design.
  • An achromatic doublet reduces but does not eliminate chromatic aberration — residual colour errors (secondary spectrum) remain because the dispersion curves of crown and flint glass are not perfectly linear.
  • Apochromatic objectives, used in high-end microscopy, bring three wavelengths to a common focus and are made with special low-dispersion materials like fluorite or extra-low dispersion (ED) glass.
  • The human eye has a small amount of longitudinal chromatic aberration (about 2 dioptres from red to violet), but the brain compensates and we do not normally perceive colour fringes.
  • In photography, "low dispersion" (LD) or "extra-low dispersion" (ED) glass elements are used in telephoto lenses to reduce chromatic aberration and produce sharper images at the edges.
