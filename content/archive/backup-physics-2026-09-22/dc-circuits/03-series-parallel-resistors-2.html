Resistors in Series and Parallel

Notes

  • Series: same current through all resistors. Req​=R1​+R2​+…. Voltage divides: Vi​=IRi​.
  • Parallel: same voltage across all resistors. 1/Req​=1/R1​+1/R2​+…. Current divides: Ii​=V/Ri​.
  • Why series increases resistance: the current must pass through each resistor sequentially, so each adds its opposition to the total.
  • Why parallel decreases resistance: multiple paths allow more current for the same voltage, equivalent to increasing the cross-sectional area of a single conductor.
  • Special case — two parallel resistors: Req​=R1​+R2​R1​R2​​. This is always less than the smaller resistor.
  • Series voltage divider: in a series chain, each resistor gets a fraction of the total voltage proportional to its resistance: Vi​=Vtotal​⋅Ri​/Rtotal​.
  • Parallel current divider: in a parallel group, each resistor gets a fraction of the total current inversely proportional to its resistance: Ii​=Itotal​⋅Req​/Ri​.
  • Power in series: P=I2R. Larger resistors dissipate more power in series because they carry the same current but have larger voltage drop.
  • Power in parallel: P=V2/R. Smaller resistors dissipate more power in parallel because they draw more current at the same voltage.
  • Identical resistors in parallel: n identical resistors of value R in parallel give Req​=R/n. This is useful for sharing current among multiple resistors.
  • Kirchhoff's laws: series resistors satisfy KVL (sum of voltages equals source). Parallel resistors satisfy KCL (sum of currents equals total).
  • Mixed networks: reduce step by step — find series or parallel groups, replace with equivalents, and repeat until one resistor remains.

Confusion

  • Resistors in series add (like capacitors in parallel). Resistors in parallel combine inversely (like capacitors in series). The rules for resistors and capacitors are always opposite.
  • The equivalent resistance of a parallel combination is always less than the smallest individual resistor. Do not think of it as simply 'dividing' — the math is Req​=(R1​R2​)/(R1​+R2​).
  • In series, the same current flows through all resistors, but the voltage drop across each is different (proportional to its resistance). In parallel, the same voltage is across each, but current differs.
  • When a resistor is added in parallel, the equivalent resistance decreases. When added in series, it increases. This is because parallel paths provide more conduction channels.
  • Two resistors of equal value R in parallel give R/2. Two resistors of equal value in series give 2R. These are the most common benchmark results in circuit analysis.

Practice

  • Three resistors 4Ω, 6Ω, and 12Ω are in parallel. Find the equivalent resistance. Solution: 1/Req​=1/4+1/6+1/12=3/12+2/12+1/12=6/12=1/2. Req​=2Ω.
  • Resistors 3Ω and 6Ω are in parallel, and this combination is in series with a 2Ω resistor. Find the total resistance. Solution: Parallel: Rp​=(3×6)/(3+6)=2Ω. Total: R=2+2=4Ω.
  • A 24 V battery is connected across a series combination of 4Ω and 8Ω. Find the current, voltage across each, and power dissipated by each. Solution: Req​=12Ω. I=24/12=2 A. V1​=2×4=8 V, V2​=2×8=16 V. P1​=I2R1​=4×4=16 W. P2​=I2R2​=4×8=32 W.
  • A 60 V battery is connected across a parallel combination of 10Ω and 15Ω. Find the current through each and the total current. Solution: I1​=60/10=6 A. I2​=60/15=4 A. Itotal​=10 A. Req​=60/10=6Ω. Check: 1/Req​=1/10+1/15=1/6.
  • Find the equivalent resistance between points A and B for a network of five 1Ω resistors arranged in a pentagon, with one resistor across each diagonal. Solution: This is a complex network. Using symmetry and nodal analysis, the equivalent resistance between adjacent vertices is 7/12Ω.

Universal Facts

  • The equivalent resistance of resistors in series is always greater than the largest individual resistance. The equivalent resistance of resistors in parallel is always less than the smallest individual resistance.
  • In household wiring, all appliances are connected in parallel so each receives the full supply voltage and can be operated independently.
  • Fuse wires are connected in series with the load because they must carry the same current as the load to protect it from overcurrent.
  • The current divider rule for two parallel resistors: I1​=Itotal​⋅R2​/(R1​+R2​). The current splits inversely with resistance — the smaller resistor gets more current.
  • For n identical resistors of value R: series gives nR, parallel gives R/n. A resistance box achieves any resistance by switching resistors in series or parallel.
