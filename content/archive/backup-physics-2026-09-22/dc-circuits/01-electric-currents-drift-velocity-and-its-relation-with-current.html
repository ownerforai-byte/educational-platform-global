Electric Currents, Drift Velocity and Current

Notes

  • Current I: rate of flow of charge. I=Q/t. Units: ampere (A).
  • Conventional current: flows from positive to negative. Electron flow is opposite, from negative to positive.
  • Drift velocity vd​: the average velocity of free electrons in a conductor under an electric field. It is very slow, about 10−4m/s.
  • Relation: I=nAevd​, where n is electron density, A is cross-sectional area, e is electron charge, and vd​ is drift velocity.
  • Why signal is fast despite slow drift:** when you switch on a circuit, the electric field propagates at nearly the speed of light, setting all electrons into drift motion simultaneously. The electrons themselves drift slowly, but the signal is instantaneous.
  • Derivation of I=nAevd​: in time dt, all electrons within distance vd​dt of a cross-section pass through it. Number of electrons =nAvd​dt. Charge dQ=nAevd​dt. Hence I=dQ/dt=nAevd​.
  • Typical drift velocity: for copper (n≈8.5×1028 electrons/m3) with I=10 A and A=1mm2=10−6 m2: vd​=I/(nAe)=10/(8.5×1028×10−6×1.6×10−19)=7.4×10−4 m/s.
  • No field, no drift: without an electric field, electrons move randomly at thermal speeds (∼105 m/s) but with zero net velocity, so no current flows.
  • Current density J: current per unit area. J=I/A=nevd​. Vector form: J=nqvd​.
  • Relaxation time τ: average time between successive collisions of an electron with the lattice ions. vd​=eEτ/m, where m is electron mass.
  • Microscopic vs macroscopic: I=nAevd​ is the microscopic view. Ohm's law V=IR is the macroscopic view. They are connected through resistivity ρ=m/(ne2τ).
  • Sign convention: current is a scalar quantity. The direction of positive current flow is defined as the direction of motion of positive charge, even though in metals the actual charge carriers (electrons) move opposite.

Confusion

  • Drift velocity is very slow (10−4 m/s) but the signal (electric field) travels at nearly the speed of light. These are two entirely different things — electrons don't race from switch to bulb; the field establishes itself instantly.
  • The magnitude of current I is the same everywhere in a series circuit. Current is not consumed — electrons drift at the same average speed everywhere, just as water flows at the same rate through a pipe of uniform cross-section.
  • A larger cross-sectional area means more charge carriers can flow simultaneously, so for the same current, the drift velocity decreases: vd​=I/(nAe). A thick wire carries current with slower drift velocity than a thin wire of the same material.
  • The thermal speed of electrons (∼105 m/s) is about 109 times the drift velocity (∼10−4 m/s). The thermal motion is random and contributes no net current; only the drift component does.
  • Current is a scalar, not a vector. It has magnitude but the 'direction' is just a convention for analysis. The actual charge carriers (electrons in metals) move opposite to the defined current direction.

Practice

  • A copper wire of cross-section 2mm2 carries a current of 5 A. Find the drift velocity. (n=8.5×1028 m−3, e=1.6×10−19 C). Solution: vd​=I/(nAe)=5/(8.5×1028×2×10−6×1.6×10−19)=5/(2.72×104)=1.84×10−4 m/s.
  • A 2 A current flows through a wire of radius 1 mm made of a material with n=6×1028 electrons/m3. Find the drift velocity and current density. Solution: A=π×10−6 m2. vd​=2/(6×1028×π×10−6×1.6×10−19)=2/(3.02×104)=6.6×10−5 m/s. J=I/A=2/(π×10−6)=6.37×105 A/m2.
  • An aluminum wire (density n=1.8×1029 m−3) of diameter 2 mm carries 10 A. How long does it take an electron to drift 1 m? Solution: A=π×10−6 m2. vd​=10/(1.8×1029×π×10−6×1.6×10−19)=10/(9.05×104)=1.1×10−4 m/s. Time =1/vd​=9090 s ≈2.5 hours.
  • A gold wire carries current density J=5×106 A/m2. Gold has n=5.9×1028 m−3. Find the drift velocity. Solution: vd​=J/(ne)=5×106/(5.9×1028×1.6×10−19)=5×106/(9.44×109)=5.3×10−4 m/s.
  • A steady current of 3 A flows for 2 minutes through a conductor. Find the total charge and the number of electrons. Solution: Q=It=3×120=360 C. Number of electrons =Q/e=360/(1.6×10−19)=2.25×1021 electrons.

Universal Facts

  • Drift velocity in a typical copper wire is only about 0.1 mm/s — a snail is millions of times faster than the electrons carrying current.
  • Despite the slow drift of electrons, the electric field propagates at a significant fraction of the speed of light (∼2×108 m/s in typical wires), which is why lights turn on instantly.
  • The product n⋅e (charge per unit volume of charge carriers) is sometimes called the charge density of the conductor and is intrinsic to the material.
  • In electrolytes and semiconductors, both positive and negative charges can contribute to current, unlike in metals where only electrons move. This affects the sign of the Hall voltage.
  • The drift velocity is proportional to the electric field: vd​=μE, where μ=eτ/m is the electron mobility. Higher mobility materials (like silver) conduct better.
