Enriched Content

Notes
  • Hooke's law statement: For small deformations, the extension (or compression) of an elastic body is directly proportional to the applied force: F = kx, where F is the applied force, x is the deformation, and k is the spring (force) constant. This linear region is governed by the interatomic potential energy well, where atoms act as if connected by springs. The proportionality limit is the point where this linear relationship breaks down, while the elastic limit is the point beyond which deformation becomes permanent (plastic).
  • Force (spring) constant k: k = F/x, measured in N/m. It measures the stiffness of the spring — a stiffer spring has a larger k and needs a bigger force for the same extension. k depends on the material, the wire diameter, the number of coils and the length of the spring. For a wire: k=EA/L, where E is Young's modulus, A is cross-sectional area, and L is length. For a helical spring, k=Gd4/(8nD3), where G is shear modulus, d is wire diameter, n is number of coils, and D is coil diameter.
  • Restoring Force: The spring exerts a restoring force F=−kx that always opposes the displacement. This is the basis of simple harmonic motion when combined with inertia.
  • What Spring constant really means: a spring of k = 200 N/m requires 200 N to stretch it by 1 metre. To stretch by any x, you need F = 200x N. It is the slope of the F–x graph in the linear (Hookean) region.
  • Two ways to combine springs: series: 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂ (softer than either); parallel: k = k₁ + k₂ (stiffer than either). Series springs stretch more for the same load — think of a long telephone-cord spring vs two short springs joined.
  • Worked example: a force of 40 N stretches a spring by 8 cm. k = F/x = 40/0.08 = 500 N/m. A 100 N force will then stretch it by x = 100/500 = 0.2 m = 20 cm.
  • Hooke's law on a graph: the F–x graph is a straight line through the origin while the elastic limit is respected. Beyond it, the graph curves; after plastic deformation, a larger force is needed for the same extension or the body does not return to its original length.
  • Application — weight balance (spring balance): the extension of a calibrated spring measures the applied weight. Since F = kx and weight = mg, the scale can be marked directly in newtons or kilograms.
  • Conditions for Hooke's law to hold: the deformation must be small, elastic (the body returns to original shape when the load is removed), and within the proportional limit. Overstretching a spring permanently changes its k.
Confusion
  • ❌ 'F = kx means force depends on extension.' ✅ Precisely that — for a spring, doubling the extension doubles the stretching force needed. The spring PULLS back with force kx (restoring force) opposite to the applied load.
  • ❌ 'k is a property only of the material.' ✅ k depends on the geometry too (length, coil radius, number of coils, wire thickness). It is the material property (Young's modulus) together with geometry that fixes k.
  • ❌ 'A stiffer spring stretches more.' ✅ A stiffer spring stretches LESS for the same force — it has a larger k, so x = F/k is smaller.
  • ❌ 'Extension is proportional to force at all loads.' ✅ Only within the elastic (proportional) limit. Beyond it the graph bends, and past the yield point the spring deforms permanently.
  • ❌ 'Series springs are stiffer.' ✅ Series springs are SOFTER (smaller total k). Two identical springs in series have k/2; in parallel they have 2k.
  • ❌ 'Force constant and friction constant (μ) are the same.' ✅ k (N/m) relates force to displacement of a spring; μ is a dimensionless friction coefficient relating friction to normal reaction.
Practice
  • A spring of length 20 cm stretches to 24 cm under a 30 N load. Find k.
  • Two springs k₁ = 100 N/m and k₂ = 200 N/m are joined (a) in series, (b) in parallel. Find the total k in each case.
  • A body of mass 0.5 kg hangs from a spring (k = 200 N/m). Find the extension it causes.
  • Find the force needed to compress a spring (k = 400 N/m) by 5 cm.
  • A spring balance reads 196 N for an unknown mass. What is the mass? (g = 9.8 m/s²)
  • A spring is cut in half. What happens to its force constant?
Universal Facts
  • Hooke (1635–1703) announced his law in 1676 as the anagram 'ceiiinosssttuv' which decoded to 'ut tensio, sic vis' — 'as the extension, so the force'.
  • Hooke's law is the basis of every spring, weighing machine, shock absorber, and suspension system. It is the first-order Taylor expansion of any potential energy function U(x) near a stable equilibrium point.
  • Below about 0.1% strain, almost every solid — steel, glass, bone — behaves approximately according to Hooke's law.
  • Combining springs in series or parallel follows rules identical to combining capacitors (series = reciprocal sum) and resistors (parallel = reciprocal sum).
  • The elastic potential energy stored in a stretched spring, ½kx², is exactly the region under the F–x graph.
  • Hertz Contact Theory: When two spheres touch, the contact area is not a point but a small circle. Hertz developed the theory for this, which is crucial for ball bearings and gear design.
Examples
  • F = kx: A 25 N force stretches a spring by 5 cm → k = 25/0.05 = 500 N/m.
  • Mass on spring: A 2 kg mass (weight 19.6 N) on a spring of k = 490 N/m sinks x = 19.6/490 = 0.04 m = 4 cm.
  • Series combination: k₁ = 300, k₂ = 600 → 1/k = 1/300 + 1/600 = 1/200 → k = 200 N/m (softer).
  • Parallel combination: k₁ = 300, k₂ = 600 → k = 900 N/m (stiffer).
  • Cutting in half: k_double; two halves in parallel restore the original k.
Practice Questions
  • A spring stretches by 6 cm when a 30 N force is applied. Find the spring constant and the energy stored.
  • Two springs of k = 120 N/m and k = 80 N/m are connected in series and hung with a 12 N weight. Find the total extension.
  • Same two springs are now connected in parallel and loaded with 12 N. Find the extension.
  • An 80 g mass hangs from a spring (k = 40 N/m). If it is pulled down 2 cm and released, find the maximum acceleration.
  • A spring (k = 500 N/m) is compressed by 4 cm and used to launch a 50 g ball. Find the launch speed.
Formulas
  • Hooke's law: F = kx
  • Spring constant: k = F/x (units N/m)
  • Series springs: 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂
  • Parallel springs: k = k₁ + k₂
  • Elastic potential energy: U = ½kx²; oscillation ω = √(k/m)
Key Points
  • F = kx holds only within the elastic limit — marked by the straight-line region of the F–x graph.
  • k measures stiffness in N/m; a larger k is a stiffer spring.
  • Series springs combine like 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂ (softer).
  • Parallel springs combine like k = k₁ + k₂ (stiffer).
  • Energy stored in a stretched spring is ½kx² — the triangle under the F–x graph.
Summary

Hooke's law (F = kx) states that deformation is proportional to applied force within the elastic limit. The spring constant k (N/m) measures stiffness. Springs combine in series (reciprocal sum, softer) and parallel (direct sum, stiffer). The stored elastic energy is ½kx², and the law underpins spring balances, suspension systems, and simple harmonic motion.

Special Notes
  • The restoring force of a spring is −kx: it always opposes the displacement from equilibrium.
  • A cut spring doubles its k — shorter springs are stiffer for the same material and geometry.
  • Working in consistent SI units: F in N, x in m, k in N/m — convert cm to m first.
  • The ½kx² energy formula comes from the area under the F–x line (triangle).
  • NEB often asks to find k from a graph (slope of the linear portion) or to combine springs.

Exam Short Tricks
  • Spring constant from graph: k = slope of F–x line in the linear region.
  • Series/parallel: use the same rule as capacitors (series) and resistors (parallel).
  • Extension is x = F/k — always convert cm to m before calculating.
  • Energy shortcut: U = ½kx² — no need to derive from the graph.
  • Cut a spring into n equal parts: each has k × n.
Exam Notes

  • Combinations of springs are a guaranteed numerical question.
  • Finding k from a graph slope is a frequent NEB short question.

  • Spring balance calibration uses F = kx with F = mg.
