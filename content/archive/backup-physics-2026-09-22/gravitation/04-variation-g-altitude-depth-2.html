Notes

  • g above the surface (altitude): at height h above Earth's surface, g' = GM/(R+h)² = g(R/(R+h))². As h increases, g decreases. For small h (h ≪ R), a binomial expansion gives g' ≈ g(1 − 2h/R) — a useful approximation.
  • g below the surface (depth): at depth d, only the mass inside the concentric sphere of radius (R−d) contributes: g' = g(1 − d/R) — gravity DECREASES linearly with depth. At the centre (d = R), g' = 0.
  • Why they differ: above the surface, whole-earth mass acts from a larger distance (1/r² law); below the surface, the shell above the point exerts zero net force (shell theorem) and only the inner sphere matters, whose mass scales as (R−d)³ while distance scales as (R−d) — giving linear decrease.
  • Worked example — altitude: at h = R (one Earth radius up), g' = g(R/2R)² = g/4 = 2.45 m/s². At h = 100 km, using the small-h formula, the fractional decrease is 2h/R = 2×100/6400 = 0.031, so g' ≈ 9.5 m/s².
  • Worked example — depth: at d = R/2 (3200 km deep), g' = g(1 − 1/2) = g/2 = 4.9 m/s². At d = R (centre) → g' = 0 (weightlessness, though pressure is immense).
  • Graphs (NEB favourite): g vs r rises linearly from 0 at the centre to g at the surface, then falls as 1/r² beyond the surface. The maximum value of g on Earth is at the surface.
  • Other causes of variation: (1) rotation of Earth: g reduced by ω²R cos²λ (λ = latitude), maximum at the equator; (2) shape: Earth is an oblate spheroid — g is larger at the poles (≈9.832) than the equator (≈9.78); (3) local density anomalies (mountains, mineral deposits) cause tiny local changes used in geophysics.
  • Altitude formula for satellites: for an orbit at height h, effective r = R + h: v = √(GM/(R+h)) and the orbital g is g' = GM/(R+h)² — the same g formula used at that radius.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'g increases with altitude.' ✅ Above the surface g DEcreases as 1/(R+h)²; below the surface it also decreases (linearly). g is max at the surface.
  • ❌ 'From height h, g falls by h/R.' ✅ The small-h approximation is 2h/R (from the square), not h/R. At h = 100 km, that is ≈ 3%.
  • ❌ 'Deep inside Earth, g keeps growing.' ✅ It decreases linearly: g' = g(1 − d/R), zero at the centre. The shell above you pulls equally in all directions (zero net).
  • ❌ 'g is the same at poles and equator.' ✅ Rotation and the oblate shape make g slightly larger at the poles (~0.5% difference).
  • ❌ 'At altitude 6400 km, g is half of surface.' ✅ At h = R, g' = g/4 — the height doubles the distance, so the square halves twice.
  • ❌ 'Weightlessness in orbit means no gravity.' ✅ Astronauts at 400 km still feel ~88% of surface gravity — they float because they are in free fall around Earth.

Universal Facts

  • Mines and boreholes show g decreasing with depth, exactly as the shell theorem predicts — gravity direction reverses inside Earth for local asymmetry reasons.
  • GOCE and GRACE satellites mapped tiny variations in Earth's g (from density differences) to model the interior and global water distribution.
  • At Earth's centre, g = 0 but pressure is ~3.6 million atmospheres — so you'd be crushed, not weightless in the usual sense.
  • The international gravity formula accounts for latitude: g(λ) ≈ 9.7803(1 + 0.0053 sin²λ) m/s².
  • Astronauts in low Earth orbit experience ~0.9 g of gravity yet float — free fall, not absence of gravity, explains weightlessness.

Examples

  • h = R: g' = g/4 = 2.45 m/s².
  • h = 200 km: g' ≈ g(1 − 2×200/6400) = 9.8 × 0.9375 = 9.2 m/s² (ISS altitude ≈ 400 km → ≈8.7 m/s²).
  • d = R/2: g' = g/2 = 4.9 m/s².
  • d = R: g' = 0.
  • Equator vs pole: g_eq ≈ 9.78, g_pole ≈ 9.83 — difference ≈ 0.5%.

Formulas

  • g at altitude h: g' = g(R/(R+h))²
  • Small-h approximation: g' ≈ g(1 − 2h/R)
  • g at depth d: g' = g(1 − d/R)
  • g at latitude λ due to rotation: g' = g − ω²R cos²λ
  • g inside a uniform sphere: g' = g·r/R (r ≤ R)

Key Points

  • Above the surface: g' = g(R/(R+h))² — decreases as 1/(R+h)².
  • Below the surface: g' = g(1 − d/R) — decreases linearly to zero at the centre.
  • g is maximum at the surface.
  • Rotation and oblateness also reduce g at the equator.
  • The g–r graph: linear rise inside, 1/r² fall outside.

Summary

g varies with altitude (g' = g(R/(R+h))²), depth (g' = g(1 − d/R)), and latitude. Above the surface g falls as the inverse square of distance; below the surface the shell theorem makes it fall linearly to zero at the centre. The g–r graph is a straight rise then a 1/r² fall.

Special Notes

  • Small-height formula: g' ≈ g(1 − 2h/R) — remember the factor 2.
  • Shell theorem is the tool for points inside Earth — shells outside contribute zero net force.
  • g at poles is slightly greater than at the equator due to rotation and oblateness.
  • NEB asks to derive both formulas and to sketch the g–r graph.
  • Satellite problems use r = R + h in g = GM/r² too.

Important Statements

  • At altitude h, g' = g(R/(R+h))².
  • At depth d, g' = g(1 − d/R).
  • g is maximum at Earth's surface.
  • Inside a uniform sphere, g varies linearly with distance from the centre.
  • Rotation and non-spherical shape cause additional small variations of g with latitude.

Exam Short Tricks

  • h = R → g/4; d = R/2 → g/2 — memorise these anchor values.
  • Percentage change at small h ≈ (2h/R)×100%.
  • For depth use linear term only — no square.
  • Inside the sphere r ≤ R: g' = g·r/R (direct proportion).
  • Weight at centre of Earth: zero apparent weight (g = 0).

Exam Notes

  • Sketch-the-graph questions (inside + outside) appear in exams.
  • Numericals mixing altitude and depth are standard.
  • The rotation term g' = g − ω²R cos²λ is a common short answer.
  • Comparing g at poles vs equator is a favourite conceptual MCQ.
