Enriched Content

Notes
  • What GPS is: the Global Positioning System is a satellite-based navigation system that tells a receiver its exact 3D position on Earth. It uses the same gravitational/orbital physics as any satellite, which is why it lives in the gravitation chapter.
  • Principle of position fixing: each GPS satellite broadcasts a radio signal stamped with its position and precise transmission time. The receiver measures the travel time of signals from at least four satellites and solves for (x, y, z, t) — the fourth equation corrects the receiver clock offset.
  • How distance is measured: distance = speed of light × time delay. Since the receiver's clock is not atomic-precise, a fourth satellite provides the clock-error term, giving accuracy of a few metres.
  • Satellite constellation: 24+ satellites orbit at ≈ 20,200 km altitude (medium Earth orbit) in six planes inclined 55° to the equator. Each satellite has a period of ≈ 11 h 58 min (half a sidereal day) and speed ≈ 3.87 km/s — from T = 2π√(r³/GM).
  • Why the orbits are chosen: six planes × four satellites each guarantee that at least 4–6 satellites are above the horizon at any place and time. The 55° inclination covers up to the poles, unlike a geostationary belt.
  • Relativity in GPS: satellite clocks run faster than ground clocks by ~38 µs/day (7 µs from special relativity + 45 µs from general relativity). This systematic shift must be compensated, otherwise GPS positions would drift by ~10 km per day — a practical proof of relativity.
  • Accuracy and sources of error: ionospheric delays, multipath reflections, satellite clocks, and geometry (DOP). With corrections (differential GPS / WAAS / dual-frequency) accuracy reaches centimetre level for surveying.
  • Applications: navigation (cars, ships, aircraft), mapping, timing synchronisation for power grids and financial networks, agriculture, disaster relief, and scientific studies (tectonic plate motion). GPS is a direct practical payoff of satellite motion physics.
Confusion
  • ❌ 'GPS signal carries your position from the satellite.' ✅ The satellite only sends its OWN position + time. YOUR receiver computes your position by triangulating delays.
  • ❌ 'Three satellites suffice for full 3D positioning.' ✅ Three give (x,y,z) only if the clock is perfect; a fourth is required for a practical unknown clock offset.
  • ❌ 'GPS works by measuring signal strength.' ✅ It measures signal TRAVEL TIME (c × Δt), not strength.
  • ❌ 'GPS satellites are geostationary.' ✅ They orbit at ≈ 20,200 km in inclined MEO orbits with ≈ 12 h periods — not geostationary.
  • ❌ 'Relativistic corrections are too tiny to matter.' ✅ Without them GPS would be off by ~10 km/day. The effect is 38 µs/day — essential.
  • ❌ 'Position accuracy is limited by the number of satellites visible.' ✅ With ≥ 4 satellites you can fix position; more satellites reduce error statistics but don't change the basic math.

  • Show that a satellite at 20,200 km altitude has a period ≈ 12 h.
  • Explain why at least four satellites are needed for a position fix.
  • Compute the extra path delay if a signal takes 67 ms to reach a receiver.
  • Find the speed of a GPS satellite from GM and r.
  • Explain the special and general relativistic clock corrections.
  • Estimate the position error if the receiver clock is off by 1 µs.
Universal Facts
  • GPS was developed by the US Department of Defense and declared fully operational in 1995; it is freely available worldwide.
  • The constellation was designed by military engineers using Newton's law and Kepler's equations — a textbook application of gravitational physics.
  • The GPS satellites transmit on L1 (1575.42 MHz) and L2 (1227.60 MHz); dual-frequency receivers cancel ionospheric delay.
  • GPS timing synchronises global telecom networks and power grids — a 1 µs clock error maps to ~300 m of position error.
  • India's NAVIC and other regional systems use the same principles as GPS.
Examples
  • Distance from delay: signal travel 0.07 s → distance = 3×10⁸ × 0.07 = 21,000 km.
  • GPS speed: v = √(GM/r) at r ≈ 26,600 km from Earth's centre ≈ 3.87 km/s.
  • GPS period ≈ 11 h 58 min (half a sidereal day).
  • Clock offset: 1 µs error → position error ≈ 300 m.
  • Relativity: 38 µs/day accumulated shift → ~10 km/day without correction.
Practice Questions
  • A GPS satellite orbits at 20,200 km. Find its orbital speed and period.
  • Why does a receiver need four satellites instead of three?
  • If a GPS signal takes 65 ms to arrive, how far away is that satellite?
  • Explain both special and general relativistic corrections to GPS clocks.
  • Estimate the daily position error if all relativistic corrections were ignored.
  • Explain how dual-frequency GPS removes ionospheric delay.
Formulas
  • Distance from delay: d = c × Δt
  • GPS speed: v = √(GM/r) (r ≈ 26,600 km)
  • GPS period: T = 2π√(r³/GM) ≈ 12 h sidereal
  • Clock error mapping: Δposition = c × Δt_clock
  • Relativistic shift: Δt ≈ 38 µs/day (GR +45 µs, SR −7 µs)
Key Points
  • GPS fixes position by measuring radio signal travel time from ≥ 4 satellites.
  • Four satellites allow solving the receiver clock error as a fourth unknown.
  • GPS satellites orbit at ≈ 20,200 km in inclined MEO with ≈ 12 h periods.
  • Relativistic corrections (~38 µs/day) are essential for metre-level accuracy.
  • GPS relies directly on satellite motion physics (v = √(GM/r), T = 2π√(r³/GM)).
Summary

The Global Positioning System uses ≥ 4 satellites whose positions and times are known to compute a receiver's (x, y, z, t) by measuring signal travel times (d = c·Δt). The constellation orbits at ≈ 20,200 km with ≈ 12 h periods, and relativistic clock corrections of ~38 µs/day are essential to maintain metre-level accuracy.

Special Notes
  • The fourth satellite is not for redundancy — it solves the unknown receiver clock offset.
  • GPS satellites are NOT geostationary; they sit in inclined medium Earth orbits.
  • The relativistic effect: satellite clocks gain ~38 µs/day; compensation keeps GPS accurate.
  • Error sources: ionosphere, multipath, clock drift, geometry (DOP).
  • NEB asks conceptual questions about the working principle and the satellite orbit numbers.
Important Statements
  • GPS determines position by measuring the travel time of radio signals from at least four satellites.
  • Each satellite broadcasts its position and time; the receiver solves for x, y, z and clock error.
  • GPS satellites orbit at about 20,200 km with a period near 12 hours.
  • Relativistic corrections of about 38 µs per day are essential for GPS accuracy.
  • GPS is a practical application of orbital mechanics and gravitational physics.
Important Notes
  • GPS needs ≥ 4 satellites to solve x, y, z and clock error simultaneously.
  • Each satellite broadcasts its own position and precise time; the receiver does the compute.
  • GPS orbit: altitude ≈ 20,200 km, period ≈ 12 h, speed ≈ 3.87 km/s.
  • Relativistic corrections (~38 µs/day) are mandatory for GPS accuracy.
  • Distance is derived from travel time (d = c·Δt), not from signal strength.
Exam Short Tricks
  • Four satellites = four unknowns (x, y, z, t) — the key reasoning line.
  • 1 µs clock error ≈ 300 m of position error — instant conversion.
  • GPS altitude 20,200 km / period 12 h — memorise as anchor numbers.
  • Relativity numbers: +45 µs (GR slower ground) − 7 µs (SR) → +38 µs/day net.
  • Distance = speed of light × delay — always use c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s.
