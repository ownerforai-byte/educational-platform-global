Enriched Content

Notes
  • Law statement: every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres: F=r2Gm1​m2​​.
  • The universal constant G: G=6.67×10−11 N m2/kg2. It is the same everywhere in the universe (hence 'universal') and was first measured by Cavendish (1798) using a torsion balance. G is very small, which is why the gravitational attraction between everyday objects is imperceptible.
  • Vector form & direction: the force on mass m₁ due to m₂ is F12​=−Gr2m1​m2​​r^12​, directed along the line joining the centres, always attractive. Newton's third law applies: F12​=−F21​.
  • Superposition: when several masses act on one particle, the net force is the vector sum of the individual forces: Fnet​=∑Fi​. Each pair obeys the inverse-square law independently.
  • Worked example — Earth and Moon: mEarth​=6×1024 kg, mMoon​=7.4×1022 kg, r=3.84×108 m. F=(3.84×108)26.67×10−11×6×1024×7.4×1022​=2.0×1020 N — enough to hold the Moon in orbit.
  • Gravitational field strength: g=mF​=r2GM​, the force per unit mass at a distance r from mass M. On Earth's surface g=9.8 m/s2, which gives Earth's mass: M=GgR2​≈5.97×1024 kg.
  • Why G is used in orbits: equating gravitational force to centripetal force (r2GMm​=rmv2​) gives orbital velocity v=rGM​​ and period T=2πGMr3​​ — Kepler's third law follows directly.
  • Important comparisons: gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces — between two protons the gravitational force is ∼10−36 times the electric force — yet it dominates cosmology because mass adds up while electric charges cancel.
Confusion
  • ❌ 'There is a force of gravity only near Earth.' ✅ Gravity acts between ANY two masses anywhere in the universe — it never switches off, it only weakens with the square of distance.
  • ❌ 'G can change with temperature or medium.' ✅ G is a universal constant — independent of temperature, pressure, medium, or the objects involved.
  • ❌ 'The force depends on the square of the distance — it dies quickly.' ✅ It dies as 1/r² but never reaches zero; at double distance the force is a quarter, at ten times it is one-hundredth.
  • ❌ 'Heavier objects fall faster because gravity pulls them harder.' ✅ Gravity pulls harder on heavier objects (F=mg), but their acceleration is the same: a=F/m=g, hence all bodies in free fall accelerate equally (ignoring air resistance).
  • ❌ 'Earth pulls the Moon but the Moon does not pull Earth.' ✅ The forces are equal and opposite (Newton's third law). Earth and Moon pull on each other with equal forces — the Moon's acceleration is larger because its mass is smaller.
  • ❌ 'Inside Earth gravity stops.' ✅ Inside a uniform sphere, g decreases linearly with distance from the centre, reaching zero at the centre — it is not zero except exactly at the centre.
Practice
  • Find the force between two 50 kg students 1 m apart. (F=126.67×10−11×50×50​=1.7×10−7 N.)
  • Two masses 2 kg and 3 kg are 2 m apart; compute the force.
  • At what distance must two 100 kg masses be placed for the force to be 10⁻⁷ N?
  • Find g at a height equal to Earth's radius above the surface (g′=g/4).
  • Show that the acceleration due to gravity is independent of the mass of the falling body.
  • Compute Earth's mass using g=9.8, R=6.4×106 m, G=6.67×10−11.
Universal Facts
  • Isaac Newton published the law of gravitation in his Principia (1687), synthesising the work of Kepler and Galileo — it was the first unified theory of terrestrial and celestial mechanics.
  • The modern value of G=6.674×10−11 is still the least precisely known fundamental constant because gravity is so weak.
  • The inverse-square character of gravity was seen by Newton as proof that planets are pulled toward the Sun rather than pushed.
  • Gravity's weakness explains why everyday objects attract negligibly but planets, stars, and galaxies dominate large-scale structure.
  • The same law that keeps you on the ground also governs satellite orbits, tidal effects, and the orbits of binary stars.
Examples
  • Two 1 kg masses 1 m apart: F=6.67×10−11 N — a trillion times weaker than a single paperweight's weight.
  • Earth–Moon force ≈2×1020 N, enough to lift a mass of ≈2×1019 kg.
  • g at 6400 km altitude (h=R): g′=g(R+hR​)2=9.8/4=2.45 m/s2.
  • Mars: M=6.4×1023 kg, R=3.4×106 m → gmars​=R2GM​≈3.7 m/s2.
  • Satellite at r=7×106 m: v=rGM​​≈7.56 km/s (low Earth orbit speed).
Practice Questions
  • Two bodies of masses 80 kg and 200 kg are 0.5 m apart. Find the gravitational force between them.
  • At what distance from the centre of Earth is the gravitational field reduced to one quarter of its surface value?
  • Calculate g on the surface of the Moon given M=7.4×1022 kg and R=1.74×106 m.
  • Find the force between Earth (6×1024 kg) and a 70 kg person standing on its surface (R=6.4×106 m).
  • Three 5 kg masses form an equilateral triangle of side 1 m; find the net force on any one mass.
  • If the distance between two masses is doubled, by what factor does the force change?

  • Gravitational field strength: g=r2GM​
  • Orbital velocity: v=rGM​​
  • Orbital period: T=2πGMr3​​
  • g at height h: g′=g(R+hR​)2
Key Points
  • F=r2Gm1​m2​​ — force is always attractive, proportional to both masses, inverse-square in distance.
  • G=6.67×10−11 N⋅m2/kg2 is universal and independent of environment.
  • g=r2GM​ is the force per unit mass; on Earth's surface it is 9.8 m/s².
  • The law explains orbital motion, tides, and planetary orbits.
  • Gravity between everyday objects is negligible because G is tiny.
Summary

Newton's law of gravitation states that every mass attracts every other mass with F=r2Gm1​m2​​. The universal constant G=6.67×10−11 N⋅m2/kg2 couples mass and distance to force. The law gives the gravitational field g=r2GM​, explains orbital motion (v=rGM​​, T=2πGMr3​​), and underpins all celestial mechanics.

Special Notes
  • Use the centre-to-centre distance in r — for spheres this is the distance between centres.
  • G is the same everywhere; g is NOT — it varies with location and height.
  • Newton's third law applies to gravity: the forces on the two masses are equal and opposite.
  • For points above Earth's surface use g=(R+h)2GM​; inside use g=g(1−d/R).
  • NEB numericals commonly give m₁, m₂, r and ask for F; or ask for g at a height.

  • The gravitational constant G=6.67×10−11 N⋅m2/kg2 is a universal constant of nature.
  • Gravitational force is always attractive and acts along the line joining the centres.
  • The gravitational field strength g=r2GM​ equals the force per unit mass.
  • Newton's law of gravitation explains planetary orbits, satellites, and tides.

  • Doubling the distance quarters the force; halving it quadruples the force (inverse-square).
  • For g at height: g′=g(R+hR​)2; at h=R, g′=g/4.

  • To find Earth's mass: M=GgR2​.
  • Orbital speed around Earth: v=rGM​​≈7.9 km/s near the surface.
Exam Notes
  • Numericals on F=r2Gm1​m2​​ are a certainty — drill at least five.
  • g at a height or depth is a recurring short question.
  • Comparing gravitational and electric forces between protons is a popular conceptual MCQ.
  • Kepler's third law can be derived from Newton's law — a common derivation question.
  • Know the difference between G (universal constant) and g (local field).
