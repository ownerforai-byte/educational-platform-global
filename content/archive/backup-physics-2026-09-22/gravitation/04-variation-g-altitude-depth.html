Enriched Content

Notes
  • g above the surface (altitude): at height h above Earth's surface, g′=(R+h)2GM​=g(R+hR​)2. As h increases, g decreases. For small h (h≪R), a binomial expansion gives g′≈g(1−R2h​) — a useful approximation.
  • g below the surface (depth): at depth d, only the mass inside the concentric sphere of radius (R−d) contributes: g′=g(1−Rd​) — gravity DECREASES linearly with depth. At the centre (d=R), g′=0.
  • Why they differ: above the surface, whole-earth mass acts from a larger distance (1/r2 law); below the surface, the shell above the point exerts zero net force (shell theorem) and only the inner sphere matters, whose mass scales as (R−d)^3 while distance scales as (R−d) — giving linear decrease.
  • Worked example — altitude: at h=R (one Earth radius up), g′=g(2RR​)2=g/4=2.45 m/s2. At h=100 km, using the small-h formula, the fractional decrease is 2h/R=2×100/6400=0.031, so g′≈9.5 m/s2.
  • Worked example — depth: at d=R/2 (3200 km deep), g′=g(1−1/2)=g/2=4.9 m/s2. At d=R (centre) → g′=0 (weightlessness, though pressure is immense).
  • Graphs (NEB favourite): g vs r rises linearly from 0 at the centre to g at the surface, then falls as 1/r2 beyond the surface. The maximum value of g on Earth is at the surface.
  • Other causes of variation: (1) rotation of Earth: g reduced by ω2Rcos2λ (λ = latitude), maximum at the equator; (2) shape: Earth is an oblate spheroid — g is larger at the poles (≈9.832) than the equator (≈9.78); (3) local density anomalies (mountains, mineral deposits) cause tiny local changes used in geophysics.
  • Altitude formula for satellites: for an orbit at height h, effective r=R+h: v=R+hGM​​ and the orbital g is g′=(R+h)2GM​ — the same g formula used at that radius.
Confusion
  • ❌ 'g increases with altitude.' ✅ Above the surface g DEcreases as (R+h)21​; below the surface it also decreases (linearly). g is max at the surface.
  • ❌ 'From height h, g falls by h/R.' ✅ The small-h approximation is 2h/R (from the square), not h/R. At h=100 km, that is ≈ 3%.
  • ❌ 'Deep inside Earth, g keeps growing.' ✅ It decreases linearly: g′=g(1−Rd​), zero at the centre. The shell above you pulls equally in all directions (zero net).
  • ❌ 'g is the same at poles and equator.' ✅ Rotation and the oblate shape make g slightly larger at the poles (~0.5% difference).
  • ❌ 'At altitude 6400 km, g is half of surface.' ✅ At h=R, g′=g/4 — the height doubles the distance, so the square halves twice.
  • ❌ 'Weightlessness in orbit means no gravity.' ✅ Astronauts at 400 km still feel ~88% of surface gravity — they float because they are in free fall around Earth.
Practice
  • Find g at a height of 1000 km (R=6400 km, g=9.8).
  • Find g at a depth of 1000 km.
  • At what height is g reduced by 1%? (Use the small-h formula.)
  • Compare g at the equator and pole given Earth's rotation speed at equator ≈465 m/s.
  • Show that g at h=R is g/4.
  • Sketch the g–r graph from the centre of Earth to 3R and label all key values.
Universal Facts
  • Mines and boreholes show g decreasing with depth, exactly as the shell theorem predicts — gravity direction reverses inside Earth for local asymmetry reasons.
  • GOCE and GRACE satellites mapped tiny variations in Earth's g (from density differences) to model the interior and global water distribution.
  • At Earth's centre, g=0 but pressure is ~3.6 million atmospheres — so you'd be crushed, not weightless in the usual sense.
  • The international gravity formula accounts for latitude: g(λ)≈9.7803(1+0.0053sin2λ) m/s2.
  • Astronauts in low Earth orbit experience ≈0.9g of gravity yet float — free fall, not absence of gravity, explains weightlessness.
Examples
  • h=R: g′=g/4=2.45 m/s2.
  • h=200 km: g′≈g(1−2×200/6400)=9.8×0.9375=9.2 m/s2 (ISS altitude ≈400 km → ≈8.7 m/s2).
  • d=R/2: g′=g/2=4.9 m/s2.
  • d=R: g′=0.
  • Equator vs pole: geq​≈9.78, gpole​≈9.83 — difference ≈0.5%.
Practice Questions
  • Find the value of g at a height of 3200 km above Earth's surface (g=9.8, R=6400 km).
  • Find g at a depth of 3200 km below Earth's surface.
  • At what height will g reduce to 81% of its surface value?
  • At what depth will g reduce to 50% of its surface value?
  • Compute the percentage change in g at the top of Mount Everest (h≈8.8 km).
  • Explain with a diagram how g varies from the centre of Earth to a distance far beyond the surface.
Formulas
  • g at altitude h: g′=g(R+hR​)2
  • Small-h approximation: g′≈g(1−R2h​)
  • g at depth d: g′=g(1−Rd​)
  • g at latitude λ due to rotation: g′=g−ω2Rcos2λ
  • g inside a uniform sphere: g′=Rgr​ (r≤R)
Key Points
  • Above the surface: g′=g(R+hR​)2 — decreases as (R+h)21​.
  • Below the surface: g′=g(1−Rd​) — decreases linearly to zero at the centre.
  • g is maximum at the surface.
  • Rotation and oblateness also reduce g at the equator.
  • The g–r graph: linear rise inside, 1/r2 fall outside.
Summary

g varies with altitude (g′=g(R+hR​)2), depth (g′=g(1−Rd​)), and latitude. Above the surface g falls as the inverse square of distance; below the surface the shell theorem makes it fall linearly to zero at the centre. The g–r graph is a straight rise then a 1/r2 fall.

Special Notes
  • Small-height formula: g′≈g(1−R2h​) — remember the factor 2.
  • Shell theorem is the tool for points inside Earth — shells outside contribute zero net force.
  • g at poles is slightly greater than at the equator due to rotation and oblateness.
  • NEB asks to derive both formulas and to sketch the g–r graph.
  • Satellite problems use r=R+h in g=r2GM​ too.
Important Statements
  • At altitude h, g′=g(R+hR​)2.
  • At depth d, g′=g(1−Rd​).
  • g is maximum at Earth's surface.
  • Inside a uniform sphere, g varies linearly with distance from the centre.
  • Rotation and non-spherical shape cause additional small variations of g with latitude.
Important Notes
  • At altitude: g′=g(R+hR​)2; at depth: g′=g(1−Rd​).
  • g is maximum at the surface and falls on both sides (linear inside, 1/r2 outside).
  • The small-height approximation g′≈g(1−R2h​) has a factor of 2.
  • Shell theorem: shells outside the point contribute zero net field.
  • Rotation reduces g at the equator: g′=g−ω2Rcos2λ.
Exam Short Tricks
  • h=Rightarrowg/4; d=R/2ightarrowg/2 — memorise these anchor values.
  • Percentage change at small h≈(2h/R)×100%.
  • For depth use linear term only — no square.
  • Inside the sphere r≤R: g′=Rgr​ (direct proportion).
  • Weight at centre of Earth: zero apparent weight (g=0).
Exam Notes
  • Deriving g′=g(1−R2h​) from the binomial expansion is frequently asked.
  • Sketch-the-graph questions (inside + outside) appear in exams.
  • Numericals mixing altitude and depth are standard.
  • The rotation term g′=g−ω2Rcos2λ is a common short answer.
  • Comparing g at poles vs equator is a favourite conceptual MCQ.
