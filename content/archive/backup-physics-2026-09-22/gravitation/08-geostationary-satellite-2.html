Notes

  • Geostationary satellite: a satellite that appears stationary relative to a point on the Earth's equator. It must satisfy three conditions: (1) orbit in the plane of the equator (equatorial plane), (2) orbit in the same direction as Earth's rotation (west → east), and (3) have a period exactly equal to Earth's rotation period (T = 24 hours).
  • Height above Earth: using T = 2π√(r³/GM) with T = 86400 s → r ≈ 4.22×10⁷ m from the centre → altitude h = r − R ≈ 35,800 km above the surface. This is about 5.6 Earth radii.
  • Orbital speed: v₀ = 2πr/T = 2π×4.22×10⁷/86400 ≈ 3.07 km/s. It matches Earth's angular speed: the satellite and the point below rotate together, so the satellite hangs over the same longitude.
  • Only the equator works: a satellite over the equator is the only way the orbit plane can contain both the satellite and Earth's rotation axis while keeping the satellite fixed over one point. An inclined orbit would carry the satellite north and south each day.
  • Why 24 h is exact: for T = T_Earth, the angular speeds match: ω_sat = ω_Earth. From r³ = GMT²/4π² the altitude is fixed (~35,800 km) — there is a unique geostationary height.
  • Applications: communications satellites (broadcast, TV, internet), weather satellites (HIMAWARI, INSAT type), and some navigation augmentation systems. Their fixed position means ground dishes never need to move.
  • Geostationary vs geosynchronous: a geosynchronous satellite has a 24-h period but may have an inclined or elliptical orbit, so it drifts north/south in the sky. A geostationary satellite is a special geosynchronous satellite confined to the equatorial plane at fixed longitude.
  • Energy at geostationary orbit: for m = 1000 kg, U = −GMm/r ≈ −9.4×10⁹ J, E_total = −GMm/2r ≈ −4.7×10⁹ J. Launching to this height needs a large boost — the standard launch uses a geostationary transfer orbit (GTO) followed by an apogee burn.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'Any 24-hour satellite is geostationary.' ✅ A geostationary satellite must ALSO be equatorial, circular, and west-to-east. A geosynchronous one just matches the 24-h period.
  • ❌ 'Geostationary orbit can be at any height above the equator.' ✅ There is exactly ONE geostationary altitude: ≈ 35,800 km. It is set by Earth's rotation period.
  • ❌ 'A geostationary satellite stays above one spot because it has no gravity.' ✅ It stays because its orbital period exactly equals Earth's rotation; gravity still holds it in orbit at v ≈ 3.07 km/s.
  • ❌ 'The satellite is stationary in space.' ✅ It moves at 3.07 km/s; it is stationary only relative to the rotating Earth.
  • ❌ 'At high latitudes people see it overhead.' ✅ A geostationary satellite is always over the equator — at high latitudes it appears low on the horizon, and near the poles it is not visible at all.
  • ❌ 'The Moon is a geostationary satellite.' ✅ The Moon has a 27.3-day period and an inclined orbit — it is not geostationary.

Universal Facts

  • Arthur C. Clarke proposed the geostationary orbit in 1945 (in Wireless World); it is sometimes called the Clarke orbit.
  • The first geostationary satellite was Syncom 3 (1964), used to broadcast the 1964 Tokyo Olympics.
  • The geostationary belt (~35,800 km over the equator) is a limited resource — equatorial countries claim sovereignty over its orbital slots above their territories.
  • Typical geostationary satellites weigh thousands of kg at launch; most is fuel used to reach and maintain the orbit.
  • Solar wind and gravitational pull of the Moon/Sun slowly tilt geostationary orbits; station-keeping burns correct this.

Examples

  • Altitude: r = (GMT²/4π²)^(1/3) ≈ 4.22×10⁷ m; h = r − R ≈ 35,800 km.
  • Speed: v = 2πr/T ≈ 3.07 km/s.
  • Period: exactly 24 hours (matches Earth's rotation).
  • Angular speed: ω = 2π/86400 ≈ 7.27×10⁻⁵ rad/s, equal to Earth's.
  • View: always over the SAME point on the equator — ideal for fixed dish antennas.

Formulas

  • Geostationary radius: r = (GMT²/4π²)^(1/3) ≈ 4.22×10⁷ m
  • Altitude: h = r − R ≈ 35,800 km
  • Orbital speed: v = 2πr/T ≈ 3.07 km/s
  • Angular speed: ω = 2π/T_Earth ≈ 7.27×10⁻⁵ rad/s
  • Energy: U = −GMm/r; E_total = −GMm/2r

Key Points

  • A geostationary satellite orbits in the equatorial plane, west-to-east, with T = 24 h.
  • Its unique altitude is ≈ 35,800 km (r ≈ 4.22×10⁷ m).
  • v ≈ 3.07 km/s — matches Earth's rotation, so it appears fixed overhead.
  • Applications: communication, broadcasting, weather satellites.
  • Geosynchronous ≠ geostationary — geosynchronous only matches the period.

Summary

A geostationary satellite orbits in the equatorial plane west-to-east with a 24-hour period, so it appears fixed over one point on the equator. It sits at a unique altitude of 35,800 km (r = 4.22×10⁷ m) and moves at 3.07 km/s. Geostationary orbits are vital for communications and weather satellites.

Special Notes

  • The key difference from geosynchronous: geostationary also requires the equatorial plane and zero inclination.
  • Altitude 35,800 km = about 5.6 Earth radii.
  • At high latitudes geostationary satellites appear low on the horizon.
  • To reach geostationary orbit rockets use a transfer ellipse + apogee burn.
  • NEB asks to compute the geostationary radius from the period or vice versa.

Important Statements

  • A geostationary satellite has a period of 24 hours and orbits the equator west-to-east.
  • Its orbital radius is fixed: r ≈ 4.22×10⁷ m (altitude ≈ 35,800 km).
  • The orbital speed is ≈ 3.07 km/s, matching Earth's rotation.
  • Geostationary satellites appear fixed above a single point on the equator.
  • They are used for communication, broadcasting and weather monitoring.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Memorise: geostationary altitude ≈ 36,000 km (35,800 km more precisely).
  • From T to r: r = (GMT²/4π²)^(1/3) — plug 86400 s directly.
  • Speed shortcut: v = 2πr/T or v = √(GM/r); both give ≈ 3 km/s.
  • If T = 24 h BUT orbit is inclined — it's geosynchronous, not geostationary (quick MCQ trap).
  • Energy: just use E = −GMm/2r; KE = GMm/2r.
