Notes

  • Law statement: every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres: F = Gm₁m₂/r².
  • The universal constant G: G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg². It is the same everywhere in the universe (hence 'universal') and was first measured by Cavendish (1798) using a torsion balance. G is very small, which is why the gravitational attraction between everyday objects is imperceptible.
  • Vector form & direction: the force on mass m₁ due to m₂ is F₁₂ = −G(m₁m₂/r²) r̂₁₂, directed along the line joining the centres, always attractive. Newton's third law applies: F₁₂ = −F₂₁.
  • Superposition: when several masses act on one particle, the net force is the vector sum of the individual forces: F_net = Σ Fᵢ. Each pair obeys the inverse-square law independently.
  • Worked example — Earth and Moon: m_Earth = 6×10²⁴ kg, m_Moon = 7.4×10²² kg, r = 3.84×10⁸ m. F = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 6×10²⁴ × 7.4×10²² / (3.84×10⁸)² = 2.0×10²⁰ N — enough to hold the Moon in orbit.
  • Gravitational field strength: g = F/m = GM/r², the force per unit mass at a distance r from mass M. On Earth's surface g = 9.8 m/s², which gives Earth's mass: M = gR²/G ≈ 5.97×10²⁴ kg.
  • Why G is used in orbits: equating gravitational force to centripetal force (GMm/r² = mv²/r) gives orbital velocity v = √(GM/r) and period T = 2π√(r³/GM) — Kepler's third law follows directly.
  • Important comparisons: gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces — between two protons the gravitational force is ~10⁻³⁶ times the electric force — yet it dominates cosmology because mass adds up while electric charges cancel.

Confusion

  • ❌ 'There is a force of gravity only near Earth.' ✅ Gravity acts between ANY two masses anywhere in the universe — it never switches off, it only weakens with the square of distance.
  • ❌ 'G can change with temperature or medium.' ✅ G is a universal constant — independent of temperature, pressure, medium, or the objects involved.
  • ❌ 'The force depends on the square of the distance — it dies quickly.' ✅ It dies as 1/r² but never reaches zero; at double distance the force is a quarter, at ten times it is one-hundredth.
  • ❌ 'Heavier objects fall faster because gravity pulls them harder.' ✅ Gravity pulls harder on heavier objects (F = mg), but their acceleration is the same: a = F/m = g, hence all bodies in free fall accelerate equally (ignoring air resistance).
  • ❌ 'Earth pulls the Moon but the Moon does not pull Earth.' ✅ The forces are equal and opposite (Newton's third law). Earth and Moon pull on each other with equal forces — the Moon's acceleration is larger because its mass is smaller.
  • ❌ 'Inside Earth gravity stops.' ✅ Inside a uniform sphere, g decreases linearly with distance from the centre, reaching zero at the centre — it is not zero except exactly at the centre.

Universal Facts

  • Isaac Newton published the law of gravitation in his Principia (1687), synthesising the work of Kepler and Galileo — it was the first unified theory of terrestrial and celestial mechanics.
  • The modern value of G = 6.674×10⁻¹¹ is still the least precisely known fundamental constant because gravity is so weak.
  • The inverse-square character of gravity was seen by Newton as proof that planets are pulled toward the Sun rather than pushed.
  • Gravity's weakness explains why everyday objects attract negligibly but planets, stars, and galaxies dominate large-scale structure.
  • The same law that keeps you on the ground also governs satellite orbits, tidal effects, and the orbits of binary stars.

Examples

  • Two 1 kg masses 1 m apart: F = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N — a trillion times weaker than a single paperweight's weight.
  • Earth–Moon force ≈ 2×10²⁰ N, enough to lift a mass of ≈ 2×10¹⁹ kg.
  • g at 6400 km altitude (h = R): g' = g(R/(R+h))² = 9.8/4 = 2.45 m/s².
  • Mars: M = 6.4×10²³ kg, R = 3.4×10⁶ m → g_mars = GM/R² ≈ 3.7 m/s².
  • Satellite at r = 7×10⁶ m: v = √(GM/r) ≈ 7.56 km/s (low Earth orbit speed).

Formulas

  • Newton's law of gravitation: F = Gm₁m₂/r²
  • Gravitational field strength: g = GM/r²
  • Orbital velocity: v = √(GM/r)
  • Orbital period: T = 2π√(r³/GM)
  • g at height h: g' = g(R/(R+h))²

Key Points

  • F = Gm₁m₂/r² — force is always attractive, proportional to both masses, inverse-square in distance.
  • G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² is universal and independent of environment.
  • g = GM/r² is the force per unit mass; on Earth's surface it is 9.8 m/s².
  • The law explains orbital motion, tides, and planetary orbits.
  • Gravity between everyday objects is negligible because G is tiny.

Summary

Newton's law of gravitation states that every mass attracts every other mass with F = Gm₁m₂/r². The universal constant G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² couples mass and distance to force. The law gives the gravitational field g = GM/r², explains orbital motion (v = √(GM/r), T = 2π√(r³/GM)), and underpins all celestial mechanics.

Special Notes

  • Use the centre-to-centre distance in r — for spheres this is the distance between centres.
  • G is the same everywhere; g is NOT — it varies with location and height.
  • Newton's third law applies to gravity: the forces on the two masses are equal and opposite.
  • For points above Earth's surface use g = GM/(R+h)²; inside use g = g(1 − d/R).
  • NEB numericals commonly give m₁, m₂, r and ask for F; or ask for g at a height.

Important Statements

  • Every particle attracts every other particle with a force directly proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
  • The gravitational constant G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² is a universal constant of nature.
  • Gravitational force is always attractive and acts along the line joining the centres.
  • The gravitational field strength g = GM/r² equals the force per unit mass.
  • Newton's law of gravitation explains planetary orbits, satellites, and tides.

Important Notes

  • Use centre-to-centre distance in Newton's law — for spheres, that is the distance between the centres.
  • G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² is universal; g = GM/r² is location-dependent.
  • The gravitational force between two bodies is independent of the medium between them.
  • The force on a body at height h above Earth uses r = R + h.
  • Newton's third law always applies: the forces are equal and opposite on the two bodies.

Exam Short Tricks

  • Doubling the distance quarters the force; halving it quadruples the force (inverse-square).
  • For g at height: g' = g(R/(R+h))²; at h = R, g' = g/4.
  • Memorise G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² — it will not be provided in exams.
  • To find Earth's mass: M = gR²/G.
  • Orbital speed around Earth: v = √(GM/r) ≈ 7.9 km/s near the surface.

Exam Notes

  • Numericals on F = Gm₁m₂/r² are a certainty — drill at least five.
  • g at a height or depth is a recurring short question.
  • Comparing gravitational and electric forces between protons is a popular conceptual MCQ.
  • Kepler's third law can be derived from Newton's law — a common derivation question.
  • Know the difference between G (universal constant) and g (local field).
