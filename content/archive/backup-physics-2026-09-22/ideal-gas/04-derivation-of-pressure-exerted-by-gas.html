Notes

  • Setup: Consider a cubical container with side length L containing N molecules of mass m. The volume is V=L3.
  • Momentum change in one collision: When a molecule with x-component of velocity vx​ hits the wall perpendicular to the x-axis, it rebounds with velocity −vx​. The change in momentum is Δp=pf​−pi​=m(−vx​)−m(vx​)=−2mvx​. The magnitude is 2mvx​.
  • Time between collisions: The molecule travels a distance 2L (to the opposite wall and back) at speed ∣vx​∣. Time between collisions with the same wall is Δt=2L/∣vx​∣.
  • Force from one molecule: F=ΔtΔp​=2L/∣vx​∣2mvx​​=∣vx​∣2mvx​​=Lmvx2​​. (Note: ∣vx​∣=vx​ if vx​>0, but we use magnitude for force magnitude).
  • Pressure contribution: Force per unit area is P=F/A=Lmvx2​​/L2=L3mvx2​​=Vmvx2​​. This is the pressure contribution from a single molecule with x-velocity vx​.
  • Averaging over all molecules: The total force is the sum of forces from all molecules. Since the wall is perpendicular to x-axis, only vx​ components matter. Total pressure P=A1​∑Fi​=L21​∑(mvix2​/L)=L3m​∑vix2​=Vm​⟨vx2​⟩.
  • Relating to total kinetic energy: The total kinetic energy is K=21​m∑vi2​=21​mN⟨v2⟩. Since ⟨v2⟩=⟨vx2​⟩+⟨vy2​⟩+⟨vz2​⟩ and by symmetry ⟨vx2​⟩=⟨vy2​⟩=⟨vz2​⟩, we have ⟨vx2​⟩=31​⟨v2⟩.
  • Final result: Substituting back, P=Vm​⋅31​⟨v2⟩=31​VmN​⟨v2⟩=32​VN​(21​m⟨v2⟩)=32​VN​⟨K⟩. Since ⟨K⟩=23​kB​T, we get P=VN​kB​T=Vn​RT=VnRT​.
  • Pressure in terms of average KE: P=32​VN​⟨K⟩. This shows the direct link between pressure and microscopic kinetic energy. The factor 2/3 comes from the 3 spatial dimensions (1/3 per dimension).
  • Derivation steps recap:
    1. Momentum change: Δp=2mvx​
    1. Time between collisions: Δt=2L/∣vx​∣
    1. Force on wall: F=L2mvx2​​
    1. Pressure: P=F/A=L3mvx2​​=Vmvx2​​
    1. Average over all molecules: P=Vm​⟨vx2​⟩
    1. Relate to total KE: ⟨vx2​⟩=31​⟨v2⟩, so P=3Vm​⟨v2⟩
    1. Substitute ⟨K⟩=21​m⟨v2⟩: P=32​VN​⟨K⟩

Confusion

  • The derivation assumes the wall is perpendicular to the x-axis. For a wall at an angle, the momentum change and force calculation would be different.
  • The factor 1/3 comes from the three spatial dimensions. In 2D, it would be 1/2, and in n-dimensions, it would be 1/n. Students often forget this geometric factor.
  • The derivation assumes the wall is stationary. If the wall were moving (e.g., piston), the relative velocity would need to be used, and the derivation would be more complex.
  • The derivation applies to ideal gases where collisions are perfectly elastic. In real gases with inelastic collisions, energy is lost and the derivation needs modification.

Universal Facts

  • The kinetic theory was developed in the 19th century by Rudolf Clausius (1850s), James Clerk Maxwell (1860s), and Ludwig Boltzmann (1870s). Maxwell's equations for the distribution of speeds were a major breakthrough.
  • The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution was first derived by James Clerk Maxwell in 1860 for gas molecules. Ludwig Boltzmann later provided a statistical interpretation and connected it to entropy (S = k_B ln Ω).
  • At room temperature (300 K), the average speed of a nitrogen molecule (N₂, molar mass 28 g/mol) is about 517 m/s, while the most probable speed is about 470 m/s. The distribution has a long tail to high speeds.
  • The kinetic theory explains why the speed of sound is higher in solids and liquids than in gases: in solids, molecules are closer together, so momentum transfer (sound waves) is more efficient even though individual molecules move slower.
  • The kinetic theory is foundational to statistical mechanics, which connects microscopic behavior to macroscopic thermodynamics. It was a key step in establishing thermodynamics as a branch of physics rather than just engineering.
