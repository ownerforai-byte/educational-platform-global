1.

A headline screams 'Ice-cream sales cause drowning!' (both peak in summer). What is the hidden third variable — and what question kills every such claim instantly?

Concept

Correlation headlines

Solution

HEAT (summer): heat drives both swimming (drownings) and ice-cream cravings independently — no causal link between the two at all. The killer question: 'What third factor could drive BOTH?' Always draw the triangle before believing the arrow. Correlation is two things moving together; causation needs a MECHANISM plus the elimination of common causes. Summer sells both cones and coffins.

2.

Native speakers say 'There's five reasons' (singular verb, plural noun) daily. If natives violate it, is the rule even real — and when must YOU still obey it?

Concept

Native speakers break rules

Solution

The rule is real but REGISTERED: speech tolerates 'there's + plural' (contraction frozen as a chunk), while formal writing enforces agreement. Natives style-shift unconsciously; learners are judged by the strictest register (exams, interviews). Obey the rule where evaluation happens, notice the violation where life happens. Grammar has dialects of formality — mastery is knowing which room you're in.

3.

Students who read English aloud improve faster than silent readers at the same hours. What extra brain channels does the voice open that eyes alone miss?

ID

Type

Concept

Reading aloud advantage

Solution

Three: (1) MOTOR memory — mouth muscles encode pronunciation patterns physically; (2) AUDITORY feedback — you HEAR errors and rhythm your eyes skip; (3) ATTENTION lock — voicing prevents the eyes from skimming ahead mindlessly. Silent reading can be fake reading (eyes moving, mind elsewhere); aloud reading forces every word through three systems. Multimodal input builds redundant retrieval paths — the voice is a second highlighter.

4.

Novels switch to present tense at climaxes ('He opens the door —'). The events are equally past. What does the present tense DO to a reader that past tense cannot?

ID

Type

Marks

Difficulty

Concept

Tense storytelling power

Solution

It removes the safety of hindsight: past tense implies the narrator SURVIVED to tell it (outcome known-ish), while present tense traps you in unknowing now — danger feels live because grammatically nothing is resolved yet. Filmmakers do this with shaky cams; writers do it with tense. Grammar manipulates TIME PSYCHOLOGY: same facts, different fear. Notice tense shifts and you see the author's hand on your pulse.

5.

Great plays give villains the BEST speeches (Shylock's 'Hath not a Jew eyes?'). Why would a playwright arm the character the audience should hate?

Concept

Villains as arguments

Solution

Because drama tests IDEAS, not people: a weak villain makes the hero's victory meaningless. Shylock's speech forces the audience to feel his humanity — then watch him crushed anyway — which implicates the 'good' characters' prejudice. The villain is the play's counter-argument given full strength; if it still loses, the theme is earned. One-sided stories preach; two-sided stories prove.

6.

Concept

Question-first reading

Solution

Purpose transforms perception: questions install a MENTAL FILTER, so relevant lines glow on contact while filler blurs past — B reads 30% of words with 100% attention instead of 100% of words with fading attention. A reads blind, remembers little, and re-reads everything. Exams reward targeted extraction, not literary appreciation. Read the questions first: let the test tell your eyes what to want.

7.

Learning 'spect' (to look) unlocks inspect, spectator, retrospect, circumspect, perspicacious… How does ONE root beat memorising twenty words separately?

Concept

Roots multiply words

Solution

Compression: one root + a few prefixes = a GENERATOR for dozens of words, each decoded on sight (retro + spect = look back). Isolated words are 20 separate debts to memory; a root family is one investment paying 20 dividends — including words you've never seen. English is ~60% Latin/Greek Lego: learn the bricks (50 key roots), and unfamiliar words become assembly puzzles. Vocabulary scales by structure, not by sweat.

8.

'The boy was sad' vs 'The boy stared at his empty plate, pushing rice in circles.' Both state sadness. Why does the second earn full marks in creative writing?

ID

prac-eng11-write

Type

practical

Marks

3

Difficulty

Unit SLUG

Concept

Show don't tell grades

Solution

The first TELLS the conclusion (blocking the reader's own inference, zero evidence); the second SHOWS data and lets the reader CONCLUDE sadness — participating, visualising, feeling. Examiners reward concrete sensory detail because it proves observation power, not adjective stock. Rule: replace every emotion word with the physical evidence that would make a stranger infer it. Don't announce the feeling; stage the symptoms.

9.

Students skip outlines to 'save time' then freeze mid-essay. How does spending 5 minutes outlining actually SHORTEN total writing time?

ID

Type

Marks

Difficulty

Unit SLUG

Concept

Outlines save time

Solution

It separates DECIDING from DRAFTING: mid-essay freezes happen when the brain must invent structure AND sentences simultaneously — two loads, one channel. An outline decides the skeleton upfront (thesis → 3 points → examples), so drafting becomes pure translation, flowing without pauses. Five planning minutes prevent twenty staring minutes plus a structural rewrite. Outlines don't cost time; they move decisions to where they're cheap.
