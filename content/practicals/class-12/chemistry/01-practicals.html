1.

Ethanol mixes with water in ALL proportions, but hexanol barely dissolves. Both are 'alcohols'. What molecular tug-of-war decides solubility, and where is the tipping point?

ID

Type

Unit SLUG

alcohols-phenols-ethers

Concept

Why ethanol mixes but oil doesn't

Solution

Two opposing parts: the –OH head hydrogen-bonds with water (dissolves), the carbon tail disrupts water's network (refuses). Short chains (ethanol): the head wins. Each added –CH₂– tips the balance until ~C5–C6, where the tail dominates and solubility collapses. Rule: solubility is a battle between the hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tail — the longer the tail, the more the molecule behaves like oil wearing an –OH hat.

2.

Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror with aldehydes but NOT ketones. Both contain C=O. What structural difference makes one oxidisable and the other stubborn?

ID

prac-che12-ald

Type

Marks

3

Difficulty

Unit SLUG

aldehydes-ketones-carboxylic-acids

Concept

Silver mirror test logic

Solution

The aldehyde carbon carries a HYDROGEN (R–CHO) that can be stripped away during oxidation to a carboxylic acid; the ketone carbon (R–CO–R′) is flanked by two carbons with no such handle — oxidising it would require breaking a C–C bond, far costlier. Tollens' test is really a hydrogen-availability test: mild oxidant + removable H = silver mirror. That is also why formalin preserves specimens (easily oxidised, reactive) while acetone just evaporates.

3.

Dead fish smell of amines, and amines turn red litmus blue. Connect the smell to the basicity: what single feature of nitrogen causes both?

ID

prac-che12-ami

Type

Marks

Unit SLUG

amines

Concept

Fishy smell basicity

Solution

The LONE PAIR on nitrogen. It accepts H⁺ from water (R–NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ R–NH₃⁺ + OH⁻) — that is the basicity. And low-molar-mass amines are volatile, so the same lone-pair molecules reach your nose — the 'fishy' smell IS airborne base hitting your receptors. Aniline smells less and is less basic: its lone pair delocalises into the benzene ring, unavailable for protons AND less volatile. One pair of electrons explains both the chemistry and the stink.

4.

Cows live on cellulose; humans starve on it — yet both are glucose polymers like the starch we digest easily. What single bonding difference defeats our enzymes?

ID

Type

Concept

Why we can't digest grass

Solution

Starch links glucose with α-1,4 bonds (all 'down', forming coils our amylase grips); cellulose uses β-1,4 bonds (alternating 'up-down', forming straight ribbons locked by hydrogen bonds into tough fibres). Human enzymes fit only the α shape — the β ribbon cannot enter the active site. Cows cheat by outsourcing: gut bacteria make cellulase for them. Same atoms, different handshake — digestion is shape recognition, not just chemistry.

5.

Milk lasts a day at room temperature but a week refrigerated — a mere ~20°C difference. Why is the slowdown so dramatic instead of proportional?

ID

Type

Marks

Unit SLUG

chemical-kinetics

Concept

Food spoils slower in fridge

Solution

Reaction rate follows the Arrhenius EXPONENTIAL (k ∝ e^(−Ea/RT)), not a straight line. Cooling shrinks the fraction of molecules with energy above Ea multiplicatively — roughly halving bacterial growth rate every 7–10°C (Q₁₀ ≈ 2–3). A 20° drop compounds to ~4–8× slower spoilage. Linear intuition ('20° cooler = 20% slower') fails because temperature lives in an exponent: small cooling, multiplied effect. That is why the fridge, not just a cool room, matters.

6.

Soap forms scum in hard water while detergents keep working. Both clean the same way. What does Ca²⁺ do to soap molecules that it cannot do to detergents?

ID

prac-che12-daily

Type

Marks

3

Difficulty

Unit SLUG

chemistry-in-everyday-life

Concept

Soap vs detergent in hard water

Solution

Soap (sodium stearate) swaps its Na⁺ for Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺, forming calcium stearate — an INSOLUBLE curd (scum) that cannot form micelles, so cleaning stops and grime redeposits. Detergents use sulfonate heads whose calcium salts stay SOLUBLE, so micelles keep forming and lifting grease regardless of hardness. Same cleaning mechanism (micelles), different head-group fate: scum is just soap molecules kidnapped by calcium before they can work.

7.

Xenon forms XeF₂ and XeF₄, yet helium forms nothing. Both are 'inert' noble gases with full shells. What quantitative factor opens xenon's shell but keeps helium sealed?

ID

prac-che12-elem

Type

practical

Marks

3

Difficulty

medium

Unit SLUG

chemistry-of-element

Concept

Noble gas reactivity

Solution

IONISATION ENERGY vs oxidiser strength. Xenon's outer electrons sit far from the nucleus (n=5, heavily shielded), so its ionisation energy (~1170 kJ/mol) is low enough that fluorine — the most electronegative element — can rip electrons away. Helium's electrons hug a bare 2-proton nucleus (1310 kJ/mol for the first, 5250 for the second) — no chemical oxidiser is strong enough. 'Inert' was never absolute; it was a price tag, and only xenon went on sale to fluorine.

8.

Your phone shows 50% then dies within minutes in winter, but lasts hours in summer. The stored charge didn't change with the season. What electrochemical quantity did?

ID

Type

Concept

Phone battery percentages

Solution

The reaction RATE (kinetics), not the capacity. Cold slows ion diffusion through the electrolyte and charge-transfer at electrodes — internal resistance soars, voltage sags under load, and the phone's fuel gauge (which reads VOLTAGE, not coulombs) panics and shuts down early. Warm the phone and the 'lost' charge reappears. Batteries are chemical engines: winter doesn't empty the tank, it clogs the fuel line.

9.

Silicon sits right below carbon, also tetravalent — yet there is no silicon-based life and no silicone plastics industry to rival carbon's millions of compounds. What TWO bond properties give carbon the monopoly?

ID

Type

Unit SLUG

Concept

Carbon's catenation monopoly

Solution

(1) C–C bonds are short and STRONG (~347 kJ/mol) while Si–Si are long and weak (~226 kJ/mol) — silicon chains fall apart; (2) carbon forms strong MULTIPLE bonds (C=C, C≡C) enabling rings, aromatics and functional diversity, while Si=Si is fragile and rare. Carbon's small size lets p-orbitals overlap sideways efficiently; silicon's larger orbitals cannot. Life bets on the element whose chains survive water, heat and time — silicon's dissolve.

10.

Premium petrol is '95 octane' and costs more. What does the number physically measure, and why do high-compression engines knock on regular fuel?

ID

prac-che12-hyd

Type

practical

Marks

3

Difficulty

medium

Unit SLUG

hydrocarbons

Concept

Octane rating meaning

Solution

Octane number measures KNOCK RESISTANCE — the fuel's patience under compression. In high-compression engines, regular fuel auto-ignites from heat/pressure BEFORE the spark (knocking: colliding flame fronts that hammer pistons). The number = % iso-octane in a reference blend matching that resistance (95 = behaves like 95% iso-octane). Branched/ring molecules (iso-octane) resist auto-ignition; straight chains (heptane, 0) detonate early. Premium buys molecular patience, not energy — burning it in a low-compression engine wastes money, not gains power.

11.

Salt melts ice at −5°C but the same salt can't melt ice at −25°C. It is the same salt on the same ice. What temperature-dependent limit defeats it?

ID

prac-che12-sol

Type

practical

Marks

3

Difficulty

Unit SLUG

solutions

Concept

Salting icy roads

Solution

Freezing-point depression ΔTf = i·Kf·m needs LIQUID water to dissolve the salt first — melting starts from a thin quasi-liquid layer, and the process itself consumes heat. At −25°C the equilibrium freezing point of even saturated brine (~−21°C for NaCl) is ABOVE ambient: no liquid layer can persist, so dissolution stalls and melting stops. Salt doesn't 'generate' melting; it shifts equilibrium — and past the eutectic point there is no equilibrium left to shift. That is why airports switch to urea/acetates or just grit in deep cold.
