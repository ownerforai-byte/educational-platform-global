# Biomolecules and Cell Biology

Life at its most fundamental level is chemistry in action. Every living cell is a miniature factory built from a handful of remarkable molecules — carbohydrates that store and supply energy, proteins that catalyse reactions and build structures, lipids that form membranes and concentrate energy, and nucleic acids that carry hereditary information. This unit of the NEB Class 11 Biology syllabus introduces these biomolecules together with the structure of the cell itself: the prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisation, the major organelles, and the processes of cell division. A firm grasp of this unit makes every later topic — from genetics to physiology — far easier to understand.

## The Major Biomolecules

| Biomolecule | Building blocks | Main functions | Energy value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose) | Instant energy; storage (starch, glycogen); structure (cellulose) | 4 kcal/g |
| Proteins | 20 standard amino acids joined by peptide bonds | Enzymes, transport, defence, structural framework | 4 kcal/g |
| Lipids | Glycerol + fatty acids (ester bonds) | Membranes, stored energy, insulation, hormones | 9 kcal/g |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotides (sugar + phosphate + nitrogen base) | Storage and transfer of genetic information (DNA, RNA) | — |
| Water / minerals | H₂O molecules; ions such as Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ | Solvent medium; osmotic balance; cofactors | — |

Enzymes deserve special mention: they are globular proteins that lower activation energy, are substrate-specific, and work best within narrow ranges of temperature and pH. Water typically forms 70–90% of protoplasm and provides the aqueous environment in which all biochemical reactions occur.

## Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

Prokaryotes (bacteria, cyanobacteria) lack a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; their genetic material lies naked in the cytoplasm as a nucleoid. Eukaryotes possess a double-membraned nucleus housing chromosomes made of DNA and histones, plus organelles such as mitochondria (aerobic respiration), endoplasmic reticulum (transport and synthesis), Golgi bodies (packaging), lysosomes (digestion), plastids (photosynthesis and storage in plants) and ribosomes (protein synthesis). Ribosomes are the one major organelle found in both types, though prokaryotic ribosomes (70S) are smaller than eukaryotic ones (80S).

## Worked Calculation: Energy from Food

```text
Question: A snack supplies 60 g carbohydrate, 20 g protein and 10 g fat.
Estimate its total energy.

Step 1: Carbohydrate energy = 60 g × 4 kcal/g = 240 kcal
Step 2: Protein energy      = 20 g × 4 kcal/g =  80 kcal
Step 3: Fat energy          = 10 g × 9 kcal/g =  90 kcal
Step 4: Total               = 240 + 80 + 90   = 410 kcal
```

## Key points

- Carbohydrates and proteins provide about 4 kcal/g while lipids provide about 9 kcal/g.
- Enzymes are protein catalysts; they are specific, reusable and pH/temperature sensitive.
- Prokaryotic cells have 70S ribosomes and naked circular DNA; eukaryotic cells have 80S ribosomes and histone-bound chromosomes.
- Mitochondria and plastids contain their own DNA and ribosomes — they are semi-autonomous organelles.
- Mitosis produces two identical diploid cells for growth and repair; meiosis produces four haploid cells for sexual reproduction and variation.

## Common mistakes

- Calling all cell structures "organelles" — structures like centrioles qualify, but cell inclusions (starch grains, crystals) do not.
- Thinking ribosomes are membrane-bound: they are not; lysosomes are.
- Confusing mitosis with meiosis: crossing over occurs only in meiosis I.
- Writing that plant cells "have no mitochondria" — plants respire too; chloroplasts never replace mitochondria.
- Assuming lipids are polymers of amino acids or nucleotides — they are esters of glycerol and fatty acids, not true polymers.
