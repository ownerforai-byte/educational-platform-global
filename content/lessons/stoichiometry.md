# Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry is the bookkeeping of chemistry: it lets us predict exactly how much product forms from given reactants and how much reactant a reaction consumes. Built on Dalton's atomic theory and the laws of chemical combination — conservation of mass, definite (constant) proportions, multiple proportions, and Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes — this NEB Class 11 unit centres on the mole concept. Mastering it is essential for every later calculation in physical chemistry.

## The Mole Concept

One mole contains Avogadro's number of particles, Nₐ = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, and its mass in grams equals the molecular (or atomic) mass. At STP (0 °C, 1 atm) one mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L. Vapour density relates to molecular mass by M = 2 × VD.

| Quantity | Symbol / relation | Typical unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of moles from mass | n = m / M | mol |
| Moles from gas volume at STP | n = V / 22.4 | mol |
| Moles from particle count | n = N / 6.022 × 10²³ | mol |
| Percentage yield | (actual / theoretical) × 100% | % |
| Empirical → molecular formula | MF = EF × n where n = M(mass) / E(formula mass) | — |

## Limiting Reactant and Yield

In real reactions reactants are rarely mixed in exact stoichiometric ratios. The limiting reactant is completely consumed first and fixes the maximum possible (theoretical) amount of product; everything left over belongs to the excess reactant. The theoretical yield divided into the actual experimental yield gives the percentage yield.

## Worked Calculation

```text
Question: What volume of CO2 at STP is produced when 25 g of CaCO3
is fully decomposed?   CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

Step 1: Molar mass of CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100 g/mol
Step 2: Moles of CaCO3      = 25 g ÷ 100 g/mol = 0.25 mol
Step 3: Mole ratio  CaCO3 : CO2 = 1 : 1
        so moles of CO2     = 0.25 mol
Step 4: Volume at STP       = 0.25 mol × 22.4 L/mol = 5.6 L

Answer: 5.6 litres of CO2 at STP.
```

A second classic type: an oxide contains 40% oxygen and 60% metal by mass; dividing each percentage by the respective atomic masses gives the empirical formula ratio, which combined with the molar mass yields the molecular formula.

## Key points

- Balanced equations are mandatory: coefficients supply the mole ratios used in every conversion.
- Mass ↔ moles ↔ particles ↔ gas volume conversions all pass through the mole as the central hub.
- 22.4 L per mole applies only to gases at STP — never to liquids or solids.
- Identify the limiting reactant before calculating theoretical yield.
- Empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number atom ratio; the molecular formula is an integer multiple of it.

## Common mistakes

- Using 22.4 L at temperatures other than STP, or forgetting that STP means 273 K and 1 atm.
- Balancing equations by changing subscripts instead of coefficients.
- Dividing by the excess reactant's moles when the limiting one controls the product amount.
- Reporting percentage yield above 100% without recognising experimental error or impure product.
- Mixing up atomicity and molarity-style ratios: always convert every substance to moles first, then apply the balanced ratio.
