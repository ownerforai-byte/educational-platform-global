chemistry/stoichiometry — Avogadro's Law and its Deductions (Molecular Mass, Vapour Density, Volume and Number of Particles)

1. Meaning and statement

Avogadro's law (Amedeo Avogadro, 1811, published as a hypothesis) states: equal volumes of all gases, under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. Hence for a gas V∝nV\propto nV∝n at constant T and P, and volume ratio = mole ratio. One mole of any gas contains the Avogadro number N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules and occupies a molar volume of 22.4 dm³ (22.4 L) at STP (273.15 K, 1 atm) or ≈24 L at RTP. The law resolved the confusion between atoms and molecules that had blocked Dalton's theory and Gay-Lussac's volume results.

2. Deduction 1 — molecular mass and vapour density

Vapour density (VD) of a gas is the ratio of the mass of a certain volume of the gas to the mass of the same volume of hydrogen under identical conditions. Because equal volumes contain equal numbers of molecules, masses are in the ratio of molecular masses, and with hydrogen as the standard (M = 2): VD=M2VD=\frac{M}{2}VD=2M​, so molecular mass = 2 × vapour density. Example: VD of CO₂ = 22 → M = 44 g mol⁻¹. (With air as reference, M = 28.94 × relative density.)

3. Deduction 2 — molecular mass and volume of gas

Since 1 mol of any gas occupies 22.4 L at STP, the mass of 22.4 L of a gas at STP equals its molar mass in grams. Hence n=V (at STP)22.4 L mol−1n=\frac{V\ (\text{at STP})}{22.4\ \text{L mol}^{-1}}n=22.4 L mol−1V (at STP)​ and M=mass of the gasvolume at STP×22.4M=\frac{\text{mass of the gas}}{\text{volume at STP}}\times22.4M=volume at STPmass of the gas​×22.4. Conversely, the volume occupied by a known mass m is V=mM×22.4V=\frac{m}{M}\times22.4V=Mm​×22.4 L at STP. This is the basis of all gas-volume stoichiometry.

4. Deduction 3 — molecular mass and number of particles

One mole contains N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ particles, so n=NNAn=\frac{N}{N_{A}}n=NA​N​, the mass of one molecule =MNA=\frac{M}{N_{A}}=NA​M​ (e.g. 3.0 × 10⁻²³ g for water), and the volume of one molecule follows from the molar volume. Gram-molecular mass is the mass in grams numerically equal to the molecular mass, and it always contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules (gram-atomic mass contains the same number of atoms).

5. Applications

• Atomicity of gases: comparing volumes of a gas with the volumes of its elements (e.g. 2 volumes H₂ + 1 volume O₂ → 2 volumes H₂O vapour) shows that H₂, O₂ and N₂ are diatomic while O₃ and P₄ are polyatomic.

• Molecular mass of gases and volatile liquids: by vapour density (M = 2 × VD) or by Victor Meyer's and Dumas' methods, and by the limiting-density/effusion methods.

• Molar-volume stoichiometry: volume–volume and mass–volume calculations for gases at STP; determination of the molar volume of a gas and of Avogadro's constant.

• Establishing formulas: Avogadro's law plus Gay-Lussac's volume law fixed the formulas H₂O, NH₃, CO₂ and HCl (which Dalton's theory alone could not).

6. Limitations

• Avogadro's law applies to gases only — it says nothing about the volume of liquids or solids, whose molar volumes differ greatly.

• It assumes ideal behaviour; real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature, so the simple 22.4 L molar volume is approximate except near STP (for NH₃ or CO₂ it can be in error by a few per cent).

• It fails for gases that dissociate or associate in the vapour state: PCl₅, N₂O₄ and NH₄Cl give anomalously high or low vapour densities, and a correction factor (degree of dissociation/association) is needed.

• It provides no information about the size, shape or mass of individual molecules; 'equal numbers of molecules' says nothing about molecular structure.

• The law was ignored for nearly half a century because Berzelius and Dalton did not accept the atom–molecule distinction — a reminder that it depends on the concept of a molecule, not merely on measurement.

• The molar volume figures are defined at specified T and P (22.4 L at STP, 24 L at RTP), so results must always state the conditions.
