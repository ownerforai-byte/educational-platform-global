Unit 6 — Money and Economy

Talking about money without flinching

Money is the theme adults discuss in whispers and good essays discuss in plain sentences. Every candidate already runs a micro-economy — pocket money, top-ups, exam fees — and Nepal's macro-story (remittances, tourism, the tap of QR payments) is in the news you already scroll. The unit's job is to give that familiarity a vocabulary and that opinion a frame, because money talk fails in two ways: too shy to be specific, too certain to be true.

The words that carry the theme

The everyday layer first, because the exam sets it there: income, expenditure, budget, savings, debt, interest, loan, instalment, tax, subsidy, remittance, inflation, recession. The digital layer: mobile banking, digital wallet, QR payment, e-commerce, OTP fraud, phishing. The national layer: GDP (a country's total output — quote it as "the value of everything produced", never as a mystery), per capita income, trade deficit, foreign employment, entrepreneurship, insurance.

The phrasal verbs are where fluency shows: to save up for a goal, pay off a loan, run into debt, cut down on expenses, live within (or dangerously beyond) your means, make ends meet, invest in, cash in on an opportunity, splash out on a treat, tighten your belt when the month outruns the money. The idioms are the theme's proverbs — money doesn't grow on trees, a penny saved is a penny earned, easy come, easy go, the best things in life are free — and the rule from the humour unit applies: one, placed well, is charm.

Grammar's quiet trap hides in the numbers: sums take singular verbs ("Five thousand rupees is enough for the week") while counted pieces take plural ("Five thousand coins are heavy"), and percentages agree with the noun behind them ("Sixty per cent of the population is rural" — but "sixty per cent of students are absent"). These two rules are tested every year in disguise.

Reading the theme like a writer — the devices of money talk

Money language is a museum of euphemism, and noticing it is analysis: a company "right-sizes" (fires), a price "adjusts" (rises), a loan is a "financial product" (a debt), a gambler "invests" (loses). Advertisements are the theme's poetry — "affordable luxury" is an oxymoron doing sales work, "limited offer" is manufactured scarcity, "everyone's switching" is the bandwagon of Unit 1 wearing a discount sticker. An essay that names one such device and says what it hides is doing the exact analysis NEB rewards.

Contrast is the theme's structural device — needs against wants, saving against spending, the remittance letter against the farewell at the airport. And the strongest money essays use concrete detail the way family essays do: the exact price of a school bag, the queue at the remittance counter, the grandfather who kept his cash in a trunk and his trust in no bank. One remembered figure beats three invented statistics.

Watch the hedges again, because money claims attract false precision: "remittances account for a significant share of national income" is honest; "remittances are 27.3% of GDP" — in an exam, from memory — is fiction with a decimal point.

The debates, held honestly

"Money can't buy happiness" is the theme's classic dialectic, and it deserves better than a slogan. Concede the ceiling: beyond needs and modest security, more money adds little to a Tuesday. Rebut the romance: poverty purchases misery with terrible reliability — money buys medicine, schooling, time and options, which is what happiness is made of. Land the synthesis: money is necessary but not sufficient; what buys contentment is spending on experiences, people and growth rather than status.

"Foreign employment: boon or bane?" is Nepal's own question and the exam knows it. The boon is real — remittances build houses, pay school fees, seed small shops, and skills return with the migrants. The bane is real too — children raised by video call, villages of grandparents, and a national habit of exporting labour instead of building the jobs that would keep them. The mature close treats migration as a symptom: the cure is an economy where staying is viable.

"Should schools teach financial literacy?" is the easiest yes in the theme, argued from the syllabus's own gaps: students solve quadratic equations and cannot read a loan agreement; compound interest appears in mathematics class but never as a warning. One semester — budgeting, interest, fraud, basic insurance — and the proposal writes itself.

Writing the theme — the report and the essay

The trend report is this unit's genre: a chart or table given, language demanded. Trends have verbs — rose, climbed, soared; fell, dropped, plunged; remained steady, fluctuated — and the report wants the overall direction first, then the extremes, then one inference. Never narrate every number; select like a journalist. "Remittance inflows climbed steadily over the decade while exports remained flat, widening the gap the country pays for at the border" — three verbs, one meaning.

The essay reuses the dialectic frames above. And the closing line is worth drafting twice, because money essays are remembered by their last sentence: "Money is necessary for happiness and sufficient for none of it — what it finally buys is options, and happiness still depends on what we choose." A sentence like that is not decoration; it is the essay, folded small.

How the exam asks it

The essay ("Money can't buy happiness", "Foreign employment: boon or bane") wants the dialectic shape, one Nepal anchor, one apt proverb — and no invented decimals. The chart interpretation wants trend verbs, extremes, inference. The short answers want definition with a pulse: "inflation is when the same note buys less than it did last year." The dialogue (advising a friend to save) wants turn-taking, modals of advice — should, ought to, had better — and a concrete plan at the end, because advice without a plan is a greeting.

Spend the first minute of chart tasks finding the highest point, the lowest, and the direction: those three facts are the report's skeleton. In essays, choose the side early and let every paragraph push it — the exam rewards a decided mind with calibrated manners.
