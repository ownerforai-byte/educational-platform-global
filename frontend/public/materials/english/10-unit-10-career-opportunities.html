Unit 10 — Career Opportunities

The theme with your name on it

Career is the unit students read most closely, for obvious reasons — the reader is choosing, or about to choose. The honest frame is that a career is neither a lottery nor a ladder but a portfolio: education, skills, connections, reputation and luck, built over years. The unit gives you the vocabulary of work, the language of applications and interviews (where marks and jobs share the same rules), and the argument frames for the debates — passion versus security, migration versus staying, degrees versus skills.

The words that carry the theme

The vocabulary of work sorts into layers. The search: vacancy, opening, candidate, applicant, CV (curriculum vitae — the story of your working life in two pages), cover letter, interview, shortlist, appointment. The working life: profession, occupation, career path, promotion, increment, resignation, retirement, internship, apprenticeship, freelance, shift. The economy's moods: job market, unemployment, self-employment, entrepreneurship, startup, gig work, brain drain (the phrase every Nepal essay needs), remittance (arriving from Unit 6).

The phrasal verbs carry the journey: to fill in a form, send off an application, take up a post, set up a business, take on staff, step down from a role, branch out into something new, get laid off (the passive voice of bad news). And the idioms the working world actually speaks: climb the ladder, get your foot in the door, a dead-end job, wear many hats, work your way up, burn out — the last one a warning the theme should say honestly.

Register is the hidden test: a CV and an interview speak formal English (I am writing to apply for…; I have three years' experience of…), while a conversation about careers speaks natural English (I'm thinking of taking up nursing). Losing the register — "I wanna apply for the job sir" — costs marks in both worlds.

Reading the theme like a writer — the language of applications

An application letter is persuasion with a format, and the format is the easy half: your address and the date, the employer's address, a salutation (Dear Sir or Madam when the name is unknown, Dear Ms Sharma when it is not), the purpose in the first line, the evidence in the middle, the polite close (Yours faithfully with the unknown name, Yours sincerely with the known one). Layout is marks lying on the table; candidates who lose them have nobody to blame but their margins.

The persuasive half is evidence shaped to the role. Every claim wants a proof: not "I am hardworking" but "I balanced a full course load with six hours of weekly tutoring". The device at work is concrete detail again — the same one that carried the family essays — and it works identically in an interview: stories are remembered; adjectives are not.

Formal tone has its own small grammar: no contractions (I am, not I'm), no slang, modals of polite request (I would be grateful if…), and the humble-brag discipline — state achievements as facts, let the reader supply the admiration. And beware the cliché pile ("dynamic team player with a proven track record"): every applicant writes it, so it proves nothing. Specific beats impressive; a specific thing you actually did beats an impressive thing nobody believes.

The debates, held honestly

"Follow your passion" versus "follow the market". The romance says passion sustains effort; the pragmatist says passion does not pay in a saturated field. The synthesis worth writing: passion determines what you will be good at, the market determines whether it pays — and the durable careers find the overlap, or build it (a hobby that becomes a trade, a skill that creates its own demand).

"Degrees versus skills." The degree certifies learning; the skill does the work. Nepal's own paradox — thousands of graduates, employer complaints of unemployability — argues for both: the degree opens the door, the skill keeps you in the room. Vocational education, from Unit 5, returns here with evidence.

"Go abroad or stay?" The honest version holds both sides: abroad pays in currency and costs in family; staying pays in family and strains in opportunity. The mature close refuses the binary — the question is not where but doing what, since a purposeful job at home beats a purposeless one abroad and vice versa. Brain drain is a symptom, as with migration in Unit 19; the cure is an economy that makes staying rational.

Grammar and style: the tense of your own story

The present perfect is the working life's home tense — "I have completed my +2", "I have worked as a tutor for two years" — because it ties the past to the present application. The simple past dates the finished chapter ("I joined the club in 2079"). Mixing them ("I have joined in 2079") is the theme's signature slip, and interviewers hear it the way musicians hear a flat note.

The conditional carries the interview's diplomacy: "I would welcome the chance to…", "If selected, I would commit to…". And the formal infinitive of purpose opens letters the exam loves: "I am writing to apply for the position of…".

One style rule above all: short sentences in the letter. Nervous writers inflate; confident writers compress. "I am confident that my experience and enthusiasm would make me a valuable addition to your team" — fine; but "I led a team of five volunteers for a year" is finer, because it is a fact and facts do not need adjectives.

How the exam asks it

The application letter (6–8 marks) is near-guaranteed: an advertisement is given, you apply. Checklist the format first (addresses, date, salutation, subject line, close), then purpose in line one, then two proofs shaped to the role. The CV-writing task wants reverse-chronological order, action verbs (led, organised, trained), no full sentences, no photo-of-your-personality prose. The essay ("Unemployment problems among youths", "Self-employment as a career") wants the dialectic frames above with one Nepal anchor. The short answers want definitions with pulse: "an internship is supervised work experience that trades salary for learning — worth more than it pays."

And one piece of advice that outlives the exam: the letter you write for marks is practice for the letter that changes your life. Learn the format now, honestly, and the exam has already paid for itself.
