Unit 11 — Hobbies

The theme that is secretly about time

A hobby is what you do when nobody is making you do anything — which is why the theme, lightly examined, turns out to be about identity, discipline and joy. The exam likes it because every candidate has material: the football at dawn, the guitar with two broken strings, the novel abandoned at page forty. The unit's job is to give that material language — the vocabulary of pastimes, the grammar of habit, and the gentle art of writing about pleasure without sounding like a brochure.

The words that carry the theme

The verb to go splits the theme neatly: you go swimming, go trekking, go fishing (go + -ing for activities away from home), but you play chess and collect stamps and read novels — the collocations are fixed, and mixing them ("I go chess") is the theme's signature error. The big families: sports and games (from Unit 3's vocabulary), creative pastimes (painting, photography, writing, playing an instrument), collecting (stamps, coins, stories about strangers), making (cooking, knitting, coding), outdoors (birdwatching, gardening, hiking — Nepal's hills make this the national hobby whether admitted or not).

The phrasal verbs of pastime: to take up a hobby (begin), give up (quit), keep up with (stay regular), get into (become absorbed), wind down (relax after work), brush up on (polish a rusting skill). And the expressions that carry the theme's philosophy: variety is the spice of life, all work and no play makes Jack a dull boy, a change is as good as a rest — one per essay, placed where it lands.

Grammar's quiet gift here is the gerund: hobbies are grammatically activities-as-nouns — "I enjoy reading", "Swimming keeps me sane", "My hobby is photographing old doors". The exam tests the verb patterns that decide what follows: enjoy, avoid, suggest, mind take -ing; want, decide, hope, plan take to + verb. "I enjoy to swim" is the theme's most common two-mark loss.

Reading the theme like a writer — writing pleasure well

Writing about a hobby fails in one specific way: the brochure voice — "Reading is very interesting and improves knowledge." The cure is the same concrete detail that carried the family and sports units: not "I love trekking" but "the tea house at 4,000 metres, where the owner's daughter does homework by lantern and the fog closes like a curtain at seven". One particular beats three adjectives; the reader must see your hobby, not a hobby.

Understatement is the hobbyist's native tone — the truly devoted downplay: "I've been known to miss a bus or two for a good sunrise" says more than "I am passionate about photography". Mild irony keeps it human: the guitar with two broken strings, the stamp collection that cost a fortune in patience. And anecdote supplies the spine: one afternoon when the hobby taught you something — patience from fishing, failure from the kiln, silence from birdwatching — is the essay's load-bearing wall.

There is also a serious register hiding in this light theme, and the exam uses it: hobbies in resumes and interviews (Unit 10's territory) must sound purposeful — "reading" becomes "wide reading across history and current affairs" — without lying. The difference between the two registers is the difference between a friend asking and an employer asking; knowing which room you are in is the skill.

The debates, held honestly

"Are hobbies a waste of time?" Concede the opportunity cost honestly — an hour of guitar is an hour not studied. Rebut with the evidence of how attention works: a rested, pleased mind learns faster than an exhausted one; the hobby is not theft from study but interest paid on it. Land the synthesis: the waste is not the hobby but the unstructured excess — the same phone that reads books can eat evenings.

"Should hobbies be turned into careers?" The dream says yes; the economics say carefully. A hobby monetised acquires deadlines, clients and invoices — and sometimes stops being a refuge. The mature answer: turn the hobby into a career only if you love the work of it, not the escape of it — they are different pleasures, and confusing them ruins both.

"Screen hobbies versus hands-on hobbies." Refuse the moral panic: reading on a phone is still reading. The real variable is making versus consuming — a screen that helps you draw, code or compose is a workshop; a screen that only scrolls is a slot machine. The distinction, stated once, is the essay.

Grammar and style: the tense of habit

Hobbies live in the present simple with adverbs of frequency — "I usually sketch on Saturdays", "We hardly ever miss the morning match" — and the exam quietly checks the position of those adverbs (before the main verb, after be). The present perfect continuous tells the hobby's history with pride: "I have been playing the madal for six years" — the duration is the devotion.

Comparatives supply the essay's judgements: "Trekking is more demanding than gardening, but less forgiving of a missed monsoon." And the used to / would pair from the family unit returns for abandoned hobbies: "I used to collect stickers; I would trade three of a kind for one I lacked" — the past tense of who you were is the essay's quiet engine.

How the exam asks it

The personal essay ("My hobby", 6–8 marks) wants one hobby deep, not five hobbies listed: what, when, how it began, one anecdote, what it taught. The dialogue (two friends on free time) wants turn-taking, gerunds flying free, and one recommendation with a reason. The short answers want the verb-pattern rules said with examples. The speech ("The importance of hobbies for students") wants the debate frame above — concede the opportunity cost, then collect the interest.

A worked miniature of the whole method: "Photography taught me to wait. For a year I chased sunrises up the hill behind our house and came home with nothing twice for every once — until the morning the fog lifted just as the light hit the stupa, and I understood that the picture was never the reward; the waiting was." — one hobby, one scene, one lesson. That is the unit, folded into two sentences.
