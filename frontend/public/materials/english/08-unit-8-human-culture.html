Unit 8 — Human Culture

Reading culture with words

Culture is everything a people make and keep — language, food, festivals, dress, beliefs, manners, buildings — and Nepal is the richest classroom for it on earth: dozens of languages, half a dozen calendars, and every street a living museum where a medieval shrine and a mobile-recharge shop share a wall. The unit asks two things of you: describe a festival so a stranger can smell it, and argue about what happens when cultures meet — which they are, everywhere, right now.

The words that carry the theme

Sort the ideas first. Material culture is the visible made thing — dress, food, tools, architecture. Non-material culture is the carried invisible — language, values, beliefs, etiquette, the sense of what is polite and what is past. Heritage names what is handed down: custom, ritual, folklore, oral tradition, heirloom, intangible heritage (the dances, songs and craft knowledge that live only in people), UNESCO World Heritage Site. And the verbs of change tell the whole story in miniature: traditions fade away, revive, are passed down, blend, are adopted — or are lost, which is the verb the essays worry about.

The vocabulary of respect matters as much as the vocabulary of description: sacred, solemn, festive, reverent, vibrant belong in culture writing; quaint and primitive do not — they are condescension wearing a compliment's coat. And the idioms: melting pot, a way of life, the old country, roots (as in put down new roots while keeping the old ones watered).

Grammar's quiet helper here is the present simple of habit: "Bhai Tika seals the bond between sisters and brothers" — customs live in the tense of always, while your remembered instance lives in the past ("Last Maghe Sankranti, my grandmother insisted…"). Descriptions that can hold both — the eternal rite and one particular afternoon — always read as authentic.

Reading the theme like a writer — the devices of culture writing

Imagery is the master device, and it is sensory: a festival described by sight alone is a postcard; described by smell (marigolds and incense), sound (deusi songs at the door), taste (sel roti eaten standing up) and touch (rice paste on the forehead), it becomes a place the reader visits. The exam's descriptive tasks are really imagery tasks with a word count.

Symbolism runs the theme: the tika is not a mark but a blessing made visible; jamara is barley and also the wish that growth be golden; a festival is never only a festival — it is a community telling itself its own story once a year. When a passage calls a lamp "a small argument against the dark", the symbol is doing philosophy, and noticing it is the analysis.

Juxtaposition — old beside new — is the theme's favourite composition: the dasho coat worn with sneakers, e-greetings travelling beside tika, a drone filming a ritual older than the drone. Writers juxtapose to ask a question without asking it; your essay can answer it: change is not betrayal — every tradition was once an innovation that survived.

And tone decides everything, as in the humour unit: affectionate observation reads as love; anthropological distance can read as condescension. The difference is in the small words, again — we versus they, our quiet streets versus their customs.

The debates, held honestly

"Globalisation is destroying local culture." Evidence for the prosecution: English crowding mother tongues out of urban playgrounds; festivals compressed into photo opportunities; the same shops, shows and shoes from Kathmandu to Kansas. Evidence for the defence: the same connectivity gave Nepali music, film and food a world audience, and diaspora communities now fund and revive rituals the villages alone could not sustain. The synthesis worth writing: globalisation is a filter, not a vacuum — what survives is what communities deliberately transmit. The danger is passive drift; the remedy is deliberate teaching — the language spoken at home, the festival celebrated properly and not merely posted.

"Tradition versus modernity" is a false war, and saying so is the top-band move: they are shelves in the same house. A family video-calls a daughter abroad during the tika ceremony. Argue practice by practice: does it still serve care, identity, joy? Then retain, reform or retire it — case by case, with reasons.

"Should tourists pay more at heritage sites?" Weigh heritage as a public good that needs funding against fairness and access; note the risk of living temples becoming tickets. Land on differentiated pricing with transparent conservation use — policy, not slogan.

The table's last row is the essay's hinge: folk culture dies quietly unless someone teaches it on purpose — which is why "preserve heritage" essays should end with verbs (teach, practise, document, fund), not adjectives (rich, vibrant, precious).

How the exam asks it

The descriptive essay ("Describe a festival you celebrate") wants one festival, three senses, one sequence, one meaning — depth beats breadth every time. The argumentative essay ("Traditional culture is being lost to globalisation") wants both shelves weighed and a deliberate-transmission close. The short answers want definitions with pulse: "intangible heritage is what lives in people — language, music, ritual, craft — not in objects; lose the speakers and you lose it all." The article or letter (to a tourist board, on protecting heritage) wants one problem, one workable proposal, one polite close.

A worked miniature of the whole method: "Tihar arrives by smell before sight: marigolds at every gate, oil lamps outnumbering electric ones for one night, the kitchen surrendering to sel roti fried in batches that never reach the plate warm. By the third day the streets ring with deusi songs that ask more than they demand, and even the stingiest householder pays the traditional tax in sweets. What the lights say, quietly, is that darkness loses when the whole lane decides together." — senses, sequence, meaning, and a last line that argues. That is the unit, folded into four sentences.
