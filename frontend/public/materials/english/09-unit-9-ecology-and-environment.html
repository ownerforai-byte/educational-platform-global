Unit 9 — Ecology and Environment

The theme with a deadline

Environment is where language has consequences: the essays you practise here are rehearsals for arguments citizens must win about the air they breathe and the water they drink. Nepal makes the stakes visible from any window — the world's highest mountains are the source of its rivers, and the cleanest hill air coexists with Kathmandu's winter smog. The unit gives you the vocabulary, the cause-and-effect grammar, and the argument frames; the exam gives you marks for all three.

The words that carry the theme

The systems first: ecosystem, biodiversity, habitat, food chain, watershed, carbon footprint, and the pair every essay should keep straight — climate (decades of averages) versus weather (one Tuesday). The problems: pollution (air, water, soil, noise, plastic), deforestation, landslides, erosion, extinction, invasive species, waste mismanagement — and Nepal's own acronym, GLOF (glacial lake outburst flood), the mountain's version of a bursting dam. The responses: conservation, afforestation, recycling, waste segregation, renewable energy, sustainable development, climate adaptation, mitigation, environmental impact assessment.

One precision worth marks: the greenhouse effect is not the villain — it keeps the Earth liveable; the enhanced effect from burning fossil fuels is the problem. Candidates who make that distinction sound like readers; candidates who do not sound like headlines.

The phrasal verbs and collocations: to cut down on emissions, phase out diesel, run out of groundwater, clean up a river, wipe out a species, set up a recycling scheme; heavy traffic, toxic fumes, a blanket of smog, arable land, flash floods. And the honest hedges: pollution is "a major contributor to" respiratory illness, not its single cause — calibration is credibility.

Reading the theme like a writer — the devices of environmental writing

Environmental writing is built on cause and effect chained together, and the chain is itself a device: "Deforestation removes roots → roots hold soil → soil washes into rivers → rivers silt up → floods deepen downstream." Write the arrows first, then convert them to sentences with as a result, consequently, which in turn — the logic becomes unbreakable, and the examiner can see the links.

Personification is the theme's most powerful figure: the mountain "bleeds" plastic, the river "chokes", the forest "loses its lungs". It works because it converts statistics into something with a pulse — but it must be earned by facts nearby, or it reads as decoration. Contrast structures every serious essay: the green hill station against the grey city; the glacier photograph from 1980 against today's; the village that banned plastic bags against the town that did not. One concrete before-and-after is worth a page of alarm.

Then there is irony, the theme's bitterest device: bottled water sold in a country with the world's largest fresh-water reserves; air-conditioners cooling rooms while warming the planet that needs the cooling; the "eco-resort" flying guests in to admire an unspoiled view their flights are spoiling. An essay that catches one such irony and explains its mechanism is doing the analysis NEB's comprehension questions pay for.

The debates, held honestly

"Development versus environment" is the theme's false boxing match, and refusing it is the top-band move. The honest frame is sustainable development — meeting present needs without stealing from the future — and it is not a rich-country luxury: hydropower is development and low-carbon; community forestry is conservation and livelihood; the question is which development, not whether. Concede the real tension (a poor country cannot be lectured into staying poor), then rebut the false choice with the examples.

"Who is responsible — governments or individuals?" Concede the comfort of personal virtue; note its ceiling — no amount of personal thrift regulates a coal plant. Land on both at their levels: individuals set norms and markets; governments set rules and infrastructure; and in a democracy the individual's sharpest tool is the demand for policy.

"What can students actually do?" wants verbs, not virtues: segregate waste, refuse single-use plastic, walk and share rides, join the school eco-club, audit the school's own bins and bulbs, obey the bag ban and defend it. End each list with a verb — that is the difference between a slogan and a plan.

Grammar and style: the theme's quiet tests

Uncountable nouns rule here and punish the careless: pollution, deforestation, waste, evidence, information, research take no plural and no "a" — "much pollution", never "many pollutions"; "a piece of research", never "a research". Quantify with a rise in, a decline of, a sharp increase in; compare with worse than, the worst, twice as much as.

Scale language keeps arguments honest: local, national, regional, global. The litter in your lane is local; the plastic in it joins an ocean-scale current; the climate driver behind the whole story is global. The phrase worth owning: "a global problem with local handles."

And the modal calibration from Unit 5 applies with force: "The government should enforce kiln standards"; "Cities might pilot odd-even traffic days"; "We must not confuse a cold winter with a refuted trend." Calibration is style; style is marks.

How the exam asks it

The problem–solution essay ("Air pollution in Kathmandu", "Plastic pollution", 8–10 marks) wants the cause chain first and solutions aimed at causes, at three scales, with one Nepal anchor: brick kilns, vehicle exhaust, road dust, crop burning — each with a handle. The speech or article ("Save the environment") wants a strong opening line, three verbs per listener, and a close worth chanting — and no statistics you cannot source. The short answers want the distinctions said plainly: weather is mood, climate is statistics; a carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gases an activity causes. The formal letter to a municipality wants layout, one problem, one workable proposal, polite insistence.

A worked miniature of the whole method: "Every winter Kathmandu's air turns toxic, and the causes stack like the smog itself — kilns firing without scrubbers, vehicles idling in unmanaged traffic, dust from unpaved roads, stubble drifting in from the Terai. Each source has a handle: kiln standards, bus lanes, paving, alternative stubble use. 'The pollution is unbearable' is not an analysis until it becomes a list with handles attached." — chain, handles, close. The unit, folded into three sentences.
