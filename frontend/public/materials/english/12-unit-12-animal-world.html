Unit 12 — Animal World

The theme that tests our vocabulary and our ethics

Animals are the theme where description and conscience share a leash: the same paragraph must name the creature precisely and decide how we owe it. Nepal makes the theme vivid — tigers and rhinos are a census success story here, elephants occasionally attend village meetings uninvited, and every monsoon the streets fill with somebody's abandoned puppies. The unit gives you the naturalist's vocabulary, the grammar of comparison and habit, and the ethics frames the exam sets.

The words that carry the theme

Precision begins with the collective nouns the exam loves: a pack of wolves, a herd of elephants, a flock of birds, a swarm of bees, a pride of lions, a school of fish. Then the family words: cub, calf, kitten, chick, foal, puppy; and the gender pairs the paper tests — lion/lioness, tiger/tigress, bull/cow, ram/ewe, stallion/mare. The homes: nest, den, burrow, stable, kennel, hive, aquarium. The sounds, because English insists: dogs bark, cats meow, lions roar, bees buzz, snakes hiss, elephants trumpet — onomatopoeia is doing the naming, and it is the theme's built-in literary device.

The conservation vocabulary arrives from Unit 9 wearing fur: endangered, vulnerable, extinct, poaching, habitat loss, wildlife sanctuary, national park, captive breeding, poacher versus ranger, human-wildlife conflict. Nepal's own evidence is exam-ready: the tiger census doubling, the rhino translocations, the conservation success that tourism pays for — one checkable fact per essay, hedged as always.

And the idioms — animals live deep in English: let the cat out of the bag, hold your horses, a fish out of water, the elephant in the room, crocodile tears, a wolf in sheep's clothing, busy as a bee, kill two birds with one stone. Each is a small story frozen in a phrase; one, used aptly, shows the examiner a reader.

Reading the theme like a writer — the devices animals carry

Personification is the theme's oldest device — animals in literature think, plot and judge because it lets writers say human things at a safe distance: the crow's persistence in the fable is really a lecture on patience. Fable and allegory build whole morals on four legs; when a passage gives a fox cunning or an owl wisdom, the animal is a symbol, and naming what it stands for is the analysis.

Imagery here is a naturalist's job: not "a big cat" but "shoulders rolling under the coat, the tail's last third flicking like a metronome". Sound imagery matters too — the theme comes with its own orchestra (§1's verbs) — and a description that can be heard is half-written.

Irony and reversal give the theme its bite: the "guard dog" asleep while the cat investigates the thief; the zoo advertising freedom; humans caging animals to study freedom. And pathetic fallacy — weather or animals mirroring mood — waits in every set passage: the dog howling before the bad news. Notice it, name it, and say what mood it carries.

The debates, held honestly

"Zoos: prisons or sanctuaries?" Concede the cage's indictment — pacing predators, concrete enclosures. Rebut with the modern zoo's changed job: breeding programmes for the near-extinct, veterinary care, the child who sees a tiger and grows up to fund its forest. Land the synthesis: the question is not whether zoos but which kind — the concrete menagerie or the conservation ark.

"Should animals be used in testing?" The hardest frame in the theme, and it deserves its difficulty: weigh human medicine saved against suffering caused, then argue the ladder — replace where possible, reduce where not, refine always. The three R's are quotable and calm; slogans are neither.

"Wildlife versus development." The road through the forest, the airport near the wetland — Nepal argues these in the papers weekly. The honest frame: costs are real on both sides, mitigation (overpasses, corridors, timing) is the art, and the long ledger says the forest pays the country back in tourism, water and stability. One concrete case — a highway and a rhino corridor — beats a paragraph of principle.

"Pets versus wildlife." The quiet frame behind the puppy on the street: a pet is a commitment of years, not a festival purchase; abandonment is the theme's most answerable cruelty. The short essay here almost writes itself — which is why it scores well when told with one specific dog and badly when told with slogans.

Grammar and style: comparison and habit

Animals invite comparison, and the grammar must keep up: "A cheetah runs faster than a deer"; "The rhino is the most heavily built of them all"; "A cat sleeps as much as sixteen hours a day". Watch the double comparatives ("more faster" — the classic slip) and the irregulars (good/better/best, bad/worse/worst) that examiners salt into every paper.

Habit and instinct live in the present simple with plural subjects — "Tigers hunt alone; elephants move in herds led by a matriarch" — and the agreement slips here (the elephant move) are the theme's quiet grammar tax. The passive returns for the species' story: "The tiger was declared the national animal; habitat has been lost to farmland; cubs are smuggled across borders." Who did it matters less than what happened — the passive's honest use.

And the reported speech of wildlife journalism — "The warden said that the herd had moved to the river" — waits for the narration unit; this theme is where it gets its practice sentences.

How the exam asks it

The descriptive essay ("The animal I like most", 6–8 marks) wants one animal, seen precisely: appearance in naturalist detail, habitat and habits in the present simple, one reason for your choice that is not "it is beautiful". The argumentative essay ("Zoos should be banned", "Save wildlife") wants the frames above with one Nepal anchor — the tiger census, the community forests. The story from an animal's point of view (a favourite creative task) wants consistent personification: the dog narrates, so the dog cannot suddenly become "it". The short answers want the collective nouns, the homes, the sounds — the theme's vocabulary table in disguise.

A worked miniature of the whole method: "The rhino is not aggressive; it is certain. It has survived ice ages, poachers and our fences by refusing to hurry, and there is a lesson in that — but the lesson is ours, not its: the animal did not ask to symbolise anything. It asks only for the tall grass, the river at dusk, and to be left certain." — precision, personification with a purpose, and an ethics close. The unit, folded into three sentences.
