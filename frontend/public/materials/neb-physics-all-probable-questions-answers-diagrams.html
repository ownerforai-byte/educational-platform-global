Q1.1 — Dimensional analysis: period of a simple pendulum

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define dimensional formula and state its three uses. [2]

(b) Using dimensional analysis, show that the period T of a simple pendulum depends only on length L and g, and deduce T ∝ √(L/g). [3]

(c) A quantity P = A²B/C has relative errors 1%, 2%, 3% in A, B, C. Find the maximum relative error in P. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Dimensional formula of a physical quantity = the product of powers to which the base quantities M, L, T (and Θ, I, N, J as needed) must be raised to express its unit. Example: force = [M L T⁻²].
Three uses: ① check dimensional correctness (homogeneity) of an equation; ② convert between unit systems; ③ deduce / verify a relation between quantities (up to a constant).

(b) Assume T = k · Lˣ · gʸ where k is dimensionless. Take dimensions:

[T] = [L]ˣ · [L T⁻²]ʸ = L^(x+y) · T^(−2y).

Equating powers of L and T on both sides (LHS = [T] → L⁰T¹):

Power of L: 0 = x + y → x = −y.
Power of T: 1 = −2y → y = −½, so x = ½.

∴ T ∝ L^(1/2) g^(−1/2) = √(L/g). The constant 2π cannot be found by dimensional analysis, giving T = 2π√(L/g).

(c) P = A²·B·C⁻¹ → relative error in P = 2(ΔA/A) + 1(ΔB/B) + 1(ΔC/C) = 2(1%) + 2% + 3%.

Maximum relative error in P = 7%.

Q1.2 — Systematic vs random error; significant figures

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Distinguish between systematic and random errors, one example each. [2]

(b) Explain why random error can be reduced by averaging but systematic error cannot. [3]

(c) A length is recorded as 5.034 cm ± 0.002 cm. Give the number of significant figures and the percentage error. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Systematic (bias) error is a directional, repeatable error — always high or always low — caused by faulty calibration, a zero error, or a personal habit (e.g. a ruler worn by 0.5 mm). Random (statistical) error is non-directional scatter caused by unpredictable small variations in reading, temperature, vibration.

(b) Random errors are distributed ± around the true value, so taking the mean of N readings reduces the mean error as σ/√N — positive and negative deviations cancel. Systematic errors are a constant bias; averaging only shifts the whole set but leaves the bias intact, so only recalibration (or a correction) can remove them.

(c) 5.034 cm → leading 5, 0, 3, 4 all significant → 4 significant figures. Percentage error = (0.002 / 5.034) × 100 = 0.0397%.

∴ ≈ 0.04%.

Q1.3 — Accuracy vs precision; measurement result

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define accuracy and precision; why can a measurement be precise but not accurate? [2]

(b) Masses 10.2, 10.1, 10.3, 10.2, 10.1 g. Find the mean, the mean absolute error and the limit error; report the final value. [3]

(c) State the rules for counting significant figures and apply to 0.004200. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Accuracy = closeness of the reading to the true value; precision = closeness / repeatability of readings to each other. A clock that is 5 min fast is precise (always 5 min high) but not accurate.

(b) Mean = (10.2+10.1+10.3+10.2+10.1)/5 = 50.9/5 = 10.18 g.
Deviations: +0.02, −0.08, +0.12, +0.02, −0.08 → absolute: 0.02, 0.08, 0.12, 0.02, 0.08. Mean absolute error = (0.32)/5 ≈ 0.064 g. Limit (maximum) error = 0.12 g.

Reported value = 10.18 ± 0.06 g (mean ± mean absolute error) — to consistent significant figures, ≈ 10.2 ± 0.1 g.

(c) Rules: (1) non-zero digits always significant; (2) zeros between non-zeros significant; (3) leading zeros NOT; (4) trailing zeros after the decimal point significant; (5) trailing zeros without a decimal are ambiguous. In 0.004200: leading zeros don't count; 4, 2, 0, 0 count → 4 significant figures.

Q1.4 — Derived quantities; viscosity vs surface tension dimensions

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define a derived quantity and give four examples with SI units. [2]

(b) Obtain the dimensional formula of coefficient of viscosity and surface tension, and show they differ. [3]

(c) Two quantities X and Y have the same dimensions. Does X = Y? Give a reason and example. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) A derived quantity is expressed in terms of base quantities. Examples: velocity m/s, force N, pressure Pa, energy J.

(b) Viscosity η: from Newton's law of viscous force F = ηA(v/h) → η = F·h/(A·v). Dimensions = [M L T⁻²]·[L] / ([L²]·[L T⁻¹]) = [M L⁻¹ T⁻¹].
Surface tension σ: force per unit length = [M L T⁻²] / [L] = [M T⁻¹].
Comparing: η = [M L⁻¹ T⁻¹] and σ = [M L⁰ T⁻¹] — clearly different (one has L⁻¹, the other L⁰).

∴ They are distinct physical quantities.

(c) No. Identical dimensions do not imply identical quantities — e.g. work, energy and torque all equal [M L² T⁻²] but work and energy are scalars while torque is a vector. Same dimensional formula, different physical meaning.

Unit 2 – Vectors

Q2.1 — Dot & cross product; A + B + C = 0 identity

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Distinguish between the dot product and the cross product (four differences). [2]

(b) Show that if A + B + C = 0, then B × C = C × A = A × B. [3]

(c) Two vectors of magnitude 3 and 4 have a resultant of 5. Find the angle between them. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Four differences: ① Dot gives a scalar, cross gives a vector; ② Dot is commutative (A·B = B·A), cross is anti-commutative (B×A = −A×B); ③ A·B = |A||B|cosθ, A×B = |A||B|sinθ n̂; ④ A·B = 0 when A ⊥ B, but A×B = 0 when A ∥ B.

(b) Given A + B + C = 0 → A + B = −C. Cross both sides by C:

C × (A + B) = C × (−C) → C×A + C×B = −(C×C) = 0 → C×A = −C×B = B×C. …(i)
Similarly A + C = −B, cross by A: A×(A+C) = −B×A → A×C = −B×A = A×B. …(ii)
From (i) and (ii): B×C = A×B = C×A. Q.E.D.

(c) |R|² = |A|² + |B|² + 2|A||B|cosθ = 9 + 16 + 2(3)(4)cosθ = 25 → 48cosθ = 0 → cosθ = 0.

∴ θ = 90° (the two vectors are perpendicular).

Q2.2 — Unit & axial vectors; equal magnitudes

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define a unit vector and explain the concept of an axial (pseudo) vector. [2]

(b) If two vectors A and B have equal magnitudes and |A + B| also equals that magnitude, find the angle between A and B. [3]

(c) A body of mass m moves in a circle of radius r with angular speed ω. Derive the centripetal acceleration. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) A unit vector has magnitude 1 and specifies direction only (â = A/|A|). An axial (pseudo) vector (ω, τ, L, B) follows the right-hand rule and, unlike a polar vector, does NOT reverse under a mirror inversion — it represents a rotation, not a straight-line quantity.

(b) Let |A| = |B| = A. Squaring |A+B| = A: |A+B|² = A² + A² + 2A²cosθ = 3A² = A² + A² + 2A²cosθ → 2A²cosθ = A² → cosθ = ½.

∴ θ = 60°.

(c) In a small time Δt the velocity vector turns through Δθ = ωΔt while keeping magnitude v. The change in velocity has magnitude Δv = v·Δθ (small-angle). Hence a = Δv/Δt = v(ωΔt)/Δt = vω.

∴ a_c = vω = v²/r = ω²r, directed toward the centre (centripetal).

Q2.3 — Vector resolution; 3D identity; river crossing

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Explain resolution of a vector into rectangular components. [2]

(b) Show that the sum of the squares of the three components equals the square of the magnitude. [3]

(c) A boat crosses a river of width d with boat-speed v_b (perpendicular to flow) and current v_c. Derive the crossing time and the downstream drift. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) A vector A in 3D is resolved into components along the axes: A = Aₓî + A_yĵ + A_zk̂, where Aₓ = |A|cosα, A_y = |A|cosβ, A_z = |A|cosγ are the projections on the x, y, z axes.

(b) By the 3-D Pythagoras theorem: |A|² = Aₓ² + A_y² + A_z². Equivalently, A·A = |A|² (from the dot product A·A = |A||A|cos0 = |A|²). Q.E.D.

(c) The crossing depends only on the transverse component of the boat's velocity. Crossing time t = d/v_b. During this time the current carries the boat downstream by

drift = v_c · t = d · (v_c/v_b).

Unit 3 – Kinematics

Q3.1 — Distance vs displacement; x = t³ − 6t² + 9t; vertical projection

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Distinguish between distance and displacement (four points). [2]

(b) A particle moves in a straight line, x = t³ − 6t² + 9t (SI). Find v and a, and the time(s) when v = 0. [3]

(c) A body is projected vertically up with speed u. Show that the time of flight is 2u/g and the maximum height is u²/2g. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) ① Distance is the total path length (scalar, always ≥ |displacement|); displacement is the net change in position (vector). ② Distance depends on the actual path; displacement depends only on start & end points. ③ Distance is never negative; displacement can be +, − or 0. ④ For a closed path, displacement = 0 but distance ≠ 0.

(b) v = dx/dt = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3). Setting v = 0 → t = 1 s or t = 3 s. Acceleration a = dv/dt = 6t − 12.

(c) Upward: v = 0 at top → 0 = u − gt_up → t_up = u/g, so total time of flight T = 2t_up = 2u/g.
At the top, using v² = u² − 2gH with v = 0 → u² = 2gH → H = u²/2g.

∴ T = 2u/g and H = u²/2g.

Q3.2 — Equations of motion; projectile range; complementary angles

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) State the three equations of uniformly accelerated linear motion. [2]

(b) A projectile is fired at angle θ with speed v₀. Derive the range and show it is maximum at θ = 45°. [3]

(c) For the same speed, two projectiles fired at complementary angles θ and 90° − θ have equal range. Show this and note the difference in time of flight. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) For constant acceleration a: ① v = u + at; ② s = ut + ½at²; ③ v² = u² + 2as.

(b) Resolving: v₀ₓ = v₀cosθ (horizontal, no acceleration), v₀y = v₀sinθ (vertical). Time of flight T = 2v₀sinθ/g (from ①, using a = −g). Range = horizontal velocity × time:

R = v₀cosθ · (2v₀sinθ/g) = v₀²(2sinθcosθ)/g = v₀²sin2θ/g.

R is maximum when sin2θ = 1 → 2θ = 90° → θ = 45°, giving R_max = v₀²/g.

(c) R(θ) = v₀²sin2θ/g and R(90°−θ) = v₀²sin(180°−2θ)/g = v₀²sin2θ/g → equal ranges. But T(θ) = 2v₀sinθ/g differs from T(90°−θ) = 2v₀cosθ/g (except at 45°).

Q3.3 — Uniform circular motion; centripetal force; rolling wheel

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration. [2]

(b) A body of mass m moves in a circle of radius r with speed v. Derive the centripetal force. [3]

(c) A wheel of radius 0.5 m rolls without slipping at 10 m/s. Find ω and the angular acceleration if it decelerates to 5 m/s in 2 s. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Uniform circular motion = motion in a circle at constant speed (|v| constant, direction changing). Centripetal acceleration = the inward acceleration a_c = v²/r required to keep the body on the circular path.

(b) In time Δt the body moves through angle Δθ = ωΔt. The velocity vector changes direction by Δθ but keeps magnitude v. The change in velocity has magnitude Δv = v·Δθ (small angle), so

a = Δv/Δt = v(ωΔt)/Δt = vω = v²/r (since v = ωr). Multiplying by mass, F_c = m v²/r = mω²r, directed toward the centre.

∴ Centripetal force F_c = m v²/r.

(c) Rolling without slipping: v = ωR → ω = 10/0.5 = 20 rad/s. Angular deceleration α = Δv/(R·Δt) = (5 − 10)/(0.5 × 2) = −5/1 = −5 rad/s² (decelerating).

Q3.4 — Simple harmonic motion: definitions, v & a, energy

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define simple harmonic motion and state its two defining conditions. [2]

(b) Show that for SHM the velocity v = ω√(A² − x²), the acceleration a = −ω²x, and that the total energy is constant. [3]

(c) A mass–spring system (k = 200 N/m, m = 0.5 kg) oscillates. Find its period and frequency. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Simple harmonic motion (SHM) = periodic to-and-fro motion about a fixed mean position such that: ① the acceleration is proportional to displacement, a ∝ −x (restoring); ② the acceleration is always directed toward the mean position.

(b) At displacement x, acceleration a = −ω²x, so KE = ½mv² and PE = ½kx² (spring, k = mω²). Total energy E = ½mv² + ½kx². Since x = A sinωt, substituting:

v² = ω²(A² − x²) → v = ω√(A² − x²) (velocity). Then E = ½mω²(A² − x²) + ½mω²x² = ½mω²A² = ½kA².

∴ E = ½kA² = constant, and a = −ω²x (restoring). Q.E.D.

(c) T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(0.5/200) = 2π√(0.0025) = 2π(0.05) = 0.314 s. Frequency f = 1/T = 1/0.314 ≈ 3.18 Hz.

Q3.5 — Oscillatory vs periodic vs SHM; circular projection; damping

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Distinguish oscillatory, periodic and simple harmonic motion. [2]

(b) Derive x = A cos(ωt + φ) for SHM from the projection of uniform circular motion. [3]

(c) Explain the effect of damping on the amplitude and frequency of oscillations. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Oscillatory = to-and-fro motion about a mean position. Periodic = a motion that repeats after a fixed time T (oscillatory motion is usually periodic). SHM = a special periodic oscillation with a restoring force a ∝ −x (sinusoidal).

(b) Consider a point P moving in a circle of radius A with angular speed ω; its x-projection Q moves in SHM. If P makes an angle (ωt + φ) from the +x axis at time t, then the projection position is

x = A cos(ωt + φ) — the equation of SHM. (Differentiating gives v and a as in Q3.4.)

(c) Damping removes energy, so the amplitude decays exponentially: A(t) = A₀e^(−γt). The damped frequency ω' = √(ω₀² − γ²) is slightly lower than the undamped ω₀ (heavy damping can suppress oscillation entirely).

Unit 4 – Heat & Temperature

Q4.1 — Temperature vs heat; scale conversion; temperature rise

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define temperature and heat; state how they differ. [2]

(b) Derive the general conversion relation among the Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin and Rankine scales, and find each at absolute zero. [3]

(c) A body's temperature rises from 25°C to 100°C. Find the rise in kelvin and in degrees Fahrenheit. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Temperature = the property that determines the direction of heat flow; a measure of the average kinetic energy of molecules (intensive, unit K). Heat = energy in transit from a higher-temperature body to a lower-temperature one due to temperature difference (unit J, extensive, not storable).

(b) All four scales are linear in T, so equal fractions of the range from ice-point to steam-point are equal: C/100 = (F − 32)/180 = (K − 273.15)/100 = °R/671.7.
At absolute zero: K = 0, °C = −273.15, °F = (9/5)(−273.15) + 32 = −459.67, °R = 0.

(c) ΔT = 100 − 25 = 75 K (kelvin and Celsius have the same unit size). In Fahrenheit: Δ°F = (9/5)·75 = 135°F.

Q4.2 — Three modes of heat transfer; Fourier's law; thermal conductivity

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Explain the three modes of heat transfer with one example each. [2]

(b) State Fourier's law of heat conduction and define thermal conductivity. [3]

(c) A metal rod (k = 400 W/m·K, area 0.01 m², length 0.2 m) has a temperature difference of 60 K across it. Find the rate of heat flow. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Conduction – heat through solids by molecular collisions (spoon in hot tea); convection – heat by bulk movement of fluids (boiling water, sea breeze); radiation – heat by electromagnetic waves, needs no medium (sunlight reaching Earth through vacuum).

(b) Fourier's law: the rate of heat flow is proportional to area and temperature gradient: dQ/dt = kA(ΔT/L), where ΔT/L is the temperature gradient along the rod. The proportionality constant k is the thermal conductivity (heat per unit area, per unit time, per unit gradient), SI unit W/(m·K).

(c) dQ/dt = kA·ΔT/L = 400 × 0.01 × 60 / 0.2 = 1200/0.2 · 0.01... computing: 400×0.01 = 4; 4×60 = 240; 240/0.2 = 1200 W.

∴ Rate of heat flow = 1200 W.

Q4.3 — Specific & linear expansion; β ≈ 3α; brass rod

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define specific heat capacity and coefficient of linear expansion. [2]

(b) Show that for an isotropic solid the coefficient of volume expansion is approximately 3 times the linear coefficient. [3]

(c) A 1 m brass rod (α = 1.9×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹) is heated by 100°C. Find its increase in length. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Specific heat capacity c = heat to raise the temperature of unit mass by one kelvin (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). Coefficient of linear expansion α = fractional increase in length per unit rise in temperature, α = (ΔL/L₀)/ΔT.

(b) For a cube of side L, V = L³. Heating by ΔT gives ΔL = αL₀ΔT. Differentiating V = L³: ΔV = 3L²ΔL. Hence β = ΔV/(V₀ΔT) = 3L²(αL₀ΔT)/L³ΔT = 3α (first order, ignoring higher-order terms).

∴ β ≈ 3α for an isotropic solid.

(c) ΔL = αL₀ΔT = 1.9×10⁻⁵ × 1 × 100 = 1.9×10⁻³ m.

∴ Increase in length = 1.9 mm.

Q4.4 — C_p vs C_v; Mayer's relation; γ values

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Distinguish specific heat at constant pressure and at constant volume. [2]

(b) Show that for an ideal gas C_p − C_v = R (Mayer's relation). [3]

(c) Find C_p/C_v for a monatomic and a diatomic ideal gas. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) C_v = specific heat at constant volume (all heat raises internal energy). C_p = specific heat at constant pressure. Since at constant pressure the gas also does expansion work PΔV, C_p > C_v.

(b) Per mole, raising by 1 K: at constant volume, heat in = C_v = ΔU. At constant pressure, heat in = C_p = ΔU + PΔV. Subtracting: C_p − C_v = PΔV. For an ideal gas, PΔV = R·ΔT = R (for 1 K). Hence

C_p − C_v = R. Q.E.D.

(c) Monatomic: C_v = 3R/2, C_p = 5R/2 → γ = C_p/C_v = 5/3. Diatomic (ordinary T): C_v = 5R/2, C_p = 7R/2 → γ = 7/5.

Q5.1 — Specific heat capacity; principle of mixtures; numerical

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define specific heat capacity; explain why water is used as a coolant. [2]

(b) State the principle of mixtures and describe the mixing method for determining the specific heat capacity of a solid metal. [3]

(c) A copper vessel of mass 0.5 kg contains 1 kg of water at 20°C. How much heat is needed to raise the temperature of the whole to 60°C? (cwater = 4186 J kg¹°C¹, ccopper = 390 J kg¹°C¹) [3]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Specific heat capacity (c): the heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K (or 1 °C); unit J kg¹ K¹. Water has a very high c (4186 J kg¹K¹), so it absorbs a lot of heat per degree without its temperature changing much — ideal as a coolant (radiators, engine cooling, thermal storage).

(b) Principle of mixtures: when two bodies at different temperatures are mixed in an insulated container, heat lost by the hotter = heat gained by the colder (neglecting the container and losses). Mixing method: heat a metal block to a known temperature (≈100°C in boiling water), quickly transfer it into cold water in a calorimeter; measure the rise in water temperature; equate heat lost by metal to heat gained by water + calorimeter: mscs(Ts − T) = (mwcw + C)(T − Tw) ⇒ cs obtained.

(c) Heat to water: Qw = 1 × 4186 × (60 − 20) = 4186 × 40 = 167 440 J.

Heat to vessel: Qc = 0.5 × 390 × 40 = 7800 J.

Total Q = 167 440 + 7800 = 175 240 J ≈ 1.75 × 10&sup5; J ≈ 419 cal × 418.6 ≈ 1.75 × 10&sup5; J.

Total heat required ≈ 1.75 × 10&sup5; J.

Q5.2 — Latent heat: fusion & vaporization; numerical with ice

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define latent heat; distinguish latent heat of fusion from latent heat of vaporization and explain why Lv > Lf for water. [2]

(b) Find the mass of ice at 0°C required to cool 200 g of water at 40°C to 0°C. (Lf = 334 kJ/kg) [3]

(c) How much heat is required to convert 100 g of ice at −10°C to steam at 100°C? (cice = 2090 J kg¹K¹, Lf = 334 kJ/kg, cwater = 4186 J kg¹K¹, Lv = 2.26 × 10&sup6; J/kg) [3]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Latent heat (L): the heat absorbed or released per unit mass during a change of state at constant temperature. Fusion (solid→liquid): breaks some bonds, Lf(water) = 334 kJ/kg. Vaporization (liquid→gas): molecules are fully separated with a large volume increase and work against pressure, so Lv(water) = 2.26 × 10&sup6; J/kg ≈ 6.8 Lf, hence Lv > Lf.

(b) Heat lost by water = mwcwΔT = 0.2 × 4186 × 40 = 33 488 J. Heat gained by ice = mi Lf = mi × 334 000.

mi = 33 488 / 334 000 ≈ 0.1 kg = 100 g of ice.

(c) Stage 1 (ice −10→0°C): Q1 = 0.1 × 2090 × 10 = 2090 J. Stage 2 (fusion): Q2 = 0.1 × 334 000 = 33 400 J. Stage 3 (water 0→100°C): Q3 = 0.1 × 4186 × 100 = 41 860 J. Stage 4 (vaporization): Q4 = 0.1 × 2.26 × 10&sup6; = 226 000 J.

Total = 2090 + 33 400 + 41 860 + 226 000 = 303 350 J ≈ 3.03 × 10&sup5; J.

100 g of ice; total heat to convert ice at −10°C into steam at 100°C ≈ 3.03 × 10&sup5; J.

Q5.3 — Calorimetry: steam & ice; equivalent weight; mechanical equivalent

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Explain the use of steam at 100°C in heating systems; define the mechanical equivalent of heat. [2]

(b) 50 g of water at 0°C is in a calorimeter. Dry steam at 100°C is passed in. Find the mass of steam that condenses to bring the whole to 50°C. (Lv = 2.26 × 10&sup6; J/kg, cw = 4186 J kg¹K¹) [3]

(c) A 2 kg metal at 80°C is dropped into 3 kg of water at 10°C; the final temperature is 20°C. Find the specific heat of the metal. [3]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Steam at 100°C delivers both sensible heat (as it cools) and a large latent heat of vaporization on condensing, so it transfers far more heat per kg than hot water — efficient heating. Mechanical equivalent of heat (J): the work needed to produce one unit of heat; J = 4.186 J/cal (1 cal = 4.186 J), experimentally found by Joule.

(b) Heat needed to raise 50 g water from 0→50°C: Q = 0.05 × 4186 × 50 = 10 465 J.

Heat released per kg of steam: condense at 100°C (Lv = 2.26 × 10&sup6; J) + cool 100→50°C (4186 × 50 = 209 300 J) ≈ 2.469 × 10&sup6; J/kg.

Mass of steam = 10 465 / 2.469 × 10&sup6; ≈ 4.24 × 10³ g — i.e. ≈ 4.2 g.

(c) Heat lost = heat gained: 2 × cm × (80 − 20) = 3 × 4186 × (20 − 10) ⇒ 120 cm = 125 580 ⇒ cm = 1046.5 J kg¹K¹ ≈ 1050 J kg¹K¹ (brass/bronze class).

Steam used ≈ 4.2 g; cmetal ≈ 1050 J kg¹K¹.

Unit 6 – Reflection at Curved Mirrors

Sign convention used throughout (New Cartesian / Indian standard): Light travels left → right. Pole P = origin. Object is to the left → u is negative. For a concave mirror the focus F and C are to the left → f = −R/2 (negative). For a convex mirror F and C are to the right → f = +R/2 (positive). Real image (in front, same side as object) → v is negative; virtual image (behind the mirror) → v is positive. m = −v/u; m < 0 → inverted, m > 0 → erect.

Q6.1 — Concave mirror: real image, object beyond C

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define a concave mirror and its focal length; state the sign convention used. [2]

(b) Draw ray diagrams to show how a concave mirror forms a real, inverted image when the object is beyond C; state the nature of the image when the object is between F and the pole. [3]

(c) An object 4 cm tall is placed 30 cm in front of a concave mirror (R = 20 cm). Find the image position, size and nature. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) A concave mirror is a converging (caved-in) spherical mirror; its focal length is f = R/2 in magnitude. Using the New Cartesian convention: object to the left → u < 0; concave mirror focus is on the left → f < 0; a real image (in front of the mirror) has v < 0, a virtual image (behind) has v > 0. Magnification m = −v/u.

(b) See Fig 6.1. Three principal rays from the object's top: ① a ray parallel to the axis reflects through F; ② a ray through C reflects back on itself; ③ a ray aimed at F reflects parallel to the axis. For an object beyond C these three reflected rays meet in front of the mirror (between F and C) → real, inverted, diminished image. When the object is between F and the pole, the reflected rays diverge; traced backwards they meet behind the mirror → virtual, erect, magnified image (dentist / shaving mirror).

(c) u = −30 cm, R = 20 cm → f = −R/2 = −10 cm. Mirror equation: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f → 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/(−10) − 1/(−30) = −3/30 + 1/30 = −2/30 = −1/15.

∴ v = −15 cm (negative → real image, 15 cm in front of the mirror, between F and C). Magnification m = −v/u = −(−15)/(−30) = −1/2. Image height = m × h_o = −½ × 4 cm = −2 cm.

The image is 15 cm in front of the mirror; it is real, inverted, and half the object's size (2 cm tall).

Q6.2 — Concave vs convex; mirror equation & magnification; convex numerical

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Distinguish between a concave and a convex mirror (five differences). [2]

(b) Derive the mirror equation 1/v + 1/u = 1/f and the magnification relation m = −v/u. [3]

(c) A convex mirror (f = +15 cm) forms a virtual image 5 cm behind it. Find the object distance and the nature of the image. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) ① Concave reflects from the inner (caved) side; convex from the outer (bulged) side. ② Concave converges parallel rays (f < 0); convex diverges them (f > 0). ③ Concave can form real and virtual images; convex only virtual. ④ A concave image may be magnified; a convex image is always diminished. ⑤ Concave has a narrow field of view; convex has a wide field (rear-view / road-corner mirrors).

Q6.3 — Real vs virtual image; searchlight; convex numerical (2 m object)

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define a real image and a virtual image. [2]

(b) Explain why a concave mirror used as a searchlight must have its source placed at the focus. [3]

(c) A convex mirror has a radius of curvature 60 cm. An object 2 m tall is placed 3 m in front. Find the image position and its height. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) A real image is formed where the actual reflected / refracted rays meet; it can be projected on a screen and, for a concave mirror, lies in front of the mirror (v < 0). A virtual image is formed where the rays only appear to diverge from (extended backwards); it cannot be projected and is seen behind the mirror (v > 0), as with plane and convex mirrors.

(b) By the reversibility of light paths, a ray that starts at the focus F of a concave mirror reflects parallel to the principal axis (the reverse of "a parallel ray reflects through F"). Placing the source at F therefore makes all reflected rays leave parallel → a strong, collimated beam, exactly what a searchlight or headlight requires.

(c) Convex: R = 60 cm → f = +R/2 = +30 cm. u = −300 cm (3 m). 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/30 − 1/(−300) = 1/30 + 1/300 = 10/300 + 1/300 = 11/300 → v = 300/11 ≈ +27.3 cm (virtual, 27.3 cm behind the mirror).

Magnification m = −v/u = −(27.3)/(−300) = 0.091. Image height = m × h_o = 0.091 × 200 cm ≈ 18.2 cm.

The image is virtual, erect and highly diminished: about 18 cm tall, 27.3 cm behind the mirror.

Key formulas (refraction at plane surfaces): Snell’s law: n&sub1; sin i = n&sub2; sin r. Refractive index of 2 w.r.t. 1: μ = n&sub2;/n&sub1; = sin i / sin r = (speed of light in 1)/(speed in 2). Q7.1 — Refractive index; Snell’s law; apparent depth numerical

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Define refractive index of a medium; state Snell’s law of refraction and explain why a pencil looks broken in water. [2]

(b) A ray of light passes from air into a liquid and the angle of refraction is 30° when the angle of incidence is 60°. Find the refractive index of the liquid and its speed of light. [3]

(c) A water tank 1.2 m deep is viewed from above. If μ of water = 4/3, find the apparent depth and the rise of the image. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Refractive index (μ): the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum (or air) to its speed in the medium, μ = c/v; equivalently by Snell’s law μ = sin i / sin r for air→medium. Snell’s law: (i) the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane; (ii) n&sub1; sin i = n&sub2; sin r. A pencil partly in water looks broken because rays from the submerged part bend away from the normal when going water→air (rarer medium); the eye traces them back in straight lines, so the submerged part appears raised (apparent depth < true depth) at the water surface.

(b) Snell’s law: nair sin i = nliq sin r ⇒ μ = sin 60° / sin 30° = (√3/2) / (1/2) = √3 ≈ 1.73.

Speed in the liquid: v = c / μ = 3 × 10&sup8; / √3 = 1.73 × 10&sup8; m/s.

(c) Apparent depth = real depth / μ = 1.2 / (4/3) = 0.9 m. Rise of the image = true depth − apparent depth = 1.2 − 0.9 = 0.3 m.

μ = √3 ≈ 1.73, v = 1.73 × 10&sup8; m/s; apparent depth = 0.9 m and the image rises by 0.3 m.

Q7.2 — Laws of refraction; total internal reflection; critical angle

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) State the laws of refraction and explain total internal reflection with conditions for it to occur. [2]

(b) Define critical angle; derive the relation θc = sin¹(1/μ). A glass prism has μ = 1.5. Find its critical angle. [3]

(c) A light ray is incident on a water–air surface from water. If μwater = 4/3, find the critical angle and state whether a ray incident at 50° is refracted or totally internally reflected. [2]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) Laws of refraction: (1) incident ray, refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence are coplanar; (2) n&sub1; sin i = n&sub2; sin r (Snell’s law). Total internal reflection (TIR): when a ray travels from a denser to a rarer medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle, no refraction occurs and the ray is entirely reflected back into the denser medium — like a perfect mirror. Conditions: (i) the ray must travel from denser → rarer medium; (ii) the angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle for that pair of media; (iii) there is no loss of intensity (unlike partial reflection at a surface).

(b) Critical angle (θc): the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is exactly 90°. At this point Snell’s law gives μ sin θc = 1 × sin 90° = 1 ⇒ θc = sin¹(1/μ).

For glass (μ = 1.5): θc = sin¹(1/1.5) = sin¹(0.667) ≈ 41.8°.

(c) θc = sin¹(3/4) = sin¹(0.75) ≈ 48.6°. Since 50° > 48.6°, the ray exceeds the critical angle ⇒ it undergoes total internal reflection at the water–air surface and does not emerge.

θc = sin¹(1/μ); glass: 41.8°; water: 48.6° — a 50° ray in water is totally internally reflected.

Unit 8 — Refraction through a Prism

Prism formulas: Deviation δ = i + e − A, where i = angle of incidence, e = angle of emergence, A = refracting/prism angle. Minimum deviation δm satisfies μ = sin((A + δm)/2) / sin(A/2). At minimum deviation the prism is symmetric: i = e and r&sub1; = r&sub2; = A/2. Dispersion: μviolet > μred so violet bends most; angular dispersion = δv − δr. Power of prism P = δ(μ − 1) in prism units.

Q8.1 — Deviation formula; minimum deviation; μ numerical

(c) A prism has A = 60° and δm = 38°. Find the refractive index of its material. [2]

(a) In the prism, let i = angle of incidence at the first face, r&sub1; = refraction angle inside at the first face, r&sub2; = incidence at the second face, e = emergence angle. Snell’s law: μ = sin i / sin r&sub1; = sin e / sin r&sub2;. In triangle A′BC: A + r&sub1; + r&sub2; = 180° and the quadrilateral at the two faces gives i + e = δ + A ⇒ δ = i + e − A.

Q8.2 — Minimum deviation conditions; spectrum; dispersion & power

PROBABLE LONG QUESTION

(a) Explain minimum deviation; state the two conditions satisfied at minimum deviation and why deviation is minimum there. [2]

(b) Explain dispersion of white light by a prism and why a spectrum is obtained; state why violet is deviated most. [3]

(c) A prism (A = 60°) gives minimum deviation δv = 42° for violet and δr = 38° for red. Find μ for each and the angular dispersion. [3]

FULL WORKED ANSWER

(a) As i is varied, δ (and hence e) changes; δ passes through a minimum δm. Conditions at minimum deviation: (i) the incident ray and emergent ray make equal angles with the base, i.e. i = e; (ii) the path inside the prism is symmetric, r&sub1; = r&sub2; = A/2. (b) White light contains all colours; μ depends on wavelength (λ): μviolet > μblue > … > μred (Cauchy’s law, μ ∝ 1/λ²). Angular dispersion = δv − δr = 42° − 38° = 4°. Power of dispersion = Δμ = μv − μr ≈ 0.04.

μv ≈ 1.55, μr ≈ 1.51, angular dispersion = 4°; violet bends most because μviolet > μred.

D) zero at the extremes
Concept: a = −ω²x: zero at x = 0, maximum at x = ±A (extremes).
3.9 In SHM, the velocity is maximum at:
A) the extreme positions
B) the mean position (x = 0)
C) halfway to the extreme
D) never; velocity is always zero
Concept: v = ω√(A² − x²); maximum at x = 0, zero at x = ±A.
3.10 The total mechanical energy of a body in SHM:
A) is maximum at the mean position
B) is maximum at the extremes
C) is constant throughout the motion
D) varies periodically
Concept: KE and PE exchange (KE ↔ PE) but the sum E = ½mω²A² is conserved.
3.11 A wheel of radius r rolls without slipping on a road with centre speed v. The speed of the topmost point is:
A) 0
B) v
C) 2v
D) v/2
Concept: Instantaneous rotation about the contact point: top = 2r×ω = 2v; contact point = 0.
3.12 The amplitude of a damped oscillation:
A) grows with time
B) stays constant
C) decreases exponentially
D) oscillates
Concept: A resistive force continuously drains energy, so amplitude falls as e³½×.

Unit 4 — Heat & Temperature

4.1 The key difference between heat and temperature is that:
A) temperature is a process quantity
B) heat is energy in transit; temperature is a state property of a body
C) both are measured in kelvin
D) heat is a state function
Concept: A body does not "contain heat"; it contains internal energy. Heat flows because of a temperature difference.
4.2 The temperature at which the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales read the same value is:
A) 0°
B) 100°
C) −40° (C° = F° = −40)
D) 32°
Concept: Setting &frac95;C + 32 = F with C = F gives C = −40.
4.3 Absolute zero in degrees Celsius is:
A) 0°C
B) −100°C
C) −273.15°C (= 0 K)
D) −459.67°C
Concept: 0 K = −273.15°C; the lowest possible temperature where molecular kinetic energy is minimum.
4.4 Heat is not transferred by convection in:
A) air
B) water
C) a vacuum (no material to carry the heat)
D) oil
Concept: Convection requires a fluid (liquid or gas) to move; a vacuum has none — only radiation can cross it.
4.5 Which material has the highest thermal conductivity?
A) wood
B) glass
C) air
D) copper (good metals conduct best; silver > copper > brass)
Concept: Metal ≈ 200–400 W/mK; wood ≈ 0.1–0.2; air ≈ 0.026 — metals are far better conductors.
4.6 The coefficient of volume expansion β for a solid is approximately:
A) β = α (the linear coefficient)
B) β ≈ 3α (three linear dimensions expand)
C) β ≈ α²
D) β = 0
Concept: Volume has three linear dimensions: dV/V = 3 dL/L ⇒ β ≈ 3α.
4.7 For an ideal monatomic gas, γ = Cp/Cv is:
A) 1.4 (7/5)
B) 1.33 (4/3)
C) 1.67 (5/3)
D) 1.0
Concept: γ: monatomic = 5/3 ≈ 1.67; diatomic = 7/5 = 1.4; polyatomic = 4/3 ≈ 1.33.
4.8 Mayer’s relation for an ideal gas (per mole) is:
A) Cp × Cv = R
B) Cp + Cv = R
C) Cp − Cv = R
D) Cp − Cv = 2R
Concept: The difference Cp − Cv equals the universal gas constant R = 8.314 J/mol/K (per mole).
4.9 A good thermal conductor is a:
A) good thermal insulator
B) poor thermal insulator
C) the same thing as an insulator
D) a vacuum
Concept: Conductor and insulator are opposites; a material with high κ is a bad insulator.
4.10 Heat radiation from the Sun reaches Earth through:
A) conduction in air
B) convection currents in space
C) electromagnetic radiation in a vacuum (infrared waves)
D) both A and B
Concept: Space is a vacuum — only radiation (infrared light) can carry the heat across it.

Unit 5 — Quantity of Heat

5.1 The specific heat capacity of water is approximately:
A) 418 J kg³K³
B) 41860 J kg³K³
C) 4186 J kg³K³ (= 4.186 kJ kg³K³)
D) 2090 J kg³K³
Concept: Water has one of the highest c values; ice is about half at 2090 J kg³K³.
5.2 During a phase change (ice → water at 0°C), the temperature:
A) rises steadily
B) falls
C) stays constant while latent heat is absorbed
D) oscillates
Concept: All energy goes into breaking molecular bonds (latent heat); no energy is available to raise T.
5.3 The latent heat of fusion of ice is:
A) 2.26 × 10³ J/kg
B) 33.4 kJ/kg
C) 334 kJ/kg (= 3.34 × 10³ J/kg)
D) 418 kJ/kg
Concept: Lf(ice) = 334 kJ/kg; Lv(water) = 2.26 × 10³ kJ/kg ≈ 6.8× Lf.
5.4 The mechanical equivalent of heat (J) is:
A) 4186 cal/J
B) 1 J/cal
C) 4.186 J per calorie
D) 4.186 cal/J
Concept: 1 cal = 4.186 J, so J (work per calorie) = 4.186 J/cal (Joule found this experimentally).
5.5 One calorie is defined as:
A) the energy to raise 1 kg of water by 1°C
B) the energy to raise approximately 1 g of water by 1°C (4.186 J)
C) the work to lift 1 kg by 1 m
D) the SI unit of energy
Concept: 1 cal ≈ 4.186 J; the SI unit of energy is the joule, not the calorie.
5.6 The principle of mixtures (calorimetry) assumes:
A) heat is lost to the surroundings
B) the container absorbs all the heat
C) no heat is lost — total heat lost = total heat gained (perfectly insulated)
D) both bodies reach the same temperature instantly
Concept: With an insulated calorimeter, ΣQlost = ΣQgained; final temperature is found by energy conservation.
5.7 Which requires more heat: 1 kg of water from 0→100°C, or 1 kg of ice from −20→0°C?
A) The ice from −20→0°C (smaller ΔT but low c)
B) Water from 0→100°C (large ΔT and high c = 4186)
C) Both are equal
D) Cannot be determined
Concept: Qwater = 4186×100 = 418.6 kJ; Qice = 2090×20 = 41.8 kJ; water needs ≈10× more.
5.8 Steam at 100°C burns more severely than water at 100°C because:
A) steam has a higher temperature
B) steam has a higher specific heat
C) on condensing, steam releases the large latent heat Lv (2.26 × 10³ J/kg)
D) steam is denser than water
Concept: Both are at 100°C, but condensing steam gives up 2.26 × 10³ J/kg extra — that is the burn.
5.9 The specific heat of ice compared with liquid water is:
A) larger (4186 J kg³K³)
B) smaller (≈ 2090 J kg³K³) — about half
C) the same
D) zero
Concept: cice ≈ 2090 ≈ ½ cwater; ice warms up faster than water for the same heat input.
5.10 The water equivalent of a body of mass m and specific heat cbody is:
A) m × cbody
B) m × cbody / cwater — the mass of water with the same heat capacity
C) cbody / m
D) m / cwater
Concept: Water equivalent is the "as if" mass of water that stores the same energy as the body.

Unit 6 — Reflection at Curved Mirrors

6.1 A concave mirror forms a real, inverted image when the object is placed:
A) between F and the pole
B) at the focus
C) beyond the centre of curvature C
D) at the pole
Concept: Object beyond C → real, inverted, diminished image between F and C; between F and pole → virtual, erect, magnified.
6.2 A convex mirror always forms:
A) a real, inverted image
B) a virtual, inverted image
C) a virtual, erect, diminished image (behind the mirror)
D) a magnified real image
Concept: Convex mirrors diverge rays; the eye traces them back to a smaller, upright image behind the mirror (used in car mirrors).
6.3 The focal length f of a spherical mirror (radius R) is:
A) R
B) 2R
C) R/2
D) R²2
Concept: For a paraxial spherical mirror, f = R/2 (the focus is halfway between the pole and the centre of curvature).
6.4 The mirror equation (New Cartesian convention) is:
A) 1/v + 1/u = 1/f
B) f = u + v
C) v = f/u
D) u = −v
Concept: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f; with the New Cartesian sign convention, u < 0, f < 0 for a concave mirror.
6.5 A plane mirror image is:
A) real and inverted
B) real and erect
C) virtual, erect, laterally inverted, same size (m = 1)
D) magnified and virtual
Concept: |m| = 1; the image is behind the mirror at the same distance as the object is in front.
6.6 When the object is placed exactly at C of a concave mirror, the image is:
A) at F, real and diminished
B) at C, real, inverted, same size (m = −1)
C) virtual and magnified
D) at the pole
Concept: u = −R = −2f ⇒ v = −R; the image coincides with the object position and m = −1.
6.7 A concave mirror is most suitably used as a shaving/dentist mirror because it:
A) always forms a diminished image
B) always forms a virtual inverted image
C) forms an erect, magnified virtual image when the object is between F and the pole
D) absorbs light
Concept: Object inside F → reflected rays diverge; traced back they form an erect, magnified virtual image behind the mirror.
6.8 A searchlight uses a source placed at:
A) C
B) the focus F — reflected rays emerge parallel to the axis
C) the pole
D) 2F
Concept: Light from F reflects as a parallel beam; conversely, parallel light focuses at F.
6.9 For a concave mirror, u = −30 cm and f = −10 cm. The image distance v is:
A) −30 cm
B) −15 cm (real image, between F and C)
C) +15 cm
D) +30 cm
Concept: 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/10 + 1/30 = −2/30 ⇒ v = −15 cm (real, 15 cm in front).
6.10 The magnification of a mirror is:
A) u/v
B) m = −v/u (= image height ÷ object height)
C) f/v
D) v − u
Concept: m < 0 ⇒ inverted; |m| > 1 ⇒ magnified; m = −1 ⇒ same size, inverted.

Unit 7 — Refraction at Plane Surfaces

7.1 When light passes from air into glass, it:
A) speeds up and bends away from the normal
B) slows down and bends toward the normal
C) keeps the same speed and direction
D) stops completely
Concept: Denser medium → slower light (v = c/μ) → the ray bends toward the normal.
7.2 Snell’s law of refraction states:
A) n2 sin i = n1 sin r (swapped order)
B) n1 sin i = n2 sin r
C) μ = sin r / sin i for all media
D) n = i/r
Concept: n1 sin i = n2 sin r; for air→medium, μ = sin i / sin r = n2 / n1.
7.3 The apparent depth of an object viewed through a liquid (near-normal rays) is:
A) greater than the real depth
B) less than the real depth (= real depth / μ)
C) equal to the real depth
D) infinite
Concept: μ = true depth / apparent depth > 1 ⇒ apparent depth = true/μ < true; objects look raised.
7.4 Total internal reflection occurs when:
A) light goes from rarer to denser and i > θc
B) light goes from denser to rarer and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle θc
C) light hits any surface at any angle
D) light goes from rarer to denser
Concept: Two conditions are both needed: denser→rarer, and i > θc = sin³³(1/μ).
7.5 The critical angle for glass with μ = 1.5 (glass–air) is approximately:
A) 30°
B) 45°
C) 41.8° (= sin³³(1/1.5) = sin³³(0.667))
D) 60°
Concept: θc = sin³³(1/μ); for μ = 1.5, θc = sin³³(0.667) ≈ 41.8°.
7.6 The refractive index of water is approximately:
A) 1.5
B) 1.33 (= 4/3)
C) 1.0
D) 2.0
Concept: μwater = 4/3 ≈ 1.33; μglass ≈ 1.5 — glass is denser optically.
7.7 A pencil half-immersed in water looks "bent" at the surface because:
A) the water refracts the part below the surface, making it appear raised relative to the part above
B) light from the submerged part bends away from the normal at the water–air boundary
C) it is purely a reflection effect
D) the pencil physically bends in water
Concept: Rays from the submerged tip bend away from the normal; the eye traces them straight back to a raised apparent position.
7.8 A ray bends toward the normal when it enters a medium with:
A) lower refractive index
B) higher refractive index (slower speed of light)
C) the same refractive index
D) no refractive index
Concept: Higher μ → slower light → the ray leans toward the normal on entry.
7.9 The speed of light in water (c = 3 × 10³ m/s, μ = 4/3) is:
A) 3 × 10³ m/s
B) 2.25 × 10³ m/s (= c/μ = 3×10³÷1.33)
C) 1.0 × 10³ m/s
D) 4.0 × 10³ m/s

A) 3 cm (the full thickness)
B) 1.5 cm
C) 1 cm (= t(1 − 1/n) = 3(1 − ½) = 1 cm, toward the observer)
D) 0
Concept: Apparent shift = t(1 − 1/n) = 3(1 − ½) = 1 cm; the mark appears raised by 1 cm.

Unit 8 — Refraction through a Prism

8.1 The angle of deviation δ of a prism (with refracting angle A) is:
A) i + e
B) i + e − A
C) A + i − e
D) 2A − i
Concept: From face geometry: δ = i + e − A (i = angle of incidence, e = angle of emergence).
8.2 At minimum deviation, the ray through the prism:
A) is asymmetric (i ≠ e)
B) passes through the apex
C) is symmetric: i = e and r1 = r2 = A/2 (parallel to the base)
D) is absorbed
Concept: Minimum deviation occurs at the symmetric path where i = e and the ray runs parallel to the base.
8.3 The refractive index of a prism material, expressed in terms of A and δ, is:
A) μ = sin((A + δ)/2) / sin(A/2)
B) μ = cos(A/2) / sin(δ/2)
C) μ = δ/A
D) μ = sin A / sin δ
Concept: At minimum deviation, i = (A + δ)/2 and r = A/2; applying Snell’s law gives μ = sin((A+δ)/2)/sin(A/2).
8.4 In a white-light prism spectrum, which colour is deviated the most?
A) red (longest λ, smallest μ)
B) green
C) violet (shortest λ, largest μ) — most deviation
D) all the same
Concept: μ ∝ 1/λ³ (Cauchy’s law): violet has the largest μ ⇒ greatest deviation; red the least.
8.5 Angular dispersion of a prism is defined as:
A) δ (total deviation of one colour)
B) A/2
C) δviolet − δred — the difference in deviations of two extreme colours
D) μviolet + μred
Concept: Angular dispersion = δv − δr; a prism separates colours by this difference.
8.6 The power of a prism P is:
A) P = δ/μ
B) P = A + μ
C) P = A(μ − 1) (for small A)
D) P = μA
Concept: For small A, δ = A(μ−1), so the deviation per unit angle A is P = A(μ−1).
8.7 A prism (A = 60°) gives minimum deviation δ = 38°. The refractive index is approximately:
A) 1.20
B) 1.00
C) 1.51 (= sin((60+38)/2)/sin(60/2) = sin 49°/sin 30° ≈ 0.755/0.5)
D) 2.00
Concept: μ = sin(49°)/sin(30°) = 0.7547/0.5 ≈ 1.51 — typical crown glass.
8.8 Two prisms arranged to cancel each other’s deviation but still separate colours form a:
A) total internal reflection setup
B) achromatic (dispersive) combination — used in lenses to remove colour fringing
C) a plain refracting prism
D) a flat mirror system
Concept: Crown + flint prisms in opposition: P1A1 = P2A2 (net deviation zero) but dispersion remains.
8.9 The red end of a prism spectrum is:
A) most deviated (further from the base)
B) least deviated (closest to the base)
C) not present
D) in the middle
Concept: Red has the smallest μ ⇒ the least deviation ⇒ it appears nearest the base of the spectrum.
8.10 At minimum deviation, the ray inside the prism runs:
A) parallel to the incident face
B) parallel to the base of the prism
C) straight through the apex
D) perpendicular to the base
Concept: The symmetric (minimum-deviation) path is parallel to the base — i = e and r1 = r2 = A/2.
