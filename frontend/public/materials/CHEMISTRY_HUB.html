Chemistry — Grade 6 to Grade 11

A chronological learning path from the first observations to Grade 11. Every concept builds on the previous one. If Grade 11 feels hard, start at the page where the chain broke — usually in Grades 6–8.

Core idea: Chemistry is one continuous story: matter → particles → atoms → bonds → reactions → energy. Every grade adds one layer.

📊 How the path works

Chemistry is taught in a sequence. This hub mirrors that sequence — each page assumes you know the page before it. Use the sidebar top-to-bottom.

Matter, Changes & Mixtures Grade 6

Chemistry begins with careful looking. Before formulas, you learn what matter is, how it behaves, and how to tell one kind of change from another.

Core idea: Matter is anything with mass and volume; a physical change keeps the substance, a chemical change makes a new one.

💡 What is chemistry?

Chemistry is the science of matter — what things are made of, what properties they have, and how they change into new substances.

Question chemistry asksEveryday example
What is it made of?Water, salt, air, gold, milk
What are its properties?Iron is hard; ice melts; sugar dissolves
How does it change?Paper burns; milk sours; rice cooks
How can we use it safely?Soap, medicine, fertiliser, batteries
You already do chemistry: dissolving sugar in tea, baking a cake, cleaning with soap, and seeing iron rust are all chemistry. A laboratory simply lets us observe these changes more carefully.

🔍 Matter and its properties

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space (volume). A book, water, air, and your body are matter. Light, heat, and sound are forms of energy — they are not matter.

PropertyWhat it meansExample
MassAmount of matter in an objectA stone has more mass than a leaf
VolumeSpace the object takes upWater fills a bottle
ShapeOutline of the objectA cube has a fixed shape
HardnessHow easily it is scratchedDiamond is harder than chalk
SolubilityAbility to dissolve in a liquidSalt dissolves in water; sand does not
DensityMass per unit volumeOil floats on water because it is less dense
Property = something you can observe or measure. Colour, smell, melting point, and conductivity are also properties. Scientists use properties to identify substances.

🌡 The three common states of matter

StateParticlesShapeVolumeCompressible?Example

Key idea: The same substance can exist in all three states. Ice, liquid water, and steam are all H₂O — only the energy and spacing of the particles are different.

🔄 Physical and chemical changes

TypeWhat happensNew substance?Examples
Physical changeSize, shape, or state changes; the substance is still the sameNoIce melting, paper tearing, salt dissolving
Chemical changeAtoms rearrange and a new substance formsYesPaper burning, iron rusting, milk souring, cooking an egg

Signs of a chemical change: gas bubbles, a new solid (precipitate), a colour change that makes a new substance, heat or light released, or a new smell.

Careful: A colour change alone is not proof. Mixing blue and yellow paint makes green, but no new substance forms. Ask: has a new substance been made?
Law of conservation of mass: In a chemical change, atoms are only rearranged — they are not created or destroyed. So the total mass before and after a reaction is the same (in a closed container).

🧪 Pure substances, mixtures, and solutions

TypeMeaningExample
Pure substanceOnly one kind of substance throughoutPure water, pure gold, oxygen
MixtureTwo or more substances physically mixed; each keeps its own propertiesAir, salt water, muddy water, salad

Heterogeneous mixtureComponents not evenly mixed; parts can be seenSand and water, oil and water, salad

In a solution, the substance that dissolves is the solute, and the liquid that does the dissolving is the solvent. In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.

Remember: A mixture can usually be separated by physical methods. A compound can only be broken into its elements by a chemical change.

⚠ Laboratory safety rules

  • Wear goggles when heating or using chemicals; tie back long hair.
  • Never taste a chemical. Smell only by wafting vapour toward your nose.
  • Do not point the mouth of a heated test tube at yourself or anyone else.
  • Heat test tubes gently and keep moving them; never heat a closed container.
  • When diluting a strong acid, add acid to water, slowly — never add water to concentrated acid.
  • Report spills immediately; wash your hands after practical work.
  • Use the smallest amount needed, and dispose of waste as instructed.
Acid in the eye: rinse with plenty of clean water for at least 15 minutes and get adult/medical help. Do not rub the eye.

🎯 Grade 6 review — idea first, then practice

The big picture: Chemistry begins with observation: classify matter, decide whether a change is physical or chemical, and separate mixtures using differences in properties.

Analogy that sticks: Think of a mixture as a bowl of different coloured beads. You can sort them by hand, size, or magnetism — the beads themselves never change. A compound is more like a single painted bead: you cannot separate the colours without breaking the bead.
Memory hook: Physical change = same substance, new look. Chemical change = new substance, new properties.

Particles, States & Solutions Grade 7

In Grade 7 you stop only describing matter and start explaining it: everything is made of tiny particles that are always moving.

Core idea: Matter is made of moving particles — temperature is a measure of their average motion.

⚛ The particle model of matter

  • All matter is made of extremely small particles (atoms, molecules, or ions).
  • The particles are always moving; higher temperature means faster average motion.
  • There are spaces between particles. Gases have the largest spaces.
  • Particles attract each other, but the attraction is strongest when they are close together.
Evidence you can see: A drop of ink spreads through water without stirring. Perfume reaches you across a room. A gas can be squeezed into a smaller volume. These make sense only if matter is made of moving particles with spaces between them.

🌡 Explaining the states with particles

StateParticle arrangement and motionForces between particlesResult
SolidClosely packed in fixed positions; vibrateVery strongFixed shape and volume; cannot be compressed easily
LiquidClose together but can slide past each otherStrong, but weaker than in a solidFixed volume; takes the shape of the container
GasFar apart; move rapidly in all directionsVery weakFills the container; easily compressed
Why gases compress: Most of a gas is empty space between particles. Squeezing pushes the particles closer together. In a solid, the particles are already touching, so it barely compresses.

🔄 Changes of state and energy

ChangeDirectionHeatExample

Why sweating cools you: Evaporation absorbs heat from your skin. The fastest particles escape, leaving slower (cooler) particles behind. Moving air speeds evaporation, so you feel cooler on a windy day.

💨 Diffusion — particles spreading out

Diffusion is the spreading of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration because of their random motion.

ExampleWhat diffuses
Smell of perfume crossing a roomPerfume vapour through air
Ink colouring a whole glass of waterInk particles through water
Tea colour spreading in hot waterTea particles through water
Temperature effect: Diffusion is faster in hot water than cold water because particles move faster and collide more often.

🧪 Solutions and concentration

TermMeaningExample
SoluteThe substance that dissolvesSalt
SolventThe substance that does the dissolvingWater
SolutionA homogeneous mixture of solute and solventSalt water
DiluteLittle solute in a given amount of solutionA weak sugar solution
ConcentratedA lot of solute in a given amount of solutionStrong sugar syrup

Factors affecting dissolving: higher temperature, stirring, and smaller particle size make dissolving faster. Solubility itself usually increases with temperature for solids in water.

Concentration in words: 5 g of salt in 100 mL of water is more concentrated than 1 g in 100 mL. The amount of solute compared with the amount of solution is what matters.

Elements, Symbols, Formulae & Separation Grade 7

Chemists need a short, exact language. Symbols name elements; formulas name compounds; separation methods let us obtain pure substances.

Core idea: A chemical formula is a ratio, not a recipe: subscripts count atoms, coefficients count whole particles.

🔬 Elements, compounds, and mixtures

TypeDefinitionExample
ElementA pure substance made of only one kind of atomOxygen (O), iron (Fe), gold (Au)
CompoundA pure substance made of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratioWater (H₂O), salt (NaCl), carbon dioxide (CO₂)
MixtureTwo or more substances physically mixed; ratio can varyAir, salt water, soil
Atom vs molecule: An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction. A molecule is a group of atoms bonded together. O₂ is a molecule made of two oxygen atoms; H₂O is a molecule made of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

⚖ Valency and the criss-cross rule

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom or ion — how many bonds it can form or how many electrons it gains, loses, or shares.

Criss-cross: swap the numerical valencies, then simplify. Example: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → Al₂O₃. Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ → MgCl₂. Ca²⁺ and OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂.

Use brackets when a polyatomic ion is needed more than once: Ca(OH)₂ means one Ca and two OH groups, not one O and two H.

Core idea: Acids and bases are opposites that neutralise each other; pH measures which side wins.

⚖ The pH scale and neutralisation

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14. pH 7 is neutral. Lower pH = more acidic; higher pH = more basic (alkaline).

Atoms, Ions & Bonding Grade 8

Grade 8 opens the atom. Once you know how electrons are arranged, you can explain ions, valency, formulas, and why substances have the properties they do.

Core idea: Atoms are neutral when protons equal electrons; losing or gaining electrons turns them into ions.

⚛ Inside the atom

ParticleLocationChargeRelative mass

The nucleus is tiny but contains almost all the mass. In a neutral atom, number of protons = number of electrons.

Models in one line: Dalton imagined solid spheres → Thomson proposed electrons embedded in positive material → Rutherford discovered the nucleus → Bohr placed electrons in fixed shells. Each model improved on the last.

Ion charge: For a neutral atom, protons = electrons. Lose 1 electron → +1 charge. Gain 2 electrons → −2 charge. The nucleus does not change in ordinary chemical reactions.

Electrons occupy shells from the inside out. For the first 20 elements, the pattern is 2, 8, 8 (the first shell holds 2, the next two hold 8 each).

Why shells matter: The outer-shell electrons (valence electrons) are the ones involved in bonding. Atoms react to reach a stable outer shell — usually 8 electrons (the octet rule), or 2 for hydrogen and helium.

🔬 Carbon — the element of life

Gases & Their Tests Grade 9

Gases are invisible, but their behaviour is predictable. Learn how to prepare, collect, test, and use the gases that appear again and again in the laboratory.

Core idea: Identify a gas by its test; choose the collection method from its density and solubility.

🌤 Properties of gases

  • Gases have no fixed shape or volume; they fill their container.
  • They are highly compressible because there is a lot of empty space between particles.
  • They diffuse quickly and mix completely with other gases.
  • They have low density compared with solids and liquids.
  • Gas pressure comes from particles colliding with the walls of the container.

Hydrocarbons & Everyday Chemistry Grade 10

Carbon compounds are fuels, plastics, medicines, and the molecules of life. Grade 10 gives you the first map of organic chemistry before Grade 11 adds functional groups and mechanisms.

Core idea: Carbon forms four bonds, so it can build chains, rings, and families with predictable reactions.

💡 Why carbon is special

  • Carbon has 4 valence electrons, so it forms four covalent bonds.
  • It forms strong bonds with itself, creating chains and rings — catenation.
  • It bonds strongly to H, O, N, S, and halogens, giving millions of compounds.
  • Its allotropes include diamond (3D network, very hard), graphite (layers, soft, conducts), and fullerene (cage-like).
Organic vs inorganic: Organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds (except simple oxides, carbonates, and bicarbonates, which are usually treated as inorganic). Organic compounds are held together mainly by covalent bonds.

🔎 Stoichiometry — using balanced equations

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the mole ratio of reactants and products.

ReactionMole ratio
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O2 : 1 : 2 (H₂ : O₂ : H₂O)
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃1 : 3 : 2
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O1 : 2 : 1 : 2
Worked example: How many moles of H₂O form from 4 mol H₂? From 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, ratio H₂:H₂O = 2:2 = 1:1 → 4 mol H₂ gives 4 mol H₂O. If instead you had 3 mol O₂, O₂:H₂O = 1:2 → 6 mol H₂O (but H₂ limits it — next card).

🔑 Limiting reactant — the one that runs out

ConceptExplanation
Limiting reactantThe reactant that is used up first — it determines how much product forms.
Excess reactantLeft over after the reaction stops.

How to find it: Convert each reactant to moles, then divide by its coefficient in the equation. The smallest result is the limiting reactant.

Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. If you have 4 mol H₂ and 3 mol O₂: H₂ → 4÷2 = 2; O₂ → 3÷1 = 3. H₂ gives the smaller number, so H₂ is limiting. Product = 4 mol H₂O, and 1 mol O₂ remains.

📈 Concentration — molarity

TermFormulaUnits
Molarity (M)moles of solute ÷ litres of solutionmol/L
DilutionM₁V₁ = M₂V₂—
Mass percent(mass solute ÷ mass solution) × 100%
Example: To make 0.5 M NaCl solution: 0.5 mol (≈29.2 g of NaCl, molar mass 58.4) dissolved in water and made up to 1 L.

📖 Laws that support stoichiometry

  • Law of conservation of mass: mass of reactants = mass of products (why equations balance).
  • Law of definite proportions: a compound always has the same elements in the same ratio by mass (water is always 11.1% H).
  • Law of multiple proportions: when two elements form several compounds, the masses of one combining with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios (CO vs CO₂).

States of Matter & Gas Laws Grade 11

Grade 11 deepens the states you met in Grade 9, with a strong focus on gases — the most mathematically tractable state.

Core idea: Gas laws link pressure, volume, temperature, and moles; kinetic theory explains why.

🌤 Kinetic molecular theory (KMT)

  • Gas particles are tiny, widely separated spheres with negligible volume.
  • They are in constant, random motion in straight lines until they collide.
  • Collisions are elastic (no kinetic energy lost).
  • No attractive/repulsive forces between particles.
  • Average kinetic energy ∝ absolute temperature (Kelvin).
Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure (particles close together → forces matter) and low temperature (slow particles → forces dominate). Ideal gas assumption is best at low P, high T.

📈 The gas laws

LawStatementFormula

📈 Filling order — Aufbau principle

Electrons fill orbitals from lowest energy upward: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d ...

  • Aufbau: fill lowest energy first.
  • Pauli exclusion: each orbital holds max 2 electrons with opposite spins.
  • Hund's rule: fill orbitals singly before pairing (max unpaired spins).
Order example — Iron (Fe, Z=26): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶. Note 4s fills before 3d.

Thermochemistry & Thermodynamics Grade 11

Every reaction either releases or absorbs energy. Thermochemistry is the quantitative study of that heat — with enthalpy at its core.

Core idea: Enthalpy, entropy, and Gibbs energy together decide whether a reaction can happen.

🔑 Exothermic vs endothermic

Terminology: ΔH = enthalpy change. Exothermic products have lower energy than reactants; endothermic products have higher energy.

📈 Hess's law — add up the steps

Hess's law: The total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same regardless of the route taken (since H is a state function).

🔨 The three laws (in 30 seconds)

  • 1st law: Energy is conserved — ΔU = q + w.
  • 2nd law: Total entropy of the universe increases in spontaneous processes.
  • 3rd law: At absolute zero, a perfect crystal has S = 0.

Chemical Equilibrium Grade 11

Many reactions do not go to completion — they settle into a dynamic balance between forward and reverse reactions called equilibrium.

Core idea: Equilibrium is dynamic: forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates.

🔌 Dynamic equilibrium

  • Forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates.
  • Concentrations of reactants and products stay constant (not equal).
  • It is dynamic — molecules keep reacting, just at cancelling rates.
  • Only in closed systems (no material escapes).
Example: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. At equilibrium, all three gases are present in fixed amounts. The double arrow ⇌ means "in equilibrium".

📈 The equilibrium constant Kc

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD:

Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ (square brackets = molar concentrations).

If Kc is...Meaning
Very large (> 10³)Mostly products — reaction goes almost to completion.
~1Significant amounts of both reactant and product.
Very small (< 10⁻³)Mostly reactants — hardly any product.
Rules: Kc depends only on temperature (not on starting amounts or pressure). Solids and pure liquids don't appear in K expressions.

💡 Le Chatelier's principle — the Goldilocks rule

"If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to counteract the disturbance."

DisturbanceShift
Add reactantShifts right (consumes added reactant; makes more product).
Remove productShifts right (tries to replace product).
Increase pressureShifts toward fewer gas moles.
Increase temperatureShifts in the endothermic direction.
Add a catalystNo shift — just reaches equilibrium faster.
Worked example: In N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (4 gas moles left, 2 right): raising pressure shifts right (towards fewer moles) → more ammonia. This is the basis of the Haber process.

Redox & Electrochemistry Grade 11

Oxidation–reduction ("redox") reactions are the most common reactions in chemistry — from rusting to batteries to metabolism.

Core idea: Redox is electron transfer; oxidation numbers are the bookkeeping.

🔎 Oxidation & reduction

TermClassic definition (O₂)Modern (electron)Oxidation number change
OxidationGain of oxygenLoss of electronsIncreases
ReductionLoss of oxygenGain of electronsDecreases
Memory aid: "OIL RIG" — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). Also "LEO says GER": Lose Electrons Oxidation; Gain Electrons Reduction.

🔗 Oxidation numbers — the bookkeeping

Oxidation number = the charge an atom would have if electrons were assigned to the more electronegative atom.

  • Elements (free state): 0 (e.g., O₂, Fe, N₂).
  • Monatomic ions: = charge (Na⁺ = +1, Cl⁻ = −1).
  • O: −2 in compounds (except peroxides −1, OF₂ +2).
  • H: +1 with nonmetals, −1 with metals (NaH).
  • Sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral compound = 0; in a polyatomic ion = charge.
  • Group 1 = +1, Group 2 = +2, halogen = −1 (mostly).
Example: In H₂SO₄: 2(+1) + S + 4(−2) = 0 → S = +6.

📈 Balancing redox (half-reaction method)

For Fe²⁺ + Cr₂O₇²⁻ → Fe³⁺ + Cr³⁺ (acidic):

  1. Split into two half-reactions (oxidation & reduction).
  2. Balance atoms other than O and H.
  3. Balance O by adding H₂O; balance H by adding H⁺ (acidic) or OH⁻ (basic).
  4. Balance charge by adding electrons.
  5. Equalise electron counts and add the halves.
Fast tip: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O. Multiply the iron half by 6, then add.

Organic Chemistry Grade 11

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon. Carbon forms chains, rings, and millions of compounds — the basis of life, fuels, plastics, and drugs.

Core idea: Functional groups decide reactivity; isomers share a formula but differ in structure.

💡 Why carbon is special

  • Carbon has 4 valence electrons → always makes 4 bonds (tetravalence).
  • It forms strong C–C bonds, so chains/rings are stable.
  • It can bond to itself and to H, O, N, halogens, etc.

Hydrocarbons: compounds of only C and H. Everything else in organic builds on them.

📚 Five study habits that make chemistry stick

  1. Explain out loud. If you cannot explain a card to a friend in simple words, you do not know it yet.
  2. Draw the invisible. Sketch particles, atoms, ions, and electron shells. A drawing exposes gaps that reading hides.
  3. Balance three equations every day. It is a skill, like multiplication — short daily practice beats one long session.
  4. Track units. Write g, mol, L, M every time. Many Grade 11 mistakes are unit mistakes, not chemistry mistakes.
  5. Connect backwards. For every new Grade 11 topic, ask: which Grade 6–10 idea is this built on? Use the table above.
Final reminder: Chemistry is cumulative. You do not need to memorise every page — you need the chain of ideas to stay unbroken. Start where the chain broke, repair it, and Grade 11 will stop feeling like a different language.
