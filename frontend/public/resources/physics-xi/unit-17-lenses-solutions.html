Unit 17 — Lenses

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  3 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Six hours, one formula, one sign convention and one unit (the dioptre). The lens formula 1/f = 1/v − 1/u with m = v/u answers almost every question; the lens maker's formula and the power add the two remaining marks.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define power of a lens and its unit; state the lens formula; why is a convex lens called converging; what is the focal length of a lens in water.
  • 4 marks — lens-formula numerical; power and combination of two lenses; lens maker's formula with given radii.
  • 5–6 marks — derive the lens formula or the lens maker's formula; tabulate image formation by a convex lens for all object positions; explain why a lens behaves differently in water.

Sign convention (Cartesian, used throughout): distances are measured from the optical centre, positive in the direction of the incident light (to the right) and negative against it. So u is negative for a real object on the left, f is positive for a convex (converging) lens and negative for a concave lens, and a positive v means a real image on the far side. m = v/u, positive m = erect (virtual) image.

Exercise 17.1 — Terms and the two types of lens

Q1. Define the optical centre, principal axis, principal focus and focal length of a lens.

TermDefinition
Optical centre (O)The central point of the lens through which a ray passes undeviated
Principal axisThe line through the optical centre perpendicular to the lens surfaces
Principal focus (F)The point on the axis where rays parallel to the axis converge (convex) or appear to diverge from (concave) after refraction
Focal length (f)The distance OF
ApertureThe effective diameter of the exposed part of the lens

O is the undeviating centre, F the point where a parallel beam is brought to a focus, and f = OF. Every lens has two foci, one on each side, equidistant from O.

Q2. Why is a convex lens called converging and a concave lens diverging?

A convex lens is thicker in the middle, so it slows the central part of a wavefront more than the edges and bends rays toward the axis, making a parallel beam converge to a real focus. A concave lens is thinner in the middle; it slows the edges more, so rays bend away from the axis and the beam diverges, appearing to come from a virtual focus on the incident side.

Convex: thicker in the middle ⇒ converging, real focus, positive f. Concave: thinner in the middle ⇒ diverging, virtual focus, negative f.

Q3. State the rules for drawing ray diagrams with a thin lens.

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes, after refraction, through the principal focus (convex) or appears to come from it (concave).
  2. A ray through the optical centre goes straight on undeviated.
  3. A ray through the principal focus (or directed at it) emerges parallel to the axis.

Any two of the three locate the image; the third checks it. The lens plane is drawn as a single line for a thin lens, which is why all heights are measured from the axis at the lens.

Parallel → through F; through O → undeviated; through F → parallel. Two rays suffice.

Q4. Tabulate the image formed by a convex lens for all object positions.

Moving the object inward from infinity moves the image outward and enlarges it; once the object is inside F the image turns virtual, erect and magnified.

Q5. Describe the image formed by a concave lens for any object position.

Whatever the object distance, a concave lens forms a virtual, erect and diminished image, located between the lens and the focus on the same side as the object. As the object moves away the image moves toward the focus and becomes smaller; as the object approaches the lens the image also approaches the optical centre and its size approaches the object's.

Always virtual, erect, diminished, on the object side between O and F — the mirror image of the convex lens behaviour.

Exercise 17.2 — The lens formula and magnification

Q6. State the lens formula, the magnification formula and the sign convention.

Lens formula: 1/f = 1/v − 1/u Magnification: m = v/u = h'/h Sign convention (Cartesian): • distances measured from the optical centre • positive in the direction of the incident light (to the right) • u negative for a real object on the left • f positive for convex, negative for concave • v positive ⇒ real image on the far side; v negative ⇒ virtual image on the object side • h' positive ⇒ erect, negative ⇒ inverted

1/f = 1/v − 1/u with m = v/u; convex f > 0, concave f < 0, real object u < 0.

Q7. Derive the lens formula for a thin convex lens.

An object AB at distance u forms an image A'B' at distance v. Using the ray through the optical centre (undeviated) and the ray parallel to the axis (through F):

Triangles ABO and A'B'O are similar: A'B'/AB = v/u … (1) Triangles (from the parallel ray) give A'B'/AB = (v − f)/f … (2) Equating (1) and (2): v/u = (v − f)/f ⇒ vf = uv − uf Divide by uvf: 1/u − 1/v = −1/f With the Cartesian signs (u negative), this becomes 1/f = 1/v − 1/u

1/f = 1/v − 1/u, obtained by equating the two similar-triangle expressions for the magnification.

Q8. An object is placed at 2F of a convex lens. Find the image position, magnification and nature.

u = −2f 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/f − 1/(2f) = 1/(2f) v = +2f m = v/u = 2f/(−2f) = −1

Image at 2F on the far side, real, inverted and the same size — the only position that gives m = −1.

Exercise 17.3 — Convex lens numericals

Q9. An object 4 cm tall is placed 30 cm from a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the image position, size and nature.

u = −30 cm, f = +10 cm 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/10 − 1/30 = (3 − 1)/30 = 2/30 v = +15 cm m = v/u = 15/(−30) = −0.5 h' = m h = −0.5 × 4 = −2 cm

Image 15 cm beyond the lens, real, inverted, 2 cm tall (diminished) — object beyond 2F, image between F and 2F, as the table predicts.

Q10. An object is placed 5 cm from a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the image and explain its nature.

u = −5 cm, f = +10 cm 1/v = 1/10 − 1/5 = (1 − 2)/10 = −1/10 v = −10 cm m = v/u = (−10)/(−5) = +2

v is negative, so the image lies on the same side as the object: the emergent rays diverge and only appear to meet, so the image is virtual. The positive m confirms it is erect, and |m| = 2 means twice the size. This is the magnifying-glass arrangement.

Virtual, erect, magnified ×2 image 10 cm from the lens on the object side (object inside F).

Q11. A convex lens forms a real image three times the size of the object at 45 cm from the lens. Find the focal length and the object distance.

Real inverted image ⇒ m = −3 = v/u ⇒ v = −3u Given v = +45 cm ⇒ u = −15 cm 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = 1/45 − 1/(−15) = 1/45 + 1/15 = (1 + 3)/45 = 4/45 f = 45/4 = 11.25 cm

f = 11.25 cm; object 15 cm from the lens (between F and 2F, which must give a magnified real image)

Q12. Where must an object be placed before a convex lens of focal length 20 cm to obtain a real image of the same size? What if it must be virtual and magnified?

Real, same size: m = −1 ⇒ v = −u ⇒ u = −2f = −40 cm (at 2F, 40 cm from the lens) Virtual, magnified: object must be inside F, u between 0 and −20 cm. For example u = −10 cm: 1/v = 1/20 + 1/10 = 3/20 ⇒ v = −6.67 cm, m = +1.5

At 40 cm (2F) for a real image of the same size; anywhere inside 20 cm (F) for a virtual magnified image.

Q13. A magnifying glass of focal length 10 cm is used with the image at the near point (25 cm). Find the magnification.

Image virtual at 25 cm on the object side: v = −25 cm, f = +10 cm 1/u = 1/v − 1/f = −1/25 − 1/10 = (−2 − 5)/50 = −7/50 u = −7.14 cm m = v/u = (−25)/(−7.14) = +3.5

(Angular magnification with the image at the near point: M = 1 + D/f = 1 + 25/10 = 3.5 ✓)

M = 3.5 — the image is 3.5 times the object as seen by the eye at the near point.

Exercise 17.4 — Concave lens numericals

Q14. An object is placed 30 cm from a concave lens of focal length 15 cm. Find the image position, magnification and nature.

u = −30 cm, f = −15 cm 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = −1/15 − 1/30 = (−2 − 1)/30 = −3/30 v = −10 cm m = v/u = (−10)/(−30) = +0.33

Image 10 cm from the lens on the object side, virtual, erect and one-third the size — always between O and F for a concave lens.

Q15. A concave lens of focal length 20 cm has its image formed 12 cm from the lens. Find the object distance.

f = −20 cm, v = −12 cm (virtual image on the object side) 1/u = 1/v − 1/f = −1/12 − (−1/20) = −1/12 + 1/20 = (−5 + 3)/60 = −2/60 u = −30 cm m = v/u = (−12)/(−30) = +0.4

Object 30 cm from the lens; image virtual, erect and 0.4 times the size.

Q16. Why can a concave lens never form a real image of a real object?

Because it is thinner at the centre, so it slows the marginal rays less than the central ones and bends them away from the axis. The emergent rays always diverge, so they never actually meet on the far side; they can meet only when produced backward, giving a virtual image on the object side. A real image needs actual convergence of light, which a diverging lens cannot produce.

Its emergent rays always diverge; the image is always virtual, erect and diminished.

Exercise 17.5 — Power of a lens

Q17. Define the power of a lens, give its unit and its sign convention.

The power of a lens is the reciprocal of its focal length expressed in metres:

P = 1/f (f in metres), unit: dioptre (D) = m⁻¹

A converging (convex) lens has positive power; a diverging (concave) lens has negative power. Power measures the ability of the lens to bend light: the shorter the focal length, the greater the power. The power of a lens of focal length 20 cm is +5 D; of a concave lens of focal length 50 cm, −2 D.

P = 1/f (m⁻¹), in dioptres; positive for convex, negative for concave; shorter f means larger power.

Q18. Find the power of (a) a convex lens of focal length 25 cm and (b) a concave lens of focal length 40 cm.

(a) P = 1/0.25 = +4.0 D (b) P = 1/(−0.40) = −2.5 D

+4.0 D (converging spectacles for long sight) and −2.5 D (diverging spectacles for short sight).

Q19. A lens has a power of −5 D. What is its focal length and type? Where must an object be placed to give a virtual image at 20 cm?

f = 1/P = 1/(−5) = −0.20 m = −20 cm (concave, diverging) v = −20 cm, f = −20 cm 1/u = 1/v − 1/f = −1/20 + 1/20 = 0 ⇒ u = −∞

A parallel beam (object at infinity) is brought to a virtual focus at 20 cm. For a nearer object the virtual image lies closer than 20 cm, always between O and F.

f = −20 cm, concave; an object at infinity gives the virtual image at 20 cm — the focus itself.

Exercise 17.6 — Combination of thin lenses in contact

Q20. Derive the relation for the focal length of two thin lenses in contact, and the corresponding power relation.

For the first lens the image at v₁ (real or virtual) acts as the object for the second: 1/f₁ = 1/v₁ − 1/u 1/f₂ = 1/v − 1/v₁ (v₁ is the object distance for lens 2) Adding: 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ = 1/v − 1/u = 1/F ⇒ 1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂, and since P = 1/f: P = P₁ + P₂

The result extends to any number of thin lenses in contact: 1/F = Σ(1/f_i). For lenses separated by a distance d the relation is modified to 1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − d/(f₁f₂).

1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ and P = P₁ + P₂ for lenses in contact; powers add algebraically.

Q21. A convex lens of focal length 10 cm is placed in contact with a concave lens of focal length 20 cm. Find the focal length and power of the combination.

1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ = 1/10 + 1/(−20) = (2 − 1)/20 = 1/20 F = +20 cm P = 1/0.20 = +5 D (check: P₁ = +10 D, P₂ = −5 D, sum = +5 D ✓)

F = +20 cm, converging, P = +5 D — the combination is still converging because the convex lens is the stronger one.

Q22. Two thin lenses of powers +4 D and −6 D are in contact. Find the focal length of the combination and its nature.

P = P₁ + P₂ = 4 − 6 = −2 D F = 1/P = 1/(−2) = −0.5 m = −50 cm

F = −50 cm; the combination is diverging because the concave lens is the stronger.

Q23. Why do the powers add algebraically when lenses are in contact?

Because the deviation produced by each lens is proportional to its power, and two thin lenses in contact act on the same ray in succession, so their deviations add. Since 1/f is proportional to that deviation, the reciprocals (the powers) add. A concave lens, with negative power, therefore subtracts its deviation — which is why a weak concave lens can neutralise part of a convex lens.

Deviations add for a ray crossing both lenses, and deviation ∝ 1/f = P, so P = P₁ + P₂.

Exercise 17.7 — The lens maker's formula

Q24. State the lens maker's formula and explain each symbol.

1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

Here n is the refractive index of the lens material relative to the surrounding medium, R₁ is the radius of curvature of the first surface (positive if its centre lies on the far side of the lens) and R₂ that of the second surface. The formula shows that the focal length of a lens is fixed by three things only: the material (n), the two curvatures and the surrounding medium.

1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂); f depends on n, R₁ and R₂, not on the object.

Q25. A biconvex lens has radii of curvature 10 cm and 15 cm and n = 1.5. Find its focal length and power.

By convention R₁ = +10 cm (centre to the right) and R₂ = −15 cm (centre to the left); 1/f = (1.5 − 1)(1/10 − 1/(−15)) = 0.5(1/10 + 1/15) = 0.5(3/30 + 2/30) = 0.5 × 5/30 = 0.0833 f = +12 cm P = 1/0.12 = +8.3 D

f = +12 cm, P ≈ +8.3 D

Q26. Why does a lens have a longer focal length in water than in air?

Because the lens maker's formula uses n relative to the surrounding medium. In air, n_rel = n_glass/1 = 1.5; in water, n_rel = 1.5/(4/3) = 1.125. The factor (n − 1) falls from 0.5 to 0.125, a factor of 4, so 1/f falls by the same factor and f becomes four times longer. A convex lens of focal length 20 cm in air has a focal length of about 80 cm in water.

The bending arises from the difference in refractive index between lens and surroundings; water reduces (n − 1) and hence lengthens f in proportion to 1/(n_rel − 1).

Q27. Can a lens of glass (n = 1.5) become diverging in a medium of higher refractive index? Explain.

Yes. If the surrounding medium has n greater than 1.5 — for example carbon disulphide, n = 1.63 — then the relative refractive index is less than 1. The lens, which is thicker at the centre, then slows its central rays less than its edges, so it diverges: its focal length changes sign. A biconvex glass lens in such a liquid behaves as a diverging lens.

If n_surround > n_lens the sign of f reverses, so a biconvex lens acts as a diverging lens.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. State the lens formula and the magnification formula.

1/f = 1/v − 1/u and m = v/u = h'/h, with distances measured from the optical centre, positive in the direction of the incident light.

C2. Define the power of a lens and give its SI unit.

P = 1/f with f in metres; the SI unit is the dioptre (D = m⁻¹). Positive for a converging lens, negative for a diverging lens; it measures the bending ability of the lens.

C3. Why is a convex lens called a converging lens?

Because it is thicker at the centre, so a parallel beam is brought to an actual focus on the far side — the emergent rays converge. It can therefore form a real image.

C4. What is the focal length of a plane glass slab treated as a lens?

Infinite. A slab has equal and opposite curvatures, so R₁ = −R₂ and 1/R₁ − 1/R₂ = 0, giving 1/f = 0 and f = ∞, P = 0. It deviates nothing but only shifts the ray laterally.

C5. Where must an object be placed to get a real image of the same size from a convex lens?

At 2F, i.e. twice the focal length in front of the lens. Then v = 2f and m = −1, so the image is real, inverted, the same size and at 2F on the other side.

C6. Why does a concave lens always give a virtual image?

Because its emergent rays always diverge: they never converge to a real point, so they can only appear to meet when produced backward, on the object side. The image is virtual, erect and diminished for every real object.

C7. What is the relation for two thin lenses in contact?

1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂, equivalently P = P₁ + P₂. The powers add algebraically, so a concave lens reduces the converging power of a convex one.

C8. Why does a lens in water have a longer focal length?

Because the lens maker's formula uses the refractive index relative to the surroundings; water raises the surrounding index toward that of glass, reducing (n_rel − 1). With less relative slowing between centre and edge, the lens bends light less, so f increases.

C9. What is the lens maker's formula? Why is it called so?

1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂). It is called the lens maker's formula because it tells a manufacturer what curvatures and what glass to use to produce a lens of a required focal length, without any object or image being involved.

C10. Distinguish between a real and a virtual image formed by a lens.

A real image is formed by the actual convergence of refracted rays, lies on the far side of the lens (positive v), can be caught on a screen and is inverted (m negative). A virtual image is formed where refracted rays only appear to meet, lies on the object side (negative v), cannot be caught on a screen and is erect (m positive).

C11. Can a convex lens form a virtual image? Under what condition?

Yes — when the object is placed between the lens and its principal focus (u between 0 and f). The rays then leave the lens diverging and the image is virtual, erect and magnified, as in a magnifying glass.

C12. Why is the focal length of a lens independent of the object distance?

Because f is a property of the lens itself — fixed by its material and curvatures through the lens maker's formula. The object distance changes where the image forms (through 1/f = 1/v − 1/u) but not the focal length.

C13. What is meant by the aperture of a lens, and why is it kept small in precision instruments?

The effective diameter of the lens that admits light. A large aperture collects more light but increases spherical and chromatic aberration and reduces the depth of focus; precision instruments therefore stop the lens down to a small aperture to obtain a sharper image.

C14. Why does a magnifying glass need a short focal length?

Because its angular magnification is M ≈ D/f (image at infinity) or 1 + D/f (image at 25 cm), where D = 25 cm is the near point. A small f therefore gives a large M; a lens of f = 5 cm gives about 6×, while f = 25 cm gives only about 2×.

C15. What is the difference between the focal length of a lens and its power?

They are reciprocals: P = 1/f. Focal length is a length in metres and describes where the image of an infinite object forms; power is in dioptres and describes how strongly the lens bends light, with the sign telling whether the lens converges or diverges.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] An object 3 cm high is placed 20 cm from a convex lens of focal length 12 cm. Find the image position, size and nature, and state which part of the lens table the case belongs to.

u = −20 cm, f = +12 cm 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/12 − 1/20 = (5 − 3)/60 = 2/60 v = +30 cm m = v/u = 30/(−20) = −1.5 h' = m h = −1.5 × 3 = −4.5 cm

Here 2f = 24 cm, so the object lies between F (12 cm) and 2F (24 cm). The table predicts an image beyond 2F, real, inverted and magnified — and indeed v = 30 cm > 24 cm with |m| = 1.5.

Image 30 cm beyond the lens, real, inverted, 4.5 cm tall; object between F and 2F → image beyond 2F, magnified.

L2. [5] Derive the lens maker's formula from refraction at the two spherical surfaces.

For refraction at a single spherical surface of radius R separating media of index n₁ and n₂, the paraxial relation is

n₂/v − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R

Applying this to the first surface (air → glass, radius R₁), the image it would form acts as the object for the second surface (glass → air, radius R₂). Adding the two relations and using the thin-lens approximation (negligible thickness, so the two surface vertices coincide) gives

1/v − 1/u = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

For an object at infinity (u = −∞, v = f) the left side is 1/f, giving the lens maker's formula.

1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), obtained by adding the single-surface refraction relations for the two faces of a thin lens.

L3. [5] A convex lens of focal length 15 cm and a concave lens of focal length 25 cm are placed in contact. Find the focal length and power of the combination, and the image position of an object 50 cm away.

1/F = 1/15 + 1/(−25) = (5 − 3)/75 = 2/75 F = +37.5 cm; P = 1/0.375 = +2.67 D For the object at 50 cm (through the combination): 1/v = 1/F + 1/u = 1/37.5 − 1/50 = (4 − 3)/150 = 1/150 v = +150 cm

F = 37.5 cm, P ≈ +2.7 D; the image of an object 50 cm away forms 150 cm beyond the combination, real and inverted.

L4. [4] Two thin lenses of focal lengths +10 cm and −10 cm are in contact. What is the focal length and power of the combination? What does the result mean physically?

1/F = 1/10 + 1/(−10) = 0 ⇒ F = ∞, P = 0 D

The two lenses have equal and opposite powers, so their deviations cancel exactly: a parallel beam emerges parallel. The combination has no net converging or diverging action — it behaves like a plane glass plate (a "zero-power" combination), though it still produces a small lateral shift and the two lenses together show chromatic effects.

F = ∞, P = 0: the combination is afocal — no net deviation of a parallel beam.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 17

1. Using the mirror formula. The lens formula is 1/f = 1/v − 1/u, with a minus before u, and m = v/u (not −v/u). Mixing them up flips every sign.

2. Forgetting to convert f to metres for power. P = 1/f needs f in metres: a 25 cm lens is +4 D, not +0.04 D.

3. Adding focal lengths instead of powers. For lenses in contact, 1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂. Two 10 cm lenses give F = 5 cm, not 20 cm.

4. Reading a negative v as "behind the lens". In the Cartesian convention, positive v is on the far side (real image); negative v is on the object side (virtual image).

5. Ignoring the surrounding medium. In water the refractive index must be the relative one; using 1.5 gives a focal length four times too short.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Write the sign convention once at the top of the answer, list u, f and v with signs, then solve. Finish with the nature of the image in words plus m — that sentence usually carries its own mark.
