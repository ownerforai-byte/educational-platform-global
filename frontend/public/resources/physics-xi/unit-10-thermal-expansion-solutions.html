Unit 10 — Thermal Expansion

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  4 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Thermal expansion is a small unit with a reliable numerical pattern: α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3 and the real/apparent expansion of a liquid. Both are worked out below, with the Dulong and Petit method described as an exam answer.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define coefficient of linear expansion; state the relation β = 2α and γ = 3α; why does a bimetallic strip bend; define apparent expansion.
  • 4 marks — linear expansion numerical (railway line, pendulum clock); real and apparent expansion of a liquid; volume expansion of a solid.
  • 5–6 marks — describe Dulong and Petit's method for finding the expansivity of a liquid; derive γ = 3α; explain the anomalous expansion of water.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: the relation γ = 3α holds for isotropic solids only, and the liquid expansion question is always "apparent = real − vessel". Write the formula that names the three coefficients before substituting.

Exercise 10.1 — Linear expansion

Q1. Define the coefficient of linear expansion and write the formula. A steel rail 10 m long is heated through 40 °C. Find its increase in length. (α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)

Definition: the fractional increase in length per unit rise of temperature, α = (ΔL/L)/ΔT; unit K⁻¹ (or °C⁻¹).

ΔL = L α ΔT = 10 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 40 = 4.8 × 10⁻³ m = 4.8 mm

ΔL = 4.8 mm; the new length is 10.0048 m — which is why railway lines are laid with expansion gaps.

Q2. A metal rod is 1.000 m long at 0 °C and 1.0024 m at 100 °C. Find α and identify the likely metal.

α = ΔL/(LΔT) = 0.0024/(1.000 × 100) = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹

The value is close to that of aluminium (2.4 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹) or brass (1.9 × 10⁻⁵); copper is 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ and steel 1.2 × 10⁻⁵. Aluminium is the closest match.

α = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, most likely aluminium

Q3. Explain how the coefficient of linear expansion of a rod is measured with a micrometer/optical lever.

Apparatus: a metal rod in a steam jacket, one end fixed, the other pressing a micrometer screw or one leg of an optical lever; a thermometer in the jacket.

1. Record the initial length L at room temperature T₁ (using a vernier or metre scale). 2. Note the micrometer/lever reading. 3. Pass steam through the jacket until the thermometer reads T₂ and the reading is steady. 4. Measure the change in length ΔL from the micrometer (or from the lever deflection, ΔL = d × deflection/(2D) for an optical lever). 5. α = ΔL/(L(T₂ − T₁)).

The optical lever multiplies a tiny expansion (of order 10⁻⁴ m) into a centimetre-scale deflection, which is why it is used rather than a direct scale.

Heat the rod by a known ΔT, measure ΔL with a micrometer or optical lever, then α = ΔL/(LΔT).

Q4. A pendulum clock keeps correct time at 20 °C. If the temperature rises to 40 °C, how much time does it lose per day? (α = 1.9 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)

Period of a pendulum T = 2π√(L/g) ⇒ ΔT/T = ½(ΔL/L) = ½αΔT ΔT/T = ½ × 1.9 × 10⁻⁵ × 20 = 1.9 × 10⁻⁴ Time lost per day = 1.9 × 10⁻⁴ × 86 400 s = 16.4 s

It loses about 16 s per day — the pendulum lengthens and the period grows, so the clock runs slow.

Q5. Why does the period of a pendulum clock change with temperature, and which way?

The period is T = 2π√(L/g). Heating lengthens the pendulum, so T increases and each swing takes longer — the clock runs slow (loses time) when hot and fast when cold. The fractional change of period is half the fractional change of length: ΔT/T = ½αΔT.

Hot ⇒ longer L ⇒ longer period ⇒ the clock loses time; the correction is compensated in precision clocks by an INVAR pendulum (α ≈ 10⁻⁷ K⁻¹) or a gridiron of dissimilar metals.

Exercise 10.2 — Superficial and cubical expansion and the 1 : 2 : 3 relation

Q6. Define the coefficients of superficial and cubical expansion. Derive β = 2α and γ = 3α for an isotropic solid.

Superficial: β = (ΔA/A)/ΔT; cubical: γ = (ΔV/V)/ΔT.

For a square of side L at temperature rise ΔT: L' = L(1 + αΔT) A' = L'² = L²(1 + αΔT)² = L²(1 + 2αΔT + α²ΔT²) Neglecting α²ΔT² (α ~ 10⁻⁵, so the squared term is ~10⁻¹⁰): A' = A(1 + 2αΔT) ⇒ ΔA/A = 2αΔT ⇒ β = 2α For a cube: V' = L'³ = L³(1 + αΔT)³ ≈ L³(1 + 3αΔT) ⇒ ΔV/V = 3αΔT ⇒ γ = 3α

β = 2α and γ = 3α, so α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3; the approximation is excellent because αΔT is tiny.

Q7. A brass plate has an area of 200 cm² at 20 °C. Find its area at 120 °C. (α = 1.9 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)

β = 2α = 3.8 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ ΔA = AβΔT = 200 × 3.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 100 = 0.76 cm² A' = 200.76 cm²

≈ 200.8 cm² (the increase is 0.76 cm²)

Q8. An iron block of volume 1000 cm³ is heated from 0 °C to 100 °C. Find its new volume. (α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)

γ = 3α = 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ ΔV = VγΔT = 1000 × 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ × 100 = 3.6 cm³ V' = 1003.6 cm³

1003.6 cm³ — note the volume increases by only 0.36%

Q9. A steel rod has a hole drilled in it. What happens to the size of the hole when the rod is heated? Explain.

The hole expands, exactly as if it were filled with the same metal. Heating expands every dimension of the body, and a hole is just the absence of metal — the surrounding material expands away from the centre of the hole equally in all directions. It is not a "shrinking hole".

The hole enlarges by the same fraction as the metal around it; the old demonstration of a ball passing through a heated ring is the standard proof.

Q10. A steel rod of length 1 m has a gap of 1 mm at one end at 20 °C. To what temperature can it be heated before the gap closes? (α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)

ΔL = LαΔT ⇒ ΔT = ΔL/(Lα) = 1 × 10⁻³/(1 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵) = 83.3 K T = 20 + 83.3 = 103 °C

≈ 103 °C — the design rule for expansion joints in bridges and rails.

Exercise 10.3 — Real and apparent expansion of liquids

Q11. Distinguish between the real and apparent expansion of a liquid, and derive γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_vessel.

Real expansion: the actual increase in volume of the liquid alone. Apparent expansion: the increase observed in a container, which is the difference between the liquid's expansion and the container's expansion.

Let V be the initial volume. After ΔT: liquid volume: V(1 + γ_r ΔT) vessel capacity: V(1 + γ_v ΔT) Observed (apparent) volume: V(1 + γ_a ΔT) ⇒ V(1 + γ_a ΔT) = V(1 + γ_r ΔT) − V(1 + γ_v ΔT) + V γ_a = γ_r − γ_v ⇒ γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_vessel

The apparent expansion is smaller than the real one by the vessel's expansion: γ_r = γ_a + γ_v. For glass γ_v is small, which is why the apparent value is used directly in a glass vessel with a correction.

Q12. A glass vessel is filled to the brim with a liquid at 0 °C and overflows by 0.5 cm³ when heated to 100 °C. If the vessel's volume is 100 cm³ and γ_glass = 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, find γ_apparent and γ_real of the liquid.

γ_apparent = ΔV_apparent/(VΔT) = 0.5/(100 × 100) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ γ_vessel = 3α_glass = 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_vessel = 7.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹

γ_apparent = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹; γ_real = 7.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ (consistent with a light oil or kerosene)

Q13. Why does the level of a liquid in a vessel first fall and then rise when heated?

At first the vessel receives the heat and expands before the liquid has warmed appreciably, so the container's capacity grows and the liquid level falls. As the liquid warms, its own expansion (which is much larger than the vessel's) overtakes the capacity increase and the level rises. The effect is clearest when the vessel is a poor conductor and the liquid has a large γ.

Vessel expands first (level falls), then the liquid's larger expansion dominates (level rises) — a direct demonstration that γ_liquid > γ_vessel.

Q14. Why is water's expansion called anomalous? State the range of the anomaly and its consequences.

Between 0 °C and 4 °C, water contracts on heating instead of expanding, and its density therefore increases, reaching a maximum at 4 °C (1000 kg m⁻³). Above 4 °C it expands normally. The anomaly arises from the collapse of the open hydrogen-bonded structure of ice as it melts.

Consequences: a pond freezes from the top downward, because the coldest, least dense water stays at the surface while 4 °C water sinks; aquatic life survives in the liquid layer beneath the ice. It also explains why water pipes burst when they freeze — ice expands on forming.

Water contracts from 0 to 4 °C with maximum density at 4 °C; hence lakes freeze from the top and life survives beneath the ice.

Exercise 10.4 — Measurement of expansivity: Dulong and Petit's method

Q15. Describe Dulong and Petit's method for determining the real expansivity of a liquid.

Principle: two communicating tubes of equal bore (one containing the liquid, the other a comparison liquid of known expansivity, in practice the same liquid in a U-tube) are maintained at different temperatures; the columns adjust until the pressures at the bottom are equal. The heights then satisfy a relation in which the vessel's expansion cancels.

Two vertical limbs A and B of a U-tube, with temperatures T₁ and T₂ and column heights h₁, h₂: pressure at the common base: ρ₁g h₁ = ρ₂g h₂ with ρ = ρ₀/(1 + γ ΔT): h₁(1 + γ(T₁ − T₀)) = h₂(1 + γ(T₂ − T₀)) ⇒ γ = (h₂ − h₁)/(h₁(T₁ − T₀) − h₂(T₂ − T₀)) (T₀ taken as the reference temperature) Because the same vessel contains both columns, the expansion of the glass affects both sides equally and cancels, so the result is the REAL expansivity, not the apparent one.

In practice the two limbs are held in separate baths (one at room temperature, one in a steam jacket), the heights are read with a travelling microscope, and γ is computed from the above expression.

Compare the hydrostatic pressures in two limbs of a U-tube at different temperatures; the vessel term cancels, so the method yields the true (absolute) expansivity γ = Δh/h relations above.

Q16. Why does Dulong and Petit's method give the real expansivity while a simple overflow method gives only the apparent one?

In the U-tube method both liquid columns are in the same container, so the expansion of the container changes the bore and both lengths by the same factor — the effect cancels in the pressure balance. In an overflow experiment the observed overflow is the liquid's expansion minus the vessel's expansion, so a further correction (adding γ_vessel) is needed to obtain the real value.

The vessel's expansion is common to both limbs and cancels in the pressure equation; in an overflow experiment it appears as a difference and must be corrected for.

Exercise 10.5 — Applications and consequences

Q17. Explain the working of a bimetallic strip and give two uses.

Two strips of different metals (brass and invar, or brass and steel) are riveted together. Brass has the larger α, so on heating it expands more and the strip bends with the brass on the outside of the curve; on cooling it bends the other way. The deflection is proportional to ΔT and to the length, and it can be made to operate a switch.

Uses: a thermostat (the strip bends and breaks or makes an electrical contact at a set temperature), a fire alarm, and the compensating balance wheel of a watch.

Unequal expansion of the two metals bends the strip; used as a thermostat switch and a fire alarm, and to compensate temperature error in clocks.

Q18. Why do railway lines have gaps between them, and how is the gap size calculated?

At the highest expected temperature the rail must be able to expand without buckling, since a restrained rail develops a large compressive stress (σ = YαΔT) that can bend the track out of alignment. The gap is sized to accommodate the maximum expansion:

gap = L α ΔT_max For L = 20 m, α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, ΔT_max = 50 K: gap = 20 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 50 = 0.012 m = 1.2 cm

Modern continuous-welded rail avoids gaps by pre-stressing the rail and using expansion joints at intervals.

Gap = LαΔT ≈ 1 cm per 20 m rail; otherwise thermal compression buckles the track.

Q19. Why does a thick glass tumbler crack when boiling water is poured into it?

Glass is a poor conductor. The inner surface touching the water heats and expands quickly while the outer surface is still cold and unexpanded. The inner layer is therefore in compression and the outer in tension; when the tensile stress exceeds the strength of the glass, a crack forms. Thin glass conducts the heat through quickly so that the temperature difference across the wall stays small — hence thin tumblers survive.

Poor thermal conduction produces a steep temperature gradient, differential expansion and tensile stress on the outer surface; thin-walled glass avoids it, and borosilicate glass has a low α.

Q20. A steel tape measures correctly at 20 °C. What is the percentage error in a 30 m measurement made at 40 °C? (α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)

ΔL/L = αΔT = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 20 = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.024% The tape is 0.024% longer, so every reading is 0.024% too small. Over 30 m the error is 30 × 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ = 7.2 × 10⁻³ m = 7.2 mm

0.024% low; 7.2 mm short over 30 m — small per metre but significant in surveying, which is why tapes are calibrated at a stated temperature.

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Expansion numericals are short, so the marks go to the reasoning items: β = 2α, γ = 3α, real versus apparent expansion, and the water anomaly. All eight shapes follow.

Q21. [Multi-step numerical] Steel rails 10 m long are laid at 15 °C. What expansion gap must be left if the maximum temperature is 55 °C? (α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹) And how much does a 2.0 m brass rod expand over the same range (α = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)?

Δθ = 55 − 15 = 40 K steel: ΔL = L₀αΔθ = 10 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 40 = 4.8 × 10⁻³ m = 4.8 mm brass: ΔL = 2.0 × 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 40 = 1.4 × 10⁻³ m = 1.4 mm

The gap must be at least 4.8 mm — a rail with no gap buckles (the classic “sun kink”), which is why modern track uses continuous welded rail with expansion joints at intervals and a pre-stress at a reference temperature. Note that Δθ appears in kelvin and in °C with the same number: a temperature difference converts 1 : 1.

Gap ≥ 4.8 mm for the 10 m steel rail; the 2 m brass rod expands 1.4 mm over the same 40 K rise.

Q22. [Derivation] Show that the coefficient of superficial expansion is twice, and the coefficient of cubical expansion three times, the coefficient of linear expansion.

Linear: L = L₀(1 + αΔθ) Area of a square of side L: A = L² = L₀²(1 + αΔθ)² = A₀(1 + 2αΔθ + α²Δθ²) α ≈ 10⁻⁵ so α²Δθ² ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ — negligible A ≈ A₀(1 + 2αΔθ) ⇒ β = 2α Volume of a cube of side L: V = L³ = V₀(1 + 3αΔθ + 3α²Δθ² + α³Δθ³) ≈ V₀(1 + 3αΔθ) ⇒ γ = 3α

The approximation is legitimate because αΔθ is of order 10⁻³ at most, so its square is 10⁻⁶ — well below the precision of any school measurement. The relations also explain the design of the bimetallic strip and of a compensated pendulum: two materials with different α expand differently, and the 1 : 2 : 3 ratio lets you convert a linear measurement into an area or a volume without new data.

Expanding (1 + αΔθ)² and (1 + αΔθ)³ and dropping α², α³ terms gives β = 2α and γ = 3α.

Q23. [Graph reading] A metal rod is 1.0000 m at 0 °C and 1.0024 m at 100 °C. (a) Find α from the graph of length against temperature. (b) What is its length at 40 °C?

(a) slope = ΔL/Δθ = (1.0024 − 1.0000)/100 = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ m K⁻¹ L = L₀(1 + αθ) ⇒ slope = L₀α α = slope/L₀ = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵/1.0000 = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ (b) L(40) = 1.0000 + 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ × 40 = 1.00096 m ≈ 1.0010 m

Aluminium is 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, so the rod is aluminium. Reading α from a slope is more reliable than from a single pair of readings, because the intercept also tells you the length at 0 °C and any zero error in the length measurement shows up there rather than corrupting α.

α = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ (aluminium); L(40 °C) = 1.0010 m.

Q24. [Combined concept] A liquid of real expansivity 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ is held in a vessel of linear expansivity 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. Find (a) the apparent expansivity, and (b) the overflow when 500 cm³ is heated through 60 K.

(a) γ_vessel = 3α = 3(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 5.4 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ γ_apparent = γ_liquid − γ_vessel = 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ − 0.54 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.26 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ (b) overflow = V γ_app Δθ = 500 × 1.26 × 10⁻⁴ × 60 = 3.8 cm³ real expansion of the liquid = 500 × 1.80 × 10⁻⁴ × 60 = 5.4 cm³ expansion of the vessel = 500 × 0.54 × 10⁻⁴ × 60 = 1.6 cm³ check: 5.4 − 1.6 = 3.8 cm³ ✓

The liquid really expands by 5.4 cm³ — you simply cannot see 1.6 cm³ of that, because the vessel grew to accommodate it. This is the whole content of “real versus apparent”: the observable overflow is always the difference, and it is smaller than the true expansion for any liquid in any solid vessel.

γ_apparent = 1.26 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹; overflow 3.8 cm³ out of a true expansion of 5.4 cm³ (the vessel absorbed 1.6 cm³).

Q25. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): A bimetallic strip bends when heated. Reason (R): The two metals have different coefficients of linear expansion, so they expand by different amounts and cannot stay straight.

Both statements are true and R explains A.

Brass α = 1.9 × 10⁻⁵, iron α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ For a 10 cm strip heated 100 K: brass expands 1.9 × 10⁻⁴ m, iron 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m difference = 0.7 × 10⁻⁴ m over 10 cm — the strip must curve, brass on the outside. On cooling the curvature reverses — the same strip is a thermometer and a thermostat.

The strip is bonded, so the two metals share one length; the one that wants to be longer forces the pair to bend, putting the brass in compression and the iron in tension. This is the mechanism of the bimetallic thermostat, the fire alarm, and the compensating balance wheel of a watch.

Both true, R explains A: unequal expansions in a bonded strip force it to curve, with the higher-α metal on the outside of the bend.

Q26. [Experiment/observation table] In a Dulong–Petit experiment on a metal bar, the shift of the microscope per 10 °C rise is recorded. Complete the last column and estimate α.

Temperature rise Δθ (K)Shift Δh (mm)Δh/Δθ (mm K⁻¹)

200.048?
300.073?

Δh/Δθ = 2.4 × 10⁻³, 2.4 × 10⁻³, 2.4 × 10⁻³ mm K⁻¹ (constant ⇒ uniform expansion) Dulong and Petit: h = L α Δθ where L = half the bar length between the supports. With L = 500 mm: α = (Δh/Δθ)/L = 2.4 × 10⁻³/500 = 4.8 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹

Q27. [Estimation and limiting case] A pendulum clock is accurate at 20 °C but runs at 45 °C. Estimate the daily error (α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹) and state what happens in the limiting case of a perfectly compensated pendulum.

T = 2π√(L/g) ⇒ ΔT/T = ½ΔL/L = ½αΔθ = ½ × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 25 = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ Fractional time error per day = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 86 400 s = 13 s So the clock LOSES 13 s per day (longer pendulum, longer period).

Limiting case: a compensated pendulum uses two materials (or a bar with a mercury cell) whose expansions cancel, so ΔL → 0 and ΔT/T → 0: the period becomes independent of temperature. The compensated grid-iron pendulum and the invar alloy (α ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹) are the two practical realisations — invar's α is small enough that a one-day error becomes 1 s rather than 13 s.

About 13 s lost per day at 45 °C; a compensated pendulum cancels the expansions so the error tends to zero.

Q28. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “Water expands continuously as it is heated from 0 °C.”
(ii) “The coefficient of linear expansion has the unit of length.”
(iii) “If a liquid overflows from a vessel, the real expansion of the liquid equals the overflow.”
(iv) “A hole in a metal plate becomes smaller when the plate is heated.”

(i) FALSE — between 0 °C and 4 °C water CONTRACTS (its density increases to a maximum at 4 °C); above 4 °C it expands. This anomaly is why lakes freeze from the top. (ii) FALSE — α has unit K⁻¹ (or °C⁻¹): α = ΔL/(L₀Δθ) is a length divided by a length times a temperature, so the length cancels. (iii) FALSE — real expansion = overflow + expansion of the vessel. The overflow is the APPARENT expansion and is always smaller. (iv) FALSE — every dimension grows in proportion, holes included. A hole in a plate behaves exactly like a disc of the same metal: it expands too, which is why a metal washer's bore widens on heating.

The hole question (iv) is the classic one-mark trap: heating expands the plate's outline and its voids alike, because expansion is a scaling of the whole shape.

(i) water contracts 0–4 °C; (ii) α is in K⁻¹; (iii) real = overflow + vessel expansion; (iv) the hole gets larger.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Define the coefficient of linear expansion and state its unit.

It is the fractional increase in length per unit rise of temperature, α = ΔL/(LΔT), with unit K⁻¹ (or °C⁻¹). It is a property of the material, not of the particular rod.

C2. State the relation between α, β and γ.

For an isotropic solid, β = 2α and γ = 3α, so α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3. The factors come from the area and volume of a body scaling as L² and L³.

C3. What is meant by apparent expansion of a liquid?

The expansion observed when a liquid is heated in a vessel, which equals the real expansion of the liquid minus the expansion of the vessel: γ_apparent = γ_real − γ_vessel.

C4. Why does a bimetallic strip bend on heating?

Because the two metals have different coefficients of linear expansion. The one with the larger α expands more, so the strip curves with that metal on the outside of the bend; on cooling it curves the other way.

C5. Why is water's expansion anomalous between 0 °C and 4 °C?

Because on melting, the open hydrogen-bonded lattice of ice partly collapses; as the temperature rises from 0 to 4 °C the molecules pack more closely and the water contracts, reaching maximum density at 4 °C. Above 4 °C ordinary thermal expansion takes over.

C6. Why do cavities in a heated solid expand?

Because heating expands every interatomic spacing of the material, including those around the cavity; the metal around a hole moves away from the hole's centre, so the hole grows by the same fractional amount as the surrounding metal.

C7. Why are metals used in thermometers but gases in the primary standard?

Metals change resistance reproducibly and quickly with temperature, so they make convenient practical thermometers. Gases are used for the standard scale because all gases approach the same pV ∝ T behaviour at low pressure, making the scale independent of the working substance.

C8. Why does a pendulum clock lose time in summer?

The pendulum rod lengthens as the temperature rises; since T = 2π√(L/g), a longer pendulum has a longer period, so each oscillation takes slightly more time and the clock runs slow. ΔT/T = ½αΔT.

C9. What is a thermostat and how does it use thermal expansion?

A thermostat is a device that keeps a temperature constant by switching a heater on and off. A bimetallic strip bends as the temperature changes and makes or breaks an electrical contact at the set point, controlling the circuit.

C10. Give two practical problems caused by thermal expansion and their solutions.

(i) Railway rails buckle when hot — solved by expansion gaps or pre-stressed continuous welded rail. (ii) A glass tumbler cracks with boiling water — solved by thin-walled or borosilicate glass; also, a tight metal lid can be loosened by warming it, since the metal expands more than the glass.

C11. Why is the coefficient of expansion of invar used in precision instruments?

Because its α is exceptionally small (about 1 × 10⁻⁷ K⁻¹, roughly a hundredth that of steel), so the lengths of invar rods and pendulum rods hardly change with temperature. This keeps clocks accurate and measuring standards stable.

C12. Why do we not take the expansion of the vessel into account when using a density bottle?

Because the bottle is calibrated at a fixed temperature and the measurement is made at that same temperature. If measurements are made at a different temperature, then both the liquid's expansion and the bottle's expansion must be corrected for, and the apparent volume change is what a direct weighing measures.

C13. Why does a metal ball not pass through a metal ring when both are heated?

Because the ball expands in three dimensions (γ = 3α) while the ring's hole expands only as its circumference (β = 2α) — but both are of the same material, and the ball's volume grows faster than the ring's hole area. Heating only the ring (not the ball) makes the hole larger and the ball passes.

C14. What is the significance of water's maximum density at 4 °C for aquatic life?

As surface water cools below 4 °C it becomes less dense and stays at the top, freezing there while the 4 °C water sinks. The ice layer insulates the pond, so the water beneath stays liquid and aquatic plants and animals survive the winter.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [5] Derive γ = 3α and state the conditions under which it fails.

Cube of side L: V = L³ At temperature rise ΔT: L' = L(1 + αΔT) V' = L³(1 + αΔT)³ = V(1 + 3αΔT + 3α²ΔT² + α³ΔT³) Keeping only the first-order term (αΔT ≪ 1): V' ≈ V(1 + 3αΔT) ⇒ ΔV/V = 3αΔT ⇒ γ = 3α

The relation assumes the expansion is the same in all directions (isotropic) and that α is constant over the temperature range. It fails for anisotropic crystals (graphite, mica, ice), where α differs along different axes and γ ≠ 3α, and over ranges where α itself changes appreciably.

γ = 3α from the binomial expansion of (1 + αΔT)³ for an isotropic solid with constant α.

L2. [6] A steel railway line is 25 m long at 15 °C. α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ and Y = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa. (a) Find the gap needed at 15 °C if the maximum temperature is 55 °C. (b) If no gap is left and the ends are fixed, find the thermal stress at 55 °C.

(a) ΔL = LαΔT = 25 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 40 = 0.012 m = 1.2 cm (b) Restrained: the rail cannot expand, so the strain that would have occurred is balanced by an equal compressive strain from the stress: σ = Y α ΔT = (2 × 10¹¹)(1.2 × 10⁻⁵)(40) = 2 × 10¹¹ × 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.6 × 10⁷ Pa

(a) gap ≈ 1.2 cm; (b) thermal stress ≈ 9.6 × 10⁷ Pa (96 MPa) — enough to buckle the track, which is exactly why the gap is needed.

L3. [5] Explain how the real expansivity of a liquid can be found from the apparent expansivity, and why γ_liquid is always larger than γ_vessel for a glass vessel.

γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_vessel = γ_apparent + 3α_glass Measurement: fill a glass vessel of known volume V to a mark at T₁, heat to T₂ and collect (or weigh) the overflow ΔV. γ_apparent = ΔV/(V ΔT) Then add 3α_glass (≈ 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ for ordinary glass).

Liquids expand far more than solids: their molecules are only loosely bound, so on heating the increased molecular separation is not resisted by a lattice. Ordinary values are γ_liquid ≈ 10⁻⁴–10⁻³ K⁻¹ (water 2.1 × 10⁻⁴, alcohol 7.5 × 10⁻⁴, mercury 1.8 × 10⁻⁴) against γ_glass ≈ 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ — a factor of 4 to 30.

γ_real = γ_apparent + 3α_glass; liquids have much larger γ because they lack a rigid lattice, so the liquid always overflows a heated glass vessel.

L4. [4] A brass rod and an iron rod are each 1.000 m long at 0 °C. At what temperature will their lengths differ by 1 mm? (α_brass = 1.9 × 10⁻⁵, α_iron = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹)

L_b − L_i = 1(1 + α_bΔT) − 1(1 + α_iΔT) = ΔT(α_b − α_i) ΔT = 1 × 10⁻³/((1.9 − 1.2) × 10⁻⁵) = 10⁻³/(7 × 10⁻⁶) = 143 K

ΔT ≈ 143 K, i.e. about 143 °C above the initial temperature (or below it, since the difference grows in magnitude in both directions).

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 10

1. Adding instead of subtracting the vessel. Apparent = real − vessel, so real = apparent + vessel. Reversing it makes the liquid look less expansive than glass, which is impossible.

2. Using α where γ is needed. For a volume question take γ = 3α first; substituting α for γ gives an answer three times too small.

3. Forgetting that the hole expands. "The hole shrinks when the metal expands" is wrong; every dimension, hole included, scales by (1 + αΔT).

4. Sign of the pendulum error. Hot ⇒ longer ⇒ slower ⇒ loses time. State the direction explicitly.

5. Degrees vs kelvin. ΔT is numerically the same in °C and K, so a temperature difference needs no +273 — but a single temperature does. Do not add 273 to a ΔT.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Name the coefficient (α, β or γ), write the formula, substitute in SI (lengths in m, ΔT in K), and give the answer with the unit and a sentence saying what it means physically (a gap, a time loss, an overflow).
