Unit 9 — Heat and Temperature

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  3 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

A short, definition-heavy unit. NEB asks one or two 2-mark items and occasionally a 4-mark explanation of the mercury thermometer or the zeroth law. The marks come from precise wording, not computation — so the model answers below are written the way a full-mark answer should read.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — distinguish heat from temperature; define thermal equilibrium; state the zeroth law; why is water unsuitable in a thermometer.
  • 4 marks — explain the working of a mercury thermometer with its fixed points; convert temperatures between scales and justify absolute zero.
  • 5–6 marks — explain heat flow in terms of molecular motion, define thermal equilibrium, and show why the zeroth law makes temperature measurement possible.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: heat is energy in transit (joules), temperature is a state (kelvin). A body does not "contain heat"; it contains internal energy. Writing "the body has 500 J of heat" loses the definition mark.

Exercise 9.1 — Molecular concept of thermal energy, heat and temperature

Q1. Distinguish between heat, temperature and internal energy.

HeatTemperatureInternal energy
Energy transferred from one body to another because of a temperature differenceA measure of the average translational kinetic energy of the moleculesThe total energy (kinetic + potential) of all the molecules of a body
Depends on mass and on the temperature differenceIndependent of mass — a property of the stateDepends on mass, temperature and state
Joule (J)Kelvin (K)Joule (J)
A process quantity — flows, never "stored"An intensive state quantityAn extensive state quantity

Heat is energy in transit (J); temperature measures average molecular KE (K); internal energy is the total molecular energy a body possesses (J = sum over all molecules).

Q2. Explain temperature in terms of molecular motion.

The molecules of any substance are in continuous random motion. For a gas the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule is

½ m ⟨v²⟩ = (3/2) k T

so T is directly proportional to the mean translational kinetic energy — temperature is a measure of how energetic the molecular motion is, not of how much energy a body holds. Doubling the absolute temperature doubles the average molecular KE; at absolute zero the translational motion would be minimal.

T ∝ average translational KE of the molecules; temperature is an intensive measure of molecular agitation, independent of how many molecules are present.

Q3. Why does a cup of hot water and a bathtub of warm water at the same temperature contain different amounts of internal energy?

Temperature fixes the average energy per molecule, but the internal energy is the sum over all molecules. The bathtub contains far more water molecules, so even at a lower or equal temperature its total internal energy is much larger. That is why a bath warms a room more than a cup does, and why temperature alone cannot tell you how much energy is available.

Same temperature ⇒ same mean energy per molecule, but the tub has vastly more molecules, so its total internal energy (and heat capacity) is larger.

Q4. On the basis of the kinetic theory, explain why the pressure of a gas rises when it is heated at constant volume.

Heating raises the average kinetic energy of the molecules, so they move faster. They strike the walls more often and each impact transfers more momentum, so the rate of change of momentum per unit area — the pressure — rises. With volume fixed the number density is unchanged, so the whole increase comes from the higher molecular speed:

p = (1/3)ρ⟨v²⟩ and ⟨v²⟩ ∝ T ⇒ p ∝ T at constant V

Faster molecules ⇒ more frequent and harder collisions ⇒ higher pressure, giving p ∝ T at constant volume (Gay-Lussac's law).

Exercise 9.2 — Cause and direction of heat flow

Q5. What causes heat to flow, and why does it always flow from a hotter to a colder body?

The cause is a temperature difference. In the hotter body the molecules have a larger average kinetic energy; collisions at the boundary transfer energy from the faster molecules to the slower ones. The net transfer continues until the average kinetic energies (temperatures) equalise.

It never runs the other way spontaneously because that would mean energy moving from a less energetic to a more energetic set of molecules on average — the total entropy would decrease, which the second law of thermodynamics forbids. Heat can be made to flow from cold to hot only by doing work, as a refrigerator does.

A temperature difference drives the flow; the direction is fixed by statistics/entropy — heat flows from hot to cold until thermal equilibrium, and reversing it requires external work.

Q6. Why does a metal spoon feel colder than a wooden spoon at the same room temperature?

Both are at room temperature, but metal conducts heat about 400 times better than wood. On touching the metal, heat flows rapidly from your finger into the spoon, lowering the skin temperature and producing the sensation of cold. Wood conducts poorly, so very little heat leaves your finger and the spoon feels neutral.

The sensation reports the rate of heat loss from the skin, not the temperature — the good conductor draws heat away fast and feels cold.

Q7. A 2 kg iron block at 80 °C is placed in 5 kg of water at 20 °C. State the direction of heat flow and the final state, without calculating.

Heat flows from the iron (80 °C) to the water (20 °C) because of the temperature difference. It continues until both reach a common temperature between 20 °C and 80 °C — the exact value determined by m₁c₁ΔT₁ = m₂c₂ΔT₂. Since water has a much larger specific heat capacity (4200 against 450 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) and a larger mass, the final temperature will be much closer to 20 °C than to 80 °C.

From iron to water; the common final temperature lies between 20 and 80 °C, near 22–25 °C because the water's heat capacity dominates.

Exercise 9.3 — Thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law

Q8. Define thermal equilibrium and state the zeroth law of thermodynamics.

Thermal equilibrium: the state in which two bodies in thermal contact have the same temperature, so no net heat flows between them.

Zeroth law: if a body A is in thermal equilibrium with body B, and B is in thermal equilibrium with body C, then A is in thermal equilibrium with C.

No net heat flow when temperatures are equal; and the zeroth law says "in thermal equilibrium with" is transitive, which is what allows temperature to be a measurable property.

Q9. Why is the zeroth law called the basis of thermometry?

To measure a temperature you must bring a thermometer into contact with the body and wait until no heat flows — i.e. until they are in equilibrium. The zeroth law guarantees that if the thermometer reads the same value for two different bodies separately, those two bodies have the same temperature and would be in equilibrium with each other. Without this transitivity, comparing temperatures would be meaningless and a temperature scale could not be defined.

It makes "has the same temperature as" an equivalence relation, so a thermometer reading is a valid comparison between any two bodies — that is exactly the logical basis of thermometry.

Q10. Distinguish between thermal equilibrium and thermodynamic equilibrium.

Thermal equilibrium requires only equal temperatures and no net heat flow. Thermodynamic equilibrium requires all three at once: thermal (uniform temperature), mechanical (no unbalanced pressure — no macroscopic motion) and chemical (no net chemical reaction or diffusion between phases). A gas allowed to expand into a vacuum reaches thermal equilibrium quickly but may still be settling mechanically.

Thermal equilibrium is only about temperature; thermodynamic equilibrium requires simultaneous thermal, mechanical and chemical equilibrium.

Exercise 9.4 — The mercury thermometer

Q11. Explain the construction and working of a mercury thermometer.

Construction: a narrow uniform capillary tube of glass sealed at both ends, with a bulb at one end filled with mercury and the space above the mercury evacuated.

Working: when the bulb is placed in contact with a body, mercury absorbs heat and expands more than the glass. The mercury column rises along the capillary until thermal equilibrium is reached. Since the tube's cross-section is uniform, the length of the column is proportional to the temperature, so the scale can be calibrated linearly.

Thermal expansion of mercury in a fine uniform capillary measures temperature; equilibrium with the body makes the reading meaningful, and the volume expansion γ relates to the column length by ΔL ∝ γΔT.

Q12. Why is mercury preferred to water or alcohol in a thermometer?

  • Wide liquid range: mercury is liquid from −39 °C to 357 °C, covering nearly all ordinary measurements.
  • Does not wet glass: the meniscus stays clean and the reading is unambiguous (water would leave a film and give false readings).
  • Opaque and shiny: the column is easy to read against the scale without a dye.
  • Good conductor: it responds quickly to the temperature of the body.
  • Uniform expansion: its volume expansion is nearly constant over the range, so the scale is linear; it also has a low specific heat, so it absorbs little heat from the body being measured.

Wide liquid range, does not wet glass, opaque, a good conductor, small specific heat and near-uniform expansion — water fails on all but one of these.

Q13. Why is water not suitable as a thermometric liquid?

  • It freezes at 0 °C, so it cannot measure ordinary winter temperatures and below.
  • It wets glass, leaving a film and making the reading unreliable.
  • Its expansion is not uniform — between 0 °C and 4 °C it contracts on heating (anomalous expansion), so it cannot even indicate the sign of the temperature change there.
  • It has a large specific heat, so a thermometer would absorb a significant amount of heat from the body measured, changing the temperature it is trying to measure; it also boils at only 100 °C and evaporates.

Narrow liquid range, wets glass, anomalous expansion near 4 °C and a high specific heat — four independent reasons.

Q14. What are the lower and upper fixed points of a thermometer? How is an arbitrary scale calibrated between them?

Lower fixed point (ice point): the temperature of pure melting ice at normal atmospheric pressure — 0 °C, 32 °F, 273.15 K.

Upper fixed point (steam point): the temperature of steam above boiling water at normal pressure — 100 °C, 212 °F, 373.15 K.

If the mercury column has length L₀ at the ice point, L₁₀₀ at the steam point and L at an unknown temperature θ, then for a linear scale: θ = 100 × (L − L₀)/(L₁₀₀ − L₀)

Ice point (0 °C) and steam point (100 °C) fix the scale; any intermediate reading is interpolated linearly from the column length between them.

Q15. Why does a clinical thermometer have a constriction just above the bulb?

The constriction allows mercury to pass when it expands (during measurement) but breaks the thread when the mercury contracts after the thermometer is removed from the patient. The mercury column is therefore held at the maximum reading until it is shaken down, so the doctor can read the temperature after the measurement. Its range is narrow, 35–42 °C, giving a fine, easily read scale.

The constriction traps the maximum reading so it can be read after removal; the narrow 35–42 °C range gives high resolution.

Exercise 9.5 — Temperature scales and thermometric properties

Q16. Derive the relation between the Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin scales, and convert 37 °C, −40 °C and 300 K.

C/100 = (F − 32)/180 = (K − 273.15)/100 37 °C → F = (9/5)(37) + 32 = 66.6 + 32 = 98.6 °F; K = 310 K −40 °C → F = (9/5)(−40) + 32 = −72 + 32 = −40 °F (the scales cross) 300 K → C = 300 − 273 = 27 °C → F = (9/5)(27) + 32 = 80.6 °F

37 °C = 98.6 °F = 310 K; −40 °C = −40 °F; 300 K = 27 °C = 80.6 °F

Q17. What is a thermometric property? Give four examples and the corresponding thermometers.

Thermometric propertyThermometer
Volume of a liquidMercury or alcohol thermometer
Pressure of a gas at constant volume (or volume at constant pressure)Constant-volume gas thermometer (the standard)
Electrical resistance of a metalPlatinum resistance thermometer
Emf of a thermocoupleThermocouple thermometer
Intensity of radiation emittedOptical / infra-red pyrometer

A thermometric property is any physical property that changes continuously and reproducibly with temperature; the gas thermometer is the primary standard because its readings differ least between different gases.

Volume, pressure, resistance, thermocouple emf and radiated intensity — each defines a different thermometer, and all agree only because they are calibrated against the fixed points.

Q18. What is absolute zero? Why can it never be reached?

Absolute zero is the temperature at which a gas would have zero volume and the molecular translational kinetic energy would be minimum — 0 K = −273.15 °C. It is the zero of the Kelvin scale, defined from the extrapolation of pV ∝ T for an ideal gas.

It cannot be reached because the third law of thermodynamics forbids cooling a system to exactly 0 K in a finite number of steps: as T falls, the heat capacity of matter also falls, so the entropy removed per step shrinks and an infinite number of steps would be needed.

0 K = −273.15 °C, where translational molecular motion is minimal; the third law forbids reaching it in any finite process.

Q19. A thermometer reads 5 cm at the ice point and 25 cm at the steam point. What temperature does a 13 cm reading correspond to?

θ = 100 × (L − L₀)/(L₁₀₀ − L₀) = 100 × (13 − 5)/(25 − 5) = 100 × 8/20 = 40 °C

40 °C

Q20. Define heat capacity and specific heat capacity, and state how they differ.

Heat capacity C = Q/ΔT: the heat needed to raise the whole body's temperature by 1 K, in J K⁻¹. It depends on the body's mass and material.

Specific heat capacity c = Q/(mΔT): the heat needed per unit mass per kelvin, in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. It is a property of the material only, independent of mass.

Relation: Q = mcΔT, and C = mc

C is a property of the object (J K⁻¹), c of the substance (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹); c = C/m, and water has an unusually large c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Distinguish between heat and temperature in one or two sentences.

Heat is energy transferred between bodies because of a temperature difference, measured in joules and dependent on mass; temperature is a measure of the average molecular kinetic energy of a body, measured in kelvin and independent of mass.

C2. Why does heat flow from a hot body to a cold body?

Because a temperature difference means the hot body's molecules have greater average kinetic energy; collisions transfer energy to the slower molecules of the cold body. The transfer increases total entropy, so this is the only spontaneous direction; the reverse needs work (a refrigerator).

C3. Define thermal equilibrium.

Two bodies in thermal contact are in thermal equilibrium when they have the same temperature, so that no net heat flows between them; their macroscopic properties then remain constant.

C4. State the zeroth law and give its importance.

If A is in thermal equilibrium with B and B with C, then A is in equilibrium with C. It makes temperature a well-defined, transitive property, and therefore makes thermometry and any temperature scale possible.

C5. Why is the gas thermometer preferred as a standard?

Because the behaviour of every gas approaches the same limiting relation pV ∝ T as the pressure is reduced; the readings are therefore independent of which gas is used. Mercury or alcohol differ between substances by comparison and must be calibrated.

C6. A body feels cold when touched. Does that mean it has no heat?

No. "Cold" only means its temperature is lower than the skin, so heat flows from the finger into the body. Every body above absolute zero has internal energy; there is no such thing as a body with no heat.

C7. Why does a clinical thermometer need a narrow range?

A narrow range (35–42 °C) allows a much longer scale per degree and hence finer resolution (typically 0.1 °C), which is needed to detect small changes in body temperature. A wide-range thermometer with the same tube length would be far less precise.

C8. What is meant by an "ideal gas temperature scale"?

A scale defined by the pressure of a fixed amount of gas at constant volume, extrapolated to zero pressure, so that it is independent of the gas used: T = 273.16 (p/p_tp) K, using the triple point of water as the single fixed point.

C9. Why is −273 °C believed to be the lowest possible temperature?

Because the absolute temperature is proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules, and kinetic energy cannot be negative. At 0 K the translational motion would be minimal, so temperatures below it have no physical meaning.

C10. Why is mercury's expansion described as "apparent" when measured in a glass tube?

Because the glass also expands, the observed rise of the column is the difference between the expansion of the mercury and that of the glass. The true (absolute) expansion of mercury is larger than the apparent one: γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_glass.

C11. Give two examples of thermal equilibrium in daily life.

A cup of tea left in a room finally reaches room temperature and stops cooling; a thermometer left in a patient's mouth settles at a steady reading. In both cases no net heat flows once the temperatures match.

C12. Explain why steam at 100 °C causes a worse burn than water at 100 °C.

Because the steam first condenses on the skin, releasing its large specific latent heat of vaporisation (≈2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹), and then the condensed water cools to skin temperature. The water at 100 °C only loses sensible heat mcΔT, so the steam delivers several times more energy.

C13. What is the significance of the triple point of water?

It is the single temperature and pressure at which ice, liquid water and water vapour coexist in equilibrium (273.16 K, 611 Pa). Because it is reproducible in any laboratory and involves only a pure substance, it is used as the defining fixed point of the Kelvin scale.

C14. Why does a thermometer have a bulb of larger volume than its capillary?

The mercury in the bulb supplies the volume change that pushes the thread along the capillary; a relatively large bulb and a very fine capillary magnify the small expansion into a visible, easily read length, which increases the sensitivity of the instrument.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [4] Explain, using the molecular model, what happens when a hot iron block is put into cold water, up to and beyond the moment of thermal equilibrium.

At the moment of contact the iron molecules have a higher average kinetic energy than the water molecules. Collisions at the boundary transfer energy from the iron lattice to the water molecules, raising their kinetic energy (temperature) and lowering that of the iron.

The process continues while a temperature difference remains. When the average molecular kinetic energies become equal, the number of collisions transferring energy in each direction balances, so no net transfer occurs — thermal equilibrium. From then on, both temperatures stay constant (apart from slow loss to the room).

Net energy transfer from the faster to the slower molecules, stopping when the average kinetic energies are equal — the definition of equal temperature.

L2. [5] Describe how a mercury thermometer is calibrated, using the ice point and steam point, and explain why the scale is nearly linear.

1. Immerse the bulb in vigorously stirred melting ice at standard pressure; mark the column position L₀ as 0 °C. 2. Immerse in steam above water boiling at standard pressure (the bulb must not touch the water); mark the position L₁₀₀ as 100 °C. 3. Divide the distance (L₁₀₀ − L₀) into 100 equal parts and extend the scale beyond both marks. 4. Any reading L maps to θ = 100 (L − L₀)/(L₁₀₀ − L₀).

The scale is nearly linear because the glass capillary is uniform (equal lengths hold equal mercury volumes) and the volume expansion of mercury is almost exactly proportional to the temperature change over the range. The small departures from linearity, plus the expansion of the glass, are corrected in a precision instrument.

Two fixed points, equal subdivision, linear interpolation; the linearity follows from uniform bore and near-constant γ, with the glass expansion as a small correction.

L3. [4] A faulty thermometer reads −5 °C at the ice point and 105 °C at the steam point. What is the true temperature when the faulty thermometer reads 60 °C?

True θ = 100 × (reading − lower fixed point)/(upper − lower) = 100 × (60 − (−5))/(105 − (−5)) = 100 × 65/110 = 59.1 °C

The error comes from a shifted zero and a stretched scale; both are corrected at once by the two-point interpolation formula.

≈ 59 °C (the faulty instrument reads slightly high near mid-scale)

L4. [6] Distinguish carefully between heat, internal energy and temperature, with a numerical illustration using 1 kg of water and 1 kg of iron both at 80 °C.

Both bodies are at the same temperature, so a molecule of either has, on average, the same translational kinetic energy. But internal energy is the sum over all molecules — and, more importantly here, the two materials have very different specific heat capacities: 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water against 450 for iron.

Heat needed to cool each from 80 °C to 20 °C: water: Q = mcΔT = 1 × 4200 × 60 = 252 000 J iron: Q = mcΔT = 1 × 450 × 60 = 27 000 J

Same temperature, mass and temperature change, but the water releases nearly ten times as much heat, because its molecules store energy in rotational and vibrational modes and in hydrogen bonding, not only in translational motion. Heat itself is not held by either body: it is the energy that crosses the boundary during cooling.

Temperature equal, but internal energy and stored heat differ by the factor c_water/c_iron ≈ 9.3; heat is a transfer, internal energy a state function.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 9

1. "The body contains heat." Bodies contain internal energy; heat is energy in transit. Correct this in your own first sentence.

2. Confusing temperature with total energy. A bathtub at 40 °C holds more internal energy than a spark at 1000 °C. Temperature is intensive, energy extensive.

3. Saying heat flows "because the hot body has heat". Give the cause (temperature difference) and the molecular mechanism (energy transfer in collisions).

4. Forgetting the ice point/steam point conditions. They are defined at standard atmospheric pressure, with pure melting ice and steam above boiling water — not in boiling water itself.

5. Kalvin/Kelvin and Celsius slips. K = °C + 273, not the reverse; write the conversion line to show which direction you used.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Every definition should carry (i) the defining formula, (ii) the unit and (iii) one example. Three short lines earn the full 2 marks; a paragraph with no formula usually earns one.
