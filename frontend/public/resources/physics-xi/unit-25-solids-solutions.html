Unit 25 — Solids

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  3 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Only three hours, and the entire unit is one diagram: the energy-band diagram with its valence band, forbidden gap and conduction band. Everything else — metal versus insulator versus semiconductor, intrinsic versus extrinsic — is read off that diagram.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — what is an energy band; define valence and conduction band; why is a semiconductor called so; define intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductor; what is doping.
  • 4 marks — distinguish metals, insulators and semiconductors on the basis of band theory with a diagram; distinguish n-type and p-type semiconductors.
  • 5–6 marks — draw and explain the band diagrams of the three classes of solids; explain intrinsic and extrinsic conduction, including the effect of temperature and of doping concentration.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: the classification is by the size of the forbidden gap, not by "the material". Draw all three band diagrams side by side and label the gap in eV — the diagram earns most of the marks.

Exercise 25.1 — Energy bands in solids

Q1. What is an energy band? Explain how bands arise in a solid.

An energy band is a continuous range of closely spaced allowed energy levels that an electron may occupy in a solid.

In an isolated atom the electrons occupy discrete levels. When many atoms are brought together to form a crystal, the Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons in the same state, so each atomic level splits into as many very closely spaced levels as there are atoms. With about 10²³ atoms per cubic centimetre, these levels merge into an effectively continuous band.

Isolated atom: discrete levels N atoms: each level splits into N levels N ≈ 10²³: the levels are so close that the result is a continuous band

The bands are separated by forbidden gaps — ranges of energy in which no electron state exists.

A band is a continuous range of allowed electron energies formed by the splitting of atomic levels when atoms bond into a crystal; bands are separated by forbidden gaps.

Q2. Define the valence band, the conduction band and the forbidden gap.

TermMeaning
Valence bandThe highest energy band that is occupied by electrons at absolute zero; its electrons are the outer, bonding electrons
Conduction bandThe next higher band, normally empty; electrons in it are free to move and carry current
Forbidden gap (Eg)The energy range between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band, in which no electron states exist

Whether a solid conducts depends on how easily electrons can be promoted from the valence band to the conduction band — that is, on the width of the forbidden gap.

Valence band: filled, bonding electrons. Conduction band: empty, mobile electrons. Forbidden gap: the energy gap Eg between them.

Q3. Explain the terms "band gap energy" and "hole".

The band gap energy Eg is the minimum energy required to lift an electron from the top of the valence band to the bottom of the conduction band. For silicon Eg ≈ 1.1 eV, for germanium 0.67 eV, for diamond about 5.5 eV.

When an electron leaves the valence band, it leaves behind a vacant state. This vacancy behaves like a positive charge carrier — it is called a hole. Holes are not real particles but the collective effect of neighbouring electrons shifting into the vacancy; they nevertheless move through the crystal and contribute to current, in the direction opposite to electron flow.

Eg is the energy needed to free an electron from the valence band; the vacancy left behind is a hole, which acts as a positive mobile carrier.

Q4. Why is the conductivity of a solid determined by its highest occupied band?

Because current requires electrons that can gain a small amount of energy and move into a vacant nearby state. A completely filled band has no vacant states, so its electrons cannot move — a filled band carries no current. A completely empty band likewise carries none. Only a partially filled band, or electrons promoted into an empty band above a small gap, can conduct.

This is why metals (partially filled or overlapping bands) conduct, while insulators (full band, huge gap) do not.

Conduction needs both mobile carriers and available empty states; a full band or an empty band carries no current, so only partially filled bands or small-gap promotion conducts.

Exercise 25.2 — Metals, insulators and semiconductors

Q5. Using band theory, distinguish between metals, insulators and semiconductors.

ClassBand structureGap EgConductivity
Metal (conductor)The conduction band is partially filled, or it overlaps the valence band so there is no gap~0Very high, falls with temperature (σ ≈ 10⁷ S m⁻¹ for copper)
InsulatorValence band completely full, conduction band completely empty, separated by a large gap> 3 eV (diamond ≈ 5.5 eV)Extremely low (10⁻¹⁰–10⁻²⁰ S m⁻¹)
SemiconductorFull valence band and empty conduction band, as in an insulator, but the gap is small≈ 1 eV (Si 1.1, Ge 0.67)Intermediate, and rises sharply with temperature (10²–10⁴ S m⁻¹)

In a metal the carriers are always present, so conductivity changes only through scattering. In a semiconductor the number of carriers is thermally generated across the gap, so the conductivity depends exponentially on temperature:

σ ≈ σ₀ e^(−Eg/2kT)

Metals have no gap or an overlapping/partly filled band; insulators have a large gap; semiconductors have a small gap (~1 eV) that thermal energy can bridge.

Q6. Why does a semiconductor have negative temperature coefficient of resistance while a metal has positive?

In a metal the number of free electrons is essentially fixed by the atomic structure, and raising the temperature increases lattice vibrations, so the electrons are scattered more and the resistance rises — a positive temperature coefficient.

In a semiconductor the carrier number is set by thermal excitation across the band gap. Raising the temperature frees many more electron–hole pairs exponentially, and this increase in carriers outweighs the extra scattering, so the resistance falls — a negative temperature coefficient. The change is much larger: the resistance of a thermistor can halve for a 10 K rise.

Metal: fixed carriers, more scattering with T ⇒ R rises. Semiconductor: exponentially more carriers with T ⇒ R falls.

Q7. Why is diamond an insulator while graphite conducts, although both are carbon?

The difference is bonding. In diamond each carbon forms four strong σ bonds in a rigid tetrahedral lattice, and all four valence electrons are locked in these bonds: the valence band is completely full, the conduction band completely empty, and the gap is about 5.5 eV, so almost no electrons can be excited across it.

In graphite each carbon uses only three of its four valence electrons in σ bonds within a layer; the fourth is delocalised in a π band that is only partly filled. With a partly filled band and no gap, the electrons move freely along the layers and graphite conducts well.

Diamond: all four electrons localised in bonds, full band and 5.5 eV gap ⇒ insulator. Graphite: one delocalised electron in a partly filled band ⇒ conductor.

Q8. Silicon has a band gap of 1.1 eV. Find the maximum wavelength of light that can excite an electron across the gap.

E = hc/λ ⇒ λ = hc/E Using hc = 1240 eV nm: λ = 1240/1.1 = 1127 nm

1127 nm lies in the near infrared, just beyond the visible. So visible light (400–700 nm, i.e. 1.8–3.1 eV) has more than enough energy to bridge silicon's gap — which is why silicon solar cells and photodiodes respond to visible light. Germanium's 0.67 eV gap corresponds to 1850 nm, further into the infrared.

λ_max ≈ 1130 nm (infrared); visible photons carry enough energy, which is why silicon absorbs visible light in solar cells.

Exercise 25.3 — Intrinsic semiconductors

Q9. What is an intrinsic semiconductor? Explain its conduction mechanism.

An intrinsic (pure) semiconductor is one with no deliberate impurity — for example pure silicon or germanium, with impurity concentrations below about 1 part in 10⁹.

At absolute zero all electrons are in the valence band and the crystal is an insulator. At room temperature a small fraction of electrons gain thermal energy greater than Eg and are promoted to the conduction band, leaving an equal number of holes in the valence band. Both species move under an applied field: electrons drift one way and holes the other, so both contribute to the current.

In an intrinsic semiconductor: n_e = n_h = n_i Conductivity: σ = e(n_e μ_e + n_h μ_h) with n_i rising exponentially with T.

A pure semiconductor conducts by thermally generated electron–hole pairs in equal numbers (n_e = n_h); its conductivity rises steeply with temperature.

Q10. Why is the conductivity of an intrinsic semiconductor small at room temperature?

Because the number of electrons with enough thermal energy to cross the gap is exponentially small. At 300 K, kT ≈ 0.026 eV, while silicon's gap is 1.1 eV — about 42 kT. The fraction of electrons excited is therefore roughly e^(−Eg/2kT) ≈ e^(−21) ≈ 10⁻⁹, so only about one electron in a thousand million is freed. The carrier density is around 10¹⁶ m⁻³ compared with about 10²⁹ m⁻³ in a metal, which is why pure silicon conducts so poorly.

Only a fraction e^(−Eg/2kT) ≈ 10⁻⁹ of electrons is excited at room temperature, so the carrier density — and hence the conductivity — is very low.

Q11. Why does an electron–hole pair recombine, and what does it release?

An electron in the conduction band can fall back into a vacant hole in the valence band, and the two carriers disappear — this is recombination. The energy released (about Eg) appears either as a photon (radiative recombination, the basis of LEDs and laser diodes) or as heat through lattice vibrations (non-radiative recombination). In equilibrium the generation rate of pairs balances the recombination rate, which is why the carrier density stays constant at a given temperature.

A conduction electron falls into a hole and the pair annihilates, releasing about Eg as a photon (LEDs) or as heat; generation and recombination balance in equilibrium.

Exercise 25.4 — Extrinsic semiconductors and doping

Q12. What is doping? What is an extrinsic semiconductor?

Doping is the deliberate addition of a small controlled amount of an impurity (typically 1 atom in 10⁶) to a pure semiconductor in order to increase its conductivity. A semiconductor that has been doped is called extrinsic.

Two types exist:

  • n-type: doped with a pentavalent element (P, As, Sb) — a donor — which contributes extra electrons.
  • p-type: doped with a trivalent element (B, Al, Ga, In) — an acceptor — which contributes extra holes.

Doping increases the carrier density by many orders of magnitude (from about 10¹⁶ to 10²² m⁻³), so the conductivity rises enormously while the crystal remains electrically neutral overall.

Doping is the controlled addition of a tiny impurity concentration; an extrinsic semiconductor is a doped one, n-type (donor, extra electrons) or p-type (acceptor, extra holes).

Q13. Explain how silicon is doped to make an n-type semiconductor.

Silicon is tetravalent: each atom forms four covalent bonds with its neighbours. If a pentavalent atom such as phosphorus (five valence electrons) replaces a silicon atom, four of its electrons complete the bonds and the fifth is left loosely bound — it needs only about 0.045 eV to be freed into the conduction band, far less than the 1.1 eV band gap.

So each donor atom supplies one nearly free electron. The electrons greatly outnumber the holes, so they are the majority carriers and holes are the minority carriers. The material is called n-type (negative, for the majority carrier), even though it is electrically neutral: the extra electron is balanced by the fixed positive charge of the phosphorus ion in the lattice.

A pentavalent donor contributes a fifth electron bound by only ~0.05 eV; these electrons become the majority carriers, so the crystal is n-type but still electrically neutral.

Q14. Explain how a p-type semiconductor is made and why it is neutral.

If a trivalent atom such as boron (three valence electrons) replaces a silicon atom, only three of the four surrounding bonds can be completed; the fourth is missing an electron, creating a hole. A nearby valence electron needs only about 0.045 eV to fill it, so the hole is mobile at room temperature.

Each acceptor atom therefore supplies one hole; holes become the majority carriers and electrons the minority carriers, so the material is p-type (positive). It remains electrically neutral: the hole is the absence of an electron, and the boron atom has one fewer proton than silicon, so the charges balance exactly. A p-type crystal is not positively charged — its name refers to the sign of its mobile carriers.

A trivalent acceptor creates a mobile hole bound by only ~0.05 eV; holes are the majority carriers, and the crystal remains neutral because the missing electron is balanced by the missing proton.

n-type: donor-doped, electrons majority, Fermi level near the conduction band. p-type: acceptor-doped, holes majority, Fermi level near the valence band. Both are electrically neutral.

Q16. Why does doping increase conductivity so dramatically?

Because the dopant atoms introduce shallow energy levels only about 0.05 eV from the nearest band, so almost every dopant atom is ionised at room temperature (kT ≈ 0.026 eV is comparable), whereas only about one atom in 10⁹ is thermally excited across the 1.1 eV gap of pure silicon. Doping at 1 part in 10⁶ therefore increases the carrier density by a factor of roughly 10⁶ to 10⁹, and since σ = neμ, the conductivity rises by the same enormous factor.

Dopant levels lie only ~0.05 eV from a band, so nearly all dopant atoms contribute carriers — increasing n by 10⁶–10⁹ and the conductivity in proportion.

Q17. Does doping change the band gap of a semiconductor?

Essentially no. Doping at the usual level of about 1 part in 10⁶ introduces only isolated impurity levels inside the gap; the valence and conduction bands themselves, and the gap between them, are unchanged. Heavy doping can slightly narrow the effective gap and shift the absorption edge, but that is a second-order effect. What doping changes is not the gap but the number of available carriers.

No — doping adds shallow levels within the gap and changes the carrier concentration, not the band gap itself.

Exercise 25.5 — Temperature, conductivity and applications

Q18. Describe how the conductivity of an extrinsic semiconductor varies with temperature.

Three regimes appear:

  1. Low temperature (freeze-out): too little thermal energy even to ionise the dopants, so few carriers are available and the conductivity is low.
  2. Room-temperature (extrinsic) range: all dopant atoms are ionised, so the carrier density is essentially constant and equal to the doping level; the conductivity falls slightly with temperature because lattice scattering increases.
  3. High temperature (intrinsic) range: thermal excitation across the band gap dominates the dopant contribution, so the material behaves like a pure semiconductor and the conductivity rises steeply. This sets the upper temperature limit for a semiconductor device.

Freeze-out at low T, a nearly constant extrinsic plateau at room temperature, then an intrinsic rise at high T; this limits the operating temperature of devices.

Q19. Why is silicon preferred to germanium for most semiconductor devices?

Silicon's larger band gap (1.1 eV against 0.67 eV) means far fewer thermally generated carriers at room temperature, so leakage current is thousands of times smaller and devices work at higher temperatures (up to about 150 °C against 70 °C for germanium). Silicon also forms a very stable, adherent native oxide (SiO₂), which makes passivation and the planar fabrication process straightforward, and it is abundant and cheap.

Silicon's wider gap gives much lower leakage and a higher working temperature, and its stable oxide makes device fabrication easy — hence its dominance.

Q20. Give three practical uses of semiconductors and state the property each relies on.

DeviceProperty used
Diode / rectifierA p–n junction conducts in one direction only, converting AC to DC
Light-emitting diode (LED)Recombination of electrons and holes emits photons of energy ≈ Eg, so the colour is set by the band gap
Solar cell / photodiodePhotons with energy above Eg create electron–hole pairs that are separated by a junction to give current
ThermistorStrong negative temperature coefficient of resistance (temperature sensing, inrush limiting)
Transistor / integrated circuitDoped regions control current flow, giving amplification and logic

Diodes (one-way conduction), LEDs and solar cells (photon–carrier coupling across Eg), thermistors (temperature-dependent carrier density), and transistors/ICs (controlled doping).

Q21. What is the difference between the conductivity of a metal and of a semiconductor at absolute zero?

At 0 K a metal still has a partly filled conduction band, so its electrons remain free to move — it stays a conductor (superconductors aside, its conductivity is high). An intrinsic semiconductor at 0 K has a completely full valence band and a completely empty conduction band, so there are no mobile carriers at all — it becomes a perfect insulator. Doped semiconductors at 0 K have their carriers frozen onto the dopant atoms and are also insulating.

Metal: still conducting (partly filled band). Pure semiconductor: perfect insulator (full band, empty band, no carriers).

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Solids questions use three different models on the same material — elastic (stress and strain), structural (unit cells and density) and transport (conductivity and band gaps). The eight shapes below exercise each model and then join them.

Q22. [Multi-step numerical] A wire 2.0 m long and 0.50 mm in diameter stretches by 1.0 mm under a load. Find the strain, the stress if Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa, the force, and the elastic energy stored.

Area: A = πr² = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m² Strain: e = ΔL/L = 1.0 × 10⁻³/2.0 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ Stress: σ = Y e = 2.0 × 10¹¹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.0 × 10⁸ Pa Force: F = σA = 1.0 × 10⁸ × 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ = 19.6 N ≈ 20 N Energy: U = ½FΔL = ½ × 19.6 × 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 9.8 × 10⁻³ J ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻² J

Two orders of magnitude are worth noticing: a 20 N load (2 kg) on a steel wire produces a stress of 10⁸ Pa, only about 0.2% of steel's breaking stress, which is why a steel cable can support tonnes. The energy ½FΔL is the same ½ke² form as a spring, with k = YA/L — the wire is a spring of stiffness 2 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹.

e = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴; σ = 1.0 × 10⁸ Pa; F ≈ 20 N; U ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻² J.

Q23. [Derivation] Derive the relation between the density of a cubic crystal, the unit-cell edge a and the number of atoms per cell n.

For the cell: volume = a³, mass = n m_atom = n(M/N_A) ρ = n M/(N_A a³) ⇒ a³ = n M/(N_A ρ) and a = [nM/(N_A ρ)]^(1/3) where M is the molar mass and n the number of atoms per unit cell (n = 1 simple cubic, 2 bcc, 4 fcc).

This one formula answers the standard NEB question in either direction: given a and ρ, find the lattice type (the calculated n identifies simple, bcc or fcc); given the lattice type, find a. For copper (fcc, M = 0.0635 kg mol⁻¹, ρ = 8960 kg m⁻³): a³ = 4 × 0.0635/(6.02 × 10²³ × 8960) = 4.71 × 10⁻²⁹ m³, so a = 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁰ m — the accepted value.

ρ = nM/(N_A a³), so a = [nM/(N_Aρ)]^(1/3), with n = 1, 2 or 4 for simple, bcc or fcc.

Q24. [Graph reading] The stress–strain graph of a metal rises linearly to 2.5 × 10⁸ Pa at a strain of 1.2 × 10⁻³, then curves to a maximum of 4.0 × 10⁸ Pa at 0.15 before breaking. Find Young's modulus, the yield strength and the ductility.

Linear region: Y = stress/strain = 2.5 × 10⁸/1.2 × 10⁻³ = 2.1 × 10¹¹ Pa Yield strength ≈ 2.5 × 10⁸ Pa (where the curve departs from the straight line) Ultimate strength = 4.0 × 10⁸ Pa Ductility = strain at break = 0.15 = 15% Energy absorbed per unit volume ≈ area under the curve ≈ ½(2.5 × 10⁸)(1.2 × 10⁻³) + 4.0 × 10⁸ × 0.15 ≈ 1.5 × 10⁵ + 6.0 × 10⁷ ≈ 6 × 10⁷ J m⁻³

The three regions visible on any such graph are elastic (recoverable, linear), plastic (permanent, work-hardening) and fracture. A large plastic region before break is what makes a material ductile and tough — the energy absorbed per unit volume is the area under the curve, which is why a tough metal (mild steel) is safer than a strong but brittle one (cast iron) in a structure subject to impact.

Y = 2.1 × 10¹¹ Pa; yield 2.5 × 10⁸ Pa; ultimate 4.0 × 10⁸ Pa; ductility 15%, so the metal is tough rather than brittle.

Q25. [Combined concept] Silicon has a band gap of 1.1 eV. Find the minimum wavelength of light it can absorb, and explain why a silicon solar cell responds poorly to red light.

E = hc/λ ⇒ λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)(3.0 × 10⁸)/(1.1 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1.99 × 10⁻²⁵/1.76 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ m = 1100 nm

So silicon absorbs all wavelengths shorter than 1100 nm (visible to the edge of the infrared) and is transparent to longer ones. Red light at 700 nm has E = 1.8 eV — above the gap, so it IS absorbed, but only just: the excess 0.7 eV becomes heat. Infrared beyond 1100 nm is not absorbed at all — the photon cannot promote an electron across the gap.

Two effects limit the cell: photons with less than 1.1 eV pass straight through (about 25% of the solar spectrum), and photons with much more than 1.1 eV waste the excess as heat. The Shockley–Queisser limit — around 33% for a single-junction cell — comes from exactly these two loss channels, and it is the reason tandem cells with several gaps are used in high-efficiency panels.

λ_max ≈ 1.1 μm (1100 nm); red light is absorbed but inefficiently, and longer-wavelength infrared passes straight through.

Q26. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): The electrical conductivity of a semiconductor increases with temperature, while that of a metal decreases. Reason (R): In a semiconductor the number of charge carriers increases with temperature, and this outweighs the reduced mobility.

Both statements are true and R explains A.

METAL: n is fixed (≈ 10²⁸ m⁻³), and lattice vibrations grow with T, so τ falls: σ = ne²τ/m ⇒ σ decreases with T (roughly as 1/T) SEMICONDUCTOR: n ∝ exp(−E_g/2kT) grows exponentially, far faster than τ falls: σ = ne²τ/m ⇒ σ INCREASES with T For silicon, σ roughly doubles for every 8 K near room temperature.

The exponential wins because it is exponential — the same reason a thermistor is so much more sensitive than a metal resistance thermometer. This contrast (negative temperature coefficient for semiconductors, positive for metals) is one of the most examinable single facts in the chapter, and it is the whole basis of the thermistor and of the temperature drift in a transistor.

Both true, R explains A: for a semiconductor the exponential rise in carrier number dominates the fall in mobility, reversing the sign of dσ/dT.

Q28. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the interatomic spacing in copper from its density and molar mass, and state what the result implies about the size of an atom.

Volume per atom = M/(N_A ρ) = 0.0635/(6.02 × 10²³ × 8960) = 1.18 × 10⁻²⁹ m³ Spacing ≈ (V)^(1/3) = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.23 nm (For the fcc lattice, a = 0.361 nm and the nearest-neighbour distance is a/√2 = 0.255 nm.)

Limiting case: as the atoms are squeezed closer, the repulsive core of the interatomic potential eventually dominates — which is why solids are nearly incompressible and why the spacing cannot fall much below 0.2 nm without enormous pressure. This same length is what sets the wavelength of X-rays used in crystallography (0.1 nm) and the resolution of any microscope: you cannot see detail smaller than the wavelength you probe with.

Compare with the gas result earlier in the course: molecules in air at STP are about 3 nm apart, roughly ten times the solid spacing — which is the quantitative meaning of “a gas is mostly empty space”.

Spacing ≈ 0.23 nm (nearest-neighbour 0.26 nm in the fcc lattice); repulsion at that core radius makes solids nearly incompressible.

Q29. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “Young's modulus has the unit N.”
(ii) “A material with a high Young's modulus is necessarily strong.”
(iii) “Adding impurity atoms to a metal always increases its conductivity.”
(iv) “A semiconductor and an insulator differ only in how they are made.”

(i) FALSE — Y = stress/strain, so its unit is N m⁻² (Pa), like stress. Stress has the unit of pressure; strain is dimensionless. (ii) FALSE — the modulus measures STIFFNESS, not strength. Glass has a high Y but breaks at low stress (brittle); rubber has a tiny Y but stretches enormously. Stiffness, strength and toughness are three different properties. (iii) FALSE — impurities generally SCATTER electrons and raise resistivity (that is why copper wire is 99.99% pure). The exception is deliberate doping of a semiconductor, where the carrier number increases enormously and conductivity does rise — but that is not a metal. (iv) FALSE — they differ in the SIZE OF THE BAND GAP: roughly 1 eV for a semiconductor and 5 eV or more for an insulator. At ordinary temperatures a 1 eV gap puts a measurable number of electrons in the conduction band; a 5 eV gap puts essentially none.

Statement (ii) and (iii) are the two ideas most often collapsed: stiffness is not strength, and a dopant that helps a semiconductor ruins a metal.

(i) Y is in Pa; (ii) stiffness ≠ strength; (iii) impurities raise a metal's resistivity; (iv) the band gap is what separates the two.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. What is an energy band?

A continuous range of allowed electron energies in a solid, formed because each atomic level splits into as many closely spaced levels as there are atoms in the crystal. Bands are separated by forbidden gaps.

C2. Define the forbidden gap.

The energy range between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band in which no electron states exist. Its width Eg determines whether the solid is a metal, insulator or semiconductor.

C3. Why is a metal a good conductor?

Because its conduction band is only partly filled (or overlaps the valence band), so there is no gap to cross and plenty of empty states adjacent in energy. Its electrons are free to accelerate under a small applied field.

C4. Why is an insulator a poor conductor?

Because its valence band is completely full and its conduction band completely empty, separated by a large gap (more than about 3 eV, and 5.5 eV for diamond). Thermal energy at room temperature (0.026 eV) cannot promote electrons across that gap, so there are essentially no mobile carriers.

C5. Why is a semiconductor so called?

Because its conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator — it conducts, but poorly, and its conductivity rises steeply with temperature. Its band gap is small (~1 eV), so a modest amount of thermal or optical energy frees carriers.

C6. What is a hole?

A vacant state in the valence band left behind when an electron is promoted to the conduction band. It behaves like a mobile positive charge, moving in the direction opposite to the electrons that fill it in turn.

C7. Define intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors.

Intrinsic: a pure semiconductor with no deliberate impurity, conducting by thermally generated electron–hole pairs in equal numbers. Extrinsic: a doped semiconductor, whose conductivity is set mainly by the deliberately added donor or acceptor impurities.

C8. What is doping, and why is it used?

The deliberate addition of a small controlled concentration (about 1 in 10⁶) of a suitable impurity to a pure semiconductor. It introduces shallow levels that supply many more carriers, increasing the conductivity by factors of 10⁶–10⁹.

C9. Why is an n-type semiconductor electrically neutral?

Because each pentavalent donor atom contributes one extra electron but also one extra proton, so the added charge is exactly balanced. The material's name refers to the sign of its majority carriers, not to a net charge.

C10. What are majority and minority carriers?

Majority carriers are the dominant mobile charges — electrons in n-type material, holes in p-type. Minority carriers are the opposite type, present in much smaller numbers (holes in n-type, electrons in p-type) because they are still thermally generated across the band gap.

C11. Why does the resistance of a semiconductor fall as it is heated?

Because heating excites exponentially more electrons across the band gap, so the number of carriers rises faster than the extra lattice scattering reduces their mobility. The net effect is a strong decrease in resistance — a negative temperature coefficient.

C12. State the relation between band gap and the colour of an LED.

Recombination across the gap emits photons of energy hf ≈ Eg, so λ = hc/Eg. A larger gap gives a shorter wavelength: infrared for GaAs (1.4 eV), red for GaAsP (~1.9 eV), blue for InGaN (~2.7 eV).

C13. Why does a semiconductor become an insulator at absolute zero?

Because at 0 K there is no thermal energy to excite electrons across the gap, so the valence band is completely full and the conduction band completely empty. With no mobile carriers, the conductivity is zero.

C14. What is the effect of temperature on a metal's resistance and on a thermistor's? Why the difference?

Metal resistance rises with temperature because its fixed carrier density is scattered more by stronger lattice vibrations. A thermistor's resistance falls because its carrier density is thermally generated and grows exponentially with temperature, outweighing the extra scattering.

C15. What is a p–n junction, and why does it conduct in only one direction?

The boundary between p-type and n-type regions of the same crystal. Diffusion of electrons and holes across it leaves a depletion region with a built-in field that opposes further diffusion. Forward bias reduces this barrier so current flows; reverse bias increases it so only a tiny leakage current passes.

C16. Why is germanium less suitable than silicon for high-temperature devices?

Because its band gap is smaller (0.67 eV against 1.1 eV), so thermal generation of carriers at a given temperature is far greater. Leakage current is much larger and the intrinsic (runaway) regime is reached at a lower temperature — about 70 °C compared with 150 °C for silicon.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Draw the energy-band diagrams for a metal, an insulator and a semiconductor, and explain each class in terms of the diagrams.

Metal: the conduction band is partly filled (or the conduction and valence bands overlap so that there is no gap). Empty states lie immediately above the occupied ones, so even a tiny applied field accelerates the electrons. Conductivity is high and falls slowly with temperature as scattering increases.

Insulator: a completely full valence band, a completely empty conduction band, and a large forbidden gap — 5.5 eV for diamond, more than about 3 eV for insulators generally. At room temperature kT ≈ 0.026 eV, which is negligible against such a gap, so essentially no electrons are excited and the solid does not conduct.

Semiconductor: the same full/empty arrangement, but the gap is small — 1.1 eV for silicon and 0.67 eV for germanium. Thermal energy can excite a small but significant fraction of electrons across it, creating equal numbers of electrons and holes; the conductivity is therefore moderate and rises steeply with temperature (σ ∝ e^(−Eg/2kT)).

Band gap values: metal ~0; Si 1.1 eV; Ge 0.67 eV; diamond 5.5 eV

Same diagram, different gap: no gap (metal), huge gap (insulator), about 1 eV (semiconductor). The gap width alone decides the class.

L2. [5] Explain intrinsic and extrinsic conduction in a semiconductor, and distinguish n-type from p-type material.

Intrinsic: in a pure crystal the only carriers are electron–hole pairs generated thermally across the band gap. Since each excitation creates one of each, n_e = n_h, and the conductivity σ = e(n_eμ_e + n_hμ_h) rises exponentially with temperature. The carrier density is very low at room temperature (about 10¹⁶ m⁻³), so conduction is weak.

Extrinsic: doping at about 1 part in 10⁶ replaces some host atoms. A pentavalent donor (P, As, Sb) contributes an electron bound by only ~0.05 eV, which is ionised at room temperature; electrons then dominate and the material is n-type. A trivalent acceptor (B, Al, Ga) creates a hole bound by the same small energy, so holes dominate and the material is p-type. The carrier density rises to about 10²² m⁻³ and the conductivity with it, by many orders of magnitude.

Both doped types remain electrically neutral, because the dopant atom's extra electron (or missing electron) is balanced by its extra (or missing) proton. The effect of doping is thus to change the carrier concentration and type, not the net charge.

Intrinsic: thermally generated pairs, n_e = n_h, low conductivity. Extrinsic: shallow dopant levels give a 10⁶–10⁹ increase in carriers; donors give n-type (electrons), acceptors give p-type (holes), and both stay neutral.

L3. [4] A semiconductor has a band gap of 2.0 eV. Find the minimum frequency and maximum wavelength of radiation that can create an electron–hole pair, and state the region of the spectrum.

E = hf ⇒ f = E/h = (2.0 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹/6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 4.83 × 10¹⁴ Hz λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸/4.83 × 10¹⁴ = 6.21 × 10⁻⁷ m = 621 nm (or λ = 1240/E = 1240/2.0 = 620 nm)

620 nm is orange-red in the visible spectrum, so photons of that colour or shorter (higher energy) can create pairs; longer wavelengths such as infrared cannot.

f ≥ 4.8 × 10¹⁴ Hz; λ ≤ 620 nm — visible orange-red light, so the material responds to visible light and could be used in a photodetector or LED of that colour.

L4. [4] Why does the conductivity of a doped semiconductor stay roughly constant at room temperature but rise sharply when it is strongly heated?

At room temperature every dopant atom is already ionised, because the dopant level is only about 0.05 eV from the band and kT ≈ 0.026 eV is comparable. The carrier density is therefore fixed by the doping concentration, and the conductivity changes only weakly (it falls slightly as lattice scattering increases with temperature). This is the extrinsic plateau.

As the temperature rises further, thermal excitation across the full band gap (about 1.1 eV for silicon) becomes significant. Its rate grows as e^(−Eg/2kT), so the intrinsic carrier density eventually exceeds the doping density and the conductivity climbs steeply. Devices must therefore be operated below the temperature at which intrinsic conduction takes over.

Extrinsic plateau: all dopants ionised, carrier density fixed by doping. Above a certain temperature, intrinsic excitation across Eg dominates and conductivity rises exponentially.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 25

1. Saying a semiconductor has a partially filled band. In an intrinsic semiconductor the valence band is full and the conduction band empty; what distinguishes it from an insulator is only the small gap.

2. Claiming n-type material is negatively charged. It is electrically neutral; "n" refers to the majority carrier (electrons), not to a net charge.

3. Confusing the temperature dependence. Metals: R rises with T. Semiconductors: R falls with T. Reversing this loses the explanation mark even if the graph is right.

4. Omitting the diagram. Band questions are marked on the diagram: three bands drawn, gap labelled in eV, filled/empty states shown. A text-only answer usually loses half the marks.

5. Saying doping changes the band gap. Doping adds shallow levels inside the gap and changes the carrier concentration; the gap itself is essentially unchanged.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Always draw the three band diagrams first, then write the explanation against them. Quote the gap in eV, state whether each band is full, empty or partly filled, and name the majority carrier for any doped material.
