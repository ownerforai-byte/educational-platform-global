Unit 11 — Quantity of Heat

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  6 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Quantity of Heat carries the paper's most predictable numerical: a calorimetry balance, or a Newton's-law-of-cooling problem. Both are worked out step by step below, together with the latent-heat questions that most students lose marks on.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define specific heat capacity and latent heat; state Newton's law of cooling; why does the temperature stay constant during melting; define the triple point.
  • 4 marks — calorimetry: method of mixtures to find a specific heat or a latent heat; Newton's law of cooling numerical.
  • 5–6 marks — describe an experiment to measure the specific latent heat of fusion of ice (or vaporisation of water); explain the cooling curve of a substance with all three phases.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: in calorimetry, always write the energy balance before substituting — heat lost by the hot bodies = heat gained by the cold bodies (including the calorimeter). Missing the calorimeter term is the single commonest error of the unit.

Exercise 11.1 — Specific heat capacity and the method of mixtures

Q1. Define specific heat capacity and molar heat capacity. How much heat is needed to raise 2 kg of water from 20 °C to 80 °C? (c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)

Specific heat capacity c is the heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass of a substance by 1 K: c = Q/(mΔT), in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Molar heat capacity C = Q/(nΔT), in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, where n is the number of moles.

Q = mcΔT = 2 × 4200 × 60 = 504 000 J = 5.04 × 10⁵ J

5.04 × 10⁵ J (≈ 504 kJ)

Q2. A 100 g iron ball at 200 °C is dropped into 200 g of water at 20 °C. Find the final temperature. (c_iron = 450 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, c_water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)

Heat lost by iron = heat gained by water 0.1 × 450 × (200 − θ) = 0.2 × 4200 × (θ − 20) 45(200 − θ) = 840(θ − 20) 9000 − 45θ = 840θ − 16 800 25 800 = 885θ θ = 29.2 °C

θ ≈ 29 °C — the water's large specific heat keeps the final value close to its starting temperature

Q3. Describe the method of mixtures for finding the specific heat capacity of a solid.

Apparatus: a calorimeter of known mass and specific heat, a thermometer, a steam jacket, a balance, water.

1. Weigh the calorimeter (m₁) and fill it with water; weigh again to obtain m_w (water mass). 2. Note the initial temperature θ₁ of the calorimeter and water. 3. Weigh a few metal shots (m₂), heat them in the steam jacket to θ₂ = 100 °C. 4. Transfer them quickly into the calorimeter, stir and record the maximum temperature θ₃. 5. Energy balance: m₂ c_s (θ₂ − θ₃) = (m₁c_c + m_w c_w)(θ₃ − θ₁) ⇒ c_s = (m₁c_c + m_w c_w)(θ₃ − θ₁) / [m₂(θ₂ − θ₃)]

Precautions: transfer as quickly as possible to avoid loss, stir gently, and note the maximum (not a later) reading. The calorimeter term must be included because the vessel itself absorbs heat.

c_s = (m₁c_c + m_wc_w)(θ₃ − θ₁)/[m₂(θ₂ − θ₃)], obtained from the equality of heat lost and heat gained.

Q4. An electric heater of 500 W is used to heat 1 kg of water. Find the time needed to raise its temperature from 20 °C to 60 °C, assuming all the energy enters the water.

Q = mcΔT = 1 × 4200 × 40 = 168 000 J t = Q/P = 168 000/500 = 336 s

336 s ≈ 5.6 min

Q5. Why is the specific heat capacity of water unusually large, and what are two consequences?

Because energy given to water is stored not only in translational motion but also in rotational and vibrational modes and in the hydrogen-bond network, so a large energy input produces only a small temperature rise (4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ against about 450 for iron).

Consequences: (i) water is an excellent coolant — radiators and the sea can absorb large quantities of heat with little temperature change; (ii) coastal climates are mild because the sea moderates temperature changes between day and night and between seasons.

Large c because of extra internal modes and hydrogen bonding; hence water is a good coolant and the sea stabilises coastal climate.

Exercise 11.2 — Newton's law of cooling

Q6. State Newton's law of cooling and explain why it holds only for small temperature differences.

Statement: the rate of loss of heat from a body is directly proportional to the excess of its temperature over that of its surroundings, provided the excess is small:

dQ/dt = −k(θ − θ₀) ⇒ (θ₁ − θ₂)/t = k[(θ₁ + θ₂)/2 − θ₀]

For large excesses, radiation (∝ T⁴) and natural convection (∝ ΔT^1.25) dominate and the loss is no longer proportional to ΔT, so the linear law fails.

Rate of cooling ∝ temperature excess; valid for small excesses, where the T⁴ radiation law linearises and convection stays laminar.

Q7. A body cools from 60 °C to 50 °C in 10 minutes when the room is at 25 °C. Find the time taken to cool from 50 °C to 42 °C.

First interval: average excess = (60 + 50)/2 − 25 = 55 − 25 = 30 K (60 − 50)/10 = k × 30 ⇒ k = 1/30 per minute Second interval: average excess = (50 + 42)/2 − 25 = 46 − 25 = 21 K (50 − 42)/t = (1/30)(21) = 0.7 ⇒ t = 8/0.7 = 11.4 min

≈ 11 min — cooling slows as the excess temperature falls

Q8. Derive Newton's law of cooling from Stefan–Boltzmann law for a body at temperature θ in surroundings at θ₀ with a small excess.

Net radiated power: P = eσA(T⁴ − T₀⁴), with T = T₀ + ΔT T⁴ − T₀⁴ = (T₀ + ΔT)⁴ − T₀⁴ ≈ 4T₀³ΔT (binomial, ΔT ≪ T₀) So dQ/dt = 4eσA T₀³ ΔT = kΔT, a constant k for given surroundings.

For small excess the T⁴ law linearises into dQ/dt ∝ ΔT, which is Newton's law of cooling; the constant k ∝ T₀³ and depends on the body's area and emissivity.

Q9. A liquid cools from 70 °C to 60 °C in 5 minutes and from 60 °C to 52 °C in the next 5 minutes. Find the temperature of the surroundings.

Let the room temperature be θ₀, and k the same in both intervals. Interval 1: (70 − 60)/5 = k[(70 + 60)/2 − θ₀] ⇒ 2 = k(65 − θ₀) Interval 2: (60 − 52)/5 = k[(60 + 52)/2 − θ₀] ⇒ 1.6 = k(56 − θ₀) Dividing: 2/1.6 = (65 − θ₀)/(56 − θ₀) 1.25(56 − θ₀) = 65 − θ₀ 70 − 1.25θ₀ = 65 − θ₀ ⇒ 5 = 0.25θ₀ ⇒ θ₀ = 20 °C

Room temperature 20 °C

Q10. State two applications and two limitations of Newton's law of cooling.

Applications: (i) determining the specific heat capacity of a liquid by the rate-of-cooling method; (ii) estimating the time of death in forensic science from the cooling of a body, and designing cooling fins and radiators.

Limitations: (i) it holds only while the temperature excess is small, since radiation and convection are non-linear; (ii) it assumes the surroundings' temperature stays constant and the body's own temperature is uniform throughout (no internal gradients), so it fails for large or poorly conducting bodies.

Used for specific heat and cooling-time estimates; valid only for small excesses with uniform body temperature and a constant ambient.

Exercise 11.3 — Change of phase and latent heat

Q11. Define specific latent heat of fusion and of vaporization, and give their values for water.

Specific latent heat of fusion L_f: the heat required to change unit mass of a solid into liquid at its melting point without any change of temperature — for ice, 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ (80 cal g⁻¹).

Specific latent heat of vaporization L_v: the heat required to change unit mass of liquid into vapour at its boiling point without a temperature change — for water, 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ (540 cal g⁻¹).

Q = mL

Q = mL; for water L_f = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ and L_v = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹.

Q12. 10 g of ice at 0 °C is added to 100 g of water at 30 °C. Find the final temperature. (L_f = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹)

Heat available from cooling the water to 0 °C: Q = 0.1 × 4200 × 30 = 12 600 J Heat to melt all the ice: Q_f = 0.010 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 3340 J 12 600 > 3340, so all the ice melts and the mixture is still above 0 °C. Remaining heat = 12 600 − 3340 = 9260 J, warming 110 g of water: ΔT = 9260/(0.110 × 4200) = 9260/462 = 20.0 K Final temperature = 0 + 20.0 = 20 °C

θ = 20 °C; the meltwater ends up at the same temperature as the remaining warm water.

Q13. Explain, with numbers, why steam at 100 °C causes a more severe burn than water at 100 °C.

1 g of steam at 100 °C condensing on skin releases L_v = 2260 J The condensed water then cools from 100 °C to skin temperature (≈ 37 °C): Q = 0.001 × 4200 × 63 = 265 J Total ≈ 2525 J 1 g of water at 100 °C only cools to 37 °C: Q = 265 J Ratio ≈ 2525/265 ≈ 9.5

Steam delivers about 10 times more energy because it first gives up its large latent heat of vaporization; the burn is roughly tenfold worse.

Q14. Why does the temperature remain constant during a change of phase, even though heat is being supplied?

Because the supplied energy goes into increasing the potential energy of the molecules, not their average kinetic energy. In melting, the ordered lattice is broken and molecules gain freedom; in vaporization, the molecules are separated against intermolecular attraction. Since temperature measures the average translational kinetic energy, and that does not change while the phase change proceeds, the thermometer reading stays fixed.

The latent heat does work against intermolecular forces (changing potential energy), so the mean kinetic energy — and hence the temperature — stays constant.

Q15. Distinguish between evaporation and boiling.

EvaporationBoiling
Occurs at the surface onlyOccurs throughout the liquid (bubbles form inside)
Takes place at any temperatureTakes place at one temperature for a given pressure (the boiling point)
Rate increases with temperature, surface area and air flow; decreases with humidityRate is set by the heat supply; the temperature stays constant
Involves only the most energetic molecules escapingRequires the saturated vapour pressure to equal the external pressure
A cooling process for the remaining liquidRequires a sustained energy supply

Evaporation is surface-only, gradual and temperature-dependent; boiling is bulk, occurs at a fixed temperature and needs the vapour pressure to equal the external pressure.

Q16. Why is the latent heat of vaporization of water much larger than its latent heat of fusion?

Melting only loosens the rigid lattice — the molecules stay in contact, held by hydrogen bonds — so the energy needed is moderate (3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹). Vaporization must separate the molecules completely against those same hydrogen bonds and against atmospheric pressure, increasing their separation by a factor of about 1600; the energy required is therefore much larger (2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹), roughly 7 times L_f.

Melting breaks the lattice order only; vaporization must overcome the full intermolecular attraction and do expansion work, so L_v ≈ 7 L_f.

Exercise 11.4 — Measurement of specific latent heat

Q17. Describe an experiment to measure the specific latent heat of fusion of ice by the method of mixtures.

Apparatus: calorimeter, balance, thermometer, ice at 0 °C, filter paper, water.

1. Weigh the empty calorimeter, m₁, with its specific heat c_c. 2. Half-fill with warm water (about 40 °C); weigh to obtain m_w and note θ₁. 3. Dry pieces of ice (at 0 °C) on filter paper and add them gradually, stirring. 4. When the temperature has fallen to θ₂ (still above 0 °C, all ice melted), weigh again → m_ice. 5. Energy balance: heat lost by water + calorimeter = heat gained by melting ice + warming the meltwater (m₁c_c + m_w c_w)(θ₁ − θ₂) = m_ice L + m_ice c_w θ₂ ⇒ L = [(m₁c_c + m_w c_w)(θ₁ − θ₂) − m_ice c_w θ₂] / m_ice

Precautions: the ice must be at 0 °C and dried, all of it must melt, the final temperature must be above 0 °C, and stirring must be gentle.

L = [(m₁c_c + m_wc_w)(θ₁ − θ₂)/m_ice] − c_wθ₂, giving ≈ 3.3 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ for ice.

Q18. Describe how the specific latent heat of vaporization of water can be measured with an electric heater.

1. Place a known mass of water in a calorimeter and boil it steadily with an immersion heater. 2. Record the mass lost (Δm) in a measured time t, and the power P of the heater. 3. The heat supplied = Pt = Δm L_v (the water is already at 100 °C, so no sensible heat is needed once boiling is established). ⇒ L_v = Pt/Δm 4. A more accurate version subtracts the heat lost to the surroundings, found by running the experiment at two powers and extrapolating the rate of loss.

L_v = Pt/Δm, using the mass evaporated during a measured time at known power; boiler and flask methods apply the same balance.

Q19. 5 g of steam at 100 °C is passed into 200 g of water at 20 °C. Find the final temperature. (L_v = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹)

Heat given up by the steam: condensing: 0.005 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 11 300 J then cooling to θ: 0.005 × 4200 × (100 − θ) = 21(100 − θ) Heat gained by the cold water: 0.2 × 4200 × (θ − 20) = 840(θ − 20) Balance: 11 300 + 21(100 − θ) = 840(θ − 20) 11 300 + 2100 − 21θ = 840θ − 16 800 25 200 = 861θ θ = 29.3 °C

θ ≈ 29 °C — a mere 5 g of steam raises 200 g of water by 9 K

Exercise 11.5 — Phase diagrams, triple point and cooling curves

Q20. Define the triple point and state its value for water. Why is it a better fixed point than the ice point?

The triple point is the unique temperature and pressure at which the solid, liquid and vapour phases of a substance coexist in equilibrium. For water: 273.16 K (0.01 °C) at 611.2 Pa.

It is preferred because it is a single, precisely reproducible state of a pure substance — all three phases are present together, so the value cannot depend on the ambient pressure. The ice point requires exactly standard pressure and air-saturated water, so it is slightly less reproducible.

Triple point of water: 273.16 K at 611 Pa; used as the defining fixed point because it is reproducible and pressure-independent.

Q21. Sketch and explain the cooling curve of a substance that is cooled from a vapour to a solid.

RegionPhase behaviour
Vapour coolingTemperature falls steadily at a rate set by the heat loss and the vapour's specific heat
First plateau (condensation)Temperature constant at the boiling point while latent heat of vaporization is released
Liquid coolingTemperature falls again, with a smaller slope (typically) than the vapour
Second plateau (freezing)Temperature constant at the freezing point while the latent heat of fusion is released
Solid coolingTemperature continues to fall to the ambient value

The plateau lengths are proportional to the latent heats: the vaporization plateau is much longer than the fusion plateau because L_v ≈ 7L_f.

Two plateaus — condensation at the boiling point and freezing at the melting point — with steady slopes in between; the plateaus occur because the latent heat is released without a temperature change.

Q22. What is sublimation? Give an example and state the condition under which it occurs.

Sublimation is the direct change of a solid into vapour without passing through the liquid state. Example: solid carbon dioxide ("dry ice") at atmospheric pressure, camphor, iodine and snow on a dry cold day.

It occurs when the substance's triple-point pressure is above the ambient pressure, so the liquid phase is not stable at that pressure and the solid converts directly to vapour.

Solid → vapour directly; it happens when the ambient pressure lies below the triple-point pressure of the substance, so the liquid phase cannot exist.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Define specific heat capacity and state its SI unit.

The heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K, c = Q/(mΔT), measured in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. For water c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

C2. Why does the temperature of a substance stay constant during melting?

Because the heat supplied goes into breaking the bonds of the crystal lattice — increasing the molecular potential energy — rather than into increasing their average kinetic energy. Since temperature measures mean translational kinetic energy, it does not change until all the solid has melted.

C3. State Newton's law of cooling.

The rate at which a body loses heat is directly proportional to the difference between its own temperature and that of its surroundings, provided the difference is small: dQ/dt = −k(θ − θ₀).

C4. Define specific latent heat of fusion.

The heat required to convert 1 kg of a solid completely into liquid at its melting point, with no change of temperature; for ice L_f = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹. In formula form, Q = mL_f.

C5. Why does sweating cool the body?

Because sweat evaporates, and the molecules that escape carry away the latent heat of vaporization (2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹), taking energy from the skin. The skin therefore loses energy and its temperature falls — which is why evaporation is a cooling process for the liquid left behind.

C6. Why does boiling water at a hill station cook food more slowly?

At high altitude the atmospheric pressure is lower, so water boils at a lower temperature (about 90 °C at 3000 m instead of 100 °C). Food cooks more slowly because the cooking temperature, not the boiling itself, sets the rate of the chemical changes.

C7. Why is a pressure cooker more effective than an open pan?

A sealed cooker raises the pressure inside, which raises the boiling point of water above 100 °C (about 120 °C at 2 atm). Cooking at a higher temperature speeds up the reactions that soften food and kills bacteria more reliably.

C8. What is meant by the specific latent heat of vaporization of water being 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹?

It means 2.26 × 10⁶ J of heat must be supplied to convert 1 kg of water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C, with no temperature change; the same energy is released when the steam condenses at 100 °C.

C9. Why does the heat capacity of a calorimeter have to be considered in a mixture problem?

Because the calorimeter vessel itself absorbs or releases heat as its temperature changes. If its heat capacity m_c c_c is neglected, the calculated specific heat or latent heat is systematically wrong; including it makes the energy balance correct.

C10. What is a cooling curve and what do its flat portions indicate?

A graph of temperature against time for a substance cooling steadily. The flat portions (plateaus) are the phase changes: the temperature stays constant while latent heat is released at the condensation or freezing points.

C11. Why does the temperature of a substance not rise above 100 °C while water is boiling, however strong the flame?

Because every extra joule supplied goes into converting water to steam at 100 °C rather than into raising the temperature of the liquid. The temperature stays at the boiling point as long as both liquid water and steam are present.

C12. Define the triple point and give its significance.

The single temperature and pressure at which the solid, liquid and vapour phases coexist in equilibrium (for water: 273.16 K at 611 Pa). It is used as the defining fixed point of the Kelvin scale because it is independent of pressure and highly reproducible.

C13. What is regelation? Give an example.

The melting of ice under pressure and its refreezing when the pressure is removed. Pressure lowers the melting point, so the ice under a wire melts, the wire descends, and the water refreezes above it — the wire passes through the ice block without cutting it.

C14. Why is ice at 0 °C a better coolant than water at 0 °C?

Because ice absorbs its large latent heat of fusion (3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹) while melting, in addition to the sensible heat as the meltwater warms. Water at 0 °C can only absorb sensible heat mcΔT, so it removes far less energy for the same mass.

C15. A thermometer is placed in boiling water and then in ice. Explain why it gives a constant reading for some time at each.

Because at each phase change the temperature remains fixed while the latent heat is transferred. In boiling water the steam/water mixture stays at 100 °C; in melting ice the ice/water mixture stays at 0 °C, which makes both states reliable fixed points for calibration.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [5] An aluminium calorimeter of mass 50 g contains 100 g of water at 25 °C. 25 g of ice at 0 °C is added. Find the final temperature. (c_Al = 900, c_w = 4200, L_f = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹)

Heat needed to melt the ice = 0.025 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 8350 J Heat available from cooling the calorimeter + water to 0 °C: water: 0.100 × 4200 × 25 = 10 500 J calorimeter: 0.050 × 900 × 25 = 1 125 J total available = 11 625 J > 8350 J ⇒ all ice melts Remaining heat = 11 625 − 8350 = 3275 J, warming (0.025 + 0.100) kg of water and the calorimeter by θ: 3275 = [0.125 × 4200 + 0.050 × 900] θ = (525 + 45)θ = 570θ θ = 5.75 °C

θ ≈ 5.7 °C

L2. [5] A body cools from 80 °C to 70 °C in 6 minutes and from 70 °C to 62 °C in the next 6 minutes. Find the surrounding temperature and the time it will take to cool to 55 °C.

Let the ambient be θ₀ and k the cooling constant (per min). (80 − 70)/6 = k(75 − θ₀) ⇒ 1.667 = k(75 − θ₀) (70 − 62)/6 = k(66 − θ₀) ⇒ 1.333 = k(66 − θ₀) Dividing: 1.667/1.333 = 1.25 = (75 − θ₀)/(66 − θ₀) 1.25(66 − θ₀) = 75 − θ₀ 82.5 − 1.25θ₀ = 75 − θ₀ ⇒ 7.5 = 0.25θ₀ ⇒ θ₀ = 30 °C k = 1.667/(75 − 30) = 0.0370 per min From 62 °C to 55 °C: average excess = (62 + 55)/2 − 30 = 58.5 − 30 = 28.5 K 7/t = 0.0370 × 28.5 = 1.055 ⇒ t = 6.6 min

θ₀ = 30 °C; about 6.6 more minutes to reach 55 °C

L3. [6] Describe an experiment to determine the specific latent heat of vaporization of water, and state five precautions.

Method (electrical): a known power P is supplied to water already boiling in a lagged calorimeter; the mass lost Δm in a measured time t is found by weighing. Then L_v = Pt/Δm.

Precautions:

  1. Lag the apparatus so heat losses to the surroundings are small (or correct for them by repeating at two powers).
  2. Wait until boiling is steady before starting the clock, so the water is already at 100 °C and all the power goes into vaporization.
  3. Measure the power with an accurate ammeter–voltmeter (P = VI), not from the heater's rating.
  4. Use a balance of adequate sensitivity: Δm is small (a few grams), so a 0.01 g accuracy matters.
  5. Prevent condensed steam from dripping back into the calorimeter; keep the outlet open and the surroundings draught-free.

L_v = Pt/Δm, with lagging, steady boiling, measured P and Δm, and no back-drip of condensate.

L4. [5] 200 g of water at 30 °C is mixed with 300 g of water at 80 °C. Find the final temperature and the heat transferred, and explain why the result is independent of the order of mixing.

0.2 × 4200 × (θ − 30) = 0.3 × 4200 × (80 − θ) 0.2(θ − 30) = 0.3(80 − θ) 0.2θ − 6 = 24 − 0.3θ 0.5θ = 30 ⇒ θ = 60 °C Heat transferred = 0.2 × 4200 × 30 = 25 200 J

The final temperature depends only on the masses and initial temperatures, because the energy balance is symmetric: the same total energy ends up shared between the two masses whatever the sequence. Only the (small) losses to the surroundings depend on how long the mixing takes.

θ = 60 °C; 25.2 kJ transferred; the equilibrium state is fixed by energy conservation alone, not by the path.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 11

1. Forgetting the calorimeter. The vessel absorbs heat too: (m_w c_w + m_c c_c). Omitting m_c c_c underestimates the heat absorbed.

2. Using mcΔT during a phase change. While ice is melting, Q = mL only; there is no temperature change to put in mcΔT. Add the sensible heat of the meltwater separately.

3. Mixing grams and kilograms. Latent heats are per kg. 10 g is 0.010 kg; leaving it as 10 multiplies the answer by 1000.

4. Checking equilibrium too late. Record the maximum (or minimum) temperature reached in a mixture experiment; reading after it has started drifting back gives a wrong balance.

5. Using Newton's law of cooling for a large excess. Linear proportionality fails beyond a small ΔT. Say so when the excess is tens of degrees, and use the average-excess form explicitly.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Write the energy balance sentence first ("heat lost by X = heat gained by Y"), then the equation with symbols, then substitute SI values. For phase-change questions, state explicitly whether all the ice melts before solving.
