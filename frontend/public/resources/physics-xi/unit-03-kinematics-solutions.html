Unit 3 — Kinematics

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  5 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Kinematics is examined almost entirely as computation: read a graph, choose an equation, substitute, answer. Here every exercise type is worked with the graph derivations NEB asks for, then the short conceptual questions with model answers.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define instantaneous velocity; distinguish distance from displacement; state the condition for maximum range; average speed of a round trip.
  • 4 marks — equations of motion from a v–t graph; a free-fall or retardation numerical; relative velocity of two trains or of rain and a walker.
  • 5–6 marks — projectile motion: derive the equation of the trajectory, then find time of flight, maximum height and range for given values.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: NEB gives marks for the sign convention. Write "taking upward as positive, a = −g" before substituting, or the height and time marks are lost even with the right numbers.

Exercise 3.1 — Distance, displacement, speed, velocity, acceleration

Q1. Distinguish between distance and displacement, and between speed and velocity.

DistanceDisplacement
Total length of path travelledShortest straight-line change of position from initial to final point
Scalar, always positiveVector, can be positive, negative or zero
Never decreases with timeCan decrease if the body turns back

Speed is the rate of covering distance (scalar); velocity is the rate of change of displacement (vector). For a body returning to its start, average velocity is zero while average speed is not.

Distance and speed are scalars with no direction; displacement and velocity are vectors with direction. Displacement ≤ distance always.

Q2. The position of a particle is x = 3t² + 2t + 5 (x in m, t in s). Find its velocity and acceleration at t = 2 s.

v = dx/dt = 6t + 2 ⇒ v(2) = 12 + 2 = 14 m s⁻¹ a = dv/dt = 6 m s⁻² (constant)

v = 14 m s⁻¹, a = 6 m s⁻² at t = 2 s

Q3. A car travels the first half of a distance at 30 km h⁻¹ and the second half at 60 km h⁻¹. Find its average speed.

Let total distance = 2d. Time = d/30 + d/60 = (2d + d)/60 = 3d/60 = d/20 Average speed = 2d/(d/20) = 40 km h⁻¹ (For equal distances: v_avg = 2v₁v₂/(v₁ + v₂) = 2(30)(60)/90 = 40)

40 km h⁻¹ — not the arithmetic mean 45, because the slower half takes longer

Q4. A body moves 4 m east, then 3 m north in 7 s. Find the distance, displacement and average velocity.

Distance = 4 + 3 = 7 m Displacement = √(4² + 3²) = 5 m, at tan⁻¹(3/4) = 37° north of east Average velocity = 5/7 = 0.714 m s⁻¹ along that direction Average speed = 7/7 = 1 m s⁻¹

Distance 7 m; displacement 5 m at 37° N of E; average velocity 0.71 m s⁻¹, average speed 1 m s⁻¹

Exercise 3.2 — Equations of motion and their graphical derivation

Q5. Derive v = u + at from a velocity–time graph.

A body starts with velocity u at t = 0 and reaches v at t, with uniform acceleration a. On the v–t graph the acceleration is the slope:

a = (v − u)/(t − 0) ⇒ v = u + at

The slope of a v–t graph is the acceleration; rearranged it gives v = u + at.

Q6. Derive s = ut + ½at² from a velocity–time graph.

The area under the v–t graph between 0 and t is the displacement. It splits into a rectangle of height u and width t, plus a triangle of height (v − u) = at and width t:

s = area of rectangle + area of triangle = ut + ½ t(at) = ut + ½at²

Displacement = area under the v–t graph = ut + ½at².

Q7. Derive v² = u² + 2as using the velocity–time graph.

Displacement s = area of trapezium = ½(u + v)t ...(i) Acceleration a = (v − u)/t ⇒ t = (v − u)/a ...(ii) Substituting (ii) in (i): s = ½(u + v)(v − u)/a = (v² − u²)/(2a) ⇒ v² = u² + 2as

v² = u² + 2as — the "no time" equation, used whenever t is neither given nor asked.

Q8. A car starting from rest accelerates uniformly at 2 m s⁻² for 10 s. Find its final velocity and the distance covered. Then it brakes to rest in 5 s; find the retardation and the braking distance.

Acceleration: v = u + at = 0 + 2(10) = 20 m s⁻¹ s₁ = ½at² = ½(2)(100) = 100 m Braking: u = 20, v = 0, t = 5 a = (v − u)/t = (0 − 20)/5 = −4 m s⁻² (retardation 4 m s⁻²) s₂ = (u + v)t/2 = (20)(5)/2 = 50 m

v = 20 m s⁻¹, s₁ = 100 m; retardation 4 m s⁻², braking distance 50 m

Q9. A body covers 40 m in the 5th second and 60 m in the 7th second of uniformly accelerated motion. Find its initial velocity and acceleration.

s_n = u + a(2n − 1)/2 5th second: 40 = u + a(9)/2 ⇒ u + 4.5a = 40 ...(i) 7th second: 60 = u + a(13)/2 ⇒ u + 6.5a = 60 ...(ii) (ii) − (i): 2a = 20 ⇒ a = 10 m s⁻² From (i): u = 40 − 45 = −5 m s⁻¹

a = 10 m s⁻², u = −5 m s⁻¹ (the body was initially moving backward and reversed)

Exercise 3.3 — Motion of a freely falling body

Q10. A stone is dropped from the top of a tower 45 m high. Find the time to reach the ground and the velocity on impact. (g = 9.8 m s⁻²)

Taking downward as positive, u = 0, a = g = 9.8, s = 45: s = ut + ½gt² ⇒ 45 = 0 + ½(9.8)t² t² = 90/9.8 = 9.18 ⇒ t = 3.03 s v = u + gt = 0 + 9.8(3.03) = 29.7 m s⁻¹

t ≈ 3.0 s; v ≈ 29.7 m s⁻¹ downward

Q11. A ball is thrown vertically upward with 19.6 m s⁻¹. Find the maximum height, the time to reach it and the total time of flight.

Upward positive, a = −9.8 m s⁻². At the top v = 0: v² = u² − 2gH ⇒ 0 = (19.6)² − 2(9.8)H H = 384.16/19.6 = 19.6 m t_up = u/g = 19.6/9.8 = 2.0 s Total time = 2t_up = 4.0 s

H = 19.6 m; time up 2.0 s; total flight 4.0 s (the descent mirrors the ascent)

Q12. Why is the acceleration of a freely falling body independent of its mass? What is meant by g?

Weight = mg, and Newton's second law gives a = F/m = mg/m = g. The mass cancels, so all bodies fall with the same acceleration in the absence of air resistance (the observation Galileo made; verified on the Moon with a feather and a hammer, and in a vacuum tube).

g is the acceleration due to gravity, ≈ 9.8 m s⁻² near the Earth's surface, directed vertically downward (toward the centre of the Earth).

Because F = mg and m cancels from a = F/m; g is the gravitational acceleration, 9.8 m s⁻² downward, independent of the body's mass.

Q13. A body falls freely from rest. Show that the distances covered in successive seconds are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : 7.

Distance in n seconds: s_n = ½gn² Distance in (n−1) seconds: s_(n−1) = ½g(n−1)² Distance in the nth second = ½g[n² − (n−1)²] = ½g(2n − 1) n = 1,2,3,4 → ½g(1, 3, 5, 7) ⇒ ratio 1:3:5:7

s ∝ (2n − 1): the odd-number ratio, Galileo's law of odd numbers.

Q14. A body dropped from a height h reaches the ground with speed v. Find the height at which its speed is v/2.

v² = 2gh ⇒ h = v²/2g (total height) Let the speed be v/2 at height y above the ground, so it has fallen h − y: (v/2)² = 2g(h − y) ⇒ v²/4 = 2g(h − y) Substituting v² = 2gh: 2gh/4 = 2g(h − y) h/2 = h − y ⇒ y = h/2

At half the original height (y = h/2), since speed ∝ √(distance fallen).

Exercise 3.4 — Relative velocity

Q15. Two trains of length 100 m each move in opposite directions at 54 km h⁻¹ and 36 km h⁻¹. How long do they take to cross each other?

Relative speed (opposite directions) = 54 + 36 = 90 km h⁻¹ = 90 × 1000/3600 = 25 m s⁻¹ Total distance to clear = 100 + 100 = 200 m t = 200/25 = 8 s

8 s

Q16. Rain is falling vertically at 10 m s⁻¹. A man walks east at 5 m s⁻¹. At what angle to the vertical must he hold his umbrella?

Velocity of rain relative to man: v_rm = v_r − v_m = (0, −10) − (5, 0) = (−5, −10) m s⁻¹ tanθ = 5/10 = 0.5 ⇒ θ = 26.6° from the vertical, tilted forward (toward the east)

26.6° from the vertical, leaning forward in the direction of walking; the relative speed of rain is √125 = 11.2 m s⁻¹

Q17. A man can swim at 4 km h⁻¹ in still water. A river 1 km wide flows at 3 km h⁻¹. Find the minimum time to cross and the shortest path time.

Minimum time — head straight across: t = 1/4 h = 15 min, drift = 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 km. Shortest path — head upstream so the resultant is perpendicular to the bank: sinφ = 3/4 = 0.75 ⇒ φ = 48.6° upstream of the perpendicular resultant speed = √(4² − 3²) = √7 = 2.65 km h⁻¹ t = 1/2.65 = 0.378 h = 22.7 min

Minimum time 15 min (with 0.75 km drift); shortest path 22.7 min by heading 48.6° upstream

Exercise 3.5 — Projectile motion

Q18. A ball is projected at 50 m s⁻¹ at 30° to the horizontal. Find (a) the time of flight, (b) the maximum height, (c) the range. (g = 9.8 m s⁻²)

ux = 50 cos30° = 43.3 m s⁻¹, uy = 50 sin30° = 25 m s⁻¹ (a) T = 2u sinθ/g = 2(25)/9.8 = 5.10 s (b) H = u²sin²θ/(2g) = (25)²/(2×9.8) = 625/19.6 = 31.9 m (c) R = u² sin2θ/g = (2500)(sin60°)/9.8 = 2500(0.866)/9.8 = 221 m

T = 5.1 s, H = 31.9 m, R = 221 m

Q19. Show that the path of a projectile is a parabola.

Horizontal: x = u cosθ · t ⇒ t = x/(u cosθ) Vertical: y = u sinθ · t − ½gt² Substituting t: y = u sinθ · x/(u cosθ) − ½g x²/(u²cos²θ) y = x tanθ − (g x²)/(2u²cos²θ)

This is of the form y = ax − bx², the equation of a parabola. The first term is the straight-line aim; the second is the fall below that line.

y = x tanθ − gx²/(2u²cos²θ), a downward-opening parabola.

Q20. Show that the range of a projectile is maximum at 45° and that two angles of projection give the same range.

R = u² sin2θ/g R is maximum when sin2θ = 1 ⇒ 2θ = 90° ⇒ θ = 45°, and then R_max = u²/g. Also, sin2θ = sin(180° − 2θ) = sin[2(90° − θ)] ⇒ R(θ) = R(90° − θ).

R_max = u²/g at 45°; complementary angles give equal ranges (e.g. 30° and 60°), the smaller angle giving the flatter, faster path.

Q21. A projectile has a range of 40 m and a maximum height of 10 m. Find its angle of projection and initial speed.

H = u²sin²θ/(2g), R = u²sin2θ/g Dividing: H/R = [sin²θ/(2g)]/[sin2θ/g] = sin²θ/(2 sin2θ) = sin²θ/(4 sinθ cosθ) = tanθ/4 10/40 = tanθ/4 ⇒ tanθ = 1 ⇒ θ = 45° R = u²/g ⇒ 40 = u²/9.8 ⇒ u = √392 = 19.8 m s⁻¹

θ = 45°, u ≈ 19.8 m s⁻¹

Q22. A stone is thrown horizontally from a cliff 80 m high at 15 m s⁻¹. Find the time to reach the ground, the horizontal distance and the velocity on landing.

Vertical (u_y = 0): 80 = ½(9.8)t² ⇒ t = √(160/9.8) = 4.04 s Horizontal: x = 15 × 4.04 = 60.6 m vy = gt = 9.8 × 4.04 = 39.6 m s⁻¹ v = √(15² + 39.6²) = √(225 + 1568) = 42.3 m s⁻¹ direction: tanα = 39.6/15 = 2.64 ⇒ α = 69.3° below the horizontal

t = 4.0 s; horizontal distance 60.6 m; v = 42.3 m s⁻¹ at 69° below the horizontal

Q23. Why is the horizontal component of a projectile's velocity constant while the vertical component changes?

Gravity acts vertically, so it has no horizontal component. With no horizontal force (air resistance neglected), there is no horizontal acceleration, and the horizontal velocity u cosθ stays constant. The vertical velocity changes at the rate g, so it decreases to zero at the top and then increases downward.

No horizontal force ⇒ no horizontal acceleration ⇒ u cosθ is constant; the whole of g acts on the vertical component.

Exercise 3.6 — Mixed numericals

Q24. A car moving at 20 m s⁻¹ is brought to rest in 50 m. Find the retardation and the stopping time.

v² = u² + 2as ⇒ 0 = 400 + 2a(50) ⇒ a = −4 m s⁻² t = (v − u)/a = (0 − 20)/(−4) = 5 s

Retardation 4 m s⁻², stopping time 5 s

Q25. Can a body have zero velocity and non-zero acceleration at the same instant? Give an example.

Yes. At the highest point of a vertical throw the velocity is zero, but the acceleration is g downward throughout. A body released from rest also has v = 0 with a = g. Velocity and acceleration are independent quantities.

Yes — at the top of a vertical throw v = 0 while a = g downward.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Define instantaneous velocity and mean velocity.

Mean velocity = total displacement/total time. Instantaneous velocity is the limit of the mean velocity as the time interval shrinks to zero, v = dx/dt — the velocity at a single instant, equal to the slope of the x–t graph at that point.

C2. Can the average velocity of a body be zero while its average speed is not? Explain.

Yes. If a body returns to its starting point, the displacement is zero, so average velocity is zero, while the distance travelled (and hence average speed) is not. A runner completing a lap of a circular track is the standard example.

C3. What does the slope of a displacement–time graph represent? What does its area represent?

The slope gives the velocity (dx/dt). A displacement–time graph has no meaningful area; it is the velocity–time graph whose slope gives acceleration and whose area gives displacement.

C4. Sketch the shape of a v–t graph for (a) uniform velocity, (b) uniform acceleration, (c) uniformly retarded motion.

(a) a horizontal straight line; (b) a straight line rising from the initial velocity with slope a; (c) a straight line falling with negative slope, cutting the time axis when the body stops.

C5. Why is the acceleration due to gravity taken negative when a body is thrown upward?

Because g always acts downward while the upward displacement is taken positive. With the sign convention "upward positive", the downward acceleration enters every equation as −g, which correctly reduces the upward velocity.

C6. State the two conditions needed to treat projectile motion with the standard formulas.

(i) Air resistance is negligible, so the only force is weight (constant g, downward). (ii) The motion takes place near the Earth's surface, where g is constant in magnitude and direction, and the range is small compared with the Earth's radius.

C7. Can a projectile have the same range for two different angles of projection? Give an example.

Yes — complementary angles: 30° and 60° both give R = u²sin60°/g. The flatter angle reaches the target sooner with a lower peak; the steeper one stays in the air longer. Both cover the same horizontal distance.

C8. Why does the time of flight of a projectile not depend on the horizontal component of velocity?

Because the vertical motion is independent of the horizontal: the time of flight is fixed by the vertical component u sinθ and g alone, T = 2u sinθ/g. Changing u cosθ only changes how far the body travels in that fixed time.

C9. What is relative velocity? Write the expression for the velocity of A relative to B.

Relative velocity is the velocity of one body as measured from another moving body: v_AB = v_A − v_B. Its magnitude for two bodies moving along the same line in opposite directions is the sum of the speeds, for the same direction it is the difference.

C10. Distinguish between uniform and non-uniform acceleration with one example each.

Uniform acceleration means equal changes of velocity in equal times (e.g. a freely falling body, where a = g). Non-uniform acceleration means the rate of change of velocity itself changes (e.g. a car braking to a stop, where the retardation grows as the brake efficiency falls, or any motion with air resistance).

C11. A body moves with constant speed in a circle. Is its velocity constant? Is its acceleration zero?

Neither. The speed is constant but the direction changes continuously, so the velocity changes and there is an acceleration of magnitude v²/r directed toward the centre (centripetal acceleration). Constant speed does not imply zero acceleration.

C12. What is the significance of the 45° angle in projectile motion?

At 45°, sin2θ = 1 and the range reaches its maximum u²/g for a given initial speed. It balances the time in the air against the horizontal speed; above or below 45° one of the two falls and the range decreases.

C13. Why does a body dropped from a moving train fall vertically as seen from the train but along a parabola as seen from the ground?

The body shares the train's horizontal velocity at release. In the train's frame this horizontal velocity is zero, so only the vertical fall is seen. On the ground the body has a constant horizontal velocity plus a vertical acceleration — a projectile path, a parabola.

C14. State the limitations of the equations of motion.

They hold only for motion in a straight line with uniform acceleration. They fail for variable acceleration (where calculus is needed), for motion along a curved path without resolution into components, and for speeds comparable to the speed of light (relativistic effects).

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Derive the three equations of motion from a velocity–time graph, and state the assumption behind each.

Graph: initial velocity u at t = 0, final v at t; straight line ⇒ uniform acceleration. Slope: a = (v − u)/t ⇒ v = u + at Area: s = ut + ½(v − u)t = ut + ½at² Area = trapezium = (u + v)t/2; with t = (v − u)/a: s = (u + v)(v − u)/(2a) = (v² − u²)/(2a) ⇒ v² = u² + 2as

The assumption is that the acceleration is constant, which makes the v–t graph a straight line — only then are the slope and trapezium area formulas exact.

v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as, all valid only for uniform acceleration in a straight line.

L2. [6] A ball thrown upward from the top of a 25 m tower at 20 m s⁻¹ is caught by a person on the ground. Find (a) the greatest height above the tower, (b) the total time of flight, (c) the velocity just before being caught.

(a) Above the tower: v² = u² − 2gh ⇒ 0 = 400 − 19.6h ⇒ h = 20.4 m (b) Full journey: taking upward positive from the launch point, s = −25 m, u = +20, a = −9.8 −25 = 20t − 4.9t² ⇒ 4.9t² − 20t − 25 = 0 t = [20 ± √(400 + 490)]/9.8 = [20 ± 29.83]/9.8 t = 5.08 s (rejecting the negative root) (c) v = u + at = 20 − 9.8(5.08) = −29.8 m s⁻¹, i.e. 29.8 m s⁻¹ downward.

20.4 m above the tower; 5.1 s; 29.8 m s⁻¹ downward

L3. [5] Derive expressions for the time of flight, maximum height and range of a projectile launched at speed u and angle θ, and show that the velocity at any instant is the vector sum of a constant horizontal part and a changing vertical part.

T: vertical displacement zero ⇒ 0 = (u sinθ)T − ½gT² ⇒ T = 2u sinθ/g H: at the top v_y = 0 ⇒ 0 = u²sin²θ − 2gH ⇒ H = u²sin²θ/(2g) R: R = u cosθ · T = u cosθ (2u sinθ/g) = u² sin2θ/g v(t) = (u cosθ)î + (u sinθ − gt)ĵ

T = 2u sinθ/g, H = u²sin²θ/2g, R = u²sin2θ/g; the î component of v is constant, the ĵ component falls linearly with time.

L4. [4] Two cars A and B start from the same point. A moves east at 15 m s⁻¹ and B moves north at 20 m s⁻¹. Find the velocity of A relative to B and the distance between them after 10 s.

v_AB = v_A − v_B = 15î − 20ĵ |v_AB| = √(225 + 400) = 25 m s⁻¹ direction: 53° south of east Separation after 10 s = |v_AB| × t = 25 × 10 = 250 m (check: √(150² + 200²) = 250 m ✓)

25 m s⁻¹ at 53° south of east; separation 250 m

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 3

1. No sign convention stated. Write "upward positive, a = −g" before you substitute. Most lost marks in free-fall questions are sign marks.

2. Using the horizontal speed in vertical equations. The vertical and horizontal motions must be handled in separate columns; mixing u and u cosθ in one equation is the commonest projectile error.

3. Averaging speeds arithmetically. Equal distances at different speeds need 2v₁v₂/(v₁+v₂), not (v₁+v₂)/2. Equal times do give the arithmetic mean.

4. Forgetting the initial height. A body thrown from a tower does not start at s = 0; set the origin and the final displacement (−h) explicitly. See L2.

5. Reporting a vector as a scalar. "v = 29.8 m s⁻¹" without "downward" loses a mark; the sign of v_y carries the direction.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. List the givens with symbols, choose the equation that contains exactly one unknown, substitute with units, then state magnitude and direction separately.
