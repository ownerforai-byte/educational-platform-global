Unit 5 — Work, Energy and Power

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  6 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

This unit is where the paper's numericals are cheapest to score and easiest to lose: one wrong sign or a forgotten cosθ and the whole 4-mark item collapses. Every exercise type is worked below with the energy statement written out in full.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define one joule and one watt; state the work–energy theorem; distinguish conservative from non-conservative forces; which collision conserves momentum but not energy.
  • 4 marks — work done by a variable force (area or integral); power and efficiency numerical; energy conversion for a falling body or a compressed spring.
  • 5–6 marks — prove that total mechanical energy is conserved for a freely falling body; derive the final velocities in a one-dimensional elastic collision; show equal masses exchange velocities.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: state the system, the initial energy and the final energy in words before the algebra. NEB awards the "energy statement" mark separately from the substitution.

Exercise 5.1 — Work done by constant and variable forces

Q1. Define work. A force of 50 N acts at 60° to the horizontal and moves a body 10 m horizontally. Find the work done.

Work done by a constant force is W = F·s = Fs cosθ, where θ is the angle between force and displacement.

W = Fs cosθ = 50 × 10 × cos60° = 500 × 0.5 = 250 J

W = 250 J

Q2. Under what conditions is the work done (a) zero, (b) negative, (c) maximum?

  • Zero: θ = 90° (force perpendicular to displacement — a porter carrying a load horizontally, or the centripetal force in circular motion).
  • Negative: 90° < θ ≤ 180° (friction, air resistance, a braking force — energy is removed).
  • Maximum: θ = 0° (force along the displacement), W = Fs.

W = Fs cosθ: zero at 90°, negative for obtuse angles, maximum at 0°.

Q3. A force F = 3x² N acts along the x-axis. Find the work done in moving a body from x = 0 to x = 2 m.

W = ∫F dx from 0 to 2 = ∫3x² dx = [x³]₀² = 8 − 0 = 8 J

Graphically this is the area under the F–x graph from 0 to 2 m.

W = 8 J

Q4. A spring of force constant 200 N m⁻¹ is stretched by 10 cm. Find the work done and the energy stored.

Extension x = 0.10 m W = ∫kx dx = ½kx² = ½(200)(0.10)² = ½(200)(0.01) = 1.0 J

All of this work is stored in the spring as elastic potential energy (assuming it stays within the elastic limit and Hooke's law holds).

W = 1.0 J = elastic PE stored

Q5. A porter carries a 20 kg load on his head and walks 50 m horizontally. What work does he do against gravity? What work does he do on the load in the direction of motion?

The load moves horizontally while gravity acts vertically, so the angle between weight and displacement is 90°:

W_gravity = mgs cos90° = 0 J

The porter exerts an upward force balancing the weight, also perpendicular to the displacement, so its work on the load is zero; his muscles nevertheless spend energy internally because of the repeated action of muscle fibres.

Zero work against gravity and zero work along the path — the muscular effort is internal energy expenditure, not mechanical work on the load.

Q6. A car of mass 1000 kg moving at 10 m s⁻¹ is accelerated to 20 m s⁻¹. Find the work done by the engine (ignoring friction).

W = ΔKE = ½m(v² − u²) = ½(1000)(400 − 100) = 500 × 300 = 1.5 × 10⁵ J

W = 1.5 × 10⁵ J (150 kJ)

Exercise 5.2 — Kinetic energy, potential energy and the work–energy theorem

Q7. State and prove the work–energy theorem.

Statement: the net work done on a body by all forces equals the change in its kinetic energy, W_net = ΔKE.

Proof for a constant force along the displacement:

F = ma, and v² = u² + 2as ⇒ s = (v² − u²)/(2a) W = Fs = ma × (v² − u²)/(2a) = ½mv² − ½mu² = KE_f − KE_i

For a variable force the same result follows from W = ∫F dx with v dv = a dx.

W_net = ½mv² − ½mu²: the net work always shows up as a change of kinetic energy, and it applies whatever the nature of the force.

Q8. A bullet of mass 20 g moving at 400 m s⁻¹ penetrates 10 cm into a wooden block and stops. Find the average resistive force.

Work–energy theorem: −F·d = 0 − ½mu² F × 0.10 = ½(0.020)(400)² = ½(0.020)(160000) = 1600 J F = 1600/0.10 = 16 000 N = 1.6 × 10⁴ N

F = 1.6 × 10⁴ N (opposing the motion)

Q9. Show that KE = p²/2m, and use it to explain why a heavy body and a light body with the same momentum have different kinetic energies.

p = mv ⇒ v = p/m KE = ½mv² = ½m(p/m)² = p²/2m

For fixed p, KE ∝ 1/m: the lighter body has the larger kinetic energy because it needs the larger speed to carry the same momentum, and KE depends on v².

KE = p²/2m; at equal momentum the lighter body carries more kinetic energy.

Q10. Define gravitational potential energy and derive an expression for it near the Earth's surface.

Gravitational PE of a body is the energy it possesses by virtue of its position in the Earth's gravitational field — the work done in lifting it there against gravity.

For a small height h (g constant): W = force × distance = mg × h ⇒ PE = mgh (taking the reference level as zero at h = 0)

The reference level is arbitrary: only changes of PE enter the energy equations, so any convenient zero may be chosen.

PE = mgh, valid for h ≪ R_E where g is constant; the value is relative to the chosen zero.

Q11. A body of mass 2 kg is raised to a height of 5 m and then allowed to fall. Find its KE and PE at the start, at 3 m and just before hitting the ground.

HeightPE = mghKETotal

The total stays 98 J: PE converts into KE, and the sum is constant.

Exercise 5.3 — Power and efficiency

Q12. Define power and its unit. A pump lifts 200 kg of water through 10 m in 20 s. Find its output power, and its input power if the efficiency is 70%.

Power is the rate of doing work, P = W/t = F·v; the SI unit is the watt (1 W = 1 J s⁻¹).

W = mgh = 200(9.8)(10) = 19 600 J P_out = 19 600/20 = 980 W Efficiency η = P_out/P_in ⇒ P_in = 980/0.70 = 1400 W

Output 980 W; input 1.4 kW (the remaining 420 W is lost to friction and turbulence)

Q13. A car moves at a steady 20 m s⁻¹ against a total resistive force of 500 N. Find the power developed by the engine.

At constant speed the driving force equals the resistance: F = 500 N P = Fv = 500 × 20 = 10 000 W = 10 kW

P = 10 kW

Q14. Express 1 horsepower in watts and 1 kWh in joules.

1 hp = 746 W (British engineering horsepower) 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J

1 hp = 746 W; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J (the unit on an electricity bill)

Q15. A machine raises a load of 500 N through 12 m in 4 s using 3 kW of power. Find its efficiency.

Useful work = 500 × 12 = 6000 J Useful power = 6000/4 = 1500 W η = 1500/3000 × 100% = 50%

50% — half the input power is lost, typically to friction in the machine

Q16. Why can the efficiency of a real machine never be 100%?

Because some input energy is always converted into forms you cannot use — heat from friction, sound, deformation and air resistance. The second law of thermodynamics forbids a cyclic device from turning all its heat input into work, so η < 1 always. Ideal (frictionless, massless) machines are an idealisation.

Part of every input is dissipated as heat and sound by friction, so output is always less than input; η = 100% requires a frictionless idealisation.

Exercise 5.4 — Conservation of mechanical energy

Q17. State the law of conservation of energy and prove that total mechanical energy is conserved for a body falling freely from height h.

Statement: energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it changes from one form to another, the total of an isolated system remaining constant.

At the top (height h): v = 0 PE = mgh, KE = 0, total E = mgh At height y (fallen h − y): v² = 2g(h − y) PE = mgy, KE = ½m·2g(h − y) = mg(h − y) total E = mgy + mg(h − y) = mgh At the ground (y = 0): v² = 2gh PE = 0, KE = ½m(2gh) = mgh, total E = mgh

Total mechanical energy = mgh at every stage; PE converts to KE and back but the sum is constant when only gravity does work.

Q18. A stone is thrown vertically upward at 19.6 m s⁻¹. Using energy conservation, find the greatest height reached.

At launch: KE = ½mu², PE = 0 At the top: KE = 0, PE = mgh ½mu² = mgh ⇒ h = u²/(2g) = 384.16/19.6 = 19.6 m

h = 19.6 m — the same result as the kinematic equation, obtained without using time.

Q19. A ball is dropped from 20 m and rebounds to 5 m. What fraction of its energy is lost in the impact?

Energy at release ∝ h₁ = 20 m Energy after impact ∝ h₂ = 5 m Fraction retained = 5/20 = 0.25 Fraction lost = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75 = 75%

75% of the mechanical energy is lost as heat, sound and deformation of the ball and floor

Q20. A pendulum bob is released from a height of 0.45 m above its lowest point. Find its speed at the lowest point.

mgh = ½mv² ⇒ v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 0.45) = √8.82 = 2.97 m s⁻¹

v ≈ 3.0 m s⁻¹ at the lowest point — the mass cancels, so the answer is independent of the bob's mass.

Exercise 5.5 — Conservative and non-conservative forces

Q21. Distinguish between conservative and non-conservative forces with examples.

ConservativeNon-conservative
Work depends only on the initial and final positions, not the pathWork depends on the path taken
Work around a closed loop is zeroWork around a closed loop is not zero
Can be derived from a potential energy functionNo potential energy function exists
Mechanical energy is conservedMechanical energy is dissipated
Gravity, spring force, electrostatic forceFriction, air resistance, viscous drag, applied push

Conservative forces store and return energy without loss; non-conservative forces convert mechanical energy irreversibly into heat.

Q22. A 2 kg block is pushed 5 m up a rough incline of 30° (μ = 0.2). Find the work done against friction and the change in mechanical energy.

N = mg cosθ = 2(9.8)(0.866) = 16.97 N f = μN = 0.2(16.97) = 3.39 N W_friction = −f d = −3.39 × 5 = −17.0 J ΔPE = mgh = mg d sinθ = 2(9.8)(5)(0.5) = 49 J ΔKE = 0 (constant speed) ⇒ ΔE_mech = 49 J Work by the applied force = 49 + 17 = 66 J

17 J is dissipated by friction; the applied force must supply 66 J, of which 49 J is stored as PE and 17 J is lost.

Q23. Show that the work done by gravity in moving a body from A to B is independent of the path.

For any path, only the vertical displacement changes the work: W = ∫F·ds = −mg ∫dy = −mg(y_B − y_A) = mg(y_A − y_B)

The horizontal parts of the path contribute nothing because gravity is perpendicular to them. The work depends only on the height difference, so gravity is conservative and a potential energy mgh exists.

W = −mgΔy for any route; path-independence is exactly what makes PE = mgh meaningful.

Exercise 5.6 — Elastic and inelastic collisions

Q24. Distinguish between elastic and inelastic collisions, and state which conservation laws apply to each.

ElasticInelastic
MomentumConservedConserved
Kinetic energyConservedNot conserved
DeformationPerfectly temporary (rebound)Permanent or partly permanent
ExampleCollision between hard billiard balls, gas moleculesTwo lumps of clay sticking, a bullet embedding in wood

Momentum is conserved in both; kinetic energy is conserved only in the elastic case, the loss appearing as heat, sound and deformation.

Q25. A 4 kg body moving at 6 m s⁻¹ collides head-on elastically with a stationary 2 kg body. Find their velocities after the collision.

v₁ = [(m₁ − m₂)u₁ + 2m₂u₂]/(m₁ + m₂) = [(4 − 2)(6) + 0]/6 = 12/6 = 2 m s⁻¹ v₂ = [(m₂ − m₁)u₂ + 2m₁u₁]/(m₁ + m₂) = [0 + 2(4)(6)]/6 = 48/6 = 8 m s⁻¹ Check momentum: 4(6) = 24; 4(2) + 2(8) = 24 ✓ Check KE: 72 J before; ½(4)(4) + ½(2)(64) = 8 + 64 = 72 J ✓

v₁ = 2 m s⁻¹ (same direction), v₂ = 8 m s⁻¹ (same direction)

Q26. Show that in a one-dimensional elastic collision between two bodies of equal mass, the velocities are exchanged.

Momentum: mu₁ + mu₂ = mv₁ + mv₂ ⇒ v₁ + v₂ = u₁ + u₂ ...(i) Energy: ½mu₁² + ½mu₂² = ½mv₁² + ½mv₂² ⇒ v₁² + v₂² = u₁² + u₂² ...(ii) From (i): v₁ = u₁ + u₂ − v₂ Substituting in (ii) and simplifying gives: (v₂ − u₁)(v₂ − u₂) = 0 ⇒ either v₂ = u₁ (which gives v₁ = u₂ — the exchange) or v₂ = u₂ (no collision).

v₁ = u₂ and v₂ = u₁ — the bodies simply swap velocities (a billiard-ball head-on hit or Newton's cradle).

Q27. A bullet of mass 10 g moving at 500 m s⁻¹ embeds in a 990 g block at rest. Find the common velocity and the kinetic energy lost.

Momentum: 0.010(500) = (1.000)v ⇒ v = 5 m s⁻¹ KE before = ½(0.010)(500)² = 1250 J KE after = ½(1.000)(5)² = 12.5 J loss = 1237.5 J ≈ 99%

v = 5 m s⁻¹; about 1.24 kJ (99%) of the kinetic energy becomes heat and deformation — the classic perfectly inelastic collision.

Q28. Derive the general expressions for the final velocities in a one-dimensional elastic collision.

Momentum: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ Energy: ½m₁u₁² + ½m₂u₂² = ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂² From the energy equation (divided by the momentum equation): u₁ + v₁ = u₂ + v₂ ...(iii) (relative velocity reverses: u₁ − u₂ = −(v₁ − v₂)) Solving (i) and (iii): v₁ = [(m₁ − m₂)u₁ + 2m₂u₂]/(m₁ + m₂) v₂ = [(m₂ − m₁)u₂ + 2m₁u₁]/(m₁ + m₂)

v₁ = [(m₁−m₂)u₁ + 2m₂u₂]/(m₁+m₂); v₂ = [(m₂−m₁)u₂ + 2m₁u₁]/(m₁+m₂). Special cases: m₁ = m₂ (exchange), m₂ ≫ m₁ (v₁ ≈ −u₁, rebound), m₁ ≫ m₂ (v₁ ≈ u₁, v₂ ≈ 2u₁).

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Work–energy questions are almost always “energy in, energy out” with one loss term. The eight shapes below cover the losses (friction, collision, spring), the graphs, and the reasoning that separates work from power.

Q29. [Multi-step numerical] A 2.0 kg block slides 5.0 m down a 30° rough incline with μ = 0.2. Find its speed at the bottom and the energy lost to friction. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻².

Height fallen h = 5.0 sin30° = 2.5 m PE lost = mgh = 2.0 × 9.8 × 2.5 = 49 J Friction force f = μmg cos30° = 0.2 × 2.0 × 9.8 × 0.866 = 3.39 N Work against friction = f × s = 3.39 × 5.0 = 17.0 J Energy left as KE = 49 − 17 = 32 J v = √(2 × 32/2.0) = √32 = 5.7 m s⁻¹

Note that the friction force uses cos θ while the height uses sin θ — two different components of the same weight. Using mg instead of mg cos θ overstates the loss by a factor of 1/cos 30° = 1.15 and gives 4.9 m s⁻¹ instead of 5.7.

v ≈ 5.7 m s⁻¹ at the bottom; 17 J (35% of the 49 J) lost to friction.

Q30. [Derivation] Derive the work–energy theorem for a constant force, then extend it to a variable force.

Constant force along the displacement: W = Fs = (ma)s v² = u² + 2as ⇒ as = (v² − u²)/2 W = m(v² − u²)/2 = ½mv² − ½mu² = ΔKE Variable force: dW = F dx ⇒ W = ∫F dx = ΔKE (still exact, with F(x) inside the integral)

For a spring obeying F = kx, the integral gives W = ½kx², which is why the energy stored is quadratic in the extension and not linear. The theorem holds for the net work: if several forces act, compute the work of each and add — or equivalently use the net force.

W = Fs = mas = ½mv² − ½mu²; for variable force W = ∫F dx, giving W = ½kx² for a spring.

Q31. [Graph reading] A force on a 4.0 kg body rises uniformly from 0 to 20 N over the first 4.0 m, then stays at 20 N for the next 6.0 m. The body starts from rest. Find the total work and the final speed.

Work = area under the F–x graph first part (triangle): ½ × 4.0 × 20 = 40 J second part (rectangle): 20 × 6.0 = 120 J total = 160 J W = ΔKE = ½mv² ⇒ v = √(2W/m) = √(2 × 160/4.0) = √80 = 8.9 m s⁻¹

Reading an F–x graph is the mirror of reading a v–t graph: there the area is distance and the slope is acceleration; here the area is work and the slope is the force's rate of change. A common error is to use the average force (10 N) over the whole 10 m, giving 100 J — correct only if the ramp continued.

W = 160 J; v = 8.9 m s⁻¹ with the body starting from rest.

Q32. [Combined concept] A 0.50 kg ball on a 1.0 m string is released from the horizontal position. Find its speed at the lowest point and the tension in the string there.

Energy (the only force doing work is gravity): mgh = ½mv², h = L = 1.0 m v = √(2gL) = √(2 × 9.8 × 1.0) = 4.4 m s⁻¹ At the lowest point (Newton's second law, toward the centre): T − mg = mv²/L T = mg + mv²/L = 0.50 × 9.8 + 0.50 × 19.6/1.0 = 4.9 + 9.8 = 14.7 N = 3mg

The result T = 3mg at the bottom (and T = 0 at the top edge of a full vertical circle) is worth memorising: it comes from combining energy conservation with the centripetal condition, and it is exactly the pair of equations the vertical-circle questions need.

v = 4.4 m s⁻¹; T = 14.7 N = 3mg at the lowest point.

Q33. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): The work done by friction on a body is always negative. Reason (R): Friction always opposes the relative motion between the surfaces in contact.

A is false, R is true — and R is precisely why A fails.

Case where friction does NEGATIVE work: block sliding on a fixed floor, friction acts backward ⇒ W = −fs Case where friction does POSITIVE work: a block resting on a conveyor belt that starts moving — the belt's friction drags the block forward, in the direction of the block's displacement ⇒ W > 0 Case of zero work: a block carried at constant speed on a horizontal belt ⇒ no relative motion ⇒ no friction

Friction always opposes relative motion, not the motion of one chosen body. When the other surface is itself moving, the two can point the same way, so the sign of the work depends on the frame and on which body you are analysing.

A is false, R is true: friction opposes relative motion, so on a moving belt it can do positive work on the block it drags.

Q35. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the power a 60 kg student develops climbing 3.0 m of stairs in 5.0 s. Then state the limiting case in which the work done by a force is zero.

W = mgh = 60 × 9.8 × 3.0 = 1.76 × 10³ J P = W/t = 1764/5.0 = 353 W ≈ 0.47 hp Sustained output of a fit adult is about 100–200 W, so this is a short burst.

Limiting case: W = Fs cos θ, so the work is zero whenever

θ = 90° — force perpendicular to displacement (carrying a bag horizontally: the force is vertical, the motion horizontal, so no work is done on the bag) F = 0 — no force s = 0 — no displacement (pushing a wall)

That is why a satellite in a circular orbit does no work despite gravity acting on it, and why the centripetal force never changes the kinetic energy.

≈ 3.5 × 10² W; work is zero when F ⊥ s, F = 0 or s = 0 — a perpendicular force changes direction, never speed.

Q36. [Error-hunting] Mark each statement true or false.
(i) “Power = work × time.”
(ii) “1 kW h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.”
(iii) “Energy is a vector because force is a vector.”
(iv) “In an elastic collision both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.”

(i) FALSE — power is work per unit time, P = W/t; multiplying would give units of J s. (ii) TRUE — 1 kW h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J (the unit on an electricity bill). (iii) FALSE — work and energy are scalars: work is the dot product F · s, which is a number, possibly negative. Energy has no direction. (iv) TRUE — that is the definition of elastic; in an inelastic collision momentum is still conserved but kinetic energy is not.

The distinction in (iii) matters because energy can be added as a plain number, whereas forces must be added as vectors — the reason energy methods usually solve problems that force methods make messy.

(i) false — P = W/t; (ii) true; (iii) false — scalars; (iv) true — momentum always, KE only when elastic.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Define one joule and one watt.

One joule is the work done by a force of 1 N when its point of application moves 1 m in the direction of the force. One watt is the power of a device that does 1 J of work per second (1 W = 1 J s⁻¹).

C2. Can work be done without any displacement? Justify.

No. W = Fs cosθ, so if s = 0 the work is zero — pushing a wall as hard as you like does no mechanical work on it. Muscular energy is still spent internally, but no work is done on the object.

C3. Why is it easier to carry a load on a level road than to lift it?

Carrying horizontally requires no work against gravity because the force is perpendicular to the displacement (cos90° = 0). Lifting raises the load through a height, doing work mgh which becomes its potential energy. The effort felt while carrying is internal muscular work, not work on the load.

C4. State the work–energy theorem and give one use of it.

The net work done on a body equals the change in its kinetic energy: W_net = ΔKE. It is used to find a stopping distance or a resistive force without knowing the time — e.g. the force that stops a bullet in a block.

C5. Why is potential energy always defined relative to a reference level?

Because only the change in potential energy has physical meaning; the absolute value depends on which height you call zero. Any choice of reference is valid and cancels in energy equations, so PE = mgh is a difference from the chosen level.

C6. Is mechanical energy always conserved? Explain.

No. It is conserved only when the forces doing work are conservative. With friction or air resistance present, mechanical energy decreases and appears as heat; the total energy is still conserved, but the mechanical part is not.

C7. Why is friction a non-conservative force?

Because the work it does depends on the path length and it does negative work around a closed loop — the energy is converted into heat and cannot be recovered. No potential energy function can describe it.

C8. A light body and a heavy body have the same kinetic energy. Which has greater momentum?

Keep KE = p²/2m fixed: p = √(2m·KE), so p ∝ √m. The heavier body has the greater momentum. Equivalently, at equal momentum the lighter body has the greater KE.

C9. What is a perfectly inelastic collision? Give one example.

A collision in which the bodies stick together and move with a common velocity, so the kinetic energy loss is maximum while momentum is still conserved. Example: a bullet embedding in a block, or two clay balls colliding and sticking.

C10. Is momentum conserved when a ball bounces off a wall? Explain.

Not for the ball alone — its momentum reverses. It is conserved for the (ball + wall + Earth) system: the Earth gains an equal and opposite momentum. Never apply momentum conservation to a single body that is acted on by an external force.

C11. Why is kinetic energy not conserved in an inelastic collision, although momentum is?

Momentum is a vector and depends on velocity to the first power, so equal and opposite internal impulses preserve it. Kinetic energy depends on v² and part of it is converted into heat, sound and permanent deformation, forms that momentum conservation does not forbid.

C12. Distinguish between power and energy.

Energy is the capacity to do work and is measured in joules; it is a quantity, not a rate. Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or work is done, P = W/t, measured in watts. A 1 kW heater and a 100 W bulb can deliver the same total energy — the heater simply delivers it ten times faster.

C13. What is the significance of the area under an F–x graph?

It is the work done by the force over that displacement, W = ∫F dx. Above the axis the work is positive (energy added); below the axis it is negative (energy removed). This is how work by a variable force is found graphically.

C14. Why does a rubber ball bounce higher than a lump of clay dropped from the same height?

Rubber deforms elastically and returns most of the stored strain energy, so KE is largely recovered (an almost elastic collision). Clay deforms plastically, dissipating nearly all the energy as heat, so almost no KE remains for the rebound (inelastic).

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [5] A 1500 kg car travelling at 20 m s⁻¹ brakes to rest over 40 m. Find the braking force and the distance it would need at 30 m s⁻¹ (same force).

W = ΔKE: −F(40) = 0 − ½(1500)(400) = −300 000 J F = 300 000/40 = 7500 N At 30 m s⁻¹: KE = ½(1500)(900) = 675 000 J d = KE/F = 675 000/7500 = 90 m

Braking force 7.5 kN; at 30 m s⁻¹ the stopping distance is 90 m — the distance scales with v², which is why speeding is so dangerous.

L2. [6] A 2 kg block slides from rest down a 5 m long incline of 30° with μ = 0.25. Find its speed at the bottom by the energy method, and verify with kinematics.

Energy method: loss of PE = mgL sinθ = 2(9.8)(5)(0.5) = 49 J work against friction = μmg cosθ × L = 0.25(2)(9.8)(0.866)(5) = 21.2 J KE at the bottom = 49 − 21.2 = 27.8 J ½(2)v² = 27.8 ⇒ v = √27.8 = 5.27 m s⁻¹ Kinematics check: a = g(sinθ − μcosθ) = 9.8(0.5 − 0.25×0.866) = 9.8(0.2835) = 2.78 m s⁻² v² = 2aL = 2(2.78)(5) = 27.8 ⇒ v = 5.27 m s⁻¹ ✓

v ≈ 5.3 m s⁻¹; the two methods agree, and 21 J is the energy dissipated by friction.

L3. [5] Show that when a body of mass m compresses a spring by x and is then released on a smooth horizontal surface, the speed it acquires is v = x√(k/m).

Energy stored in the spring = ½kx² On release this becomes the body's kinetic energy: ½kx² = ½mv² v² = kx²/m ⇒ v = x√(k/m)

The spring force is conservative, so no energy is lost on a smooth surface; the block oscillates with this maximum speed at the equilibrium position.

v = x√(k/m) — maximum at the natural length, zero at maximum compression.

L4. [6] A 3 kg ball moving at 8 m s⁻¹ collides head-on with a 5 kg ball at rest. After the collision the 3 kg ball moves at 2 m s⁻¹ in the opposite direction. Find the velocity of the 5 kg ball and determine whether the collision is elastic.

Momentum: 3(8) + 0 = 3(−2) + 5v₂ 24 = −6 + 5v₂ ⇒ v₂ = 6 m s⁻¹ KE before = ½(3)(64) = 96 J KE after = ½(3)(4) + ½(5)(36) = 6 + 90 = 96 J

v₂ = 6 m s⁻¹ in the original direction, and KE is unchanged (96 J before and after), so the collision is elastic.

L5. [4] A pump lifts water from a 10 m deep well at 5 kg s⁻¹ and delivers it at 4 m s⁻¹. Find the power required.

Power to raise: P₁ = (dm/dt)gh = 5(9.8)(10) = 490 W Power to give speed: P₂ = ½(dm/dt)v² = ½(5)(16) = 40 W Total = 530 W

≈ 530 W (plus friction losses in a real pump)

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 5

1. Dropping cosθ. A force at 60° to the displacement does half the work of the same force along it (250 J not 500 J).

2. Applying energy conservation with friction present. Write "loss of PE = gain in KE + work against friction" explicitly; setting ½mv² = mgh with friction on gives an answer that is too large.

3. Forgetting that momentum is a vector. In a head-on collision a rebounding ball has a negative velocity; using +2 instead of −2 in L4 flips the answer.

4. Mixing PE references. Choose one zero level for the whole problem and state it. A pendulum problem with the zero at the top instead of the bottom changes every sign.

5. Assuming kinetic energy is conserved "because collisions are elastic". Check it: compute KE before and after and state the comparison. That calculation is the mark.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Name the system and the forces doing work, write the energy statement in words, substitute, then answer with the unit. For collisions: momentum equation, energy equation, check line.
