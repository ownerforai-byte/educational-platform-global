Unit 26 — Recent Trends in Physics

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  6 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

A modern-physics unit with almost no derivations: it is examined through definitions, classification (quarks, leptons, baryons, mesons) and two short calculations (Hubble's law and the Schwarzschild radius). Learn the vocabulary precisely and the marks follow.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — what are quarks; define baryons and mesons; what is an antiparticle; state Hubble's law; what is the cosmic microwave background; what is a black hole; what are gravitational waves.
  • 4 marks — quark composition of proton and neutron; classify given particles as baryons/mesons/leptons; Hubble-law numerical; Schwarzschild radius numerical.
  • 5–6 marks — explain the evidence for the Big Bang; describe the Standard Model classification of particles; explain dark matter and its evidence and distinguish it from dark energy; explain black holes and the significance of gravitational-wave detection.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: the classification must be stated correctly — baryons have three quarks, mesons one quark and one antiquark, leptons have no quarks. "Meson = 2 quarks" is wrong; it is a quark–antiquark pair.

Exercise 26.1 — Particles and antiparticles

Q1. What is an antiparticle? Give three examples of particle–antiparticle pairs.

Every elementary particle has a corresponding antiparticle with the same mass and lifetime but opposite values of all additive quantum numbers — electric charge, baryon number and lepton number.

ParticleAntiparticleCharge
Electron e⁻Positron e⁺−e / +e
Proton pAntiproton p̄+e / −e
Neutron nAntineutron n̄0 / 0 (but opposite baryon number)
Neutrino νAntineutrino ν̄0 / 0 (opposite lepton number)
Up quark uAnti-up ū+2e/3 / −2e/3

A particle and its antiparticle annihilate on meeting, their total mass–energy appearing as photons. Some neutral particles, such as the photon and the Z boson, are their own antiparticles.

Same mass, opposite charge and opposite quantum numbers; e⁻/e⁺, p/p̄ and u/ū are examples, and a pair annihilates into radiation.

Q2. Explain pair production and annihilation with an equation for each.

Pair production (in the field of a nucleus, so that momentum is conserved): γ → e⁻ + e⁺ Threshold energy = 2m_e c² = 1.022 MeV Annihilation: e⁻ + e⁺ → 2γ Each photon carries 0.511 MeV when the pair is essentially at rest.

Two photons are emitted rather than one so that momentum is conserved in the centre-of-mass frame. Both processes are observed routinely: PET scanners detect the back-to-back 0.511 MeV photons from positron annihilation, and cosmic-ray showers are full of pair-production events.

γ → e⁻e⁺ above 1.02 MeV; e⁻e⁺ → 2γ of 0.511 MeV each. Both conserve charge, energy, momentum and lepton number.

Q3. State the conservation laws obeyed in particle reactions.

  1. Energy and momentum (including rest mass energy) — always.
  2. Electric charge — the total charge before and after is the same.
  3. Baryon number — protons and neutrons count +1, antiparticles −1; the total is conserved (which is why the proton cannot decay into lighter particles in the Standard Model).
  4. Lepton number — electrons, muons, tau and their neutrinos count +1; antileptons −1.
  5. Angular momentum and strangeness (strangeness only in strong and electromagnetic interactions).

A reaction that violates any of these is forbidden. For example, p → e⁺ + γ is forbidden because baryon number and lepton number cannot both be conserved.

Energy–momentum, charge, baryon number, lepton number and angular momentum must all balance; strangeness is conserved except in weak interactions.

Exercise 26.2 — Quarks, baryons and mesons

Q4. What are quarks? Name the six flavours and give their charges.

Quarks are the fundamental constituents of hadrons — the particles that feel the strong nuclear force. They carry fractional electric charge and are never found free (quark confinement).

GenerationQuarkChargeBaryon number
Firstup (u)+2e/3+1/3
Firstdown (d)−e/3+1/3
Secondcharm (c)+2e/3+1/3
Secondstrange (s)−e/3+1/3
Thirdtop (t)+2e/3+1/3
Thirdbottom (b)−e/3+1/3

Each quark also carries one of three "colour" charges (red, green, blue) and has an antiquark with opposite charge and baryon number −1/3. The top quark is by far the heaviest, about 173 GeV/c² — heavier than a whole tungsten nucleus.

Six flavours in three generations: u, c, t carry +2e/3 and d, s, b carry −e/3, all with baryon number +1/3; antiquarks have the opposite signs.

Q5. Define hadrons, baryons and mesons, and give the quark content of the proton and neutron.

Hadrons are particles made of quarks that feel the strong force; they divide into baryons and mesons.

Baryons are hadrons made of three quarks (qqq), with baryon number +1. Examples: proton, neutron, and the Λ, Σ and Ω particles.

Mesons are hadrons made of one quark and one antiquark (qq̄), with baryon number 0. Examples: pion π⁺ (u d̄), π⁻ (ū d), π⁰ (uū − dd̄)/√2, and the kaon K⁺ (u s̄).

Proton p = uud: charge = 2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1 e ✓ Neutron n = udd: charge = 2/3 − 1/3 − 1/3 = 0 ✓

Baryons are fermions (half-integer spin), while mesons are bosons (integer spin). Mesons are the exchange particles that carry the residual strong force between nucleons, which is why the nucleus holds together despite proton repulsion.

Hadrons are quark-bound particles; baryons = qqq (p = uud, n = udd), mesons = qq̄ (π⁺ = ud̄), and mesons mediate the residual nuclear force.

Q6. Why are quarks never observed in isolation?

Because of colour confinement: the strong force between quarks does not weaken with distance in the way gravity and electromagnetism do. Instead the force remains roughly constant (about 10⁴ N) as quarks are pulled apart, so the energy stored in the colour field grows until it is large enough to create a new quark–antiquark pair. The original quark is then bound into a new hadron rather than being freed.

This is why high-energy collisions produce jets of hadrons rather than individual quarks. The only known exception is the quark–gluon plasma formed at extremely high temperature and density, as in heavy-ion collisions and in the early universe.

Colour confinement: the force does not fall with distance, so pulling quarks apart creates new quark pairs instead of freeing one — only colour-neutral hadrons are observable.

Q7. What is a gluon?

The gluon is the exchange particle (gauge boson) of the strong interaction, the particle that binds quarks into hadrons. It is massless, electrically neutral, has spin 1, and — uniquely among gauge bosons — carries colour charge itself, so gluons interact with one another. There are eight types of gluon, corresponding to the eight independent colour combinations.

The gluon exchange is what produces the potential that grows with separation and hence confinement; the residual effect leaking out of hadrons binds protons and neutrons into nuclei.

The massless, colour-carrying boson of the strong force that binds quarks; its self-interaction produces confinement and asymptotic freedom.

Exercise 26.3 — Leptons and neutrinos

Q8. What are leptons? Name the six leptons and state their charges.

Leptons are fundamental particles that do not feel the strong interaction — they are not made of quarks. There are six, in three generations:

GenerationCharged leptonChargeNeutrino
Firstelectron e⁻−eν_e
Secondmuon μ⁻−eν_μ
Thirdtau τ⁻−eν_τ

Each has an antiparticle with opposite charge and lepton number: e⁺, μ⁺, τ⁺ and the antineutrinos. The muon is about 207 times the electron mass, the tau about 3477 times. Leptons interact through the weak force (all of them) and the electromagnetic force (the charged ones), which is why neutrinos are so penetrating.

Six leptons: e, μ, τ (charge −e) and their three neutrinos (neutral); they do not feel the strong force and interact only weakly (and electromagnetically if charged).

Q9. What are neutrinos? State four of their properties.

Neutrinos are neutral, nearly massless leptons produced in weak interactions — beta decay, nuclear fusion in the Sun, and supernova collapse.

  • Electrically neutral and uncharged, so they do not feel electromagnetism or the strong force.
  • Extremely small mass (less than about 1 eV/c², at least a million times lighter than the electron); they were long thought to be exactly massless.
  • They interact only through the weak force, so they pass through matter almost unimpeded — about 10¹⁴ solar neutrinos cross your body every second, and a light-year of lead would stop only about half of them.
  • They come in three flavours and can oscillate between them, a phenomenon that proved they have mass and earned the 2015 Nobel Prize.

Neutral, nearly massless leptons that interact only weakly, are produced copiously in the Sun and in beta decay, and oscillate between three flavours.

Exercise 26.4 — The fundamental forces and the Standard Model

Q11. Name the four fundamental forces, their relative strengths and their exchange particles.

ForceRelative strengthRangeExchange particle
Strong nuclear1~10⁻¹⁵ mGluon (8 types)
Electromagnetic~10⁻²Infinite (1/r²)Photon
Weak nuclear~10⁻⁶~10⁻¹⁸ mW⁺, W⁻, Z⁰ bosons
Gravitational~10⁻³⁹Infinite (1/r²)Graviton (hypothetical)

The strong force binds quarks into hadrons and, residually, nucleons into nuclei. The weak force is responsible for beta decay and for the fusion reactions in the Sun. Gravity, though by far the weakest per particle, dominates on astronomical scales because mass always adds while positive and negative charge cancel.

Strong (gluon), electromagnetic (photon), weak (W/Z) and gravitational (graviton, not yet detected), in the strength ratio 1 : 10⁻² : 10⁻⁶ : 10⁻³⁹.

Q12. What is the Standard Model of particle physics?

The Standard Model is the theory that describes all known elementary particles and three of the four fundamental forces (strong, weak and electromagnetic), excluding gravity.

Matter particles: 6 quarks + 6 leptons (and their antiparticles) Force carriers: gluon, photon, W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰ (spin-1 gauge bosons) Scalar boson: Higgs boson (spin 0), which gives the others their mass Total: 12 matter particles + 12 antiparticles + 5 gauge bosons + 1 Higgs

Its predictions have been tested to extraordinary precision — the electron's magnetic moment agrees with theory to more than ten significant figures. Its known shortcomings are that it does not include gravity, gives no account of dark matter or dark energy, and contains about 19 free parameters whose values must be measured rather than derived. The Higgs boson, predicted in 1964, was discovered at CERN in 2012, completing the model.

The theory of quarks, leptons, gauge bosons and the Higgs that describes the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces — everything except gravity, dark matter and dark energy.

Q13. What is the Higgs boson and why is it important?

The Higgs boson is the quantum of the Higgs field, an all-pervading field that interacts with the fundamental particles and gives them their mass. Without it the quarks, leptons and W and Z bosons would be massless and would all travel at the speed of light, so atoms could not form.

It was predicted by Higgs, Englert, Brout and others in 1964 and discovered at the Large Hadron Collider in 2012, with a mass of about 125 GeV/c². The discovery confirmed the mechanism of electroweak symmetry breaking and completed the particle content of the Standard Model; Higgs and Englert shared the 2013 Nobel Prize.

The quantum of the field that gives elementary particles their mass; found at CERN in 2012 at about 125 GeV, completing the Standard Model.

Q14. Verify the quark composition of the proton and neutron, and show that the pion π⁺ has charge +e.

Proton (uud): 2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1 e ✓ Neutron (udd): 2/3 − 1/3 − 1/3 = 0 ✓ Pion π⁺ (u d̄): 2/3 − (−1/3) = 2/3 + 1/3 = +1 e ✓ Pion π⁻ (ū d): −2/3 + (−1/3) = −1 e ✓

Baryon number also checks out: each quark is +1/3, so a proton has 1 and a meson (quark − antiquark) has 1/3 − 1/3 = 0, consistent with the definition.

uud = +1e, udd = 0, ud̄ = +1e, ūd = −1e; baryon number is +1 for baryons and 0 for mesons, as required.

Exercise 26.5 — The Big Bang and the expanding universe

Q15. State the Big Bang theory and describe its three main pieces of evidence.

The Big Bang theory holds that the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. It is not an explosion in space but an expansion of space: every point moves away from every other, and there is no centre.

  1. Hubble expansion: distant galaxies recede with speeds proportional to their distance (v = H₀d), and the redshift of their light shows they were closer together in the past.
  2. Cosmic microwave background (CMB): a uniform 2.7 K radiation filling all space, the cooled remnant of the hot early universe, released about 380 000 years after the Big Bang when the plasma became transparent. Its tiny temperature ripples (about 10⁻⁵ K) are the seeds of galaxies.
  3. Primordial element abundances: the observed 75% hydrogen, 25% helium and trace lithium match the predictions of Big Bang nucleosynthesis in the first few minutes; no stellar process can make that much helium.

Expansion (Hubble's law), the 2.7 K CMB, and the 75/25 hydrogen–helium abundance — all three fit a hot dense origin about 13.8 Gyr ago.

Q16. What is the cosmic microwave background and why is it important?

The CMB is microwave radiation arriving almost equally from every direction, with a blackbody spectrum at 2.725 K (peak wavelength about 1.9 mm). It was discovered accidentally by Penzias and Wilson in 1965 (Nobel Prize 1978).

It originated about 380 000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe had cooled to about 3000 K and protons and electrons combined into neutral hydrogen. Before that, free electrons scattered light and the universe was opaque; afterwards, light travelled freely and has been stretched by the expansion ever since, from visible/near-infrared wavelengths to microwaves.

Its near-perfect uniformity confirms that the early universe was extremely homogeneous, and the tiny anisotropies mapped by COBE, WMAP and Planck are the density fluctuations from which galaxies grew. It is the most direct observational evidence for the hot Big Bang.

A 2.7 K blackbody glow from all directions, released when the universe became transparent at 380 000 years; its uniformity and ripples confirm the hot Big Bang and seed structure formation.

Q17. What is cosmological redshift?

Cosmological redshift is the stretching of light's wavelength caused by the expansion of space while the light is in transit. As space expands, the wavelength of a photon travelling through it is stretched in proportion to the expansion factor:

z = (λ_observed − λ_emitted)/λ_emitted = Δλ/λ 1 + z = a_now/a_then (a = scale factor of the universe) For small z: v ≈ cz For large z: the relativistic and cosmological relations must be used.

Redshift is not a Doppler effect in the ordinary sense (the galaxies are not moving through space so much as space is expanding between them), but for nearby galaxies the two descriptions coincide. Measuring z gives both the distance and the look-back time, which is why redshift surveys map the universe's history.

z = Δλ/λ measures the stretching of wavelengths by cosmic expansion; for small z it gives v ≈ cz and hence, through Hubble's law, the distance.

Exercise 26.6 — Hubble's law and its consequences

Q18. State Hubble's law, define the Hubble constant and estimate the age of the universe from it.

Hubble's law: the recession speed of a distant galaxy is proportional to its distance from us:

v = H₀ d H₀ ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ (modern values lie between 67 and 73)

If the expansion has been roughly uniform, the time since all galaxies were together is the distance divided by the speed:

t = d/v = 1/H₀ H₀ = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ = (70 × 10³ m s⁻¹)/(3.086 × 10²² m) = 2.27 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ t = 1/(2.27 × 10⁻¹⁸) = 4.4 × 10¹⁷ s = 1.4 × 10¹⁰ yr

This gives about 14 billion years, close to the accepted 13.8 Gyr (the agreement is not exact because the expansion has not been perfectly uniform — it is accelerating).

v = H₀d with H₀ ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹; t ≈ 1/H₀ ≈ 14 Gyr, the Hubble time.

Q19. A galaxy is at a distance of 100 Mpc. Find its recession speed.

v = H₀ d = 70 × 100 = 7000 km s⁻¹

v ≈ 7.0 × 10³ km s⁻¹ (about 2.3% of the speed of light)

Q20. A galaxy recedes at 21 000 km s⁻¹. Estimate its distance.

d = v/H₀ = 21 000/70 = 300 Mpc = 300 × 3.086 × 10²² m = 9.3 × 10²⁴ m ≈ 9.8 × 10⁸ light years

d ≈ 300 Mpc ≈ 1 billion light years

Q21. Does Hubble's law imply that the Earth is at the centre of the universe? Explain.

No. The law has exactly the same form for an observer in any galaxy: every observer sees all other galaxies receding with speeds proportional to their distances. This is the hallmark of a uniform expansion of space, not of motion away from a special point.

The standard analogy is a rising raisin cake or a balloon being inflated: every raisin moves away from every other, and no raisin is at the centre. Similarly, the Big Bang happened everywhere at once — it was not an explosion at a point in pre-existing space.

No — a uniform expansion looks identical from every galaxy, so there is no centre; the Big Bang occurred everywhere, not at a point.

Q22. What is meant by the "look-back time" of a distant galaxy?

Because light travels at a finite speed, observing a galaxy 1 billion light years away means seeing it as it was 1 billion years ago. Distant objects are therefore seen at an earlier stage of cosmic history — the further we look, the further back in time we see. This is how the CMB lets us observe the universe at 380 000 years old, and how deep surveys reveal galaxies forming in the first billion years.

The light-travel time from the object; distant galaxies are seen as they were in the past, so looking out in space is looking back in time.

Exercise 26.7 — Dark matter and dark energy

Q23. What is dark matter? Describe two pieces of evidence for it.

Dark matter is matter that exerts gravity but does not emit, absorb or reflect light — it is invisible at every wavelength. It is inferred to make up about 27% of the energy content of the universe, roughly five times the ordinary (baryonic) matter.

Evidence 1 — Galaxy rotation curves: stars in the outer parts of spiral galaxies orbit far too fast for the visible mass. From v² = GM/r, the enclosed mass should fall off beyond the visible disc and the rotation curve should decline as 1/√r; instead the curves stay flat, implying a large unseen mass in a halo.

Evidence 2 — Gravitational lensing and clusters: clusters of galaxies bend background light far more than their visible mass allows, and the galaxies within them move too fast to be bound by the visible mass alone. The Bullet Cluster shows the gravitational mass and the hot gas separated during a collision, which is most naturally explained by collisionless dark matter.

Invisible matter detected only through gravity; flat galaxy rotation curves and excess gravitational lensing/cluster velocities are the main evidence, with about 27% of the cosmic energy budget.

Q24. What is dark energy? How does it differ from dark matter?

Dark energy is the unknown component that makes the expansion of the universe accelerate, discovered in 1998 from distant type Ia supernovae that appeared fainter — and therefore farther — than a decelerating universe would predict. It accounts for about 68% of the energy content of the universe.

Dark matterDark energy
Attractive gravity; clumps into halosRepulsive effect; uniform, does not clump
About 27% of the energy budgetAbout 68%
Slows the expansionAccelerates the expansion
Detected through rotation curves and lensingDetected through supernova distances and the CMB

The simplest description of dark energy is Einstein's cosmological constant — an energy density of empty space itself (about 10⁻⁹ J m⁻³), whose nature remains one of the biggest unsolved problems in physics.

Dark energy is a uniform, repulsive component (68%) driving accelerated expansion; dark matter is clumpy, attractive and only 27%. They are entirely different in both effect and evidence.

Q25. What is the composition of the universe according to current measurements?

68% dark energy, 27% dark matter, 5% ordinary matter — the visible universe is a small minority of the whole.

Exercise 26.8 — Black holes

Q26. What is a black hole? Derive or state its Schwarzschild radius.

A black hole is a region of space in which the gravitational field is so strong that nothing — not even light — can escape. Its boundary is the event horizon, and the radius of that horizon for a non-rotating black hole of mass M is the Schwarzschild radius.

Set the escape velocity equal to c: ½mv² = GMm/r with v = c ⇒ r_s = 2GM/c² For the Sun (M = 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg): r_s = 2(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(1.99 × 10³⁰)/(3 × 10⁸)² = 2.65 × 10²⁰/9 × 10¹⁶ = 2.95 × 10³ m ≈ 3 km For the Earth: r_s ≈ 9 mm.

Material inside the horizon must collapse to a singularity at the centre, where known physics breaks down. Black holes are not "cosmic vacuum cleaners": at a distance, their gravity is exactly that of an ordinary mass M.

An object whose escape velocity exceeds c, bounded by the event horizon at r_s = 2GM/c² — about 3 km for the Sun's mass and 9 mm for the Earth's.

Q27. How do stellar-mass and supermassive black holes form?

Stellar-mass black holes (3–100 M☉): a massive star (more than about 20 M☉) burns through its nuclear fuel and its iron core can no longer support itself by fusion. The core collapses in less than a second, driving a supernova explosion and leaving a remnant too massive for a neutron star to resist gravity — so it collapses within its own Schwarzschild radius.

Supermassive black holes (10⁶–10¹⁰ M☉): found at the centres of most galaxies, including Sagittarius A* in the Milky Way (about 4 × 10⁶ M☉). Their formation is still debated — likely seeds from early massive stars or gas clouds that grew by mergers and accretion over billions of years. Their masses correlate with the bulge mass of their host galaxy, suggesting co-evolution.

Stellar-mass: core collapse of a massive star in a supernova. Supermassive: seeds grown by accretion and mergers at galactic centres, now observed through stellar orbits and radio emission.

Q28. If nothing can escape a black hole, how do we detect one?

Indirectly, through its gravitational effects and the radiation of matter falling into it:

  • Stellar orbits: stars whipping around an invisible point at the galactic centre (S2 around Sgr A*) fix the mass precisely.
  • Accretion discs and X-rays: infalling gas heats to millions of kelvin and radiates X-rays before crossing the horizon; quasars are the extreme case.
  • Gravitational waves: merging black holes radiate ripples in spacetime, detected by LIGO since 2015.
  • Event Horizon Telescope: in 2019 the shadow of the supermassive black hole in M87, and in 2022 that of Sgr A*, were imaged at millimetre wavelengths.

Hawking radiation (thermal emission from quantum effects near the horizon) is theoretically predicted but far too faint to observe for astrophysical black holes.

Through stellar dynamics, X-ray emission from accretion, gravitational waves from mergers, and direct imaging of the event-horizon shadow by the EHT.

Q29. What happens to time near a black hole?

According to general relativity, clocks run slower deeper in a gravitational field (gravitational time dilation). For a distant observer, a clock falling toward a black hole appears to slow down and stop as it approaches the event horizon, and the infalling light is redshifted ever more strongly. The falling object itself, however, experiences nothing special at the horizon (for a large black hole) and crosses it in a finite proper time.

For a static observer at radius r outside a mass M: t_∞ = t_local/√(1 − 2GM/rc²) As r → r_s the denominator → 0 and the far-away time interval → ∞.

Gravitational time dilation makes distant observers see infalling clocks slow to a halt and light redshift to infinity at the horizon, while the infalling object crosses it in finite proper time.

Exercise 26.9 — Gravitational waves

Q30. What are gravitational waves? State their origin and speed.

Gravitational waves are ripples in the fabric of spacetime produced by accelerating masses — specifically, by a changing quadrupole moment of a mass distribution. They are predicted by Einstein's general relativity (1916) and travel at the speed of light.

As a wave passes, it alternately stretches and squeezes space in one direction while compressing it in the perpendicular direction, so distances between freely falling objects change by a fractional amount h (the strain) — of order 10⁻²¹ for astrophysical sources.

The strongest sources are compact-object mergers: two black holes or neutron stars spiralling together and coalescing. Continuous sources include rotating neutron stars with asymmetries, and there is a stochastic background from the early universe.

Ripples in spacetime from accelerating masses (especially compact mergers), travelling at c and producing a strain of order 10⁻²¹ that alternately stretches and squeezes space.

Q31. Describe the first direct detection of gravitational waves and its significance.

On 14 September 2015 the two LIGO detectors (Hanford and Livingston, USA) recorded the same signal, GW150914, separated by the 7 ms light-travel time between them. The waveform matched the inspiral and merger of two black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses, forming a 62 M☉ black hole about 1.3 billion light years away, with about 3 M☉ of mass radiated as gravitational waves.

LIGO is a Michelson interferometer with 4 km arms; it detects the strain by measuring changes in the arm lengths of about 10⁻¹⁸ m — a thousandth of a proton diameter — using laser interference and elaborate seismic isolation.

Significance: it confirmed a century-old prediction, provided the first direct evidence that black holes exist as predicted, opened an entirely new window on the universe (gravity rather than light), and earned the 2017 Nobel Prize. The 2017 neutron-star merger GW170817, seen also in light, launched multi-messenger astronomy.

GW150914 (2015): two black holes merging, detected by LIGO's 4 km interferometers; it confirmed general relativity's prediction, proved black holes exist, and opened gravitational-wave astronomy.

Q32. Why are gravitational waves so difficult to detect?

Because gravity is enormously weaker than electromagnetism, so the strain produced even by a catastrophic event is minute — about 10⁻²¹, which for a 4 km arm means a change in length of about 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ m. Detecting that requires:

  • laser interferometry with mirrors suspended as pendulums to isolate them from seismic noise;
  • operation in ultra-high vacuum at cryogenic or well-isolated temperatures to suppress thermal noise;
  • very high laser power and squeezed light to beat shot noise;
  • coincidence between two or more widely separated detectors to reject local disturbances.

Even so, meaningful signals are rare — the first detection came only after decades of development.

Because the strain is only ~10⁻²¹ (a length change of 10⁻¹⁸ m over 4 km), so seismic, thermal and quantum noise must all be suppressed and multiple detectors used in coincidence.

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

This unit is conceptual, but it still carries numericals: quark charges, annihilation energies, Hubble's law, binding energies of a black hole. The eight shapes below are the ones NEB actually sets.

Q33. [Multi-step numerical] A proton is made of uud quarks. Verify the charge of the proton, the antiproton, and the neutron (udd), and find the charge of a Σ⁺ (uus) and a Δ⁰ (udd).

Quark charges: u = +2/3 e, d = −1/3 e, s = −1/3 e Antiquarks carry the opposite sign. p = uud: 2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1 e ✓ n = udd: 2/3 − 1/3 − 1/3 = 0 ✓ p̄ = ūūd̄: −2/3 − 2/3 + 1/3 = −1 e ✓ Σ⁺ = uus: 2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1 e ✓ Δ⁰ = udd: 0 ✓

Note that all three quark charges are thirds of e, so a free quark would have a non-integer charge — which is one reason free quarks are never observed (confinement). The fractional values are not a bookkeeping trick: they are measured from the ratio of production cross-sections in e⁺e⁻ collisions, and they fit the 2/3 : 1/3 pattern exactly.

uud = +1e, udd = 0, ūūd̄ = −1e, uus = +1e; all charges are built from ±2/3e and ±1/3e.

Q34. [Derivation] Show that a particle and its antiparticle can annihilate into two photons, and find the minimum energy of each photon for an electron–positron pair at rest.

Conservation laws: charge 0 = 0, momentum needs two photons travelling in opposite directions, energy: 2m_e c² = 2hν hν = m_e c² = 0.511 MeV λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)(3.0 × 10⁸)/(0.511 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹³) = 2.4 × 10⁻¹² m Each photon gets exactly the rest energy; the pair cannot annihilate into ONE photon, because momentum could not then be conserved in the centre-of-mass frame.

The two-photon result is the basis of PET scanning: a positron emitted by a radiotracer annihilates within millimetres and emits two 511 keV photons back to back, and a ring of detectors records the coincidence line — locating the tumour on the line between them. The same number — 1.02 MeV total — is the threshold for pair production in a heavy nucleus's field.

Two photons of 511 keV each (λ ≈ 2.4 pm), back to back; one photon is forbidden by momentum conservation.

Q35. [Graph reading] A graph of recession speed against distance for galaxies is a straight line through the origin with slope 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. Find the Hubble constant, the age of the universe in years, and the speed of a galaxy at 100 Mpc.

Slope = H₀ = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ = 70 × 10³ m s⁻¹/(3.09 × 10²² m) = 2.27 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ Age t ≈ 1/H₀ = 1/2.27 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 4.4 × 10¹⁷ s = 4.4 × 10¹⁷/(3.15 × 10⁷) = 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years ≈ 14 billion years At 100 Mpc: v = 70 × 100 = 7000 km s⁻¹ = 7.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ (2.3% of c)

The straight line is Hubble's law, v = H₀d, and its slope is the constant itself — the most important number in cosmology. The reciprocal 1/H₀ gives the age only if the expansion has been uniform; the actual age (13.8 Gyr) is slightly less because the expansion was faster in the past, so 1/H₀ is the “Hubble time”, an upper estimate.

H₀ = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹; t ≈ 1/H₀ ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years; v = 7000 km s⁻¹ at 100 Mpc.

Q36. [Combined concept] Find the Schwarzschild radius of a 10 M☉ black hole, and explain why a black hole formed from a massive star cannot be observed directly but is detected through its effects.

R_s = 2GM/c² = 2(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(10 × 2.0 × 10³⁰)/(3.0 × 10⁸)² = 2.67 × 10²¹/9.0 × 10¹⁶ = 2.96 × 10⁴ m ≈ 30 km

Why it cannot be seen: light emitted inside R_s cannot escape, so the object emits nothing. Why it is detected: the accretion disc outside the horizon is heated to millions of kelvin and shines in X-rays; the gravitational pull on a companion star produces a measurable orbit; and merging black holes emit gravitational waves (LIGO, 2015). The 30 km radius for 10 solar masses is a useful scale: a black hole of stellar mass is the size of a small town, yet contains ten suns.

R_s ≈ 30 km for 10 M☉; it is invisible directly but revealed by X-ray emission, companion-star orbits and gravitational waves.

Q37. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): The four fundamental forces were unified in stages as the early universe cooled. Reason (R): Higher temperatures correspond to higher particle energies, allowing the symmetries between forces to be restored.

Both statements are true and R explains A.

Approximate unification sequence going back in time: ~10¹⁵ K (10⁻¹² s): electroweak force separates into electromagnetic + weak ~10²⁷ K (10⁻³⁶ s): strong force separates (GUT transition) ~10³² K (Planck era): gravity separates from the rest Higher T means higher kT, so particles can reach the energy where the symmetry holds. Today the electroweak symmetry is hidden because kT ≪ 100 GeV.

The logic is the same as a phase transition in matter: ice and water are the same substance, but the symmetry is broken at low temperature. Here the “substance” is the force, which is why particle accelerators are time machines — each new energy frontier looks further back into the cooling universe.

Both true, R explains A: high kT restores broken symmetries, so the forces appear unified at high temperature and separate as the universe cools.

Q39. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the energy density of the cosmic microwave background today (T = 2.7 K) and compare it with the energy density of matter.

Radiation energy density: u = aT⁴ with a = 7.57 × 10⁻¹⁶ J m⁻³ K⁻⁴ u = 7.57 × 10⁻¹⁶ × (2.7)⁴ = 7.57 × 10⁻¹⁶ × 53.1 = 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ J m⁻³ Matter: ρ_crit = 3H₀²/8πG ≈ 9 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m⁻³, times c² ⇒ 8 × 10⁻¹⁰ J m⁻³ (with only ~5% in ordinary baryonic matter: 4 × 10⁻¹¹ J m⁻³) So radiation is smaller than matter by roughly 10³–10⁴ today.

Limiting case: going back in time, matter density grows as (1+z)³ while radiation grows as (1+z)⁴, so radiation DOMINATED in the early universe — the two were equal at z ≈ 3400, about 50,000 years after the Big Bang. That is why the early universe is described as a radiation epoch, and why the CMB, which is the relic of that era, still carries a near-perfect blackbody spectrum.

u_CBM ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹⁴ J m⁻³, far below the matter density today; in the past radiation grew as (1+z)⁴ and dominated until z ≈ 3400.

Q40. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “The Higgs boson is the particle that carries the strong force.”
(ii) “Dark matter and dark energy are the same thing seen at different distances.”
(iii) “The Big Bang was an explosion of matter into pre-existing space.”
(iv) “Gravitational waves carry electric charge.”

(i) FALSE — the Higgs boson is the quantum of the Higgs FIELD, which gives particles mass. The strong force is carried by GLUONS; the weak by W⁺, W⁻ and Z⁰; the electromagnetic by photons; gravity (hypothetically) by gravitons. (ii) FALSE — dark matter ATTRACTS (it clusters and explains galactic rotation curves), while dark energy REPELS (it accelerates the expansion, w ≈ −1). They are separate components: about 27% and 68% of the energy budget. (iii) FALSE — there was no outside: space itself expanded, carrying matter with it. The correct picture is a stretching of the metric, not matter flying into a void. (iv) FALSE — gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime and carry energy and angular momentum, but no charge. They are quadrupole (not dipole) waves, which is why they are so weak: the amplitude falls as 1/r and requires an accelerating asymmetric mass.

Statement (i) and (ii) are the two most frequent mix-ups: the Higgs gives mass (it is not the strong force), and dark matter clumps while dark energy stretches.

(i) Higgs gives mass; gluons carry the strong force; (ii) dark matter attracts, dark energy repels; (iii) space itself expanded; (iv) gravitational waves carry energy, not charge.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. What is an antiparticle?

A particle with the same mass as its partner but opposite charge and opposite additive quantum numbers (baryon number, lepton number). Examples are the positron (anti-electron) and the antiproton; a particle and its antiparticle annihilate into photons.

C2. What are quarks?

The fundamental constituents of hadrons, carrying fractional charge (+2e/3 or −e/3) and baryon number 1/3. Six flavours exist (u, d, s, c, b, t), and they are never found free because of colour confinement.

C3. Define baryons and mesons.

Baryons are hadrons made of three quarks (qqq), e.g. the proton (uud) and neutron (udd), with baryon number 1. Mesons are hadrons made of a quark and an antiquark (qq̄), e.g. π⁺ (ud̄), with baryon number 0.

C4. Give the quark composition of the proton and the neutron.

Proton = uud (charge 2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1); neutron = udd (charge 2/3 − 1/3 − 1/3 = 0). Both have baryon number 1.

C5. What are leptons? Give two examples.

Fundamental, point-like particles that do not feel the strong force — the electron, muon, tau and their three neutrinos (plus antiparticles). The electron and muon are charged leptons; neutrinos are neutral.

C6. State three properties of neutrinos.

They are electrically neutral, have extremely small (but non-zero) mass, and interact only through the weak force, so they pass through matter almost unhindered. They are produced copiously in beta decay and in solar fusion.

C7. What is a gluon?

The massless exchange boson of the strong interaction that binds quarks into hadrons. It carries colour charge itself, so gluons interact with one another — the origin of confinement.

C8. State Hubble's law and give the value of the Hubble constant.

v = H₀d: recession speed is proportional to distance. H₀ ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, so a galaxy 1 Mpc away recedes at about 70 km s⁻¹. Its reciprocal gives the Hubble time, about 14 Gyr.

C9. What is the cosmic microwave background?

A nearly uniform 2.725 K blackbody radiation filling the universe, released about 380 000 years after the Big Bang when the plasma became neutral and transparent. Its discovery in 1965 is the strongest evidence for the hot Big Bang.

C10. What is dark matter?

Matter that interacts gravitationally but not electromagnetically, so it emits and absorbs no light. It makes up about 27% of the universe's energy content and is inferred from flat galaxy rotation curves, cluster dynamics and gravitational lensing.

C11. What is dark energy?

The unknown component (about 68% of the energy budget) that makes cosmic expansion accelerate, discovered through distant supernovae in 1998. Unlike dark matter it is uniform, does not clump, and has a repulsive gravitational effect.

C12. What is a black hole and what is its event horizon?

A region where gravity is so strong that the escape velocity exceeds c. The event horizon is the boundary at r_s = 2GM/c², about 3 km for a solar mass, beyond which no light or matter can return.

C13. What are gravitational waves?

Ripples in spacetime produced by accelerating masses, predicted by general relativity and travelling at c. They alternately stretch and squeeze space, with a strain of order 10⁻²¹ for astrophysical mergers, and were first detected by LIGO in 2015.

C14. Name the four fundamental forces in order of decreasing strength.

Strong (1) > electromagnetic (10⁻²) > weak (10⁻⁶) > gravitational (10⁻³⁹), with exchange particles the gluon, photon, W/Z bosons and (hypothetically) the graviton.

C15. Why is the universe said to be expanding rather than exploding?

Because the expansion is uniform and has no centre: every galaxy moves away from every other with speed proportional to distance, and the same law holds for observers anywhere. An explosion would have a centre and an edge; the Big Bang instead stretched space itself everywhere at once.

C16. What is meant by the Standard Model?

The theory of the 12 fundamental matter particles (6 quarks and 6 leptons), the gauge bosons that carry the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces, and the Higgs boson. It excludes gravity and does not explain dark matter or dark energy.

C17. Why was the discovery of the Higgs boson important?

Because it confirmed the mechanism by which elementary particles acquire mass. Without the Higgs field the quarks and leptons would be massless, atoms could not form, and there would be no ordinary matter. It was found at CERN in 2012 with a mass of about 125 GeV.

C18. What is the significance of the 2017 neutron-star merger GW170817?

It was the first event observed in both gravitational waves and light, launching multi-messenger astronomy. It confirmed that gravitational waves travel at the speed of light to within one part in 10¹⁵, and showed that heavy elements such as gold are forged in neutron-star mergers.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Describe the Standard Model classification of particles, giving the quark content of the proton, neutron and pion, and naming the exchange particles of the four forces.

Matter particles divide into quarks and leptons, each in three generations with antiparticles:

Quarks: u, d (first); c, s (second); t, b (third) — charges +2e/3 and −e/3 Leptons: e, ν_e; μ, ν_μ; τ, ν_τ — charges −e and 0

Hadrons are quark composites. Baryons are qqq: proton uud (+1e), neutron udd (0). Mesons are qq̄: π⁺ = ud̄ (+1e), π⁻ = ūd (−1e), π⁰ is a quark–antiquark mixture. Mesons mediate the residual strong force between nucleons.

Force carriers: gluon (strong), photon (electromagnetic), W⁺, W⁻ and Z⁰ (weak), and the hypothetical graviton (gravity). The Higgs boson is the scalar quantum of the field that gives masses to all the others.

12 matter particles (quarks + leptons) with antiparticles, hadrons built as qqq (baryons) or qq̄ (mesons), and gauge bosons gluon/photon/W/Z plus the Higgs; gravity alone lies outside the model.

L2. [6] State Hubble's law, describe the three main evidences for the Big Bang, and explain why the law does not imply a centre to the universe.

Hubble's law, v = H₀d with H₀ ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, expresses the uniform expansion of space. Its reciprocal gives the Hubble time, 1/H₀ ≈ 14 Gyr, comparable to the age of the oldest stars — a first indication that everything began at a finite time.

Evidence: (i) the redshift–distance relation itself, showing that galaxies were closer together in the past; (ii) the 2.7 K cosmic microwave background, the relic radiation from when the universe became transparent at 380 000 years; (iii) the primordial abundances of about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium, which only Big Bang nucleosynthesis can produce.

No centre: in a uniform expansion every observer sees the same v = H₀d law, because it is space itself that is stretching, not galaxies flying through pre-existing space. The raisin-cake or balloon analogy makes this clear: all raisins separate from all others, none is central. The Big Bang occurred everywhere at once, not at a point.

v = H₀d; evidence = expansion, CMB and light-element abundances; no centre because a uniform expansion looks identical from every galaxy and the Big Bang occurred everywhere.

L3. [5] Calculate the Schwarzschild radius of a black hole of 10 solar masses, and explain what happens to an object that crosses the event horizon.

r_s = 2GM/c² with M = 10 × 1.99 × 10³⁰ = 1.99 × 10³¹ kg = 2(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(1.99 × 10³¹)/(9 × 10¹⁶) = 2.65 × 10²¹/9 × 10¹⁶ = 2.95 × 10⁴ m ≈ 30 km

So a 10 M☉ black hole has a horizon about 30 km across in radius — a city-sized region containing ten solar masses, giving an average density far higher than a nucleus.

An object crossing the horizon notices nothing locally (for a large black hole, tidal forces at the horizon are modest), but once inside, all paths lead inward: no signal or light can return. A distant observer sees the object's clock slow and its light redshift without limit, approaching but never appearing to cross. Inside, the object is drawn inevitably to the singularity, where tidal forces become infinite and known physics breaks down.

r_s ≈ 30 km for 10 M☉; the infalling object crosses without local drama but cannot return, while a distant observer sees its clock freeze and its light redshift away.

L4. [5] Explain dark matter and dark energy, giving the evidence for each and the composition of the universe.

Dark matter (about 27%) is invisible matter known through its gravity. The evidence is that galaxy rotation curves remain flat far beyond the visible disc — the stars orbit too fast for the luminous mass enclosed — and that galaxy clusters bend background light, and hold their members, as if they contained far more mass than is seen. It clumps into halos and slows the expansion.

Dark energy (about 68%) is a uniform component with a repulsive gravitational effect that accelerates the expansion. It was inferred in 1998 when distant type Ia supernovae proved fainter than expected for a decelerating universe, and it is independently supported by the CMB and by baryon acoustic oscillation measurements.

Composition: about 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter and only 5% ordinary baryonic matter — so everything we can actually see is a small minority of the universe. Identifying the nature of the dark 95% is the central open problem of modern physics.

Dark matter (27%) clumps and gravitates, inferred from rotation curves and lensing; dark energy (68%) is uniform and repulsive, inferred from supernova distances. Ordinary matter is only 5%.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 26

1. Calling a meson "two quarks". A meson is a quark and an antiquark, so its baryon number is 0. Two quarks would have baryon number 2/3, which is not observed.

2. Mixing up dark matter and dark energy. Dark matter clumps and attracts; dark energy is uniform and repels. Their evidence and their fractions (27% vs 68%) are entirely different.

3. Saying the Big Bang was an explosion at a point. Space itself expanded; the Big Bang happened everywhere, and there is no centre or edge.

4. Forgetting the Mpc conversion in Hubble-law numericals. 1 Mpc = 3.086 × 10²² m and H₀ must be used consistently, either as 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ with d in Mpc or as 2.27 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ with d in metres.

5. Using r_s = GM/c² or forgetting the 2. The Schwarzschild radius is r_s = 2GM/c², from equating the escape velocity to c. Dropping the 2 halves every answer.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. For classification questions, write the composition first (qqq, qq̄, or "no quarks"), then the charge check. For cosmology questions, give the number (2.7 K, 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, 13.8 Gyr, 68/27/5%) — specific values earn the mark far more reliably than general prose.
