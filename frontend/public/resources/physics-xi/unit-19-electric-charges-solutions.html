Unit 19 — Electric Charges

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  3 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Coulomb's law is the only formula, but two thirds of the marks in this unit come from definitions and explanations. Write the properties of charge and the three methods of charging until they are automatic; the numericals will then take care of themselves.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — state Coulomb's law; define quantisation of charge; state the properties of electric charge; how does a rod become charged by induction; why does a charged rod attract neutral paper.
  • 4 marks — Coulomb's law numerical in free space or in a medium; net force in a three-charge line; number of electrons transferred.

Exercise 19.1 — Electric charge and its properties

Q1. What is electric charge? State its four basic properties.

Electric charge is the intrinsic property of matter that causes it to experience and exert electric force. It exists in two kinds, positive (protons) and negative (electrons), and like charges repel while unlike charges attract.

  1. Quantisation: all free charges are integral multiples of the elementary charge e: q = ne, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  2. Conservation: the net charge of an isolated system is constant; charges can be transferred but never created or destroyed.
  3. Additivity: charges add algebraically as scalars; total charge is q₁ + q₂ + … with their signs.
  4. Invariance: the charge of a particle is the same in all reference frames, whatever its speed.

Charge is the property of matter responsible for electric force; it is quantised, conserved, additive and invariant.

Q2. Why do the two kinds of charge exist, and how do the quantities of positive and negative charge in a neutral body compare?

A body is neutral when the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the net charge is zero. When electrons are removed the body becomes positive (deficient in electrons); when electrons are added it becomes negative. Protons are bound in nuclei and are not transferred in ordinary charging, so in practice negatively charged bodies have gained electrons and positively charged bodies have lost them.

Neutral means equal numbers of protons and electrons; charging is the transfer of electrons only, protons staying in the nucleus.

Q3. Explain why a charged rod attracts small pieces of paper although the paper is neutral.

The charged rod creates an electric field that polarises the paper: the molecules of the paper have their charges slightly displaced, so the side nearer the rod acquires a charge of opposite sign and the far side a like charge. The attractive force on the nearer, opposite charge is stronger than the repulsive force on the farther, like charge (Coulomb's law falls with distance), so the net force is attractive.

Because a neutral body can be polarised in this way, a charged body can always attract small neutral objects — a fact used to test whether an object is charged.

Induced polarisation brings an opposite charge nearer than the like charge, and the nearer attraction wins because Coulomb's force decreases with distance.

Exercise 19.2 — Quantisation and conservation of charge

Q4. State and explain the quantisation of charge. Find the number of electrons in −1 C and in −1 µC.

Quantisation means that the charge on any body is an integral multiple of the elementary charge e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C:

q = ne (n is an integer, positive or negative) For 1 C: n = q/e = 1/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons For 1 µC: n = 10⁻⁶/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.25 × 10¹² electrons

Quantisation arises because charge is carried by discrete particles. At the macroscopic level the unit e is so small that charge appears continuous, which is why large charges are always quoted as multiples but any charge equivalent to a fraction of e is impossible.

q = ne with e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; 1 C is 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons and 1 µC is 6.25 × 10¹² electrons.

Q5. State the law of conservation of charge and give two illustrations of it.

The net electric charge of an isolated system remains constant: charge may be transferred from one body to another, but the algebraic total never changes.

  • Identical spheres in contact: +6 µC and −2 µC share their net +4 µC equally, giving +2 µC each — total +4 µC before and after.
  • Pair production: a photon of energy greater than 1.02 MeV in the field of a nucleus creates an electron (−e) and a positron (+e), so the net charge stays zero.

Charge conservation is the most firmly established conservation law of physics; no experiment has ever seen it violated.

The algebraic total charge of an isolated system is constant; contact sharing and electron–positron creation both preserve it exactly.

Q6. Two identical conducting spheres carry +8 µC and −4 µC. They are brought into contact and separated. Find the charge on each, and the number of electrons transferred.

Total charge = 8 + (−4) = +4 µC Identical spheres share equally: q = +4/2 = +2 µC each The negative sphere loses electrons to the positive one until both reach +2 µC. Change of the originally negative sphere: from −4 to +2 = 6 µC of charge lost, i.e. 6 µC of electrons transferred. n = 6 × 10⁻⁶/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.75 × 10¹³ electrons

Each sphere ends with +2 µC; 3.75 × 10¹³ electrons move from the negative sphere to the positive one.

Q7. Can a body have a charge of (a) 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, (b) 8 × 10⁻²⁰ C, (c) 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C? Justify.

(a) 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.0 → possible (one electron/proton) (b) 8 × 10⁻²⁰/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 0.5 → impossible (half an electron) (c) 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 0.75 → impossible

Only (a) is possible. The charge must be an integral multiple of e; a fractional multiple violates quantisation.

Exercise 19.3 — Charging by friction, contact and induction

Q8. Describe the three methods of charging a body.

MethodHow it worksResult
FrictionTwo dissimilar insulators are rubbed together; electrons are transferred from the one with lower electron affinity to the otherBoth bodies acquire equal and opposite charges
Conduction (contact)A charged conductor touches an uncharged one; charge flows until the potential is equalThe second body acquires the same sign as the first (total charge shared)
InductionA charged body is brought near, but not touching, an isolated conductor; charges separate inside it, and the conductor is then earthed (or split)The conductor acquires the opposite sign to the inducing body, without contact

Friction: equal and opposite charges. Conduction: same sign, charge shared. Induction: opposite sign, no contact, requires earthing or separation.

Q9. Explain charging by induction step by step, using a positively charged rod and a neutral metal sphere.

  1. Bring the positively charged rod near the neutral sphere (without touching). The sphere's free electrons are attracted toward the rod, so the near side becomes negative and the far side positive.
  2. Connect the sphere briefly to earth (or touch it with a finger). The electrons repelled to the far side — and those repelled from the far side — flow to earth, leaving the sphere with a net negative charge.
  3. Remove the earth connection while the rod is still in place.
  4. Remove the rod. The remaining negative charge redistributes uniformly over the sphere.

The sphere ends up with the opposite charge to the rod. Note that the earth connection must be broken before the rod is removed, otherwise the induced charge flows back to earth.

Polarise, earth, break the earth, then remove the rod; the sphere is left with charge opposite to the inducing rod.

Q10. Why must the earth connection be removed before the inducing rod is taken away?

While the rod is near, the sphere holds an induced negative charge on the far side and positive on the near side. Earthing allows charge to flow and leaves the net negative charge trapped. If the rod were removed first while the earth connection remained, the sphere's remaining electrons would be repelled by nothing and would flow to earth, neutralising the sphere. Removing the earth first locks the excess charge in place.

Because with the earth still connected and the rod gone, the trapped charge would simply drain back to earth; the order of operations fixes the final charge.

Q11. Distinguish between charging by conduction and by induction.

ConductionInduction

Contact gives the same sign and divides the charge; induction gives the opposite sign without contact and without using up the inducing charge.

Exercise 19.4 — Coulomb's law

Q12. State Coulomb's law and write it in scalar and vector form.

Statement: the electrostatic force of attraction or repulsion between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, and acts along the line joining them.

Scalar form: F = k q₁q₂/r², k = 1/(4πε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻² Vector form: F₁₂ = k q₁q₂ r̂₁₂/r² (force on 1 due to 2) where r̂₁₂ is the unit vector from 2 to 1. The sign of q₁q₂ automatically gives attraction (negative product) or repulsion (positive product). In a medium of dielectric constant K (relative permittivity ε_r): F_medium = F_vacuum/K

F = kq₁q₂/r² along the line of centres; k = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻², and the force is reduced by the factor K inside a dielectric.

Q13. What is the physical meaning of the permittivity of free space ε₀, and what is its value?

ε₀ is the constant that measures the ability of free space to allow an electric field to be established — the "electrical stiffness" of vacuum. It appears in Coulomb's law as 1/(4πε₀) and in Gauss's law. Its value is

ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻² (F m⁻¹) so 1/(4πε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²

In a material medium the permittivity is ε = ε_r ε₀ = Kε₀, and the force between the same two charges falls to 1/K of its vacuum value because the medium's molecules polarise and partly screen the field.

ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹ is the permittivity of vacuum; a dielectric of constant K reduces the force by K because its polarised molecules screen the charges.

Q14. Compare the electric force with the gravitational force between two protons.

F_e/F_g = [k e²/r²]/[G m_p²/r²] = k e²/(G m_p²) = (9 × 10⁹)(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)²/[(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)²] = (9 × 10⁹ × 2.56 × 10⁻³⁸)/(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 2.79 × 10⁻⁵⁴) = 2.30 × 10⁻²⁸/1.86 × 10⁻⁶⁴ ≈ 1.2 × 10³⁶

The electric repulsion between two protons is about 10³⁶ times stronger than their gravitational attraction — yet gravity dominates on astronomical scales because matter is almost exactly neutral, so the electric forces cancel while mass always adds.

F_e/F_g ≈ 10³⁶; electricity is vastly stronger per particle, but neutrality makes gravity the ruler of the large scale.

Exercise 19.5 — Coulomb's law numericals

Q15. Two charges of +2 µC and +3 µC are 30 cm apart in air. Find the force between them.

q₁ = 2 × 10⁻⁶ C, q₂ = 3 × 10⁻⁶ C, r = 0.30 m F = kq₁q₂/r² = (9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² = (9 × 10⁹ × 6 × 10⁻¹²)/0.09 = 0.054/0.09 = 0.60 N

F = 0.60 N, repulsive (like charges), acting along the line joining them

Q16. Two point charges, +4 µC and −4 µC, are 20 cm apart in air. Find the force and state its nature.

F = k|q₁q₂|/r² = (9 × 10⁹ × 4 × 10⁻⁶ × 4 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = (9 × 10⁹ × 16 × 10⁻¹²)/0.04 = 0.144/0.04 = 3.6 N

F = 3.6 N, attractive — each charge is pulled toward the other

Q17. Two identical charges repel each other with 0.9 N when 10 cm apart. Find the magnitude of each charge.

F = kq²/r² ⇒ q² = Fr²/k q² = (0.9 × 0.01)/(9 × 10⁹) = 1 × 10⁻¹² q = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ C

Each charge is 1 µC (both of the same sign, since the force is repulsive)

Q18. How does the force between two charges change if (a) the distance is doubled, (b) each charge is doubled and the distance is halved, (c) the whole system is immersed in water (K = 81)?

(a) F ∝ 1/r²: doubling r gives F/4 (b) F ∝ q₁q₂/r²: doubling both charges ×4, halving r ×4 ⇒ 16F (c) F_water = F_air/81

(a) one quarter, (b) sixteen times, (c) one eighty-first — the dielectric constant screens the interaction.

Q19. Two charges +5 µC and −5 µC are 1 m apart. Where must a third charge be placed so that it is in equilibrium, and what is the nature of the equilibrium?

The third charge must lie on the line joining the two, on the side of the smaller-magnitude... both have equal magnitude, so the null point lies at the midpoint. Let it be at distance x from +5 µC toward the −5 µC. There the fields from the two charges are equal and opposite only if the charge is between them (outside, both fields point the same way).

At the midpoint, x = 0.5 m: field due to +5 µC: k(5 × 10⁻⁶)/0.5², pointing away from +5 µC field due to −5 µC: k(5 × 10⁻⁶)/0.5², pointing toward −5 µC (same direction) Net field = 2 × k(5 × 10⁻⁶)/0.25, not zero.

So for two opposite charges of equal magnitude the field does not vanish anywhere on the line outside or between them at the midpoint — the two fields add everywhere between them. Equilibrium is possible only for same-sign charges, where the null point lies between them, closer to the smaller one.

For equal opposite charges there is no neutral point on the axis (the fields add). A neutral point exists only between two like charges, at the point where the two field magnitudes are equal.

Q20. Charges of +4 µC and +1 µC are 30 cm apart. Locate the neutral point.

Let the neutral point be x from the +4 µC charge, on the line between them. k(4)/x² = k(1)/(0.30 − x)² Taking square roots: 2/x = 1/(0.30 − x) 2(0.30 − x) = x ⇒ 0.60 = 3x ⇒ x = 0.20 m

The neutral point is 20 cm from the +4 µC charge (10 cm from the +1 µC charge), closer to the smaller charge as expected.

Exercise 19.6 — Force in a medium and the superposition principle

Q21. State the principle of superposition of electrostatic forces.

The net electrostatic force on a charge due to several other charges is the vector sum of the individual forces that each would exert if the others were absent:

F_net = F₁ + F₂ + F₃ + … (vector addition)

Each pair interacts independently of the presence of the others, which is what allows any complicated charge distribution to be handled by adding pair-wise Coulomb forces. The same principle underlies the superposition of electric fields.

The net force is the vector sum of the individual pair-wise Coulomb forces; each pair acts as if the others were not there.

Q22. Two charges of +2 µC and −2 µC are placed 10 cm apart in air. A third charge of +1 µC is placed midway between them. Find the net force on it.

The middle charge is 5 cm from each. Force due to +2 µC: F₁ = 9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 1 × 10⁻⁶/(0.05)² = 1.8 × 10⁻²/2.5 × 10⁻³ = 7.2 N, repulsive → away from the +2 µC Force due to −2 µC: same magnitude 7.2 N, attractive → toward the −2 µC Both forces point in the same direction (from the +2 µC toward the −2 µC). Net force = 7.2 + 7.2 = 14.4 N

F_net = 14.4 N, directed from the positive toward the negative charge (the two forces add rather than cancel).

Q23. A charge of 2 µC is placed 10 cm from a charge of 3 µC in a medium of dielectric constant 5. Find the force.

F_air = (9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.10)² = 0.054/0.01 = 5.4 N F_medium = F_air/K = 5.4/5 = 1.08 N

F = 1.08 N, repulsive — one fifth of the vacuum value.

Q24. Why does a dielectric reduce the force between two charges?

The molecules of a dielectric are polarised by the field of the charges: their positive and negative parts shift slightly in opposite directions, creating a field of their own that opposes the applied field. The net field inside the medium is therefore weaker than in vacuum, and the force on each charge — which depends on the field of the other as modified by the medium — is reduced by the factor K (the dielectric constant or relative permittivity).

Polarised molecules of the medium set up an opposing field that partially screens the charges, so F is divided by K.

Exercise 19.7 — The Faraday ice pail experiment

Q25. Describe the Faraday ice pail experiment and state what it proves.

A metal bucket (the "ice pail") stands on an insulating support, with a gold-leaf electroscope connected to it. A charged metal ball on an insulating handle is lowered inside the bucket without touching it:

  1. As the ball enters, the electroscope diverges — induced charge appears on the outside of the bucket, of the same sign as the ball.
  2. The divergence increases until the ball is fully inside; then it stays constant however deep the ball goes.
  3. If the ball is allowed to touch the inside of the bucket, the electroscope's divergence does not change. The ball is then found to be completely discharged.
  4. On removing the ball, the electroscope remains diverged, holding the whole charge.

What it proves: all the charge of a conductor resides on its outer surface; the inside of a hollow conductor carries no charge. It also shows that charge is conserved (transferred completely, not created) and provides the standard test of the sign of a charge.

Charge resides only on the outer surface of a conductor; touching the inside transfers all the charge, and the electroscope reading is unchanged in the process.

Q26. How can the Faraday ice pail be used to determine the sign of an unknown charge?

First charge the bucket with a known sign (say positive) using a known charged rod, and note the direction of the electroscope's deflection. Discharge everything, then introduce the unknown charge into the bucket. If the electroscope deflects in the same direction as before, the unknown charge is positive (it induces the same sign on the outside of the bucket); if it deflects the opposite way, the charge is negative.

Compare the direction of deflection produced by a known charge with that produced by the unknown: the same direction means the same sign.

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Electrostatics questions mix three tools: Coulomb's law, the field of a configuration, and the logic of charge sharing and induction. The eight shapes below are built from those three.

Q27. [Multi-step numerical] Three charges are placed on a line: +4.0 μC at x = 0, −2.0 μC at x = 0.30 m, +1.0 μC at x = 0.60 m. Find the net force on the middle charge.

Force on the −2 μC from the +4 μC (0.30 m away, attractive, towards −x): F₁ = 9 × 10⁹ × 4 × 10⁻⁶ × 2 × 10⁻⁶/(0.30)² = 7.2 × 10⁻²/0.09 = 0.80 N towards −x Force on the −2 μC from the +1 μC (0.30 m away, attractive, towards +x): F₂ = 9 × 10⁹ × 1 × 10⁻⁶ × 2 × 10⁻⁶/(0.30)² = 6.0 × 10⁻³ = 0.20 N towards +x Net force = 0.80 − 0.20 = 0.60 N towards the +4 μC charge (towards −x).

The middle charge is pulled both ways, so the net force is a difference — the standard trap is to add the magnitudes. Note also that the two forces act at the same point, so once the directions are settled the problem is one-dimensional bookkeeping.

Net force 0.60 N on the middle charge, directed towards the +4.0 μC charge.

Q28. [Derivation] Show from Coulomb's law that the electric field inside a uniformly charged spherical shell is zero.

Consider the point P inside a uniformly charged shell of radius R. Draw a double cone through P with its apex at P. The two caps cut from the shell have areas A₁ and A₂ at distances r₁ and r₂ from P. By similar triangles (or the solid angle): A₁/r₁² = A₂/r₂² Charge on each cap ∝ area, and its field at P ∝ q/r²: E₁ ∝ (A₁/r₁²)σ, E₂ ∝ (A₂/r₂²)σ These are equal because A₁/r₁² = A₂/r₂². The two caps push in OPPOSITE directions and cancel. Since every such pair cancels, the net field at P is zero.

This is the same argument that makes Newton's shell theorem work for gravity, and it is the reason a conductor's charge resides entirely on its outer surface: if any charge were inside, the field at a point in the cavity wall would not vanish. Note the contrast with a solid charged sphere, where the field inside is not zero but grows linearly from the centre.

Opposite caps subtend equal A/r², so their fields cancel pairwise and E = 0 everywhere inside a uniform shell.

Q29. [Graph reading] The graph of force between two small charged spheres against separation 1/r² is a straight line of slope 2.7 × 10⁻² N m². Find the product of the charges.

F = kq₁q₂/r², so a graph of F against 1/r² has slope kq₁q₂: kq₁q₂ = 2.7 × 10⁻² N m² q₁q₂ = 2.7 × 10⁻²/9 × 10⁹ = 3.0 × 10⁻¹² C² So, for example, q₁ = q₂ = √(3.0 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.7 × 10⁻⁶ C = 1.7 μC each.

A straight line through the origin is the verification of the inverse-square law; its slope gives the product of the charges, never the individual charges — that is why the question asks for the product. Curvature in the plotted points would signal either that the spheres are too close (charge redistribution) or that a background charge is present.

q₁q₂ = 3.0 × 10⁻¹² C² (e.g. 1.7 μC each); the straight line verifies F ∝ 1/r².

Q30. [Combined concept] Two identical conducting spheres carrying +5.0 μC and −1.0 μC are touched and then separated by 0.10 m. Find the final force between them.

Touching: total charge = +5.0 − 1.0 = +4.0 μC, shared equally: q = +2.0 μC on each Separated by 0.10 m: F = 9 × 10⁹ × (2.0 × 10⁻⁶)²/(0.10)² = 9 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻¹²/0.010 = 3.6 × 10⁻²/0.010 = 3.6 N (repulsive)

Two steps, two ideas: charge conservation decides the total, and symmetry (identical spheres) decides the share. The force changes sign from attractive to repulsive, which is the interesting part of the question — a student who computes only the magnitude misses the physics. Inside a conductor the charges redistribute so that the field is zero, which is why the spheres equalise.

+2.0 μC each after touching; F = 3.6 N repulsive at 0.10 m.

Q31. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): The charge on a conductor resides on its outer surface. Reason (R): Inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium the field must be zero, and like charges repel as far apart as possible.

Both statements are true and R explains A.

If E were not zero inside, free electrons would keep moving — not equilibrium. Gauss's law with a surface just inside the conductor gives enclosed charge = 0, so all the charge lies on the surface. Like charges then repel to the outermost possible positions, which is the surface itself. Consequences: the field just outside is σ/ε₀ and perpendicular to the surface; charge collects at points of high curvature (the lightning rod).

The curvature point deserves a line of its own: at a sharp point the surface charge density is largest, so the local field is strongest and can ionise the air — the mechanism of action of a lightning conductor as well as the reason a charged balloon crackles near a sharp edge.

Both true, R explains A: E = 0 inside forces all charge to the surface, where mutual repulsion spreads it outward.

Q33. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the ratio of the electric force to the gravitational force between two protons, and state what the result implies for atomic structure.

F_e/F_g = kq²/(Gm²) = (9 × 10⁹)(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)²/[(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)²] = (9 × 10⁹)(2.56 × 10⁻³⁸)/[(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(2.79 × 10⁻⁵⁴)] = 2.3 × 10⁻²⁸/1.86 × 10⁻⁶⁴ ≈ 1.2 × 10³⁶

Implication: the electric repulsion between protons is 10³⁶ times their gravitational attraction — utterly dominant. Nuclei are held together by the strong nuclear force, which is why gravity is irrelevant in atoms and why heavy nuclei eventually fission. Conversely, gravity only wins on astronomical scales because matter is almost exactly neutral: the residual charge imbalance of the universe is smaller than one part in 10³⁰.

The limiting case worth stating: if the electric force were only slightly stronger relative to the strong force, no nucleus heavier than hydrogen would exist; if it were much weaker, atoms would not form before collapsing.

F_e/F_g ≈ 10³⁶ — electromagnetism rules the atom and gravity only rules bulk neutral matter.

Q34. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “Coulomb's law applies to charged spheres of any size at any separation.”
(ii) “The electric force between two charges changes if a third charge is placed nearby.”
(iii) “A body can have a charge of 1.6 × 10⁻²⁰ C.”
(iv) “Charging by induction requires contact between the bodies.”

(i) PARTLY FALSE — Coulomb's law holds for POINT charges or for uniformly charged spheres ONLY when their separation is much greater than their radii. Close up, the charge redistributes and the inverse square fails. (ii) FALSE — the force between two charges is pairwise; a third charge adds a third force vector but does not alter the original pair. (Superposition.) (iii) FALSE — 1.6 × 10⁻²⁰ C is one hundredth of the elementary charge; charge is quantised in units of e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, so only integer multiples are possible. (iv) FALSE — induction is charging WITHOUT contact: bring a charged rod near an isolated conductor, earth it briefly, then remove the earth and the rod.

Statement (iii) is the quantisation trap: any free charge must be N e with N an integer, so 1.6 × 10⁻²⁰ C (0.1 e) is impossible.

(i) point charges or far-separated spheres; (ii) forces superpose, the pair is unaffected; (iii) charge is quantised in units of e; (iv) induction needs no contact.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. State Coulomb's law in words and in symbols.

The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation, acting along the joining line: F = kq₁q₂/r² with k = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². It is repulsive for like charges and attractive for unlike charges.

C2. Define the coulomb.

One coulomb is the charge that, when placed one metre from an equal charge in vacuum, experiences a force of 9 × 10⁹ N — equivalently, the charge carried by 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons.

C3. State the quantisation of charge.

Any charge is an integral multiple of the elementary charge: q = ne, with e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and n an integer. Fractional charges are not observed in free particles.

C4. State the law of conservation of charge.

The net charge of an isolated system is constant: charge may be transferred between bodies but the algebraic total is unchanged. It is verified by contact sharing of charges and by electron–positron pair creation.

C5. What is meant by additivity of charge?

Charges add as scalars with their signs: the total charge of a system is q₁ + q₂ + q₃ + …, independent of their positions. This is why a +3 µC and a −1 µC charge together constitute a net +2 µC, not 4 µC.

C6. Why is charge called invariant?

Because the charge of a particle is the same in every inertial frame: it does not depend on the particle's speed. Mass and length change relativistically, but charge does not; this is confirmed by the exact neutrality of atoms even though their electrons move fast.

C7. Explain charging by friction.

When two dissimilar insulators are rubbed, electrons are transferred from the material with the lower electron affinity to the other. The body losing electrons becomes positive and the body gaining them negative, with equal magnitudes because the total charge is conserved.

C8. Why does a charged body attract a neutral body?

The charged body polarises the neutral one, bringing an opposite charge nearer and a like charge farther away. Since the Coulomb force falls with distance, the nearer attraction exceeds the farther repulsion and the net force is attractive.

C9. What is the dielectric constant and how does it affect the force?

The dielectric constant K = ε/ε₀ is the ratio of the permittivity of a medium to that of vacuum. The force between two charges in that medium is reduced to F_vacuum/K, because the polarised molecules of the medium produce an opposing field that partially screens the charges.

C10. State the superposition principle for electric forces.

The net force on a charge due to several others is the vector sum of the individual forces each would exert alone: F = F₁ + F₂ + …. Each pair interacts independently of the rest.

C11. Why does charge reside on the outer surface of a conductor?

Because like charges repel, they push each other as far apart as the conductor allows, which is the outer surface. Inside a conductor the field must be zero in equilibrium, so no charge can remain there; the Faraday ice pail experiment demonstrates this directly.

C12. What is meant by a test charge?

A small positive charge used to probe an electric field. It must be small enough not to disturb the charge distribution producing the field, and it is taken as positive so that the direction of the force on it gives the direction of the field.

C13. Give two differences between electric and gravitational forces.

(i) Electric forces can be attractive or repulsive, gravitational forces are always attractive. (ii) The electric force between two protons is about 10³⁶ times stronger than their gravitational attraction, yet gravity dominates on large scales because matter is almost neutral. (Also: gravity acts on mass, electricity on charge.)

C14. Why can a conductor not be charged by friction as easily as an insulator?

Because charges in a conductor are free to move, so any charge produced locally by rubbing immediately spreads over the whole surface or leaks to the hand and the surroundings. Insulators hold the transferred charge locally, so a net charge can accumulate on the rubbed region.

C15. What happens if two charged spheres touch and then separate? State the general rule.

The total charge is shared. For identical spheres it divides equally: each gets (q₁ + q₂)/2. For unequal spheres the charge divides in proportion to the capacitances (radii, for isolated spheres). In every case the algebraic total is conserved.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] State Coulomb's law and express it in vector form. Hence explain how the sign of the product q₁q₂ determines the direction of the force.

Statement as in Q12. In vector form, with r̂₂₁ the unit vector from charge 2 to charge 1:

F₂₁ = k q₁q₂ r̂₂₁/r² If q₁q₂ > 0 (like charges), F₂₁ is along +r̂₂₁ — that is, away from charge 2: repulsion. If q₁q₂ < 0 (unlike charges), F₂₁ is along −r̂₂₁ — toward charge 2: attraction. By Newton's third law, F₁₂ = −F₂₁, so the forces are equal and opposite regardless of the magnitudes of the charges.

The 1/r² dependence makes the force fall rapidly with distance; k = 1/(4πε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻² in vacuum.

F₂₁ = kq₁q₂r̂₂₁/r²; a positive product gives repulsion along r̂, a negative product gives attraction along −r̂, and the two forces on the pair are equal and opposite.

L2. [5] Two charges, +3 µC and +12 µC, are 40 cm apart in air. Find the point on the line between them where the net force on a small test charge is zero.

Let the point be x from the +3 µC charge and (0.40 − x) from the +12 µC charge. k(3 × 10⁻⁶)/x² = k(12 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.40 − x)² Merely equating magnitudes: 3/x² = 12/(0.40 − x)² (0.40 − x)²/x² = 4 (0.40 − x)/x = 2 0.40 − x = 2x ⇒ 0.40 = 3x ⇒ x = 0.133 m

The neutral point is 13.3 cm from the +3 µC charge and 26.7 cm from the +12 µC charge — nearer the smaller charge, as always for like charges.

13.3 cm from the +3 µC charge (and 26.7 cm from the +12 µC charge); a test charge there feels equal and opposite forces.

L3. [5] Explain charging by induction in detail and state three differences between charging by induction and by conduction.

See Q9 for the four steps: polarise with the inducing charge, earth the conductor, remove the earth, then remove the inducer. The conductor ends with the opposite sign and the inducing body keeps its charge.

ConductionInduction
Contact requiredNo contact

Induction uses polarisation plus earthing (or splitting); the induced charge is opposite in sign, no contact occurs and the inducing charge is not consumed.

L4. [4] A charge of 5 µC is 20 cm from a charge of −3 µC in air. Find the magnitude and direction of the force on each, and state what happens if both are placed in a liquid of dielectric constant 6.

F = k|q₁q₂|/r² = (9 × 10⁹ × 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = (9 × 10⁹ × 15 × 10⁻¹²)/0.04 = 0.135/0.04 = 3.375 N In the liquid: F' = 3.375/6 = 0.5625 N

The force on each charge is 3.38 N, directed toward the other (attractive), and the two forces are equal and opposite by Newton's third law. In the liquid the force falls to 0.56 N, a factor of 6 smaller.

3.38 N attractive in air, 0.563 N attractive in the liquid; equal and opposite on the two charges in both cases.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 19

1. Forgetting the micro. 2 µC must be written 2 × 10⁻⁶ C before squaring r and substituting. The commonest error in the whole unit.

2. Squaring the charges instead of the distance. r² in the denominator, not q². Write the formula out before substituting.

3. Ignoring direction in multi-charge problems. Coulomb's law gives magnitudes; you must draw the arrows and add vectors. Two forces in the same direction add, opposite ones subtract.

4. Using the absolute index instead of K. In a medium, divide the vacuum force by the dielectric constant; do not multiply by it.

5. Saying charge is created by friction. Charge is only transferred; the total is conserved. Say "electrons are transferred", never "charge is produced".

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Convert all charges to coulombs and all distances to metres on the first line, write the formula, substitute, then state the direction in words ("repulsive, along the line away from the other charge").
