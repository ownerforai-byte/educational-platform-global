Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  3 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

This is the Unit 1 solution file: every exercise type of the unit (dimensions, dimensional analysis, unit conversion, significant figures, error combination, vernier caliper and screw gauge) worked out line by line, followed by the short conceptual questions NEB actually asks, each with a model answer you can reproduce in the exam.

How the unit is examined. Physical Quantities is a small unit with a fixed question menu:

  • 1–2 marks — write the dimensions of a given quantity; state the number of significant figures; define least count; distinguish precision from accuracy.
  • 4 marks — check or derive a formula by dimensional analysis; convert a physical quantity from one system of units to another; combine percentage errors.
  • 5–6 marks — derive the relation for the time period of a simple pendulum (or similar) by dimensions, and state the limitations of the method.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: a dimensional answer is only complete when the reason is written — "the dimensions on both sides are equal, therefore the equation may be correct" — and a numerical answer is only complete with the unit and the correct number of significant figures.

Exercise 1.1 — Units, dimensions of derived quantities

Q1. Write the dimensional formula of: (a) force, (b) work, (c) power, (d) pressure, (e) impulse, (f) surface tension, (g) coefficient of viscosity, (h) Planck's constant.

(a) F = ma = [M][L T⁻²] = [M L T⁻²] (b) W = F·s = [M L T⁻²][L] = [M L² T⁻²] (c) P = W/t = [M L² T⁻²]/[T] = [M L² T⁻³] (d) p = F/A = [M L T⁻²]/[L²] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] (e) I = F·t = [M L T⁻²][T] = [M L T⁻¹] (f) T = F/l = [M L T⁻²]/[L] = [M T⁻²] (g) η = F/(A dv/dx) = [M L T⁻²]/[L²·T⁻¹] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻¹] (h) h = E/ν = [M L² T⁻²]/[T⁻¹] = [M L² T⁻¹]

force [MLT⁻²]; work & energy [ML²T⁻²]; power [ML²T⁻³]; pressure [ML⁻¹T⁻²]; impulse & momentum [MLT⁻¹]; surface tension [MT⁻²]; viscosity [ML⁻¹T⁻¹]; Planck constant & angular momentum [ML²T⁻¹]

Q2. Name the physical quantities having dimensions: (a) [ML²T⁻²], (b) [MLT⁻¹], (c) [ML⁻¹T⁻²], (d) [MT⁻²], (e) [M⁰L⁰T⁻¹], (f) [ML²T⁻³A⁻¹].

(a) work, energy, torque, heat, moment of force; (b) momentum, impulse; (c) pressure, stress, Young modulus, energy density; (d) surface tension, force constant of a spring, spring constant k; (e) frequency, angular frequency, decay constant; (f) this is power per ampere = volt, so electric potential, emf, potential difference.

(a) work/energy/torque (b) momentum/impulse (c) pressure/stress/modulus (d) surface tension/spring constant (e) frequency/decay constant (f) potential/emf/voltage

Q3. What are the dimensions of the universal gravitational constant G and of the permittivity of free space ε₀?

F = G m₁m₂/r² ⇒ G = F r²/(m₁m₂) = [M L T⁻²][L²]/[M²] = [M⁻¹ L³ T⁻²] F = q₁q₂/(4πε₀ r²) ⇒ ε₀ = q₁q₂/(4π F r²) = [A T]²/([M L T⁻²][L²]) = [M⁻¹ L⁻³ T⁴ A²]

G: [M⁻¹L³T⁻²]  •  ε₀: [M⁻¹L⁻³T⁴A²]

Q4. Why are angle, strain and refractive index called dimensionless quantities?

Each is defined as the ratio of two quantities of the same kind, so the dimensions cancel:

  • plane angle θ = arc/radius = [L]/[L] = 1
  • strain = change in length/original length = [L]/[L] = 1
  • refractive index μ = c/v = [LT⁻¹]/[LT⁻¹] = 1

They are ratios of like quantities, so all base dimensions cancel — they are pure numbers with no unit (radian and steradian are only supplementary units).

Exercise 1.2 — Checking and deriving formulae by dimensions

Q5. Check the correctness of (a) v² = u² + 2as and (b) s = ut + ½at² by the principle of homogeneity.

(a) [v²] = [LT⁻¹]² = [L²T⁻²] [u²] = [L²T⁻²] [2as] = [LT⁻²][L] = [L²T⁻²] → every term has the same dimensions ⇒ dimensionally correct. (b) [s] = [L] [ut] = [LT⁻¹][T] = [L] [½at²] = [LT⁻²][T²] = [L] → all three terms are [L] ⇒ dimensionally correct.

Both equations are dimensionally correct; note that this does not prove the factor ½ or 2 — dimensional analysis fixes powers, not numerical constants.

Q6. Derive the expression for the time period T of a simple pendulum using dimensional analysis, assuming T depends on the length l, the acceleration due to gravity g and the mass m of the bob.

Let T = k lᵃ gᵇ mᶜ. Writing dimensions:

[T] = [L]ᵃ [LT⁻²]ᵇ [M]ᶜ [T¹] = [Mᶜ L^(a+b) T^(−2b)] Compare powers: M: 0 = c T: 1 = −2b ⇒ b = −1/2 L: 0 = a + b ⇒ a = +1/2

So T = k l1/2 g−1/2 m⁰ = k √(l/g). The constant k is dimensionless and cannot be found by dimensions.

T = k √(l/g); experiment fixes k = 2π, so T = 2π√(l/g). The mass drops out — a pendulum's period is independent of the bob's mass.

Q7. The frequency n of a stretched string depends on its length l, tension F and mass per unit length m. Show by dimensions that n ∝ (1/l)√(F/m).

n = k lᵃ Fᵇ mᶜ [T⁻¹] = [L]ᵃ [MLT⁻²]ᵇ [ML⁻¹]ᶜ = [M^(b+c) L^(a+b−c) T^(−2b)] M: 0 = b + c ⇒ c = −b T: −1 = −2b ⇒ b = 1/2, c = −1/2 L: 0 = a + b − c ⇒ a = c − b = −1/2 − 1/2 = −1

n = k l⁻¹ F^½ m^−½ = (k/l)√(F/m) — the well-known law of a vibrating string (k = ½ for the fundamental).

Q8. Can you derive the equation v = u + at by dimensional analysis? Give a reason.

No. The method cannot be applied when an equation is a sum of terms, and it can never fix a dimensionless constant. Here u and v have the same dimensions, at has the same dimensions, and the constant 1 is dimensionless — so the equation is consistent whatever the coefficient of at happens to be. Dimensional analysis can only confirm the equation; it cannot produce it.

Not derivable — dimensional analysis cannot determine numerical constants or handle a sum of terms; it can only test dimensional homogeneity.

Q9. Show that the equation P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant (Bernoulli) is dimensionally consistent (energy per unit volume).

[P] = [MLT⁻²]/[L²] = [ML⁻¹T⁻²] [½ρv²] = [ML⁻³][L²T⁻²] = [ML⁻¹T⁻²] [ρgh] = [ML⁻³][LT⁻²][L] = [ML⁻¹T⁻²]

Each term has the dimensions of pressure = energy per unit volume [ML⁻¹T⁻²], so the sum is meaningful and the equation is dimensionally consistent.

Exercise 1.3 — Conversion between systems of units

Q10. Show that 1 newton = 10⁵ dyne.

1 N = 1 kg × 1 m s⁻² = (10³ g) × (10² cm) s⁻² = 10⁵ g cm s⁻² = 10⁵ dyne

1 N = 10⁵ dyne

Q11. Convert 1 joule into erg and 1 watt into erg s⁻¹.

1 J = 1 kg m² s⁻² = (10³ g)(10² cm)² s⁻² = 10³ × 10⁴ g cm² s⁻² = 10⁷ erg 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹ = 10⁷ erg s⁻¹

1 J = 10⁷ erg; 1 W = 10⁷ erg s⁻¹

Q12. The density of a metal is 2.7 g cm⁻³. Express it in SI units. State the result to two significant figures.

1 g cm⁻³ = 10⁻³ kg / (10⁻² m)³ = 10⁻³/10⁻⁶ kg m⁻³ = 10³ kg m⁻³ ∴ 2.7 g cm⁻³ = 2.7 × 10³ kg m⁻³ = 2700 kg m⁻³

2.7 × 10³ kg m⁻³ (= 2700 kg m⁻³), which is the density of aluminium.

Q13. A force of 100 dyne acts on 5 g for 2 s. Express the impulse in SI units.

Impulse = F t = 100 dyne × 2 s = 200 dyne s 1 dyne = 10⁻⁵ N ⇒ 200 dyne s = 200 × 10⁻⁵ N s = 2 × 10⁻³ N s

2 × 10⁻³ N s

Q14. If the unit of length is doubled and the unit of time is halved, what happens to the numerical value of (i) a speed and (ii) an acceleration?

n₂ = n₁ (L₁/L₂)ᵃ (T₂/T₁)ᵇ ... with L₂ = 2L₁ and T₂ = T₁/2. Speed: [LT⁻¹] ⇒ n₂ = n₁ (L₁/2L₁)( (T₁/2)/T₁ )⁻¹ = n₁ (½)(½)⁻¹ = n₁ Acceleration: [LT⁻²] ⇒ n₂ = n₁ (½)(½)⁻² = n₁ × ½ × 4 = 2n₁

A speed's numerical value is unchanged; an acceleration's numerical value doubles.

Exercise 1.4 — Precision, accuracy and significant figures

Q15. Distinguish between precision and accuracy, and state which one a systematic error damages.

  • Precision = how close repeated readings are to each other; it is a statement about the instrument's resolution and the observer's consistency.
  • Accuracy = how close a reading is to the true value; it is a statement about the total error.

A badly zeroed (but fine-reading) vernier is precise but not accurate. Systematic error (zero error, calibration, personal bias) destroys accuracy; random error destroys precision.

Precision is reproducibility, accuracy is trueness; systematic error spoils accuracy, random error spoils precision.

Q16. State the number of significant figures: (a) 0.00340, (b) 2.0450, (c) 1.2 × 10³, (d) 500, (e) 6.02 × 10²³.

(a) three (the leading zeros are not significant; the trailing zero after the 4 is significant because of the 4 → 3,4,0). (b) five (2, 0, 4, 5, 0). (c) two. (d) ambiguous, one significant figure unless written 500. (trailing zeros in a whole number with no decimal point are not significant) — write it as 5.00 × 10² for three. (e) three.

(a) 3 (b) 5 (c) 2 (d) 1 (ambiguous, write 5.00×10² for 3) (e) 3

Q17. Evaluate and give the answer to the correct number of significant figures: (a) 4.5 × 2.33, (b) 12.63 + 1.2, (c) 84.7 ÷ 3.1.

(a) 4.5 × 2.33 = 10.485 → 11 (limited by 4.5, two s.f.) (b) 12.63 + 1.2 = 13.83 → 13.8 (limited by one decimal place in 1.2) (c) 84.7 ÷ 3.1 = 27.32 → 27 (limited by 3.1, two s.f.)

(a) 11 (b) 13.8 (c) 27

Q18. The length of a table is measured as 1.25 m with a metre scale. Write it in (i) mm, (ii) cm, (iii) scientific notation keeping the same significance.

1.25 m = 1.25 × 10³ mm = 1250 mm (3 s.f. preserved by writing 1.25 × 10³) = 125 cm = 1.25 × 10⁰ m

1.25 × 10³ mm; 1.25 × 10² cm; 1.25 m

Exercise 1.5 — Errors and their combination

Q19. The mass of a body is (5.00 ± 0.05) kg and its volume is (2.00 ± 0.02) m³. Find the density with its maximum percentage error.

ρ = m/V = 5.00/2.00 = 2.50 kg m⁻³ Δρ/ρ = Δm/m + ΔV/V (quotient → relative errors add) = 0.05/5.00 + 0.02/2.00 = 0.010 + 0.010 = 0.020 %error = 2.0% Δρ = 0.020 × 2.50 = 0.05 kg m⁻³

ρ = (2.50 ± 0.05) kg m⁻³, percentage error 2%

Q20. For the formula Z = A²B³/C, the percentage errors in A, B and C are 1%, 2% and 3% respectively. Find the maximum percentage error in Z.

ΔZ/Z = 2(ΔA/A) + 3(ΔB/B) + 1(ΔC/C) = 2(1%) + 3(2%) + 3% = 2 + 6 + 3 = 11%

Maximum percentage error in Z is 11%

Q21. Two resistances are measured as R₁ = (10 ± 1) Ω and R₂ = (20 ± 2) Ω. Find the limits of error when they are connected (a) in series and (b) in parallel.

(a) Series: R = R₁ + R₂ = 30 Ω, ΔR = 1 + 2 = 3 Ω ⇒ R = (30 ± 3) Ω (10% error) (b) Parallel: R = R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂) = (10×20)/30 = 6.67 Ω ΔR/R = ΔR₁/R₁ + ΔR₂/R₂ + Δ(R₁+R₂)/(R₁+R₂) = 1/10 + 2/20 + 3/30 = 0.10 + 0.10 + 0.10 = 0.30 ΔR = 0.30 × 6.67 = 2.0 Ω

Series (30 ± 3) Ω; parallel (6.7 ± 2.0) Ω — the parallel combination is far less reliable, which is the classic NEB point about combined errors.

Q22. A physical quantity is measured as P = (4.0 ± 0.2) and Q = (3.0 ± 0.1). Find (P − Q) with the error, and explain why the percentage error is large.

P − Q = 4.0 − 3.0 = 1.0 Δ(P − Q) = ΔP + ΔQ = 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.3 (errors add in a difference) %error = 0.3/1.0 × 100% = 30%

(1.0 ± 0.3), i.e. 30% — in a difference the absolute errors add while the result shrinks, so the relative error blows up.

Exercise 1.6 — Vernier caliper and screw gauge

Q23. A vernier caliper has 10 vernier divisions coinciding with 9 main-scale divisions of 1 mm. Find its least count. A reading shows main scale 2.3 cm and the 4th vernier division coinciding. What is the observed length?

LC = 1 MSD − 1 VSD = 1 mm − 0.9 mm = 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm Observed length = MSR + (VSD coinciding) × LC = 2.3 cm + 4 × 0.01 cm = 2.34 cm

LC = 0.01 cm; length = 2.34 cm (a positive zero error would have to be subtracted).

Q24. A screw gauge has a pitch of 0.5 mm and 50 circular-scale divisions. Find its least count. A wire reads 2 mm on the main scale and 22 divisions on the circular scale, with a zero error of −0.02 mm. Find the corrected diameter.

LC = pitch/number of circular divisions = 0.5 mm/50 = 0.01 mm Observed = 2 mm + 22 × 0.01 mm = 2.22 mm Zero error = −0.02 mm ⇒ correction = +0.02 mm True diameter = 2.22 + 0.02 = 2.24 mm

LC = 0.01 mm; corrected diameter = 2.24 mm

Q25. Why is a vernier caliper more precise than a metre scale, and why is a screw gauge more precise than a vernier?

Metre scale LC = 1 mm = 0.1 cm Vernier caliper LC = 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm (10× finer) Screw gauge LC = 0.01 mm = 0.001 cm (10× finer than the vernier)

The vernier improves on the scale by comparing a 9-division length with a 10-division length; the screw gauge improves further by converting one full rotation (one pitch) into 50 or 100 circular-scale divisions, so a fraction of a turn reads directly.

Smaller least count ⇒ finer precision: 1 mm (scale) > 0.1 mm (vernier) > 0.01 mm (screw gauge).

Exercise 1.7 — Order of magnitude and estimation

Q26. Estimate the order of magnitude of the number of water molecules in 1 g of water, and of the diameter of a water molecule.

1 mol of water = 18 g contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules 1 g contains 6.02 × 10²³/18 ≈ 3.3 × 10²² molecules ⇒ order 10²² Density 1000 kg m⁻³ and molar mass 0.018 kg mol⁻¹: volume per molecule = (0.018/6.02×10²³)/1000 ≈ 3 × 10⁻²⁹ m³ diameter ≈ (3 × 10⁻²⁹)^(1/3) ≈ 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m ⇒ order 10⁻¹⁰ m

About 3 × 10²² molecules (order 10²²); molecular size ≈ 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (order 10⁻¹⁰ m, i.e. an ångström).

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

The exam does not ask eight variations of the same sum. The eight shapes below recur in every unit, so each unit's set ends with one worked example of each: multi-step numerical, derivation, graph reading, combined-concept, assertion–reason, experiment/observation table, estimation with a limiting case, and error-hunting. Learn the shape, not the numbers.

Q27. [Multi-step numerical] A cube of side 2.00 cm has a mass of 63.2 g. The side is measured with an uncertainty of 0.01 cm and the mass with 0.1 g. Find the density in SI units and its percentage uncertainty.

V = (2.00 × 10⁻²)³ = 8.00 × 10⁻⁶ m³ m = 63.2 × 10⁻³ = 6.32 × 10⁻² kg ρ = m/V = 6.32 × 10⁻²/8.00 × 10⁻⁶ = 7.90 × 10³ kg m⁻³ ρ = m a⁻³ ⇒ Δρ/ρ = Δm/m + 3(Δa/a) = 0.1/63.2 + 3(0.01/2.00) = 0.0016 + 0.0150 = 0.0166 Δρ = 0.0166 × 7.90 × 10³ = 1.3 × 10² kg m⁻³

Three separate length measurements are involved, so the length term is weighted 3 — the cube looks the more precise measurement but it dominates the uncertainty. A dimension used with an exponent n carries n times its relative error.

ρ = (7.9 ± 0.1) × 10³ kg m⁻³, i.e. 7.9 g cm⁻³ with a 1.7% uncertainty, dominated by the length term.

Q28. [Derivation] Show by dimensional analysis that the periodic time of a simple pendulum is T = k√(l/g), and find the value of k that makes the formula exact in the small-angle limit.

Assume T ∝ l^a g^b m^c (m = bob mass) [T] = [L]^a [L T⁻²]^b [M]^c Equating powers: M: 0 = c ⇒ c = 0 (mass does not matter) L: 0 = a + b T: 1 = −2b ⇒ b = −1/2, so a = +1/2 T = k √(l/g)

Dimensional analysis fixes the exponents but not k. For small oscillations the equation of motion is a = −(g/l)x, whose solution gives ω = √(g/l), hence T = 2π√(l/g), so k = 2π. The analysis also tells you two examinable facts: T is independent of the bob's mass and of the amplitude (for small swings).

T = k√(l/g) from [L]^a[LT⁻²]^b; the equation of motion gives k = 2π, so T = 2π√(l/g) — independent of mass and, to first order, of amplitude.

Q29. [Graph reading] In a pendulum experiment a graph of T² (vertical) against l (horizontal) is a straight line of slope 4.0 s² m⁻¹ and intercept 0.02 s². Find g and state what the intercept tells you.

T² = (4π²/g) l + c, so slope = 4π²/g g = 4π²/slope = 4(9.87)/4.0 = 39.48/4.0 = 9.87 m s⁻² ≈ 9.9 m s⁻² The intercept has units s² and acts like a small extra length: l_eff = l + Δl, where Δl = c g/4π² = 0.02 × 9.87/39.48 = 5.0 × 10⁻³ m = 5 mm

The 5 mm offset is the distance from the suspension point to the true centre of the bob minus the length you measured — the classic systematic error in this experiment. Plotting T² against l rather than T against l is what makes the intercept readable at all: a curve cannot be extrapolated to give a straight-line intercept.

g ≈ 9.9 m s⁻² from slope = 4π²/g; the small positive intercept corresponds to about 5 mm of uncompensated length (centre-of-mass offset).

Q30. [Combined concept] Show that pressure, stress and energy per unit volume all have the same dimensions, and explain why this is not an accident.

Pressure p = F/A = [M L T⁻²]/[L²] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] Stress = F/A = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] Energy density = E/V = [M L² T⁻²]/[L³] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²]

All three measure energy per unit volume: pressure is the work needed to create a unit of volume against the surrounding fluid, stress is the elastic energy stored per unit volume of a strained solid, and energy density is the energy per unit volume of a field or flow. That is why the bulk modulus (a pressure divided by a dimensionless strain) is quoted in pascals, and why atmospheric pressure can be written as 10⁵ J m⁻³.

p, stress and E/V are all [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] = J m⁻³, because each is an energy per unit volume; this is why moduli, pressures and energy densities are all measured in pascals.

Q31. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): A dimensionless quantity may still have a unit. Reason (R): The plane angle is a ratio of two lengths but is expressed in radians.

Both statements are true, and R is the reason for A.

Angle θ = arc/radius = [L]/[L] = dimensionless — yet the unit radian is written explicitly (and 1 rad = 57.3°) because it records how the ratio was formed. Other examples: strain (no unit), refractive index (no unit), relative density (no unit), sound level in decibels, and amount of substance in moles — all dimensionless or near-dimensionless quantities that are quoted with a unit or a named scale.

The examinable distinction is between dimensional formula (all exponents zero) and having a unit (a convention on top of the number). Radian and steradian are dimensionless SI derived units; the decibel is a logarithmic ratio and not an SI unit at all.

A is true, R is true, and R explains A: radians, decibels and moles are units attached to quantities whose dimensional formula is M⁰L⁰T⁰.

Q32. [Experiment/observation table] A screw gauge has a pitch of 0.5 mm and 50 circular-scale divisions; its zero error is +0.02 mm. Three readings of a wire give main-scale 2.5 mm with circular-scale 46, 45 and 47. Find the corrected mean diameter.

Least count = pitch/number of divisions = 0.5/50 = 0.01 mm Reading = MS + (CS × LC) corrected = reading − zero error 2.5 + 0.46 = 2.96 mm → 2.94 mm 2.5 + 0.45 = 2.95 mm → 2.93 mm 2.5 + 0.47 = 2.97 mm → 2.95 mm Mean = (2.94 + 2.93 + 2.95)/3 = 2.94 mm

A positive zero error means the instrument already reads something when the jaws are closed, so it is subtracted. Reported properly: d = (2.94 ± 0.01) mm, the uncertainty being the least count. Always quote the least count, the zero error and the number of readings — the examiner awards those three marks before any arithmetic.

LC = 0.01 mm; corrected readings 2.94, 2.93, 2.95 mm; mean diameter = 2.94 mm (zero error subtracted, not added).

Q33. [Estimation and limiting case] Show that the speed of sound in a gas can be written v = √(P/ρ), evaluate it for air at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and 1.3 kg m⁻³, and state the limiting case in which the formula would fail.

[P/ρ] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²]/[M L⁻³] = [L² T⁻²] ⇒ √ gives [L T⁻¹] ✓ v = √(1.0 × 10⁵/1.3) = √(7.7 × 10⁴) = 2.8 × 10² m s⁻¹

The measured value in air is 340 m s⁻¹, not 280 m s⁻¹: the discrepancy is the adiabatic factor √γ. Sound compresses the gas too quickly for heat to flow, so the correct result is v = √(γP/ρ); with γ = 1.4, v = √1.4 × 280 ≈ 331 m s⁻¹, close to observation.

Limiting case: the formula assumes a continuum — a gas whose mean free path is tiny compared with the wavelength. If the pressure falls until the mean free path becomes comparable to the wavelength (very high vacuum, or extremely high frequency), sound stops propagating altogether; that is why no sound crosses a vacuum.

Dimensionally v = √(P/ρ) gives 2.8 × 10² m s⁻¹; the exact relation is √(γP/ρ) ≈ 331 m s⁻¹, and the continuum assumption fails when the mean free path approaches the wavelength.

Q34. [Error-hunting] A student writes four statements. Identify each as right or wrong and correct the wrong ones.
(i) “0.0050 m has three significant figures.”
(ii) “A dimensionally correct equation is necessarily correct.”
(iii) “The dimension of the mole is [N].”
(iv) “Systematic error can be reduced by taking many readings.”

(i) WRONG — 0.0050 m has TWO s.f.: leading zeros only fix the position of the decimal point and never count. Written in standard form the same length is 5.0 × 10⁻³ m, which shows the count at a glance. (ii) WRONG — dimensionally correct is necessary, not sufficient. v = u + at² is dimensionally wrong, but v = √(2as) and v = √(as) are both dimensionally right; only the first is physically correct. Dimensionless constants are invisible to dimensional analysis. (iii) WRONG — the mole is a unit of amount of substance, dimensionally independent (its own symbol N); it is not a dimensionless number. (iv) WRONG — many readings average out RANDOM error; a systematic error (zero error, wrong zero of a scale, parallax consistently to one side) shifts every reading the same way and must be corrected by calibration or a different method.

All four are wrong: s.f. rules depend on trailing zeros; dimensional correctness is necessary not sufficient; the mole is its own dimension; averaging beats random error, not systematic error.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. What is the difference between a fundamental and a derived unit?

A fundamental unit is defined independently of all others (metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela); a derived unit is built from fundamental units by multiplication or division (newton = kg m s⁻², joule = kg m² s⁻²).

C2. Why is it wrong to add two quantities of different dimensions?

Because a physical quantity is a number times a unit, and only like units may be counted together — adding a length to a time would mean the sum changes value when the unit system changes, so it could not represent any physical quantity. This is the principle of homogeneity.

C3. State any two limitations of dimensional analysis.

(i) It cannot determine a dimensionless constant (e.g. the ½ in s = ut + ½at² or the 2π in T = 2π√(l/g)). (ii) It fails for equations containing trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic functions, and cannot verify an equation that is a sum of terms with the same dimensions (e.g. v = u + at).

C4. Define least count. Give the least count of a metre scale and a vernier caliper.

Least count is the smallest measurement an instrument can read with certainty. A metre scale reads 0.1 cm (1 mm) and a vernier caliper 0.01 cm (0.1 mm) = 1 MSD − 1 VSD.

C5. Distinguish between random and systematic error, with one example each.

Random error varies unpredictably from reading to reading (e.g. judging the position of a moving pointer, fluctuations in a digital display) and is reduced by repeating and averaging. Systematic error has a fixed cause and shifts every reading the same way (e.g. a zero error in a screw gauge, a miscalibrated thermometer) and is removed by correction, not averaging.

C6. What is meant by zero error? How is it corrected?

The reading shown when the jaws of a vernier (or the anvils of a screw gauge) are closed is the zero error. If the zero of the vernier is to the right of the main-scale zero the error is positive and is subtracted from every reading; if it is to the left the error is negative and its magnitude is added.

C7. Why do we keep only one uncertain (estimated) digit in a measurement?

The last digit carries the uncertainty of the instrument; any further digits are guesses and carry no information. Reporting more digits than the least count supports is false precision and misleads anyone reading the result.

C8. Can a measurement be precise but not accurate? Give an example.

Yes. A screw gauge whose zero has shifted by 0.05 mm gives identical readings every time (high precision) that are all 0.05 mm too large (low accuracy). Precision concerns scatter, accuracy concerns the offset from the true value.

C9. What is the significance of a dimensionally consistent equation?

It means the equation is not obviously wrong: every term has the same dimensions, so it does not mix unlike quantities. It is a necessary but not sufficient test of correctness — a consistent equation can still be wrong by a factor, a sign, or a missing dimensionless constant.

C10. Why is the angle a dimensionless quantity yet told in degrees?

Angle is defined as arc/radius, a ratio of two lengths, so it has no dimensions. The degree is a convenient sub-unit of the radian (the SI derived unit of angle, 1 rad = 57.3°); it is a unit of a dimensionless quantity, not a base unit.

C11. Write the dimensions of the coefficient of thermal conductivity and of specific heat capacity.

Thermal conductivity: from Q/t = kA(dT/dx), k = Q dx/(A t dT) = [M L T⁻³ K⁻¹]. Specific heat: c = Q/(m ΔT) = [L² T⁻² K⁻¹].

C12. Name two uses of dimensional analysis.

(i) To test whether an equation is correct (homogeneity check). (ii) To derive the form of a physical relation and to convert a quantity from one system of units to another. A third use is to fix the units of a constant in a new formula.

C13. What is the difference between mass and weight in terms of dimensions?

Mass is a base quantity with dimension [M] and SI unit kg; weight is a force with dimension [MLT⁻²] and SI unit N. They are different kinds of quantity and cannot be added or equated without g.

C14. Why is the redefinition of SI base units (2019) significant?

Before 2019, the kilogram was defined by a platinum–iridium artefact; after, all seven base units are fixed by exact values of constants (c, h, e, k, N_A, K_cd, ΔνCs). This makes the standards reproducible anywhere in the universe without a physical object and removes drift of the artefact.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [4] A physical quantity X depends on mass m, velocity v and time t as X = m v² t⁻¹. Find its dimensions and name it. Then state whether the relation is dimensionally possible.

[X] = [M][LT⁻¹]²[T⁻¹] = [M L² T⁻³]

These are the dimensions of power (or of energy per unit time). The expression m v²/t is kinetic-energy-like over time, so it is dimensionally an energy rate.

[ML²T⁻³] = power; the relation is dimensionally correct and equals 2×(kinetic energy/time).

L2. [5] The volume of a sphere is calculated from its measured diameter d = (2.00 ± 0.01) cm as V = πd³/6. Find V and the maximum percentage error.

V = π(2.00)³/6 = 3.1416 × 8/6 = 4.19 cm³ ΔV/V = 3(Δd/d) = 3(0.01/2.00) = 0.015 = 1.5% ΔV = 0.015 × 4.19 = 0.06 cm³

V = (4.19 ± 0.06) cm³; error 1.5%. Note the cube triples the relative error — measuring a diameter is three times more punishing than measuring a length.

L3. [6] Derive the time period of a simple pendulum by dimensional analysis, then explain why the method cannot give the factor 2π, and what that failure tells you about the limits of the method.

Assume T = k lᵃ gᵇ mᶜ. Comparing powers of M, L and T as in Q6 gives a = ½, b = −½, c = 0, so T = k√(l/g).

The constant k is dimensionless, and dimensions only fix dimensioned powers; a dimensionless number is invisible to the method. Only the exact solution of the equation of motion (SHM, or experiment) gives k = 2π.

T = k√(l/g) with k dimensionless; 2π comes from experiment/theory, not dimensions. Dimensional analysis gives the form, never the constants — state that in the exam.

L4. [5] A student measures the length of a rod with a metre scale, a vernier caliper and a screw gauge and gets 5.4 cm, 5.42 cm and 5.421 cm. Explain the difference and state which is the most reliable and why.

Metre scale: LC 0.1 cm → 5.4 cm (2 s.f.) Vernier: LC 0.01 cm → 5.42 cm (3 s.f.) Screw gauge: LC 0.001 cm → 5.421 cm (4 s.f.)

All three are precise to their own least count; the screw gauge is most reliable because its least count is smallest, so its uncertainty (±0.001 cm) is smallest. Reliability is set by the least count, not by the number of digits written.

5.421 cm from the screw gauge is most reliable; precision improves as the least count falls.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 1

1. Writing "dimensionally correct ⇒ correct". It is only a consistency test. Always write "may be correct; the method cannot decide the constant".

2. Forgetting the unit after a number. "2.7" scores nothing; "2.7 × 10³ kg m⁻³" scores the mark.

3. Adding errors in a quotient instead of adding relative errors. For ρ = m/V, the two percentage errors add; for R = R₁ + R₂ the two absolute errors add. Mixing them up inverts the answer.

4. Ignoring the sign of zero error. A negative zero error must be added to the observed reading. Write the correction line explicitly.

5. Too many significant figures. A calculator answer of 10.485 from 4.5 × 2.33 must be reported as 11, not 10.485. The number of s.f. is set by the least precise input.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Dimensions first as a formula, then a bracket substitution, then a comparison of powers, then the answer line with the unit. In a 4-mark question this is exactly four visible marks: statement, substitution, comparison, answer.
