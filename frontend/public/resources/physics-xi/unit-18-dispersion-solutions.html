Unit 18 — Dispersion

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  3 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Dispersion is short and definition-heavy: angular dispersion, dispersive power, the pure spectrum, the rainbow and the achromatic combination. One numerical type (dispersive power of a thin prism) plus the rainbow diagram covers most of the paper.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define dispersion; define angular dispersion and dispersive power; why is violet deviated more than red; what is an achromatic combination.
  • 4 marks — calculate dispersive power or angular dispersion; explain the formation of a rainbow.
  • 5–6 marks — explain the formation of a pure spectrum with a ray diagram; explain primary and secondary rainbows; derive the condition for an achromatic prism combination.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: dispersion is caused by the variation of refractive index with wavelength, not by the prism bending light. The prism only separates colours that already travel at different speeds in glass; deviation and dispersion are two different quantities and must be defined separately.

Exercise 18.1 — Dispersion and the spectrum

Q1. Define dispersion of light and explain its cause.

Dispersion is the splitting of white light into its component colours when it passes through a refracting medium such as a prism. It occurs because the refractive index of a material depends on wavelength: violet light is slowed more than red, so n_violet > n_red. Each colour therefore leaves the prism at a slightly different angle, and the colours fan out into a spectrum.

In vacuum all colours travel at the same speed and there is no dispersion. The effect appears only in a material medium.

Dispersion is the wavelength-dependent splitting of light, caused by n increasing from red to violet, so each colour is deviated by a different amount.

Q2. Arrange the colours of the visible spectrum in order of increasing wavelength and of increasing deviation.

Wavelength (increasing): violet → indigo → blue → green → yellow → orange → red 380 nm …………………………………………………… 700 nm Deviation (increasing): red → orange → yellow → green → blue → indigo → violet Refractive index (increasing): same order as deviation

Mnemonic: VIBGYOR for decreasing wavelength / increasing deviation. Red is deviated least because it has the longest wavelength and the smallest refractive index.

Wavelength: V < I < B < G < Y < O < R. Deviation and n: R < O < Y < G < B < I < V.

Q3. Why does a parallel-sided glass slab not produce a visible spectrum, though its material disperses light?

Because the two faces of the slab are parallel. Each colour is deviated by a different amount at the first face, but the second face deviates it back by exactly the same amount, so the emergent rays of all colours are parallel and overlap again. The colours are separated only inside the slab, where the thickness is small, so no visible fan emerges. In a prism the second face is inclined to the first, so the second refraction adds to the separation instead of cancelling it.

A slab's parallel faces cancel the dispersion (emergent rays parallel, no spread); a prism's inclined faces add to it.

Q4. What is meant by a pure and an impure spectrum?

A pure spectrum is one in which the colours occupy distinct, well-separated bands with no overlap, formed by a narrow source, a collimator producing parallel light, and a prism set at minimum deviation with the emergent light focused by a lens (as in a spectrometer). An impure spectrum has overlapping colours and blurred edges, as produced by a broad source or a prism not at minimum deviation.

An impure spectrum also arises if the slit is wide, because each colour then produces a band of finite width which overlaps the neighbouring colour.

Pure: colours sharply separated with no overlap (parallel beam, narrow slit, minimum deviation). Impure: overlapping, blurred bands (wide slit or non-parallel light).

Exercise 18.2 — Angular dispersion and dispersive power

Q5. Define angular dispersion and dispersive power, and write their formulas.

Angular dispersion (θ): the angle between the emergent violet and red rays of a prism — the angular spread of the spectrum.

For a thin prism: θ = δ_v − δ_r = (n_v − n_r)A

Dispersive power (ω): the ratio of the angular dispersion to the deviation of the mean colour — a measure of how strongly the material disperses, independent of the prism angle.

ω = θ/δ_y = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1)

Here n_y refers to the middle (yellow) colour. Water ≈ 0.030, crown glass ≈ 0.035, flint glass ≈ 0.060, diamond ≈ 0.044.

θ = (n_v − n_r)A = δ_v − δ_r; ω = θ/δ_y = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1), dimensionless.

Q6. A glass has n_red = 1.510, n_violet = 1.530 and n_yellow = 1.520. Find its dispersive power. If a thin prism of this glass has an angle of 5°, find the angular dispersion and the deviation of the yellow ray.

ω = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1) = (1.530 − 1.510)/(1.520 − 1.000) = 0.020/0.520 = 0.0385 Angular dispersion: θ = (n_v − n_r)A = 0.020 × 5° = 0.10° Deviation of yellow: δ_y = (n_y − 1)A = 0.520 × 5° = 2.60° Check: ω = θ/δ_y = 0.10/2.60 = 0.0385 ✓

ω ≈ 0.038; θ = 0.10°; δ_y = 2.6° — a visible but small fan from a 5° prism.

Q7. Why is dispersive power quoted without the prism angle, while angular dispersion is not?

Because the prism angle appears in θ = (n_v − n_r)A but cancels in ω = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1). Angular dispersion therefore depends on how thick the prism is (how much material the light passes through), while dispersive power is a property of the material only. Two prisms of the same glass but different angles give different angular dispersions but the same ω.

The A dependence cancels in ω, so ω is a material property; θ also depends on the prism angle.

Q8. Distinguish between deviation and dispersion.

Deviation is the overall bend, produced by n; dispersion is the spread of colours, produced by Δn between red and violet.

Exercise 18.3 — Numericals on dispersive power

Q9. A thin prism of angle 6° is made of glass with n_r = 1.514, n_y = 1.524 and n_v = 1.538. Find the deviation of each colour and the angular dispersion.

δ_r = (1.514 − 1) × 6 = 0.514 × 6 = 3.08° δ_y = 0.524 × 6 = 3.14° δ_v = 0.538 × 6 = 3.23° Angular dispersion: θ = δ_v − δ_r = 3.23 − 3.08 = 0.15° (check: (n_v − n_r)A = 0.024 × 6 = 0.144° ✓) Dispersive power: ω = 0.024/0.524 = 0.0458

δ_r = 3.08°, δ_y = 3.14°, δ_v = 3.23°; θ ≈ 0.14–0.15°; ω ≈ 0.046

Q10. Crown glass has ω = 0.016 and flint glass ω = 0.040. Which is more dispersive and by what factor? What does that mean in practice?

Ratio = 0.040/0.016 = 2.5

Flint glass disperses 2.5 times as strongly for the same deviation. In practice, flint glass is used for the dispersing element and crown glass for the converging (achromatic) element in a spectrometer or an achromatic doublet: the crown lens provides most of the bending while the flint lens cancels the colour spread.

Flint glass, by a factor of 2.5; that is exactly why pairs of crown + flint lenses/prisms can be made achromatic.

Q11. A prism produces a deviation of 40° for yellow light and an angular dispersion of 2°. Find the dispersive power.

ω = θ/δ_y = 2/40 = 0.050

ω = 0.050 — a typical flint glass value.

Q12. For a thin prism of angle 4° and n_y = 1.5, ω = 0.04. Find the angle between the red and violet emergent rays.

δ_y = (n_y − 1)A = 0.5 × 4 = 2° θ = ω δ_y = 0.04 × 2 = 0.08°

θ = 0.08° ≈ 4.8 minutes of arc — a spectrum too narrow to see with the naked eye, which is why demonstration prisms are 60° rather than 4°.

Exercise 18.4 — The pure spectrum

Q13. Describe how a pure spectrum is obtained in the laboratory, and state the purpose of each part.

Source (sodium lamp / white light) → narrow slit → collimator lens (parallel beam) → prism at minimum deviation → telescope / screen (focuses the emergent rays)

  • Narrow slit: makes each colour a narrow line rather than a broad band, preventing overlap.
  • Collimator: produces a parallel beam, so every ray has the same incidence on the prism and the deviation is well defined.
  • Prism at minimum deviation: the image is sharpest and the spectrum is spread symmetrically with least aberration.
  • Telescope or screen: brings the parallel emergent rays to a focus so that the spectrum can be observed or photographed.

Narrow slit + collimated beam + prism at minimum deviation + focusing telescope = pure spectrum with sharply separated, non-overlapping colours.

Q14. What are Fraunhofer lines, and what do they tell us?

They are dark absorption lines crossing the solar spectrum, first mapped by Joseph von Fraunhofer. They arise because the cooler outer layers of the Sun (and the Earth's atmosphere) absorb specific wavelengths that are emitted by the hotter layers below. Each line corresponds to a known atomic transition, so the lines identify the chemical elements present in the Sun — helium, for instance, was discovered in the solar spectrum before it was found on Earth.

Dark absorption lines at specific wavelengths in the solar spectrum; they fingerprint the elements in the Sun's outer layers and the Earth's atmosphere.

Q15. How did Newton show that the colours of the spectrum are not produced by the prism itself?

Newton passed the spectrum from one prism through a second, inverted prism. The colours recombined into white light, showing that the prism had merely separated colours already present in white light and had not created them. He also showed that a single colour (say red) could not be further split by a second prism — it was already "simple".

By recombining the spectrum with a second inverted prism to recover white light; a single colour cannot be split further, so the prism only separates, never creates, colour.

Exercise 18.5 — The rainbow

Q16. Explain the formation of a primary rainbow.

A primary rainbow is formed by sunlight entering spherical raindrops. Each drop acts as a tiny prism:

  1. The ray refracts as it enters the drop, splitting into colours (dispersion).
  2. The colours travel to the back of the drop and are totally internally reflected once.
  3. They refract again as they leave the drop, bending further and spreading out.

The light emerges at about 42° from the direction opposite the Sun (the antisolar point). Because violet is deviated most and red least, red appears on the outer edge and violet on the inner edge. The observer must have the Sun behind them and the drops in front.

Refraction → one internal reflection → refraction, with an emergence angle of about 42°; red outside, violet inside, Sun behind the observer.

Q17. How does a secondary rainbow differ from a primary one?

PrimarySecondary
One internal reflection inside the dropTwo internal reflections
Emerges at about 42° from the antisolar pointEmerges at about 51°
Red on the outside, violet insideColour order reversed: violet outside, red inside
BrighterFainter (extra reflection and refraction lose light)

The dark band between the two, around 42°–51°, is Alexander's dark band, where light from the drops is not directed toward the observer.

Secondary: two internal reflections, larger radius (51°), reversed colours, fainter; a dark band separates it from the primary.

Q18. Why is a rainbow seen as an arc, and why is it seen only when the Sun is behind the observer?

Every drop that lies at about 42° from the antisolar direction sends the same colour toward the eye. The set of all such directions forms a cone around the antisolar axis, which appears as a circular arc on the sky. The ground cuts the circle, so usually only an arc is visible; from an aeroplane a full circle can be seen.

The Sun must be behind the observer because the light has to travel from the Sun, into the drops in front, and then back toward the observer after reflection. The antisolar point is then in front of the observer, high enough for the arc to be above the horizon.

The 42° cone of dispersed rays projects as a circular arc around the antisolar point; the Sun must be behind the observer for the reflected light to return to the eye.

Exercise 18.6 — Achromatic combination of prisms

Q19. What is an achromatic combination? Derive the condition on two thin prisms of different glass that produce deviation without dispersion.

An achromatic combination deviates light without splitting it into colours. Take two thin prisms of angles A₁ and A₂, of different glasses, placed with their deviations in opposition:

Net deviation: δ = (n₁ − 1)A₁ − (n₂ − 1)A₂ Net angular dispersion: θ = (n_v1 − n_r1)A₁ − (n_v2 − n_r2)A₂ Condition for no dispersion: θ = 0 ⇒ (n_v1 − n_r1)A₁ = (n_v2 − n_r2)A₂ Dividing by the deviation condition (using ω = θ/δ for each prism): ω₁ δ₁ = ω₂ δ₂ ⇒ ω₁/ω₂ = A₂(n₂ − 1)/(A₁(n₁ − 1)) For the combination to still deviate, δ ≠ 0, so the two prisms must be of different materials (ω₁ ≠ ω₂).

ω₁δ₁ = ω₂δ₂ with δ₁ ≠ δ₂ — deviation without dispersion needs two materials of different dispersive power, so that the dispersions cancel while the deviations do not.

Q20. Two thin prisms, one of crown glass (ω = 0.016) and one of flint glass (ω = 0.040), are to form an achromatic combination deviating by 2°. Find the deviation each must produce.

Condition: ω₁δ₁ = ω₂δ₂ ⇒ 0.016 δ₁ = 0.040 δ₂ ⇒ δ₁ = 2.5 δ₂ Net deviation: δ₁ − δ₂ = 2° ⇒ 2.5δ₂ − δ₂ = 1.5δ₂ = 2° ⇒ δ₂ = 1.33° ⇒ δ₁ = 3.33°

The crown prism must deviate by 3.33° and the flint prism by 1.33° in the opposite sense, giving a net 2° with the colours recombined.

Q21. What is an achromatic doublet, and how does it achieve the same result with lenses?

An achromatic doublet is a cemented pair of lenses — usually a converging crown-glass lens and a diverging flint-glass lens — designed so that the chromatic aberrations cancel while the combination still converges. Because flint glass has a larger dispersive power, a weak diverging flint element can cancel the colour spread of a stronger converging crown element with only a modest reduction in overall power.

Condition: P₁/ω₁ + P₂/ω₂ = 0 (powers in dioptres, ω dispersive powers) with P₁ + P₂ > 0 so the doublet still converges.

A cemented crown + flint pair with P₁/ω₁ + P₂/ω₂ = 0; the same ω-cancellation principle as the prism, applied to focal powers.

Exercise 18.7 — Scattering of light

Q22. What is scattering of light? State Rayleigh's law and use it to explain the blue sky and the red sunset.

Scattering is the redirection of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength — molecules of air, dust, water droplets. Rayleigh showed that the intensity scattered by particles much smaller than λ varies as

I ∝ 1/λ⁴

Blue light (λ ≈ 450 nm) is scattered about (700/450)⁴ ≈ 5.9 times as strongly as red (λ ≈ 700 nm). So the sky, seen by scattered light, looks blue. At sunset the sunlight travels a long slant path through the atmosphere; most of the blue has been scattered out of the direct beam, so the surviving direct light is red and orange.

I ∝ 1/λ⁴: short wavelengths scatter far more, so the sky is blue (scattered light) and the setting Sun is red (transmitted light depleted of blue).

Q23. Why do clouds appear white while the sky is blue?

Cloud droplets are much larger than the wavelength of visible light (typically 10 µm), so they scatter by Mie scattering rather than Rayleigh scattering. Mie scattering is essentially independent of wavelength across the visible range and is strong in all directions, so all colours are scattered equally and the cloud looks white. The air molecules, being far smaller than λ, scatter selectively and make the clear sky blue.

Cloud droplets ≫ λ ⇒ non-selective Mie scattering ⇒ white; air molecules ≪ λ ⇒ selective Rayleigh scattering ⇒ blue sky.

Exercise 18.8 — Chromatic and spherical aberration

Q24. What is chromatic aberration? State its cause and two ways of reducing it.

Chromatic aberration is the failure of a lens to bring all colours to the same focus: since the focal length depends on n, and n is larger for violet than for red, a lens has a shorter focal length for violet. A parallel white beam is therefore focused at different points along the axis, giving a coloured fringe instead of a sharp image.

For a thin lens: 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), so f_v < f_r Longitudinal chromatic aberration = f_r − f_v

Reduction: (i) use an achromatic doublet — a converging crown lens cemented to a diverging flint lens chosen so that their dispersions cancel; (ii) use a monochromatic source or a narrow-band filter, as in spectroscopy with a sodium lamp.

Different colours focus at different points because f depends on n; correct it with an achromatic doublet or by using monochromatic light.

Q25. What is spherical aberration, and how does it differ from chromatic aberration?

Spherical aberration is the failure of a spherical surface to focus all rays of a single colour to one point: rays passing through the outer zone (marginal rays) are refracted more strongly than paraxial rays and cross the axis closer to the lens. The image is blurred in a way that does not depend on colour.

Reduction: use a small aperture (a stop) so that only paraxial rays are used, orient a plano-convex lens with its curved face toward the parallel beam, or combine two lenses of opposite aberration (a plano-convex + plano-concave pair), or use an aspheric surface.

Chromatic aberrationSpherical aberration
Depends on colour; different f for each wavelengthIndependent of colour; different focus for different zones of the lens
Cured by an achromatic doubletCured by a small aperture or aspheric/combined surfaces
Produces coloured fringesProduces overall blur and a soft focus

Spherical aberration is a zone-dependent focus error for a single colour; chromatic aberration is a wavelength-dependent focus error. The cures and the appearance of the images differ.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Define dispersion of light.

The splitting of white light into its component colours on passing through a refracting medium, caused by the refractive index depending on wavelength, so that different colours are deviated by different amounts.

C2. Define angular dispersion.

The angle between the emergent violet and red rays from a prism: θ = δ_v − δ_r = (n_v − n_r)A for a thin prism. It measures the angular width of the spectrum.

C3. Define dispersive power.

The ratio of the angular dispersion to the deviation of the mean colour: ω = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1) = θ/δ_y. It is a dimensionless property of the material, independent of the prism angle.

C4. Why is violet deviated more than red?

Because the refractive index of glass is larger for shorter wavelengths: n_violet > n_red. By Snell's law a larger n gives a smaller angle of refraction for the same incidence, and hence a larger deviation from the original direction.

C5. Why does a prism produce a spectrum while a glass slab does not?

The prism's two faces are inclined, so the dispersion at the second face adds to that at the first. In a slab the faces are parallel and the second refraction exactly cancels the first, leaving all colours parallel and overlapping — no visible spectrum.

C6. What is an achromatic combination?

A combination of two prisms (or lenses) of different dispersive powers arranged so that the angular dispersions cancel while a net deviation (or net converging power) remains. It deviates light without splitting it into colours.

C7. State the condition for a combination of two thin prisms to be achromatic.

ω₁δ₁ = ω₂δ₂, with δ₁ ≠ δ₂ (so a net deviation remains). This requires two different materials, since equal ω would cancel the deviation as well as the dispersion.

C8. Why is red on the outside of a primary rainbow?

Because red is deviated least by the water drop, so it emerges at the largest angle from the antisolar direction (about 42°), which corresponds to the outer edge of the arc. Violet, deviated most, emerges at the smallest angle and forms the inner edge.

C9. How many internal reflections occur in a primary and in a secondary rainbow?

One internal reflection in a primary rainbow (with two refractions), and two internal reflections in a secondary rainbow (with two refractions). The extra reflection reverses the colour order and makes the secondary fainter.

C10. Why is the sky blue?

Because air molecules scatter short wavelengths much more strongly than long ones, following Rayleigh's law I ∝ 1/λ⁴. Blue light from the Sun is scattered in all directions and reaches the eye from every part of the sky, so the sky appears blue.

C11. Why is the setting Sun red?

At sunset sunlight travels a long slant path through the atmosphere, so much of the blue is scattered out of the direct beam. The light that reaches the eye directly has lost most of its short wavelengths and is dominated by red and orange, so the Sun looks red.

C12. Why are clouds white?

Because cloud droplets are much larger than the wavelength of light, so they scatter all wavelengths almost equally (Mie scattering). Since no colour is preferentially removed, the scattered light is white.

C13. What is a pure spectrum and how is it produced?

A spectrum in which the colours are distinct and non-overlapping. It is produced by a narrow slit, a collimator to make the light parallel, a prism set at minimum deviation and a lens or telescope to focus the emergent rays.

C14. What are Fraunhofer lines?

Dark absorption lines in the solar spectrum, produced when cooler outer gases absorb specific wavelengths emitted by the hotter layers below. Their positions identify the elements present in the Sun and in the Earth's atmosphere.

C15. Why is dispersion not observed in vacuum?

Because in vacuum all wavelengths travel at exactly the same speed c, so the refractive index is 1 for every colour and there is no wavelength-dependent deviation. Dispersion requires a material medium in which speed varies with wavelength.

C16. Distinguish between deviation and dispersion.

Deviation is the overall bending of the beam from its original direction, governed by the mean refractive index (δ = (n − 1)A for a thin prism). Dispersion is the separation of colours within the beam, governed by the difference of refractive indices (θ = (n_v − n_r)A). A beam can be deviated without being dispersed, as in an achromatic combination.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Explain the formation of a primary rainbow with a ray diagram in words, and account for the colour order and the 42° angle.

A ray of white sunlight enters a spherical drop and refracts at the surface, being dispersed because n depends on λ. It travels to the back of the drop, where it is totally internally reflected (the angle of incidence inside water exceeds the critical angle of 48.6° for the relevant geometry), and then refracts out at the far side, being dispersed again in the same sense.

Total deviation of a ray after one internal reflection: D = (i − r) + (180° − 2r) + (i − r) = 180° + 2i − 4r The emergent ray makes an angle 180° − D = 4r − 2i with the incident direction. This is stationary (extremum) near i ≈ 59.5°, r ≈ 40.2°, giving about 42° for red and about 40° for violet.

Because the deviation is stationary at the extremum, a large fraction of the light of each colour emerges near that angle, which is why the bow is bright there. Red, deviated least, emerges at the larger angle and forms the outer edge; violet forms the inner edge. The observer sees a circular arc of radius 42° around the antisolar point.

Refraction, one internal reflection, refraction; the stationary deviation at i ≈ 59.5° concentrates light near 42°, with red outermost because it has the smallest n.

L2. [5] A thin prism of angle 5° is made of glass with n_r = 1.500, n_y = 1.510, n_v = 1.525. Find the deviation of red and violet, the angular dispersion and the dispersive power.

δ_r = (1.500 − 1) × 5 = 2.50° δ_v = (1.525 − 1) × 5 = 2.625° θ = δ_v − δ_r = 0.125° ω = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1) = 0.025/0.510 = 0.049

δ_r = 2.50°, δ_v = 2.63°, θ = 0.125°, ω ≈ 0.049 (flint-glass-like)

L3. [5] Explain why an achromatic combination needs two different glasses, and show that two prisms of the same glass can never be achromatic while still deviating.

For two thin prisms in opposition:

δ = δ₁ − δ₂ = (n₁ − 1)A₁ − (n₂ − 1)A₂ θ = ω₁δ₁ − ω₂δ₂ Achromatic requires θ = 0 ⇒ ω₁δ₁ = ω₂δ₂. If both prisms are of the same glass, ω₁ = ω₂, so the condition forces δ₁ = δ₂, and then the net deviation δ = δ₁ − δ₂ = 0. So the combination would not deviate at all.

Hence two different materials (different ω) are required: a strong deviating crown prism and a weaker, more dispersive flint prism, whose dispersion cancels the crown's while leaving part of the deviation.

Same glass ⇒ ω₁ = ω₂ ⇒ δ₁ = δ₂ ⇒ zero net deviation. Achromatism with deviation requires ω₁ ≠ ω₂, i.e. two different glasses.

L4. [4] Explain, using Rayleigh's law, why the sky is blue at noon but red at sunset, and why the sky above the atmosphere (as seen by an astronaut) is black.

I_scattered ∝ 1/λ⁴

At noon the Sun is overhead and the path through the atmosphere is shortest. Blue light is scattered about six times as strongly as red, so scattered blue light reaches the eye from all directions and the sky looks blue.

At sunset the path is much longer (many air masses), so nearly all the blue has been scattered out of the direct beam before it reaches the eye; the transmitted direct light is therefore red-orange.

Above the atmosphere there are no molecules to scatter light, so no light enters the eye except from the Sun and stars directly — the sky appears black.

1/λ⁴ scattering makes the short-path sky blue and the long-path Sun red; with no atmosphere there is nothing to scatter light into the eye, so space looks black.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 18

1. Confusing deviation with dispersion. δ = (n − 1)A is the bend; θ = (n_v − n_r)A is the spread. Defining one when the question asks the other loses the mark outright.

2. Reversing the colour order. Violet is deviated most because n is largest at short wavelength. Writing "red bends most" is the single most common error in this unit.

3. Thinking ω depends on the prism angle. The A cancels in ω = (n_v − n_r)/(n_y − 1); ω is a material property. Angular dispersion, by contrast, does depend on A.

4. Saying two prisms of the same glass can be achromatic and still deviate. ω₁ = ω₂ forces δ₁ = δ₂ and hence zero net deviation. Two materials are essential.

5. Getting the rainbow colour order wrong. Primary: red outside. Secondary: colours reversed (violet outside). Draw the drop and mark the two paths before writing.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Always separate the two quantities in the opening line — "deviation is the bend, dispersion is the spread" — then quote the formulas. For rainbow answers, number the three stages (refraction, internal reflection, refraction) and state the angle and the colour order explicitly.
