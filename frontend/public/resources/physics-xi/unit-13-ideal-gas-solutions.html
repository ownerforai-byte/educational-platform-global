Exercise 13.1 — The ideal gas equation

Q1. Derive the ideal gas equation pV = nRT from the combined gas laws, and state its two equivalent forms.

Boyle: pV = constant at constant T Charles: V/T = constant at constant p Gay-Lussac: p/T = constant at constant V Combining: pV/T = constant for a fixed mass of gas. Evaluating the constant for one mole at standard conditions gives R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹: pV = nRT (n = number of moles) With N molecules and N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹: nR = (N/N_A)R = Nk, k = R/N_A = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ ⇒ pV = NkT

pV = nRT = NkT; R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (universal), k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (Boltzmann constant).

Q2. Calculate the volume occupied by 2 moles of an ideal gas at 27 °C and 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa.

T = 27 + 273 = 300 K V = nRT/p = (2 × 8.31 × 300)/(1.0 × 10⁵) = 4986/10⁵ = 4.99 × 10⁻² m³

V ≈ 5.0 × 10⁻² m³ = 50 litres (22.4 L per mole at STP, and more at 27 °C)

Q3. A gas at 27 °C and 1 atm is heated at constant volume to 327 °C. Find the new pressure.

p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂ (constant V) T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 600 K p₂ = p₁T₂/T₁ = 1 × 600/300 = 2 atm

2 atm — doubling the absolute temperature doubles the pressure at constant volume

Q4. State Avogadro's law and Dalton's law of partial pressures.

Avogadro's law: equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules; at STP, one mole occupies 22.4 L and contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.

Dalton's law: the total pressure exerted by a mixture of non-reacting gases is the sum of the partial pressures each would exert if it alone occupied the whole volume: p = p₁ + p₂ + …

Equal volumes ⇒ equal molecules (Avogadro); total pressure = sum of partial pressures for a mixture of ideal gases (Dalton).

Q5. Under what conditions does a real gas deviate from ideal behaviour?

The ideal gas model assumes (i) negligible molecular volume and (ii) no intermolecular forces. These fail at high pressure (molecules are pushed close together so their own volume and mutual attraction matter) and at low temperature (slow molecules are captured by attractive forces and may condense). At low pressure and high temperature the deviations become negligible and real gases approach ideal behaviour.

High pressure and low temperature — that is, near condensation — break the two ideal-gas assumptions; the gas obeys van der Waals' equation instead.

Exercise 13.2 — Molecular properties and the kinetic model

Q6. State the assumptions of the kinetic-molecular theory of gases.

  1. A gas consists of a very large number of identical molecules, each of negligible volume compared with the volume of the container.
  2. The molecules move randomly in all directions with a spread of speeds; their average behaviour is what we measure.
  3. There are no intermolecular forces except during collisions; between collisions the molecules move in straight lines.
  4. Collisions with each other and with the walls are perfectly elastic, so kinetic energy is conserved.
  5. The duration of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  6. The molecules obey Newton's laws of motion.

Tiny molecules, random motion, no forces between collisions, elastic impacts, short collision times, Newtonian mechanics — these six assumptions underlie every derivation in the unit.

Q7. What is meant by molecular properties of matter? Name three measurable consequences of molecular motion.

Matter is made of molecules in ceaseless random motion; their kinetic energy, collisions and intermolecular forces explain the macroscopic properties of a substance.

  • Pressure: molecular impacts on a surface exert a steady force per unit area.
  • Temperature: proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules.
  • Diffusion: the random migration of molecules mixes gases and dissolves solutes.

Brownian motion (jitter of smoke particles) is the direct visual evidence for molecular motion, first analysed by Einstein in 1905.

Macroscopic behaviour follows from molecular motion; pressure, temperature and diffusion are the three directly measurable consequences, with Brownian motion as the visual proof.

Q8. Explain why a gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container, using the kinetic model.

Each molecule that strikes the wall rebounds with its momentum reversed, so the wall must supply an impulse and, by Newton's third law, the molecule exerts an equal and opposite impulse on the wall. Over an enormous number of impacts per second, the many tiny impulses combine into a steady force, and the force per unit area is the pressure. The wall's area being fixed, the pressure rises with (i) the number of molecules per unit volume and (ii) the mean square molecular speed:

p = (1/3)(N/V)m⟨c²⟩ = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩

Continuous elastic impacts deliver momentum to the wall at a steady rate; the momentum delivered per second per unit area is the pressure.

Exercise 13.3 — Derivation of the pressure of a gas

Q9. Derive p = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩ for an ideal gas from the kinetic model.

Consider a cubical box of side L containing N molecules, each of mass m. Take one molecule moving with velocity component u along the x-axis.

Momentum change per impact on the x-face = 2mu Time between impacts on that same face = 2L/u Rate of change of momentum = 2mu/(2L/u) = mu²/L Summing over all N molecules with components u₁, u₂, …, u_N: Force on the face = (m/L)(u₁² + u₂² + … + u_N²) = (mN/L)⟨u²⟩ Pressure = force/area = (mN/L³)⟨u²⟩ = (mN/V)⟨u²⟩ No direction is preferred, so ⟨u²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩ = ⟨w²⟩ and ⟨c²⟩ = ⟨u²⟩ + ⟨v²⟩ + ⟨w²⟩ = 3⟨u²⟩ ⇒ ⟨u²⟩ = ⟨c²⟩/3 Therefore p = (1/3)(mN/V)⟨c²⟩ = (1/3)(Nm/V)⟨c²⟩ = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩

p = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩ = (1/3)(N/V)m⟨c²⟩ — the fundamental result of kinetic theory.

Q10. Using p = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩ and pV = NkT, show that ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT.

p = (1/3)(Nm/V)⟨c²⟩ and p = NkT/V Equating: (1/3)Nm⟨c²⟩ = NkT ⇒ m⟨c²⟩ = 3kT ⇒ ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT

The average translational kinetic energy of one molecule depends only on the absolute temperature and not at all on the mass or identity of the molecule — all gases have the same molecular kinetic energy at the same temperature.

½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT; temperature is a direct measure of the average translational kinetic energy.

Q11. Find the average translational kinetic energy of a gas molecule at 300 K, and its total translational kinetic energy in one mole.

per molecule: ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J per mole: (3/2)RT = 1.5 × 8.31 × 300 = 3.74 × 10³ J

6.2 × 10⁻²¹ J per molecule; 3.74 kJ per mole. Note this is the translational part only — a diatomic gas stores more in rotation.

Exercise 13.4 — Average translational kinetic energy and temperature

Q12. What is absolute zero on the kinetic scale? Why is it the lowest attainable temperature?

From ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT, the absolute temperature is proportional to the mean translational kinetic energy. Absolute zero (0 K = −273.15 °C) is the temperature at which that translational kinetic energy would be minimum. Since kinetic energy cannot be negative, T cannot fall below zero, so 0 K is a genuine lower bound.

0 K is the state of minimum molecular translational energy; negative kinetic energy is impossible, so no lower temperature exists (and the third law forbids reaching it exactly).

Q13. Two gases, hydrogen and oxygen, are at the same temperature. Compare their average molecular kinetic energies and their rms speeds.

Same T ⇒ same average translational KE: ½m_H⟨c_H²⟩ = ½m_O⟨c_O²⟩ = (3/2)kT rms speed: c_rms = √(3kT/m) ⇒ c ∝ 1/√m c_H/c_O = √(m_O/m_H) = √(32/2) = √16 = 4

Equal average kinetic energies; hydrogen molecules move 4 times faster than oxygen molecules at the same temperature, because they are 16 times lighter.

Q14. At what temperature does the rms speed of a nitrogen molecule equal 500 m s⁻¹? (M = 28 g mol⁻¹)

c_rms = √(3RT/M) 500² = 3 × 8.31 × T/0.028 250 000 = 890.4 T ⇒ T = 281 K

T ≈ 281 K (≈ 8 °C) — remarkably close to room temperature, which is why nitrogen molecules at room temperature move at about the speed of sound.

Exercise 13.5 — Boltzmann constant and rms speed

Q15. Define Boltzmann's constant, give its value and its relation to R and N_A.

Boltzmann's constant is the gas constant per molecule: k = R/N_A = 8.31/(6.02 × 10²³) = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹. In the expression ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT it plays the role of R for a single molecule, and it is the constant that connects temperature with energy at the microscopic level.

k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ = R/N_A; it is the energy per molecule per kelvin.

Q16. Write the three molecular speeds (average, rms and most probable) and state their order.

c_rms = √(3RT/M) = √(3kT/m) c_avg = √(8RT/πM) c_mp = √(2RT/M) Order: c_mp < c_avg < c_rms (ratio 1 : 1.128 : 1.225)

The distribution of speeds (Maxwell–Boltzmann) has a long tail of fast molecules, so the root-mean-square speed is the largest of the three.

c_rms = √(3RT/M), c_avg = √(8RT/πM), c_mp = √(2RT/M); c_mp < c_avg < c_rms.

Q17. Find the rms speed of oxygen molecules at 300 K. (M = 32 g mol⁻¹)

c_rms = √(3RT/M) = √(3 × 8.31 × 300/0.032) = √(7479/0.032) = √233 700 = 483 m s⁻¹

≈ 4.8 × 10² m s⁻¹ (about 1700 km h⁻¹ — comparable to the speed of sound in air)

Q18. Why does the rms speed of a gas increase with temperature but not depend on pressure?

From c_rms = √(3RT/M), the speed depends only on T and M. Raising the temperature increases the average kinetic energy, so the speed rises as √T. Pressure is not in the formula because compressing a gas at constant temperature raises its pressure (more impacts per second) without changing the speed of individual molecules.

c_rms ∝ √T and ∝ 1/√M only; pressure affects the collision rate with the walls, not the molecular speeds.

Exercise 13.6 — Degrees of freedom and heat capacities

Q19. Explain degrees of freedom and state the number for monatomic, diatomic and polyatomic gases.

A degree of freedom is an independent mode in which a molecule can store energy — a way it can move or rotate.

Gas typeDegrees of freedom (f)Basis
Monatomic (He, Ne, Ar)3Three translational directions only
Diatomic (O₂, N₂, H₂) at ordinary T53 translational + 2 rotational (rotation about the bond axis stores nothing)
Polyatomic (CO₂, NH₃)63 translational + 3 rotational (plus vibrational modes at high T)

By the law of equipartition, each degree of freedom carries ½kT per molecule (½RT per mole) of energy in thermal equilibrium.

f = 3 (monatomic), 5 (diatomic), 6 (polyatomic); each active degree holds ½kT per molecule.

Q20. Derive Cp − Cv = R and hence the expressions for the molar heat capacities.

Cv = heat needed at constant volume = ΔU/ΔT = (f/2)R (all energy goes into internal energy) Cp: at constant pressure the gas also does work pΔV = RΔT per mole: Cp = Cv + R = (f/2)R + R = R(f/2 + 1) ⇒ Cp − Cv = R, and γ = Cp/Cv = (f + 2)/f Monatomic (f = 3): Cv = 1.5R, Cp = 2.5R, γ = 5/3 = 1.67 Diatomic (f = 5): Cv = 2.5R, Cp = 3.5R, γ = 7/5 = 1.40 Polyatomic (f = 6): Cv = 3R, Cp = 4R, γ = 4/3 = 1.33

Cp − Cv = R (Mayer's relation); γ = (f+2)/f = 1.67, 1.40, 1.33 for monatomic, diatomic and polyatomic gases.

Q21. Why is Cp always greater than Cv for a gas?

At constant volume all the heat supplied raises the internal energy (temperature) only. At constant pressure the gas expands as it is heated, doing external work pΔV on its surroundings. The same temperature rise therefore needs an extra amount of heat equal to that work, which for one mole is R. Hence Cp = Cv + R.

At constant pressure part of the heat becomes expansion work pΔV, so more heat is needed for the same ΔT: Cp = Cv + R.

Q22. What is the internal energy of 2 moles of a monatomic ideal gas at 300 K?

U = (f/2)nRT = (3/2)(2)(8.31)(300) = 7479 J

U ≈ 7.5 × 10³ J — for an ideal gas the internal energy depends only on T, not on volume or pressure.

Q23. A gas has γ = 1.40. Identify its atomicity and find Cv and Cp.

γ = Cp/Cv = 1.40 = 7/5 ⇒ f = 5 (diatomic) Cv = (f/2)R = 2.5 × 8.31 = 20.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ Cp = γCv = 1.40 × 20.8 = 29.1 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (= 3.5R ✓)

Diatomic; Cv ≈ 20.8, Cp ≈ 29.1 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹

Exercise 13.7 — Heat capacities of solids

Q24. State Dulong and Petit's law for solids and explain it with the equipartition theorem.

Law: the molar heat capacity of most simple crystalline solids is approximately the same at high temperature, about 3R ≈ 25 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (6 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).

Each atom in a solid can vibrate in three independent directions. For a vibrating atom, each direction has both kinetic and potential energy, contributing kT (not ½kT); with three directions this gives 3kT per atom, i.e. 3R per mole. Since Cv = dU/dT, Cv ≈ 3R.

C ≈ 3R ≈ 25 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹: three vibrational directions × kT each = 3kT per atom. The law fails at low temperature, where the vibrational modes are frozen out (Einstein/Debye theory).

Q25. Why do solids have only one molar heat capacity while gases have two?

A gas can be heated at constant volume (no expansion work) or at constant pressure (expanding while doing work), giving two different values Cv and Cp. A solid is held by its own rigidity: its volume changes only very slightly on heating (γ ≈ 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹), so the expansion work is negligible and Cv ≈ Cp. Effectively there is only one measurable heat capacity, and Dulong and Petit's 3R applies to it.

The expansion work of a solid is negligible, so Cp ≈ Cv and a single value is quoted; a gas does significant pΔV work, so Cp − Cv = R.

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Ideal-gas numericals reduce to PV = nRT plus the kinetic relations. The eight shapes below cover the gas laws, the kinetic interpretation, and the limiting cases where the model fails.

Q26. [Multi-step numerical] 2.0 L of gas at 27 °C and 1.0 atm is heated at constant pressure to 127 °C. Find the new volume, the number of moles, and the number of molecules.

T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 400 K, constant pressure: V₂ = V₁(T₂/T₁) = 2.0 × 400/300 = 2.67 L n = PV/RT = (1.01 × 10⁵ × 2.0 × 10⁻³)/(8.31 × 300) = 202/2493 = 8.1 × 10⁻² mol N = nN_A = 8.1 × 10⁻² × 6.02 × 10²³ = 4.9 × 10²² molecules

Temperatures must be in kelvin — using 27 and 127 gives a ratio of 4.7 instead of 1.33, which is the single most common error in the unit. Note that the pressure was never needed for the volume ratio (Charles's law) but is needed for the mole count.

V₂ = 2.7 L; n = 8.1 × 10⁻² mol; N ≈ 4.9 × 10²² molecules.

Q27. [Derivation] Starting from the kinetic model result p = ⅓ρc̄², derive the average translational kinetic energy per molecule, and show that it depends only on temperature.

p = ⅓ρc̄² with ρ = Nm/V: pV = ⅓Nmc̄² Compare with the ideal gas law pV = NkT: ⅓Nmc̄² = NkT ⇒ ½mc̄² = (3/2)kT So the mean translational KE per molecule: ε = ½mc̄² = (3/2)kT and per mole: (3/2)RT Internal energy of a monatomic ideal gas: U = (3/2)nRT

The derivation contains the whole content of the kinetic theory: pressure is the momentum flux of molecular collisions, and temperature is a direct measure of mean translational KE. Because the mass cancels, a hydrogen molecule and an oxygen molecule at the same temperature have the same mean KE — but very different rms speeds, since ½mc̄² is fixed and c̄² ∝ 1/m.

pV = ⅓Nmc̄² = NkT gives ε = (3/2)kT per molecule, (3/2)RT per mole — independent of the gas.

Q28. [Graph reading] On a V–T graph at constant pressure, a sample gives a straight line through the origin with slope 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ m³ K⁻¹. If the pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, find the number of moles.

V = (nR/P) T, so slope = nR/P n = slope × P/R = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.0 × 10⁵/8.31 = 0.80/8.31 = 9.6 × 10⁻² mol Check at T = 300 K: V = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 300 = 2.4 × 10⁻³ m³ = 2.4 L, and PV/RT = (1.0 × 10⁵ × 2.4 × 10⁻³)/(8.31 × 300) = 0.096 mol ✓

A straight line through the origin on a V–T graph is Charles's law, and its slope carries nR/P. Two examinable features of the graph: the line must pass through the origin only if T is absolute (in Celsius it intersects at −273 °C), and a steeper line means more gas at the same pressure or lower pressure for the same amount.

Slope = nR/P, so n = 9.6 × 10⁻² mol; the intercept is the origin only on the kelvin scale.

Q29. [Combined concept] Find the rms speed of oxygen molecules at 300 K and compare it with hydrogen at the same temperature. (M_O₂ = 0.032 kg mol⁻¹, M_H₂ = 0.002 kg mol⁻¹)

c_rms = √(3RT/M) O₂: c_rms = √(3 × 8.31 × 300/0.032) = √(2.34 × 10⁵) = 4.8 × 10² m s⁻¹ H₂: c_rms = √(3 × 8.31 × 300/0.002) = √(3.74 × 10⁶) = 1.9 × 10³ m s⁻¹ ratio = √(32/2) = 4.0 (same as √(M_O₂/M_H₂))

Equal temperatures give equal mean kinetic energy but speeds in the ratio √(M_H₂ : M_O₂) = 1 : 4. This is why hydrogen escapes from the Earth's atmosphere and oxygen does not: at 300 K the hydrogen rms speed (1.9 km s⁻¹) is already an appreciable fraction of the 11.2 km s⁻¹ escape speed, and the high-speed tail of the distribution supplies the rest. It is also why helium balloons deflate faster than air-filled ones.

O₂ ≈ 4.8 × 10² m s⁻¹, H₂ ≈ 1.9 × 10³ m s⁻¹ — equal mean KE, speeds inversely proportional to √M.

Q30. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): A gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container. Reason (R): Molecules collide with the walls and their momentum is reversed, so a force acts on the wall.

Both statements are true and R explains A.

One molecule, mass m, normal component u, rebounding elastically: Δp = 2mu per collision Collisions per second on area A (number density n): rate = ½ n A u (the ½ accounts for molecules moving away from the wall) Force = Δp × rate = 2mu × ½nAu = nAmu² Pressure = F/A = nmu² → averaged over directions: ⅓nmc̄²

The factor ⅓ is the one examinable detail: at any instant only one third of the mean square velocity is directed at a given pair of walls. The derivation also shows why pressure is independent of the mass of the molecules at fixed N, V and T — the heavier molecule moves slower but delivers more momentum per collision, and the two effects cancel exactly.

Both true, R explains A: pressure is the rate of momentum transfer per unit area, giving p = ⅓nmc̄² = ⅓ρc̄².

Q31. [Experiment/observation table] In a Boyle's-law experiment the following pressure–volume readings are taken at constant temperature. Complete the last column and state the conclusion.

P (× 10⁵ Pa)V (× 10⁻³ m³)PV (J)
1.002.40?
1.501.60?
2.001.20?
3.000.80?

PV = 1.00 × 10⁵ × 2.40 × 10⁻³ = 240 J 1.50 × 10⁵ × 1.60 × 10⁻³ = 240 J 2.00 × 10⁵ × 1.20 × 10⁻³ = 240 J 3.00 × 10⁵ × 0.80 × 10⁻³ = 240 J

PV is constant at 240 J, which is Boyle's law: at constant temperature p ∝ 1/V. The constant is also nRT, so n = 240/(8.31 × T); at 300 K that is 0.096 mol — the same sample as the V–T graph above. A graph of P against 1/V would be a straight line through the origin with slope 240 J, which is the preferred way to present the result because a straight line is easier to verify than a hyperbola.

Q32. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the number of air molecules in a room 5 m × 4 m × 3 m at 20 °C and 1.0 atm, and state the two limits in which the ideal-gas model fails.

V = 60 m³, T = 293 K, P = 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa n = PV/RT = (1.01 × 10⁵ × 60)/(8.31 × 293) = 6.06 × 10⁶/2435 = 2.5 × 10³ mol N = nN_A = 2.5 × 10³ × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.5 × 10²⁷ molecules mass ≈ n × 0.029 = 72 kg of air (about the mass of a person — air is heavy!)

Limits of the ideal-gas model:

High pressure / low temperature — molecules are close enough for intermolecular forces to matter and the gas liquefies; the van der Waals equation (P + a/V²)(V − b) = nRT corrects for attraction (a) and finite molecular volume (b). Very low density — even then the model is excellent, which is why it is used for atmospheres and for the interstellar medium.

The kinetic model also assumes molecular chaos and negligible collision duration; both hold at ordinary pressures, which is why the ideal-gas law describes air to better than 0.1%.

About 2.5 × 10³ mol, 1.5 × 10²⁷ molecules and roughly 72 kg of air; the model fails at high pressure/low temperature (liquefaction) where molecular volume and attraction matter.

Q33. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “At constant temperature the rms speed of a gas doubles if the pressure doubles.”
(ii) “The internal energy of an ideal gas depends on both its pressure and volume.”
(iii) “Absolute zero is the temperature at which an ideal gas would have zero volume.”
(iv) “All molecules of a gas move with the same speed.”

(i) FALSE — c_rms = √(3RT/M) depends only on T and M. At constant T it is unchanged; doubling the pressure at constant T halves the volume, not the speed. (ii) FALSE — for an IDEAL gas U = (3/2)nRT depends only on T; the molecules have no potential energy. (For a real gas it does depend on V through intermolecular forces.) (iii) TRUE for the ideal-gas model — extrapolating V ∝ T to V = 0 gives 0 K. But real gases liquefy first, so the statement is a limit of the model, not a physical fact. (iv) FALSE — molecular speeds follow the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution: a small fraction is always far above the rms value, which is what makes evaporation, chemical reaction and atmospheric escape possible at ordinary temperatures.

Statement (i) is the classic trap: pressure and temperature are independent variables, and only temperature sets the mean kinetic energy.

(i) c_rms depends only on T; (ii) U = (3/2)nRT for an ideal gas; (iii) true as a model limit (real gases liquefy); (iv) speeds follow a Maxwell distribution.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. State the ideal gas equation and explain the symbols.

pV = nRT, where p is pressure, V volume, n the number of moles, R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ the universal gas constant and T the absolute temperature. In molecular form pV = NkT, with N the number of molecules and k = R/N_A.

C2. Define rms speed of a gas molecule.

The square root of the mean of the squares of the molecular speeds: c_rms = √[(c₁² + c₂² + … + c_N²)/N] = √(3RT/M) = √(3kT/m). It is used rather than the average speed because kinetic energy depends on c².

C3. Why is temperature proportional to average molecular kinetic energy?

Because the kinetic model gives ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT: the pressure a gas exerts depends on the molecular speeds, and equating the kinetic expression to pV = NkT shows that the mean translational kinetic energy is set by T alone. Temperature is therefore a direct measure of molecular agitation.

C4. What is meant by the mean free path?

The average distance a gas molecule travels between successive collisions with other molecules. It is inversely proportional to the number density and the collision cross-section, and at ordinary pressures it is of order 10⁻⁷ m — thousands of molecular diameters.

C5. Why do different gases have the same average molecular kinetic energy at the same temperature but different rms speeds?

Because the kinetic energy ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT depends only on T, so at equal temperatures all molecules have equal mean kinetic energy. But c_rms = √(3kT/m) depends on the molecular mass, so lighter molecules must move faster to carry the same energy.

C6. State Mayer's relation.

Cp − Cv = R for one mole of an ideal gas. It follows because the extra heat at constant pressure supplies the expansion work pΔV = RΔT in addition to raising the internal energy.

C7. Why does a monatomic gas have γ = 1.67 while a diatomic gas has γ = 1.40?

Because γ = (f + 2)/f, and f = 3 for a monatomic gas (translation only) against f = 5 for a diatomic gas (translation plus two rotations). The extra rotational degrees of freedom absorb energy without doing work, lowering the ratio Cp/Cv.

C8. Why is the internal energy of an ideal gas independent of its volume?

Because ideal gas molecules have no intermolecular forces, so there is no potential energy term depending on separation. The internal energy is purely kinetic and depends only on T: U = (f/2)nRT.

C9. Give two pieces of evidence for the kinetic theory of gases.

(i) Brownian motion: smoke or pollen grains jitter as a result of unequal molecular impacts. (ii) The measured ratio of specific heats of monatomic gases is close to the predicted 1.67, and the observed gas pressure matches p = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩.

C10. What happens to the rms speed of a gas if its absolute temperature is quadrupled?

c_rms ∝ √T, so quadrupling T doubles the rms speed. The average kinetic energy, however, quadruples, since it is proportional to T rather than √T.

C11. Why does a gas fill its container completely?

Because the molecules have negligible intermolecular attraction and move in straight lines between random collisions, so they travel until they strike a wall. Only gravity (very weakly) or attractive forces would confine them, and for an ideal gas neither is significant.

C12. Define the degree of freedom and state the law of equipartition of energy.

A degree of freedom is an independent mode of storing energy (a way a molecule can translate or rotate). The equipartition law states that in thermal equilibrium each active degree of freedom carries an average energy of ½kT per molecule (½RT per mole).

C13. What is the physical significance of Boltzmann's constant?

It is the gas constant per molecule, k = R/N_A = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹, and it is the conversion factor between temperature and molecular energy: each degree of freedom carries ½kT. It appears in every microscopic thermal expression, from kinetic energy to entropy.

C14. Why is an ideal gas assumed to have no intermolecular forces?

Because at low pressure and moderate temperature the molecules are so far apart that attractive forces are negligible and potential energy can be ignored. This makes the internal energy purely kinetic and the gas law pV = nRT exact, which is what makes the model tractable and accurate for real gases near ambient conditions.

C15. Why is the specific heat of a gas at constant volume independent of the amount of gas but its heat capacity is not?

Cv is a molar (or specific) quantity — the value per mole (or per kg) — so it is a property of the gas itself. The heat capacity of a particular sample is nCv, which depends on how many moles are present. Compare specific heat capacity with heat capacity in calorimetry.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Derive p = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩ and deduce pV = (1/3)Nm⟨c²⟩, then use it to show that the kinetic energy of translation per mole is (3/2)RT.

See Q9 for the derivation of p = (1/3)(Nm/V)⟨c²⟩, obtained by summing the momentum delivered to one face by all N molecules in a cubical box.

Multiplying by V: pV = (1/3)Nm⟨c²⟩ = (2/3)N × ½m⟨c²⟩ = (2/3)E_trans Measured gas law: pV = nRT = (2/3)E_trans ⇒ E_trans = (3/2)nRT (per mole: 3/2 RT; per molecule: 3/2 kT)

p = (1/3)ρ⟨c²⟩ ⇒ pV = (2/3)E_trans; comparing with pV = nRT gives E_trans = (3/2)nRT, so average translational KE per molecule is (3/2)kT.

L2. [5] 0.05 m³ of a gas at 300 K and 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa is compressed adiabatically to 0.02 m³. Find the final pressure and the work done if γ = 1.4.

Adiabatic: p₁V₁^γ = p₂V₂^γ p₂ = p₁(V₁/V₂)^1.4 = 1.0 × 10⁵ × (0.05/0.02)^1.4 = 1.0 × 10⁵ × (2.5)^1.4 (2.5)^1.4 = e^(1.4 ln 2.5) = e^(1.4 × 0.916) = e^1.283 = 3.61 p₂ = 3.61 × 10⁵ Pa Work done on the gas: W = (p₂V₂ − p₁V₁)/(γ − 1) = (3.61 × 10⁵ × 0.02 − 1.0 × 10⁵ × 0.05)/0.4 = (7220 − 5000)/0.4 = 2220/0.4 = 5550 J

p₂ ≈ 3.6 × 10⁵ Pa; about 5.6 kJ of work is done on the gas, and its temperature rises accordingly.

L3. [5] Show that the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule of any ideal gas at 27 °C is the same, and calculate it. Why does a mole of hydrogen contain the same translational energy as a mole of oxygen at that temperature?

½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT = 1.5(1.38 × 10⁻²³)(300) = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J per mole: (3/2)RT = 1.5(8.31)(300) = 3740 J

The expression contains only k and T — the mass m cancels when ⟨c²⟩ = 3kT/m is substituted. So every gas has the same mean translational energy per molecule (and per mole) at the same temperature, even though the lighter molecules move faster.

6.2 × 10⁻²¹ J per molecule (3.74 kJ per mole) for every gas at 300 K; the mass affects only the speed, not the energy.

L4. [4] A cylinder contains 2 g of hydrogen at 27 °C. Find the number of molecules, the total translational kinetic energy and the rms speed. (M = 2 g mol⁻¹)

n = 2/2 = 1 mol ⇒ N = 6.02 × 10²³ molecules E_trans = (3/2)nRT = 1.5 × 1 × 8.31 × 300 = 3.74 × 10³ J c_rms = √(3RT/M) = √(3 × 8.31 × 300/0.002) = √(7479/0.002) = √3.74 × 10⁶ = 1934 m s⁻¹

N = 6.02 × 10²³; E = 3.7 kJ; c_rms ≈ 1.9 × 10³ m s⁻¹ (about 4 times that of oxygen at the same temperature, as √(32/2) predicts)

L5. [5] Explain why the measured value of γ for a diatomic gas falls from 1.40 towards 1.30 at high temperature.

At high temperature the vibrational modes of the molecule become thermally excited. Vibration adds two degrees of freedom (one kinetic and one potential), so f rises from 5 towards 7. Then

γ = (f + 2)/f: f = 5 → 1.40; f = 7 → 9/7 = 1.29

The measured value therefore decreases towards about 1.3. At low temperature the rotations may also freeze out, driving γ towards 1.67 as only translation remains. The temperature dependence of γ is one of the clearest experimental proofs of quantised molecular energy levels.

Vibrational modes activate at high T, increasing f and lowering γ = (f+2)/f from 1.40 towards 1.29; the effect is direct evidence of quantisation.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 13

1. Molar mass in grams. c_rms = √(3RT/M) needs M in kg mol⁻¹. Using 32 instead of 0.032 makes the speed 31.6 times too small.

2. Celsius temperatures. The kinetic relations use absolute temperature; 27 °C must become 300 K, and the gas laws also require T in kelvin.

3. Mixing Cv and Cp. Cv = (f/2)R and Cp = Cv + R. Using Cv where Cp is required (or assigning the rotational energy to Cp alone) breaks Mayer's relation.

4. Saying the rms speed depends on pressure. It depends on T and M only; pressure affects collision frequency, not molecular speed.

5. Forgetting γ = Cp/Cv is a ratio of molar quantities. Specific (per kg) heat capacities give the same γ, since the mass factor cancels — but C_p − C_v = R holds for molar capacities only; per kg the difference is R/M.
