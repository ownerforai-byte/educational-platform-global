Unit 8 — Elasticity

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  5 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Elasticity questions are almost all Young-modulus numericals plus one definition-heavy 2-mark item. The marks are in the ratio form Y = FL/(AΔL) written with the units converted, and in the stress–strain diagram drawn with all five points labelled.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define stress and strain with units; state Hooke's law; define Poisson's ratio; why is steel more elastic than rubber.
  • 4 marks — Young modulus numerical for a stretched wire; energy stored in a stretched wire; bulk modulus of a liquid or gas.
  • 5–6 marks — draw and explain the stress–strain curve for a ductile material; derive elastic potential energy = ½ × stress × strain × volume.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: convert mm² to m² and mm to m before substituting. Nine of ten errors in this unit are unit-conversion errors, not physics errors.

Exercise 8.1 — Hooke's law and the force constant

Q1. State Hooke's law and define the force constant. A spring extends by 2 cm under a load of 10 N. Find its force constant and the extension under 25 N.

Hooke's law: within the elastic limit, the extension produced is directly proportional to the applied force: F = kx.

Force constant k is the force needed per unit extension, k = F/x, in N m⁻¹ — the stiffness of the spring.

k = F/x = 10/0.02 = 500 N m⁻¹ For F = 25 N: x = F/k = 25/500 = 0.05 m = 5 cm

k = 500 N m⁻¹; extension = 5 cm

Q2. Two springs of constants 200 N m⁻¹ and 300 N m⁻¹ are joined (a) in series, (b) in parallel. Find the effective spring constant in each case.

(a) Series: 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂ = 1/200 + 1/300 = 5/600 ⇒ k = 120 N m⁻¹ (b) Parallel: k = k₁ + k₂ = 500 N m⁻¹

Series 120 N m⁻¹ (softer than either); parallel 500 N m⁻¹ (stiffer than either) — the same rule as resistors in reverse.

Q3. Draw a force–extension graph for a wire and explain what its slope and its limit points mean.

  • Slope = F/x = k, the force constant (stiffness). A steeper line means a stiffer wire.
  • O to A — a straight line through the origin: Hooke's law holds, the region of proportionality.
  • Beyond A — the line curves: the elastic limit is passed but the wire can still return to its original length (elastic behaviour), then the yield point is reached.
  • Beyond the yield point — permanent (plastic) deformation; the wire does not return to its original length when the load is removed.
  • At the breaking point — the wire snaps; the corresponding stress is the breaking stress (tensile strength).

Slope = k; the points mark the limit of proportionality, the elastic limit, the yield point and the breaking point.

Exercise 8.2 — Stress, strain and the elastic moduli

Q4. Define stress and strain, give their units and dimensions, and state Hooke's law in terms of them.

Stress = restoring force per unit area, σ = F/A; unit N m⁻² (Pa), dimensions [ML⁻¹T⁻²].

Strain = fractional change of dimension, ε = ΔL/L (for a wire); dimensionless, no unit.

Hooke's law: stress ∝ strain ⇒ σ = E ε The constant E is the modulus of elasticity of the material.

σ = F/A in Pa, [ML⁻¹T⁻²]; ε = ΔL/L, dimensionless; σ = Eε, where E is Young modulus for a wire.

Q5. Distinguish between elasticity and plasticity, giving examples.

Elasticity is the property by which a body regains its original shape and size when the deforming force is removed (a spring, a rubber band, a steel wire within the elastic limit).

Plasticity is the property by which a body retains a permanent deformation after the force is removed (clay, putty, a wire stretched beyond its yield point).

Elastic bodies recover fully; plastic bodies deform permanently. Steel shows both, depending on the stress — elastic below the yield point and plastic above it.

Q6. Which is more elastic, steel or rubber? Justify.

Steel. "More elastic" means a larger modulus of elasticity, i.e. a smaller strain for the same stress. Y_steel ≈ 2 × 10¹¹ Pa against Y_rubber ≈ 10⁶–10⁷ Pa. For the same stress, steel stretches about 10 000 times less than rubber, so it recovers from a much larger stress without permanent deformation.

Steel is more elastic because its Young modulus is many orders larger — the everyday intuition that "rubber stretches more, so it is more elastic" confuses elasticity with flexibility.

Exercise 8.3 — Young modulus numericals

Q7. A steel wire of length 2 m and cross-section 1 mm² stretches by 0.5 mm under a load of 10 kg. Find the Young modulus of steel.

F = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N A = 1 mm² = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m² L = 2 m, ΔL = 0.5 mm = 5 × 10⁻⁴ m Y = FL/(AΔL) = (98)(2)/[(1 × 10⁻⁶)(5 × 10⁻⁴)] = 196/(5 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 3.92 × 10¹¹ Pa

Y ≈ 3.9 × 10¹¹ Pa (the accepted value for steel is 2 × 10¹¹ Pa; the load or extension in this question is exaggerated)

Q8. A wire of length 1.5 m and diameter 0.5 mm is stretched by 1 kg. If Y = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa, find the extension.

r = 0.25 × 10⁻³ m A = πr² = 3.1416(6.25 × 10⁻⁸) = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m² F = 9.8 N ΔL = FL/(AY) = 9.8 × 1.5/(1.96 × 10⁻⁷ × 2 × 10¹¹) = 14.7/(3.93 × 10⁴) = 3.74 × 10⁻⁴ m

ΔL ≈ 0.37 mm — a 1 kg load stretches a half-millimetre steel wire by less than half a millimetre, which is why steel is used for structural cables.

Q9. Two wires of the same material have lengths in the ratio 1 : 2 and radii in the ratio 1 : 2. If the same load is applied to both, find the ratio of their extensions.

ΔL = FL/(AY), with Y the same and F the same: ΔL ∝ L/A ∝ L/r² ΔL₁/ΔL₂ = (L₁/L₂)(r₂/r₁)² = (1/2)(2/1)² = (1/2)(4) = 2 So ΔL₁ : ΔL₂ = 2 : 1

2 : 1 — the shorter, thinner wire stretches twice as much as the longer, thicker one. Extension depends on L/A, not on length alone.

Q10. A wire breaks under a load of 100 N. If its cross-sectional area is 0.5 mm², find the breaking stress.

σ = F/A = 100/(0.5 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.0 × 10⁸ Pa

2.0 × 10⁸ Pa = 200 MPa (the tensile strength of the material)

Q11. The Young modulus of a material is 1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa. What strain is produced by a stress of 2.4 × 10⁸ Pa? Is the deformation within the elastic limit?

ε = σ/Y = 2.4 × 10⁸/1.2 × 10¹¹ = 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 0.2%

A 0.2% strain is well inside the elastic region of most metals (their elastic limit is around 0.1–0.5% strain, and the breaking strain is commonly 10–30%), so the deformation is elastic and the material returns to its original length.

ε = 2 × 10⁻³ (0.2%), within the elastic limit.

Exercise 8.4 — Bulk modulus and shear modulus

Q12. Define bulk modulus and compressibility. For water, K = 2.2 × 10⁹ Pa — find the fractional volume decrease under a pressure of 2.2 × 10⁶ Pa.

Bulk modulus K = −Δp/(ΔV/V): the pressure needed per unit fractional change of volume (the minus sign keeps K positive, since volume decreases as pressure rises). Compressibility = 1/K.

ΔV/V = −Δp/K = −2.2 × 10⁶/2.2 × 10⁹ = −1.0 × 10⁻³

The volume decreases by 0.1% — water is about 100 times less compressible than air, which is why hydraulics works.

Q13. Define shear modulus and give one example where it matters.

Shear modulus (modulus of rigidity) G = shear stress/shear strain = (F/A)/(θ), the ratio of the tangential force per unit area to the angle of shear, in Pa.

It measures resistance to a change of shape at constant volume. Examples: a book pushed sideways on a table (the pages shear), the twisting of a shaft or a bone, or the steel in a bridge girder resisting sideways loads.

G = (F/A)/θ; it governs shape change at constant volume — twisting shafts, shear in beams, and the rigidity of solids (fluids have G = 0).

Q14. Why do solids have a shear modulus while liquids and gases do not?

Shear requires a permanent sideways displacement of layers. In a solid the atoms are held in a fixed lattice, so a displacement produces a restoring force proportional to the displacement — a finite G. In a liquid or gas the molecules can slide freely past one another, so any shear produces flow rather than a restoring force, and G = 0 (they only support compressive stress, which is why they have a bulk modulus but no shear modulus).

Solids resist shear by lattice forces (G > 0); fluids flow under shear (G = 0) and only resist volume change.

Q15. Why is the bulk modulus of a gas different for isothermal and adiabatic compression?

Isothermal: pV = constant ⇒ K = p Adiabatic: pV^γ = constant ⇒ K = γp

In an isothermal change the gas is allowed to exchange heat with its surroundings and stays at constant temperature; in an adiabatic change no heat is exchanged, the temperature rises on compression and the pressure rises faster, giving a larger K by the factor γ (≈1.4 for air). Sound waves travel adiabatically, so sound speed uses γp/ρ.

K_iso = p, K_adiabatic = γp; the adiabatic value is larger because compression heats the gas.

Exercise 8.5 — Poisson's ratio

Q16. Define Poisson's ratio and state its theoretical limits.

Poisson's ratio σ is the ratio of the lateral (transverse) strain to the longitudinal strain:

σ = −(Δr/r)/(ΔL/L)

The minus sign makes σ positive for ordinary materials, because a wire lengthens while thinning. Its value lies between 0 and 0.5: σ = 0 means no lateral contraction (cork, ≈0), σ = 0.5 means volume is unchanged (rubber, ≈0.5). A value above 0.5 would imply a volume increase under tension, which is thermodynamically impossible for isotropic materials.

σ = −lateral strain/longitudinal strain, 0 ≤ σ ≤ 0.5; steel ≈ 0.28, rubber ≈ 0.5, cork ≈ 0.

Q17. A wire of length 2 m and diameter 1 mm is stretched by 1 mm. If Poisson's ratio is 0.3, find the change in diameter.

Longitudinal strain = ΔL/L = 1/2000 = 5 × 10⁻⁴ Lateral strain = σ × longitudinal strain = 0.3 × 5 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ Δd = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 1 mm = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ mm = 1.5 × 10⁻⁷ m

The diameter decreases by 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ mm (0.15 µm) — a tiny contraction, which is why lateral strain is hard to measure directly.

Exercise 8.6 — Elastic potential energy

Q18. Derive the expression for the elastic potential energy stored in a stretched wire.

Work done in stretching = area under the F–ΔL graph (a straight line) W = ½ F ΔL Using F = YA(ΔL)/L: W = ½ × [YA ΔL/L] × ΔL = ½ × Y × (ΔL/L)² × AL W = ½ × stress × strain × volume Energy density = W/volume = ½ stress × strain = ½ × σ²/Y = ½ Y ε²

U = ½FΔL = ½kx² = ½ × stress × strain × volume; the energy density is ½σε, which is the area under the stress–strain curve up to the given strain.

Q19. A wire of length 2 m and area 1 mm² is stretched by 1 mm under a force of 100 N. Find the energy stored.

U = ½ F ΔL = ½(100)(1 × 10⁻³) = 0.05 J Check via energy density: stress = 100/10⁻⁶ = 10⁸ Pa; strain = 10⁻³/2 = 5 × 10⁻⁴ energy density = ½(10⁸)(5 × 10⁻⁴) = 2.5 × 10⁴ J m⁻³ volume = 10⁻⁶ × 2 = 2 × 10⁻⁶ m³ ⇒ U = 0.05 J ✓

U = 0.05 J (50 mJ), stored as strain energy in the stretched bonds

Q20. A spring of constant 400 N m⁻¹ is compressed by 5 cm. Find the energy stored and the speed given to a 0.1 kg mass released against it.

U = ½kx² = ½(400)(0.05)² = 200 × 2.5 × 10⁻³ = 0.5 J On release: ½kx² = ½mv² 0.5 = ½(0.1)v² ⇒ v² = 10 ⇒ v = 3.16 m s⁻¹

U = 0.5 J; v ≈ 3.2 m s⁻¹

Exercise 8.7 — The stress–strain curve

Q21. Draw and explain the stress–strain curve for a ductile material (mild steel), marking five important points.

PointNameWhat happens
O–ALimit of proportionalityStress ∝ strain; Hooke's law holds; the graph is a straight line through the origin
AElastic limit (yield point)Beyond this the material no longer returns exactly to its original length
BYield point / upper yieldA sudden extension with little increase of stress (the material "gives")
CUltimate tensile strengthMaximum stress the material can bear
DFracture pointNecking, then the specimen breaks

Young modulus is the slope of the initial straight part. A ductile material has a long plastic region (large strain before fracture); a brittle material (glass, cast iron) fractures soon after the elastic limit with almost no plastic region.

O–A linear (Hooke), A elastic limit, B yield, C ultimate strength, D fracture; slope of OA gives Y, and the area under the curve is the energy density.

Q22. What is meant by elastic hysteresis? Give one practical consequence.

On loading and unloading a material (rubber especially) the unloading curve does not retrace the loading curve; the loop between them is the hysteresis loop, and its area is the energy dissipated as heat per unit volume per cycle. The material lags behind the applied stress.

The loop area is energy lost as heat each cycle; it is why a repeatedly flexed rubber or a cycling tyre warms up, and it is deliberately large in shock absorbers and deliberately small in watch springs.

Q23. Why is a hollow tube stronger than a solid rod of the same mass in bending and torsion?

In bending, the stress is greatest at the outer surface and zero at the neutral axis, so the material near the axis contributes little to the bending strength. Removing it and placing the same mass farther out increases the second moment of area (and the polar moment for torsion), which raises the stiffness roughly as r² for the same mass. That is why bicycle frames, bones and drill shafts are hollow tubes.

The outer material does most of the work, so a hollow tube of equal mass has a larger radius and a much larger moment of area — greater strength and stiffness with no extra weight.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Define stress and strain with their SI units.

Stress is the internal restoring force per unit area, σ = F/A, in N m⁻² (pascal). Strain is the fractional deformation, ε = ΔL/L, which is dimensionless and has no unit.

C2. State Hooke's law and its limits.

Within the elastic limit, stress is directly proportional to strain (equivalently, extension is proportional to the applied force). It fails beyond the limit of proportionality and does not apply to plastic deformation.

C3. Why does a steel wire stretch less than a rubber cord of the same length under the same load?

Because ΔL = FL/(AY) and steel has a far larger Young modulus (≈2 × 10¹¹ Pa against ≈10⁷ Pa for rubber). For the same F, L and A the extension is inversely proportional to Y, so the steel wire stretches thousands of times less.

C4. What is the physical significance of Young modulus?

It measures the stiffness of a material: the stress needed to produce unit strain. A large Y means the material resists stretching strongly and is "more elastic" in the physicist's sense (small strain for a given stress).

C5. Distinguish between Young modulus, bulk modulus and shear modulus.

Young modulus applies to a change of length of a wire or rod (Y = FL/AΔL). Bulk modulus applies to a change of volume under pressure (K = −Δp/(ΔV/V)), and applies to solids, liquids and gases. Shear modulus applies to a change of shape at constant volume (G = (F/A)/θ) and exists only for solids.

C6. Why is strain dimensionless?

Because it is the ratio of two lengths (ΔL/L) or of two angles, so the units cancel. It is often quoted in percent or as microstrain (10⁻⁶).

C7. What is meant by the elastic limit of a material?

The largest stress up to which the material returns exactly to its original dimensions when the load is removed. Beyond it the material is permanently deformed (plastic behaviour) even after unloading.

C8. Give two examples where high elasticity is desirable and two where it is a nuisance.

Desirable: springs and shock absorbers, suspension cables and bridges, catapults, and the elastic cartilage in joints. A nuisance: unwanted sag of a long beam under load, vibration of a machine shaft, energy lost in a bouncing ball, and the stretching of a measuring tape.

C9. Why is the stress–strain curve of a ductile material different from that of a brittle one?

A ductile material (steel, copper) yields and then extends a great deal before fracture, so its curve has a long plastic region; a brittle material (glass, cast iron) fractures soon after the elastic limit with almost no plastic deformation, so its curve falls steeply to zero.

C10. What happens to the energy stored in a wire stretched beyond its elastic limit?

The deformation becomes permanent, so only the elastic part of the stored energy is recovered on unloading; the remainder is dissipated as heat (and in generating crystal defects and slip planes), which is why a wire stretched too far never springs back completely.

C11. Define compressibility and state its relation to bulk modulus.

Compressibility is the fractional decrease of volume per unit increase of pressure, C = −(1/V)(ΔV/Δp). It is the reciprocal of the bulk modulus, C = 1/K, and is measured in Pa⁻¹.

C12. Why does a rubber band warm up when stretched quickly and cool when released?

Stretching aligns the polymer chains and reduces the entropy of the rubber (it becomes more ordered), and the heat released at constant entropy raises its temperature. On rapid release the chains regain disorder, absorb heat and the band cools — a demonstration of the thermoelastic (Gough–Joule) effect.

C13. Why is the breaking stress of a material independent of the dimensions of the wire?

Breaking stress is F/A, a property of the material's interatomic bonds, so it is a constant for that material. A thicker wire can carry a larger force but breaks at the same stress, which is why strength data are quoted in Pa.

C14. What is meant by the tensile strength of a material?

The maximum tensile stress a material can withstand before breaking — the stress at the ultimate strength point (or at fracture) of the stress–strain curve. Steel is about 400–2000 MPa, human bone about 130 MPa.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [5] A steel wire of length 2.0 m and cross-sectional area 1.5 mm² is loaded with 5 kg. Given Y = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa, find the extension, the strain, the stress and the energy stored.

F = 5 × 9.8 = 49 N; A = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ m² stress σ = 49/1.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 3.27 × 10⁷ Pa strain ε = σ/Y = 3.27 × 10⁷/2 × 10¹¹ = 1.63 × 10⁻⁴ ΔL = εL = 1.63 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.0 = 3.27 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.33 mm U = ½FΔL = ½(49)(3.27 × 10⁻⁴) = 8.0 × 10⁻³ J

ΔL = 0.33 mm; ε = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴; σ = 3.3 × 10⁷ Pa; U = 8.0 mJ

L2. [6] Derive U = ½ × stress × strain × volume and use it to explain why a long thin wire stores more energy than a short thick one of the same volume under the same stress.

U = ½FΔL, with F = σA and ΔL = εL: U = ½(σA)(εL) = ½σε(AL) = ½σε × volume

At fixed stress the strain is fixed (ε = σ/Y), so the energy stored is proportional to the volume of material, not to its shape. A wire of the same volume therefore stores the same energy; what changes with shape is the extension (ΔL = εL, larger for a long wire) and the force (F = σA, larger for a thick wire). The "long thin" wire is preferred in a spring because it gives a large useful extension at a modest force.

U = ½σε × volume; energy depends on the volume and the stress, while the force–extension trade-off is set by the shape.

L3. [5] A copper wire and an iron wire of the same length and diameter are joined end to end and loaded with 2 kg. Given Y_Cu = 1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa and Y_Fe = 1.9 × 10¹¹ Pa, find the ratio of their extensions and the total extension if each is 1 m long and 1 mm in diameter.

ΔL ∝ 1/Y at fixed F, L, A: ΔL_Cu/ΔL_Fe = Y_Fe/Y_Cu = 1.9/1.2 = 1.58 A = π(0.5 × 10⁻³)² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ m²; F = 19.6 N ΔL_Cu = 19.6 × 1/(7.85 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.2 × 10¹¹) = 2.08 × 10⁻⁴ m ΔL_Fe = 19.6/(7.85 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.9 × 10¹¹) = 1.31 × 10⁻⁴ m Total = 3.39 × 10⁻⁴ m

ΔL_Cu : ΔL_Fe = 1.58 : 1; total extension ≈ 0.34 mm

L4. [6] Explain, with the stress–strain curve, why a ductile material is preferred for a bridge cable while a brittle material is avoided, and why the working stress is kept well below the yield stress.

A ductile material has a long plastic region: it yields visibly and stretches before breaking, giving warning and redistributing the load instead of snapping. A brittle material fractures almost at the elastic limit with no warning, which is catastrophic in a structure.

Even for steel the working stress is kept to about one-third to one-half of the yield stress, because (i) loads may exceed the design value, (ii) repeated loading causes fatigue cracking at stresses well below the static strength, and (iii) small undetected flaws concentrate stress locally. The safety factor covers all three.

Ductility gives warning and load redistribution; brittle fracture is sudden. Keeping the working stress below yield leaves a margin for overload, fatigue and stress concentration.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 8

1. mm² and mm. 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², not 10⁻³; 1 mm = 10⁻³ m. Convert area and extension in the first line, before any substitution.

2. Diameter used as radius. A = πd²/4. Using r = d gives an area four times too large and an extension four times too small.

3. Calling rubber "more elastic". Elasticity is measured by the modulus, so steel is more elastic than rubber. Write the reason (larger Y, smaller strain for the same stress).

4. Forgetting the half in the energy formula. U = ½FΔL, not FΔL. The half comes from the triangular area under a straight F–ΔL graph; without it the answer doubles.

5. Using the wrong modulus. A change of length uses Y, a change of volume uses K, a change of shape uses G. Naming the right modulus is itself a mark.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Write the formula in symbols, then list the substituted SI values in a line, then the answer to 2–3 significant figures with the unit. For definitions, give the formula and the unit in the same sentence — that pair is the mark.
