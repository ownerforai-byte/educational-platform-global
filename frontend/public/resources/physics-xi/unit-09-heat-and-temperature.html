Unit 9: Heat and Temperature

Physics XI | NEB (Grade 11) | Phy. 101  •  Content area: Heat & Thermodynamics

The distinction between heat and temperature is the doorway to thermodynamics. Heat is energy in transit, temperature is a property of a body, and the zeroth law is what makes thermometers possible at all.

What the model asserts

  1. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; it is a process quantity, not a substance stored inside an object.
  2. Temperature measures the average microscopic kinetic energy of particles, with the equipartition theorem distributing energy across the available degrees of freedom.
  3. The zeroth law - if A equals B thermally and B equals C, then A equals C - is what makes a thermometer a thermometer.
  4. Temperature is intensive; heat and internal energy are extensive, so they scale with the amount of matter.
  5. The kelvin is defined by the Boltzmann constant, k = 1.380649e-23 J/K exactly, and is the only SI scale proportional to a true zero.
  6. Thermal equilibrium is dynamic: microscopic energy exchange continues forever, but with no net flow in either direction.
  7. Temperature is a state variable made meaningful by the zeroth law, while heat is a path-dependent transfer; the two are different kinds of quantity and are not interchangeable inside an equation.

Conditions, assumptions and limits

  • 'Temperature equals average kinetic energy' is exact for a monatomic ideal gas; for molecules with rotation and vibration the equipartition theorem spreads the energy over more degrees of freedom.
  • A thermometer reading is the equilibrium temperature of the thermometer-object pair, so the reading lags and can measurably disturb a small object.
  • At nanometre and smaller scales, different definitions of temperature (electronic, vibrational, configurational) need not agree, so 'the temperature' of a nanoscale system can be ambiguous.
  • Temperature scales other than kelvin are proportional only over a limited range; Celsius and Fahrenheit diverge as you approach absolute zero.
  • Thermodynamic equilibrium requires thermal and mechanical balance together, so a sealed system can be at thermal equilibrium and still be pushing on its surroundings.

Common misconceptions — 3 to unlearn

“Heat is a substance contained in a body, and a hot body has more heat in it.”

Why it fails: Heat is not stored; it is energy in transit across a boundary because of a temperature difference. A body contains internal energy, and calling that 'heat' leads to statements like 'the heat of a body is proportional to its temperature' that have no meaning.

Correct: Heat Q is energy transferred thermally. A body contains internal energy U; the part of it that can be transferred as heat is the internal energy, and the transfer stops when the temperatures are equal.

“If two bodies are at the same temperature there is no heat flowing between them.”

Why it fails: At equal temperature the NET heat transfer is zero, but microscopic energy exchange continues in both directions all the time. Thermal equilibrium is a statistical statement about the balance of many exchanges.

Correct: At equal temperature the gross exchange of energy continues in both directions, but the rates are equal, so the net flow is zero. That is the content of the zeroth law and of thermal equilibrium.

“Temperature and heat are the same thing, so a smaller body with the same temperature contains less heat.”

Why it fails: Temperature is an intensive property (it does not depend on the amount of matter); heat is an energy transferred, and internal energy is extensive. The confusion is very common in exam questions that mix the two words.

Correct: Temperature T is a state variable (the average kinetic energy of the particles, defined through the zeroth law). Heat Q is energy transferred, and the heat required for a given temperature change is Q = m c dT, which does depend on the mass.

Workbook — 3 questions with answers

numerical

A 200 g aluminium block at 100 degrees C is dropped into 100 g of water at 20 degrees C. Take c_aluminium = 900 J/(kg K) and c_water = 4200 J/(kg K). Find the equilibrium temperature.

Answer: Heat lost by aluminium = heat gained by water: 0.2 x 900 x (100 - T) = 0.1 x 4200 x (T - 20), i.e. 180 (100 - T) = 420 (T - 20). Expanding: 18000 - 180 T = 420 T - 8400, so 26400 = 600 T and T = 44 degrees C. (The aluminium has the smaller heat capacity, so the equilibrium temperature sits much closer to the water's initial temperature.)

derivation

State the zeroth law of thermodynamics and show how it is the precondition for the definition of temperature, and hence for the existence of a thermometer.

Answer: The zeroth law states: if A and B are in thermal equilibrium, and B and C are in thermal equilibrium, then A and C are in thermal equilibrium. The temperature of a body is defined as that quantity whose equality is equivalent to thermal equilibrium. Because the law is transitive, a body A can be compared with a reference body C by interposing B, and this is exactly the working of a thermometer: the liquid column of a mercury thermometer is body B, in equilibrium with the unknown A, and the scale is calibrated against a fixed reference. Without the zeroth law there would be no consistent scale, and thermometers could not be compared with one another.

reasoning

A hot steel spoon is placed in a bowl of cold water. Describe the direction of heat flow at the microscopic level, and explain why the water's temperature rises even though no 'heat' is stored in it at the start.

Answer: At the steel-water interface the surface metal atoms vibrate more energetically than the adjacent water molecules, so energy passes from the steel to the water: as an exchange of photons (or, more accurately, of vibrational energy quanta) across the interface. The water gains internal energy, which shows up as a rise in the average kinetic energy of its molecules and hence as a rise in temperature. Nothing 'containing heat' moved into the water; what moved was energy, and the water's internal energy increased by exactly the amount the steel lost (minus a small part carried away as sound and to the surroundings).

Open questions and mysteries

Can two bodies at the same temperature still exchange heat?

Under active scrutiny

Yes - equilibrium is statistical and exchange continues in both directions, but the net flow vanishes; the statistical statement is what makes the zeroth law a law.

Is there a single, unique temperature for a system far from equilibrium?

Open question

Different definitions diverge for non-equilibrium and nanoscale systems, and no universal choice is settled.
