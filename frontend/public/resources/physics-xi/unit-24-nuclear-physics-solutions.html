Unit 24 — Nuclear Physics

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  4 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Two numerical families dominate: mass defect → binding energy (×931.5 MeV per u), and half-life → remaining amount (halving repeatedly). The rest of the unit is explanation: decay properties, the binding-energy curve and fission versus fusion.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define mass defect and binding energy; define half-life; state the properties of alpha, beta and gamma rays; define isotopes and isobars; why is binding energy per nucleon a measure of stability.
  • 4 marks — binding energy from a mass defect; half-life numerical; balancing a decay equation; decay constant and activity.
  • 5–6 marks — explain the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve and use it to explain fission and fusion; derive or apply the radioactive decay law N = N₀e^(−λt); compare fission and fusion as energy sources.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: mass defect is in atomic mass units, and 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c². Convert with 931.5, not 931.5 × 10⁶ or 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹, and quote energy in MeV.

Exercise 24.1 — The nucleus and its composition

Q1. Describe the composition of a nucleus and define atomic number, mass number and nuclide.

A nucleus consists of protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons.

QuantitySymbolMeaning
Atomic numberZNumber of protons; determines the element and the electron count in a neutral atom
Mass numberATotal number of nucleons, A = Z + N (N = neutrons)
NuclideᴬZXA specific nucleus with given Z and A

Protons carry charge +e and neutrons are neutral; both have almost the same mass. The nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force, which acts over a range of about 1 fm and is far stronger than the electric repulsion between protons.

A nucleus is Z protons plus N neutrons, with A = Z + N; the nuclide is written ᴬZX, and the strong force binds the nucleons.

Q2. Distinguish between isotopes, isobars and isotones, with one example of each.

TermDefinitionExample
IsotopesSame Z, different A (same element, different neutron number)¹²C and ¹⁴C; ¹H, ²H and ³H
IsobarsSame A, different Z (different elements, same nucleon number)⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca; ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N
IsotonesSame neutron number N, different Z¹⁴C and ¹⁶O (both N = 8)

Isotopes have nearly identical chemical properties (the chemistry is set by Z) but different nuclear properties such as mass and stability; some isotopes are radioactive while others are stable. Isobars and isotones are not chemically related.

Isotopes: same Z, different A. Isobars: same A, different Z. Isotones: same N. Only isotopes share chemical behaviour.

Q4. Why are nuclei with too many protons unstable, and why is the ratio of neutrons to protons important?

Because there is no traceable... protons repel one another electrically, and the strong nuclear force must overcome that repulsion. The strong force acts only over a very short range (about 1 fm), whereas the Coulomb repulsion acts across the whole nucleus, so as Z grows the repulsion grows faster and more neutrons are needed to provide additional binding without adding charge. Nuclei with too many protons (or too few) are unstable and decay; light stable nuclei have N ≈ Z, while heavy stable nuclei have N/Z up to about 1.5, and beyond Z = 83 no nucleus is stable at all.

Electrical repulsion between protons accumulates with Z while the short-range strong force does not; extra neutrons stabilise heavy nuclei, and beyond Z = 83 no number of neutrons suffices.

Exercise 24.2 — Nuclear size and density

Q5. State the relation for the radius of a nucleus and show that nuclear density is independent of the mass number.

R = R₀A^(1/3), R₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m = 1.2 fm Volume: V = (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)πR₀³A ∝ A Density: ρ = mass/volume ≈ (A × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷)/((4/3)πR₀³A) ∝ A/A = constant ρ ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³

The A cancels because volume grows in proportion to the number of nucleons. This constant density is the same for all nuclei and is about 10¹⁴ times the density of water — a neutron star is essentially a giant nucleus held by gravity.

R = R₀A^(1/3); since V ∝ A, ρ ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ for every nucleus.

Q6. Find the approximate radius of a nucleus with A = 64.

R = R₀A^(1/3) = 1.2 × (64)^(1/3) = 1.2 × 4 = 4.8 fm = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ m

R ≈ 4.8 fm (4.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ m)

Q7. Compare the size of a nucleus with the size of an atom.

Atomic radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m; nuclear radius ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m. Ratio ≈ 10⁴ to 10⁵

If the nucleus were 1 cm in radius, the atom would be 100 m to 1 km across. Matter is therefore almost entirely empty space, with virtually all the mass concentrated in the tiny nucleus.

The nucleus is about 10⁴–10⁵ times smaller than the atom; nearly all the mass sits in that tiny volume.

Exercise 24.3 — Mass defect and binding energy

Q8. Define mass defect and binding energy, and derive the relation between them.

Mass defect (Δm): the difference between the total mass of the separated nucleons and the mass of the assembled nucleus:

Δm = [Z m_p + (A − Z)m_n] − M_nucleus

Binding energy (BE): the energy released when the nucleons assemble into the nucleus, equivalently the energy needed to pull the nucleus apart into its separate nucleons. By Einstein's mass–energy relation:

BE = Δm c² Using 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c² = 9 × 10¹⁶ m² s⁻²: 1 u ≡ 931.5 MeV/c² ⇒ BE (MeV) = Δm (u) × 931.5

The mass defect appears because the bound state is lower in energy: the released energy carries away the missing mass.

Δm = Σ(m nucleons) − M_nucleus; BE = Δmc² = Δm × 931.5 MeV per u — the energy that holds the nucleus together.

Q9. Calculate the binding energy and the binding energy per nucleon of helium-4. (m_p = 1.007276 u, m_n = 1.008665 u, M_He = 4.001506 u)

Δm = [2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665)] − 4.001506 = (2.014552 + 2.017330) − 4.001506 = 4.031882 − 4.001506 = 0.030376 u BE = 0.030376 × 931.5 = 28.30 MeV BE/A = 28.30/4 = 7.07 MeV per nucleon

BE = 28.3 MeV; BE/A ≈ 7.1 MeV per nucleon — unusually high for so light a nucleus, which is why helium-4 is exceptionally stable

Q10. The mass of a ¹²C nucleus is 12.000000 u. Find its binding energy per nucleon. (m_p = 1.007276 u, m_n = 1.008665 u)

Δm = [6(1.007276) + 6(1.008665)] − 12.000000 = 6(1.007276 + 1.008665) − 12 = 6(2.015941) − 12 = 12.095646 − 12 = 0.095646 u BE = 0.095646 × 931.5 = 89.1 MeV BE/A = 89.1/12 = 7.43 MeV per nucleon

BE = 89.1 MeV; BE/A ≈ 7.4 MeV per nucleon

Q11. Why is the mass of a nucleus less than the sum of the masses of its constituents?

Because energy is released when the nucleons bind together, and that released energy carries away an equivalent mass according to E = mc². The bound nucleus is in a lower energy state than the separated nucleons, so it has a correspondingly lower mass. The "missing" mass is exactly the binding energy divided by c². This is a direct consequence of mass–energy equivalence: mass is not conserved separately but as part of the total energy.

The binding energy released on formation removes an equivalent mass Δm = BE/c², so the bound nucleus is lighter than its separated nucleons.

Q12. A nucleus has a mass defect of 0.2 u. How much energy is released when it forms?

E = 0.2 × 931.5 = 186.3 MeV

≈ 186 MeV released (about 3 × 10⁻¹¹ J) — a typical nuclear-scale energy, millions of times a chemical bond energy

Exercise 24.4 — The binding-energy-per-nucleon curve

Q13. Describe the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve and state its significance.

Plotting BE/A against mass number A gives a curve that:

  • rises steeply for light nuclei, with sharp peaks at the very lightest (helium-4 at 7.1 MeV, and the "magic" nuclei);
  • flattens to a broad maximum of about 8.8 MeV per nucleon around A ≈ 56 (iron-56 is the most tightly bound);
  • declines slowly to about 7.6 MeV per nucleon for uranium (A ≈ 238).

The height of the curve measures nuclear stability: the higher BE/A, the more energy is needed to break the nucleus apart, so the more stable it is. Because iron sits at the peak, energy can be released either by splitting very heavy nuclei (fission moves them toward the peak) or by joining very light ones (fusion also moves them toward the peak). Nuclei near iron are the endpoints of both processes.

BE/A rises to ~8.8 MeV at A ≈ 56 and then falls slowly; the peak marks maximum stability and explains why both fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light nuclei release energy.

Q14. Use the binding-energy curve to explain why fission of uranium releases energy.

Uranium-238 has BE/A ≈ 7.6 MeV, while the fission fragments (masses around A ≈ 95 and 140) have BE/A ≈ 8.5 MeV. Each nucleon therefore gains about 0.9 MeV of binding energy in going from the parent to the fragments, and that difference is released:

Energy released ≈ 236 × 0.9 ≈ 200 MeV per fission

The fragments are more tightly bound, so the products have slightly less mass than the parent; the missing mass appears as the kinetic energy of the fragments and as neutrons and gamma rays. This is why a kilogram of uranium releases about 8 × 10¹³ J, thousands of times more than burning a kilogram of coal.

Fragments have higher BE/A than U-238, so the 0.9 MeV per nucleon difference is released as ~200 MeV per fission, with a corresponding mass loss.

Q15. Use the binding-energy curve to explain why fusion of light nuclei releases energy.

Light nuclei such as deuterium and tritium have low BE/A (about 1.1 MeV for deuterium, 2.8 MeV for tritium), while helium-4 has 7.1 MeV. Fusing them into helium-4 therefore raises the binding energy per nucleon by several MeV, and the difference is released:

²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV

The product is far more tightly bound than the reactants, so energy is released and the total mass falls. The same process, on a much larger scale, powers the Sun: four protons fuse into one helium-4 nucleus with a net release of about 26.7 MeV, which is why deuterium–tritium fusion is the target of terrestrial fusion reactors.

Light nuclei have low BE/A and helium-4 has high BE/A, so fusing them raises the binding per nucleon and releases the difference — 17.6 MeV for D–T fusion.

Exercise 24.5 — Radioactivity and the three radiations

Q16. Define radioactivity and state the properties of alpha, beta and gamma radiation.

Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of particles or electromagnetic radiation from unstable nuclei, which transform into more stable nuclei. It is random, independent of temperature, pressure and chemical state, and cannot be switched on or off.

α = helium nucleus (heavy, +2e, strongly ionising, weakly penetrating); β = electron (−e, moderately penetrating); γ = photon (neutral, most penetrating, least ionising).

Q17. Write balanced equations for the alpha decay of ²²⁶Ra and the beta decay of ¹⁴C.

Alpha decay of radium-226: ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He Check: 226 = 222 + 4 ✓ and 88 = 86 + 2 ✓ Beta decay of carbon-14: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ Check: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓ and 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓ (the antineutrino ν̄ carries away momentum and energy)

In alpha decay both A and Z fall by 4 and 2; in beta-minus decay A is unchanged while Z rises by 1 because a neutron converts into a proton.

²²⁶Ra → ²²²Rn + ⁴He and ¹⁴C → ¹⁴N + e⁻ + ν̄, with mass number and charge conserved in both.

Q18. Why is there a continuous beta spectrum, and what particle was postulated to explain it?

If beta decay emitted only an electron and the recoiling nucleus, energy and momentum conservation would force the electron to have a single, fixed energy. In fact beta electrons are observed with a continuous range of energies up to a maximum. Wolfgang Pauli therefore postulated a nearly massless, neutral particle — the antineutrino — emitted alongside the electron, carrying away the balance of the energy and momentum. The neutrino was later detected, confirming the prediction.

Because energy and momentum must be shared with a third particle; Pauli's antineutrino makes the electron energy continuous and was later detected.

Q19. State the conservation laws that must hold in every nuclear reaction.

  1. Conservation of charge (Z): the total atomic number before equals that after.
  2. Conservation of nucleon number (A): the total mass number is unchanged.
  3. Conservation of energy–mass: total relativistic energy (including rest mass) is conserved; kinetic energy is released if the products are more tightly bound.
  4. Conservation of linear momentum: the total momentum of the products equals that of the parent.
  5. Conservation of angular momentum and of lepton number in weak decays.

Charge (Z), nucleon number (A), energy–mass and momentum must balance in every nuclear equation; lepton number too in beta decay.

Q20. Why is radioactivity unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical combination?

Because radioactive decay is a nuclear process, and the energy involved is of order MeV per nucleus — thousands of times the thermal and chemical energies available at ordinary temperatures (about 0.025 eV). Temperature and chemical bonding affect only the outer electrons, which barely influence the nucleus. The decay rate is therefore set by the internal structure of the nucleus alone and is not changed by its environment; this is why the half-lives used in radiometric dating are reliable.

Decay energies are MeV-scale while thermal and chemical energies are eV-scale, so the environment cannot affect the nucleus — the rate is intrinsic and constant.

Exercise 24.6 — The decay law and half-life

Q21. Derive or state the radioactive decay law and the relation between the decay constant and half-life.

The rate of decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present:

dN/dt = −λN (λ = decay constant, s⁻¹) Integrating from N₀ at t = 0: N = N₀ e^(−λt) Half-life T½: the time for half the nuclei to decay, N = N₀/2: N₀/2 = N₀ e^(−λT½) ln(1/2) = −λT½ T½ = ln 2/λ = 0.693/λ Mean life τ = 1/λ = T½/0.693 = 1.44 T½

The activity A = −dN/dt = λN also decays exponentially: A = A₀e^(−λt). Activity is measured in becquerel (1 Bq = 1 decay s⁻¹) or curie (1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq).

N = N₀e^(−λt); T½ = 0.693/λ; activity A = λN decays the same way.

Q22. A sample contains 8 g of a radioactive isotope of half-life 10 days. How much remains after 30 days?

Number of half-lives: n = 30/10 = 3 N = 8 × (1/2)³ = 8/8 = 1 g Or with the exponential law: λ = 0.693/10 = 0.0693 day⁻¹ N = 8 e^(−0.0693 × 30) = 8 e^(−2.079) = 8 × 0.125 = 1 g ✓

1.0 g remains after 30 days; 7 g has decayed

Q23. The half-life of carbon-14 is 5730 years. Find its decay constant and the fraction remaining after 11460 years.

λ = 0.693/T½ = 0.693/5730 = 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ yr⁻¹ t/T½ = 11460/5730 = 2 half-lives Fraction remaining = (1/2)² = 1/4 = 25%

λ = 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ yr⁻¹; one quarter (25%) remains after 11 460 years — the basis of radiocarbon dating

Q24. A radioactive sample has an activity of 8000 Bq. After 12 hours it is 1000 Bq. Find the half-life.

A = A₀(1/2)^(t/T½) 1000 = 8000(1/2)^(12/T½) 1/8 = (1/2)^(12/T½) ⇒ 12/T½ = 3 ⇒ T½ = 4 hours

T½ = 4 hours (three half-lives in 12 h)

Q25. A sample of 1 g of a substance of molar mass 200 g mol⁻¹ has a half-life of 5 years. Find the number of atoms initially and the initial activity.

N₀ = (1/200) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²¹ atoms λ = 0.693/(5 × 3.15 × 10⁷ s) = 0.693/1.577 × 10⁸ = 4.40 × 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹ A₀ = λN₀ = 4.40 × 10⁻⁹ × 3.01 × 10²¹ = 1.32 × 10¹³ Bq

N₀ ≈ 3.0 × 10²¹ atoms; A₀ ≈ 1.3 × 10¹³ Bq (about 360 Ci) — a very active source

Q26. Why can the age of a substance be found from its radioactivity?

Because the decay law N = N₀e^(−λt) links the present amount to the initial amount and the elapsed time, and λ is known for each isotope. If the initial amount can be inferred — for example, living things maintain a constant carbon-14 level while alive and it decays only after death — then measuring the present activity gives t. The method works over a range of a few half-lives: carbon-14 for hundreds to tens of thousands of years, uranium–lead for billions of years, and potassium–argon for geological timescales.

The decay law fixes the ratio N/N₀ for a given t, and λ is known, so measuring the present amount (or activity) gives the age — reliable over a few half-lives.

Exercise 24.7 — Nuclear fission and fusion

Q27. Define nuclear fission and write a typical equation. What is a chain reaction?

Fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter nuclei of comparable mass, accompanied by the release of several neutrons and about 200 MeV of energy.

²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n + ~200 MeV

A chain reaction occurs when the neutrons released by one fission trigger further fissions. If on average more than one of these neutrons causes another fission, the reaction grows (supercritical); if exactly one, it is steady (critical, as in a power reactor); if less than one, it dies out (subcritical).

Fission splits a heavy nucleus into two mid-mass fragments plus neutrons and ~200 MeV; a chain reaction is self-sustaining when the released neutrons trigger further fissions.

Q28. Define nuclear fusion and explain why it requires extremely high temperature.

Fusion is the combining of two light nuclei into a heavier, more tightly bound nucleus with the release of energy:

²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + 17.6 MeV (the Sun: 4 ¹₁H → ⁴₂He + 2e⁺ + 2ν + 26.7 MeV)

The nuclei are positively charged, so they repel each other strongly. To bring them within the ~1 fm range of the strong force they must approach with enough kinetic energy to overcome the Coulomb barrier, which requires temperatures of order 10⁸ K (about 10 keV of thermal energy). At those temperatures matter is a plasma. Fusion also requires sufficient density and confinement time (the Lawson criterion) for the reactions to occur faster than the plasma escapes.

Fusion joins light nuclei and releases energy; it needs ~10⁸ K to overcome the Coulomb barrier, together with adequate density and confinement.

Q30. How much energy is released when 1 kg of U-235 undergoes fission? Compare it with burning 1 kg of coal.

Number of U-235 nuclei in 1 kg: N = (1000/235) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.56 × 10²⁴ Energy released: E = 2.56 × 10²⁴ × 200 MeV = 2.56 × 10²⁴ × 200 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ J = 8.2 × 10¹³ J Coal (≈ 3 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹) gives 3 × 10⁷ J per kg. Ratio ≈ 8.2 × 10¹³/3 × 10⁷ ≈ 2.7 × 10⁶

About 8 × 10¹³ J from 1 kg of U-235, some 2.7 million times the energy from 1 kg of coal — the reason nuclear fuel is so compact.

Exercise 24.8 — Discovery of the nucleus, packing fraction, creation and annihilation

Q31. Describe Rutherford's alpha-scattering experiment and state its conclusions.

A beam of alpha particles from a radioactive source was directed at a thin gold foil (about 10⁻⁷ m thick), and the scattered alphas were detected by a zinc-sulphide screen that flashed on impact. Observations:

  • Most alphas passed almost straight through, with little or no deflection.
  • A small fraction (about 1 in 8000) were deflected through more than 90°, some almost straight back.

Conclusions: the atom is mostly empty space (hence the undeflected majority), but its positive charge and almost all its mass are concentrated in a tiny central region — the nucleus, about 10⁻¹⁵ m across. Only a close approach to this concentrated charge could produce the large-angle repulsion observed.

Most alphas pass through but a few rebound, so the atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, massive, positively charged nucleus at its centre.

Q32. Define packing fraction and explain what it indicates.

The packing fraction is the mass defect per nucleon:

Packing fraction = Δm/A = (M − A)/A (using M in atomic mass units for the nuclide and A its mass number)

It measures how tightly the nucleons are packed. A negative packing fraction means the nucleus is more tightly bound than the reference (A in u) and hence stable; a positive value indicates a loosely bound, unstable nucleus. Plotting packing fraction against A shows a minimum near the most stable nuclei (around iron) and rising values for both very light and very heavy nuclei — the same information as the binding-energy curve, expressed per nucleon rather than as an energy.

Packing fraction = Δm/A; negative and small near the middle of the periodic table (stable), positive at the extremes (unstable).

Q33. What is meant by creation and annihilation of particles? Give one example of each.

Creation (pair production): a photon of sufficient energy, in the field of a nucleus, converts into a particle–antiparticle pair. The threshold energy is the total rest energy of the pair:

γ → e⁻ + e⁺ requires E ≥ 2m_e c² = 1.02 MeV (momentum is conserved by the recoiling nucleus)

Annihilation: a particle and its antiparticle combine and disappear, their total mass–energy appearing as photons:

e⁻ + e⁺ → 2γ (each photon 0.511 MeV when the pair is at rest)

Two photons are produced rather than one, so that momentum can be conserved. Both processes conserve charge, energy, momentum and lepton number, and both are routinely observed in PET scanners and in cosmic-ray showers.

Creation: a photon above 1.02 MeV becomes an electron–positron pair. Annihilation: an electron and positron vanish into two 0.511 MeV photons. Both conserve charge, energy, momentum and lepton number.

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Nuclear questions combine three toolkits: mass–energy conversion (E = Δm c², 1 u = 931 MeV), decay/activity exponentials, and conservation bookkeeping in reactions. The eight shapes below cover all three.

Q34. [Multi-step numerical] In the reaction ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n, the masses are 235.0439 u, 1.0087 u, 140.9144 u and 91.9262 u. Find the energy released in MeV, and the energy per nucleon of uranium.

Mass before = 235.0439 + 1.0087 = 236.0526 u Mass after = 140.9144 + 91.9262 + 3(1.0087) = 235.8667 u Δm = 236.0526 − 235.8667 = 0.1859 u E = 0.1859 × 931.5 = 173 MeV Per nucleon of uranium: 173/236 = 0.73 MeV per nucleon (compare fusion: about 6 MeV per nucleon — see the estimation item below)

About 3 × 10⁻¹¹ J per fission, which sounds minute but is enormous per atom: 1 kg of ²³⁵U contains 2.6 × 10²⁴ nuclei and so releases about 8 × 10¹³ J — the energy of 20 kilotonnes of TNT. Note that the reaction conserves nucleon number (236 = 141 + 92 + 3) and charge (92 = 56 + 36), which are the two checks that catch a mis-typed equation.

Δm = 0.1859 u → E = 173 MeV per fission (0.73 MeV per nucleon).

Q35. [Derivation] Derive the decay law N = N₀e^(−λt) from the statement that the decay rate is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei.

Rate law: dN/dt = −λN Separate and integrate from N₀ at t = 0 to N at time t: ∫ dN/N = −λ ∫ dt ln N − ln N₀ = −λt N = N₀ e^(−λt) Activity: A = −dN/dt = λN = λN₀e^(−λt) = A₀e^(−λt) Half-life: N₀/2 = N₀e^(−λT) ⇒ T = ln2/λ = 0.693/λ Mean life: τ = 1/λ = T/ln2 = 1.44T

The exponential is not an extra assumption: it is the unique solution of “the more nuclei there are, the more decay per second”, which is why every radionuclide follows the same curve with its own λ. The three constants λ, T and τ carry the same information, and the 1.44 conversion factor appears in nearly every numerical question.

Integrating dN/dt = −λN gives N = N₀e^(−λt), hence A = A₀e^(−λt), T = 0.693/λ and τ = 1.44T.

Q36. [Graph reading] A decay curve passes through 8.0 × 10¹⁰ nuclei at t = 0 and 1.0 × 10¹⁰ at t = 90 days. Find the half-life, the decay constant and the activity at t = 0.

The count falls from 8.0 to 1.0 × 10¹⁰, a factor of 8 = 2³, in 90 days: three half-lives in 90 days ⇒ T = 30 days λ = 0.693/T = 0.693/30 = 0.0231 day⁻¹ = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹ A₀ = λN₀ = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷ × 8.0 × 10¹⁰ = 2.2 × 10⁴ Bq

Reading a decay curve is a matter of spotting powers of two — every drop to half takes the same time, which is the defining property of exponential decay. Converting λ to s⁻¹ is compulsory before quoting activity in becquerels, and the conversion is where most marks are lost.

T = 30 days; λ = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹; A₀ = 2.2 × 10⁴ Bq.

Q37. [Combined concept] A 1.0 g sample of ²²⁶Ra (half-life 1600 years, molar mass 226 g mol⁻¹) — find the number of atoms, the activity, and the fraction remaining after 4800 years.

N = (1.0/226) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.66 × 10²¹ atoms λ = 0.693/(1600 × 3.15 × 10⁷ s) = 0.693/5.05 × 10¹⁰ = 1.37 × 10⁻¹¹ s⁻¹ A = λN = 1.37 × 10⁻¹¹ × 2.66 × 10²¹ = 3.6 × 10¹⁰ Bq 4800 years = 3 half-lives ⇒ remaining fraction = (½)³ = 1/8 = 12.5%

Note that the activity of 1 g of radium is 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq — the historical definition of the curie, chosen because this sample was the standard source. The three-step structure (atoms → λ → activity) is the standard NEB numerical, and every step costs a mark if omitted.

N = 2.7 × 10²¹ atoms; A = 3.6 × 10¹⁰ Bq; 12.5% remains after 4800 years.

Q38. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): The mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its constituent nucleons. Reason (R): The missing mass appears as binding energy, which must be supplied to separate the nucleons.

Both statements are true and R explains A.

Mass defect: Δm = [Zm_p + (A − Z)m_n] − M_nucleus Binding energy: BE = Δm c² = Δm × 931.5 MeV (with Δm in u) Example (⁴He): Δm = 2(1.00728) + 2(1.00867) − 4.00151 = 0.03037 u BE = 0.03037 × 931.5 = 28.3 MeV → 7.1 MeV per nucleon

The per-nucleon value is the useful quantity: it peaks near ⁵⁶Fe at about 8.8 MeV, rises steeply for light nuclei and falls slowly for heavy ones. That single curve explains both fission (heavy nuclei split towards the peak) and fusion (light nuclei combine towards it) — the two reactions that release energy are the two directions of approach to the same maximum.

Both true, R explains A: Δm c² is the binding energy, and it peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon near iron.

Q40. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the energy released per nucleon in the fusion of four protons into helium, and compare it with fission. Then state why fusion is hard to achieve on Earth.

4p → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν Mass of 4 protons: 4 × 1.00728 = 4.02912 u Mass of ⁴He: 4.00151 u Δm ≈ 0.0276 u (neglecting the positron masses, which largely cancel the electron binding) E = 0.0276 × 931.5 ≈ 25.7 MeV for 4 nucleons ⇒ about 6.4 MeV per nucleon Compare fission: 173/236 ≈ 0.73 MeV per nucleon — fusion yields about 9× more.

Why it is hard: the protons must approach within about 10⁻¹⁵ m, where the Coulomb barrier is ~1 MeV while thermal energies at 10⁷ K are only ~1 keV. Only the high-energy tail of the Maxwell distribution can tunnel through, which is why the Sun burns slowly (it takes ~10⁹ years for a given proton) and why a terrestrial reactor needs 10⁸ K and magnetic or inertial confinement.

About 26 MeV per helium nucleus, 6.4 MeV per nucleon — nine times fission's yield, but the Coulomb barrier demands 10⁸ K.

Q41. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “In beta decay the atomic number stays the same.”
(ii) “Half-life is the time for half the atoms of a sample to disappear completely.”
(iii) “Alpha particles penetrate further than beta particles because they are heavier.”
(iv) “Nuclear force is a form of gravitational attraction between nucleons.”

(i) FALSE — β⁻ decay converts a neutron to a proton, so Z increases by 1 (and A is unchanged). For β⁺ decay Z decreases by 1. (ii) FALSE — the atoms do not disappear, they TRANSMUTE into a different nuclide. Half-life is the time for half the parent nuclei to decay; the daughter nuclei remain. (iii) FALSE — alpha particles are the LEAST penetrating (stopped by paper) because they are heavy and doubly charged, so they ionise densely and lose energy fast. Beta penetrates a few mm of aluminium, gamma several cm of lead. (iv) FALSE — the nuclear force is a short-range strong interaction (attractive at ~1 fm, repulsive below ~0.5 fm), not gravity. It is roughly 10³⁸ times stronger than gravity at nucleon separations.

Statement (ii) is the one that most often loses a mark: “disappear” versus “decay into something else” is a real physical distinction, and the daughter products are what make dating methods work.

(i) β⁻ raises Z by 1; (ii) nuclei transmute, they do not vanish; (iii) alpha is least penetrating; (iv) the nuclear force is the short-range strong force.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Define mass defect.

The difference between the total mass of the separated nucleons and the mass of the assembled nucleus: Δm = [Zmp + (A−Z)mn] − M. It corresponds to the binding energy through BE = Δmc².

C2. Define binding energy.

The energy released when nucleons combine to form a nucleus, equivalently the energy required to separate the nucleus completely into its constituent nucleons: BE = Δmc², usually quoted in MeV.

C3. Why is binding energy per nucleon a measure of stability?

Because it is the average energy needed to remove one nucleon from the nucleus; the larger it is, the more tightly each nucleon is held and the harder the nucleus is to break apart. The curve peaks near iron-56 at 8.8 MeV.

C4. Define half-life.

The time in which half the nuclei in a radioactive sample decay, related to the decay constant by T½ = 0.693/λ. It is a constant for each isotope and independent of the sample size.

C5. Define the decay constant.

The probability per unit time that a given nucleus will decay: λ = −(1/N)dN/dt, in s⁻¹. Its reciprocal is the mean life of the nucleus.

C6. State the properties of alpha particles.

They are helium-4 nuclei (2 protons + 2 neutrons, charge +2e, mass 4 u), travel at about 5% of c, are strongly ionising, and are stopped by a few centimetres of air or a sheet of paper. They are deflected slightly toward the negative plate in an electric field.

C7. Why are gamma rays not deflected by electric or magnetic fields?

Because they are neutral photons with no electric charge, so neither the electric force qE nor the magnetic force qv × B acts on them. They travel in straight lines and are absorbed rather than deflected.

C8. Write the radioactive decay law.

N = N₀e^(−λt), where N₀ is the initial number of nuclei, λ the decay constant and t the elapsed time. The activity follows the same exponential: A = A₀e^(−λt).

C9. What are isotopes? Give one pair.

Nuclei of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers, e.g. ¹²C and ¹⁴C, or ¹H, ²H and ³H. They have nearly identical chemistry but different masses and nuclear stability.

C10. Why does alpha decay reduce the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4?

Because the emitted alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus carrying away 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Charge and nucleon number are conserved, so the daughter has Z − 2 and A − 4.

C11. Why does beta-minus decay increase the atomic number by 1 without changing the mass number?

Because a neutron inside the nucleus converts into a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄. The proton number rises by 1, but the total number of nucleons is unchanged, so A stays the same.

C12. State the relation between nuclear radius and mass number.

R = R₀A^(1/3) with R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm. Since the volume is then proportional to A, all nuclei have approximately the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.

C13. What is a chain reaction?

A self-sustaining series of fissions in which the neutrons released by one fission trigger further fissions. It is critical when on average exactly one neutron per fission causes another, supercritical when more, and subcritical when fewer.

C14. Why does fusion need a very high temperature?

Because the fusing nuclei are positively charged and repel each other; they must approach within about 1 fm, against the Coulomb barrier, which requires kinetic energies of order 10 keV — corresponding to temperatures near 10⁸ K, at which matter is a plasma.

C15. What is the significance of iron-56 in nuclear physics?

It has the highest binding energy per nucleon (about 8.8 MeV), so it is the most stable nucleus. Energy can be released by moving toward it from either side — by fission of heavier nuclei or fusion of lighter ones — which is why iron is the end point of stellar nuclear burning.

C16. Why is radioactivity said to be a random process?

Because it is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay; only the probability per unit time (λ) is fixed. For a large number of nuclei the statistical decay law N = N₀e^(−λt) emerges, exactly like the mortality statistics of a large population.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Explain the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve and use it to account for energy release in both fission and fusion.

The curve rises steeply from hydrogen, peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon near A = 56 (iron), and then declines slowly to about 7.6 MeV at uranium. The height measures the average energy binding each nucleon, so the peak is the region of maximum stability.

Fission: a heavy nucleus such as U-235 (7.6 MeV per nucleon) splits into fragments around A ≈ 95 and 140 (about 8.5 MeV per nucleon). The 0.9 MeV per nucleon difference is released — about 200 MeV per fission — and the fragments are lighter than the parent by Δm = E/c².

Fusion: light nuclei such as deuterium (1.1 MeV per nucleon) and tritium (2.8 MeV per nucleon) combine into helium-4 (7.1 MeV per nucleon), releasing 17.6 MeV. Here the gain per nucleon is even larger than in fission, which is why fusion yields more energy per kilogram of fuel.

In both cases the products lie closer to the iron peak than the reactants, so the total binding energy increases and the difference is released as kinetic energy and radiation.

The curve peaks at Fe-56; moving toward the peak from either side (fission of heavy, fusion of light) increases binding and releases the difference — ~200 MeV per fission, 17.6 MeV per D–T fusion.

L2. [5] A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 20 minutes. What fraction of a sample remains after 1 hour? What fraction has decayed?

n = t/T½ = 60/20 = 3 half-lives Remaining fraction = (1/2)³ = 1/8 = 12.5% Decayed fraction = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8 = 87.5% Check with the exponential law: λ = 0.693/20 = 3.47 × 10⁻² min⁻¹ N/N₀ = e^(−3.47 × 10⁻² × 60) = e^(−2.079) = 0.125 ✓

1/8 (12.5%) remains and 7/8 (87.5%) has decayed after one hour — three half-lives.

L3. [5] Calculate the energy released in the fusion reaction ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n, given the masses: ²H = 2.014102 u, ³H = 3.016049 u, ⁴He = 4.002603 u, n = 1.008665 u.

Mass of reactants = 2.014102 + 3.016049 = 5.030151 u Mass of products = 4.002603 + 1.008665 = 5.011268 u Mass defect = 5.030151 − 5.011268 = 0.018883 u E = 0.018883 × 931.5 = 17.6 MeV

17.6 MeV per fusion event (≈ 2.8 × 10⁻¹² J). Combined with Avogadro-scale fuel quantities, this is why fusion is such an attractive energy source.

L4. [4] A sample's activity falls from 6400 Bq to 400 Bq in 24 days. Find the half-life and the decay constant.

A/A₀ = 400/6400 = 1/16 = (1/2)⁴ ⇒ 4 half-lives in 24 days ⇒ T½ = 24/4 = 6 days λ = 0.693/T½ = 0.693/6 = 0.1155 day⁻¹ = 0.1155/86400 = 1.34 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 24

1. Using the wrong conversion factor. 1 u = 931.5 MeV, not 931.5 × 10⁶ eV and not 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹. Write "× 931.5 MeV" explicitly in the working line.

2. Forgetting the neutrons in Δm. For a nucleus with A nucleons, there are Z protons and (A − Z) neutrons. Using Z neutrons instead of (A − Z) ruins the calculation.

3. Mixing up half-life and mean life. T½ = 0.693/λ, while τ = 1/λ = 1.44T½. Using T½ = 1/λ makes every answer 44% too large.

4. Unbalanced nuclear equations. Always check that A and Z balance separately before writing the daughter nuclide. A single unbalanced line loses the mark even if the rest is right.

5. Confusing fission and fusion. Fission splits heavy nuclei (U, Pu) and needs a neutron; fusion joins light nuclei (H isotopes) and needs 10⁸ K. They move toward the iron peak from opposite directions.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. In binding-energy questions, write the mass-defect line with every proton and neutron shown, then convert once. In decay questions, count half-lives first ("n = t/T½") and only use the exponential if n is not a simple number — it saves time and arithmetic errors.
