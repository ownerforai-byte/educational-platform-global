Unit 6 — Circular Motion

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  6 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Circular motion questions are formula-driven but the marks live in three places: the direction of the centripetal force, the difference between the top and bottom of a vertical circle, and the condition for skidding on a banked road. All three are worked out below.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — define angular velocity and give its unit; derive v = rω; why is centripetal force called a centre-seeking force; condition for maximum safe speed on an unbanked curve.
  • 4 marks — angular kinematics (rev → rad s⁻¹ → v); centripetal acceleration and the friction needed; banking angle numerical.
  • 5–6 marks — conical pendulum derivation; motion in a vertical circle with the minimum speed at the top and the tension at the bottom.

Rule that keeps losing students marks: the direction. Centripetal acceleration is always toward the centre, tangential acceleration acts along the path, and the radial and tangential directions must be kept separate in every answer.

Exercise 6.1 — Angular displacement, velocity and acceleration

Q1. Define angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration, with units and dimensions.

QuantityDefinitionUnitDimensions
Angular displacement θAngle swept by the radius vector, Δθraddimensionless [M⁰L⁰T⁰]
Angular velocity ωRate of change of angular displacement, dθ/dtrad s⁻¹[T⁻¹]
Angular acceleration αRate of change of angular velocity, dω/dtrad s⁻²[T⁻²]

θ (rad), ω = dθ/dt (rad s⁻¹, [T⁻¹]), α = dω/dt (rad s⁻², [T⁻²]); all are pseudo-vectors along the axis of rotation by the right-hand rule.

Q2. A wheel rotates at 300 revolutions per minute. Express its angular velocity in rad s⁻¹ and find the linear speed of a point 20 cm from the axis.

ω = 300 rpm = 300 × 2π/60 rad s⁻¹ = 31.4 rad s⁻¹ v = rω = 0.20 × 31.4 = 6.28 m s⁻¹

ω = 31.4 rad s⁻¹; v = 6.28 m s⁻¹

Q3. A wheel starting from rest reaches 20 rad s⁻¹ in 5 s with uniform angular acceleration. Find α, the angular displacement and the number of revolutions.

α = (ω − ω₀)/t = (20 − 0)/5 = 4 rad s⁻² θ = ω₀t + ½αt² = 0 + ½(4)(25) = 50 rad revolutions = 50/(2π) = 7.96 rev

α = 4 rad s⁻²; θ = 50 rad ≈ 8 revolutions

Q4. Write down the three equations of angular motion and state their linear counterparts.

AngularLinear
ω = ω₀ + αtv = u + at

The correspondence is exact under s = rθ, v = rω, a = rα, so any linear-motion problem can be converted to an angular one by dividing out the radius.

Same three equations with (θ, ω, α) replacing (s, v, a); valid for uniform angular acceleration.

Exercise 6.2 — Relation between angular and linear quantities

Q5. Derive the relation v = rω and state the relation between linear and angular acceleration for circular motion.

Arc length s = rθ, with θ in radians. Differentiate with respect to time (r constant): ds/dt = r dθ/dt ⇒ v = rω Differentiating v = rω (speed along the path): dv/dt = r dω/dt ⇒ a_t = rα (tangential acceleration)

In addition, the direction of v turns, giving a radial (centripetal) acceleration a_r = v²/r = rω². The total acceleration is the vector sum of the two components, of magnitude √(a_t² + a_r²).

v = rω, tangential a = rα, radial a = v²/r = rω²; the resultant is √(a_t² + a_r²) = r√(α² + ω⁴).

Q6. Two points on a rotating disc are at 5 cm and 15 cm from the axis. Compare their angular velocities, linear speeds and centripetal accelerations.

Angular velocity: the same for a rigid body ⇒ ω₁ = ω₂ (ratio 1 : 1) Linear speed: v = rω ⇒ v₁ : v₂ = 5 : 15 = 1 : 3 Centripetal acceleration: a = rω² ⇒ a₁ : a₂ = 5 : 15 = 1 : 3

ω equal; v and a both in the ratio 1 : 3 — each of those is proportional to the radius.

Q7. Explain the difference between tangential acceleration and centripetal acceleration.

Tangential acceleration (a_t = rα) is along the tangent: it changes the magnitude of the velocity (the speed). It is zero for uniform circular motion.

Centripetal acceleration (a_r = v²/r) is along the radius toward the centre: it changes the direction of the velocity. It is never zero for circular motion, even at constant speed.

Tangential changes speed, centripetal changes direction; uniform circular motion has a_t = 0 but a_r = v²/r ≠ 0.

Exercise 6.3 — Centripetal acceleration and centripetal force

Q8. Define centripetal force and derive F = mv²/r. Why is the work done by it zero?

Definition: the net force directed toward the centre of a circular path, needed to keep a body moving in that circle; it is not a new kind of force but is provided by tension, friction, gravity or a normal reaction.

For uniform circular motion the acceleration is a = v²/r toward the centre, so by Newton's second law: F = ma = mv²/r = mrω² = 4π²mr/T²

Work done = F·s = Fs cos90° = 0 because the force is always perpendicular to the displacement (the velocity is tangential). Hence the speed stays constant.

F = mv²/r = mrω² = 4π²mr/T² directed toward the centre; it does zero work because it is perpendicular to the velocity.

Q9. A car of mass 800 kg takes a curve of radius 50 m at 20 m s⁻¹. Find the centripetal force and the minimum coefficient of friction needed.

F = mv²/r = 800(400)/50 = 6400 N This must be supplied by static friction: μmg = mv²/r ⇒ μ = v²/(rg) = 400/(50 × 9.8) = 0.816

F = 6400 N; μ_min ≈ 0.82 — a very high value, which is why such a curve would be banked in practice

Q10. Find the maximum safe speed of a car on an unbanked curve of radius 100 m if μ = 0.5.

Friction provides the centripetal force: μmg = mv²/r v = √(μrg) = √(0.5 × 100 × 9.8) = √490 = 22.1 m s⁻¹

v_max ≈ 22 m s⁻¹ (≈ 80 km h⁻¹); above this the car skids outward

Q11. A stone tied to a string 1 m long is whirled at 2 rev s⁻¹ in a horizontal circle. Find the tension if the mass of the stone is 0.5 kg.

ω = 2 × 2π = 12.57 rad s⁻¹ T = mrω² = 0.5 × 1 × (12.57)² = 0.5 × 158 = 79 N

T ≈ 79 N (gravity neglected in the horizontal-circle approximation; the string in reality sags slightly)

Q12. Explain how a centrifuge separates cream from milk.

In a rotating vessel each particle needs a centripetal force proportional to its mass and to rω². The denser particles (cream/water droplets of higher density, or the heavier sediment) cannot be held on the circular path and move outward to the wall, while the lighter phase is displaced inward. Since a_r = rω² can be thousands of times g, separation that would take hours under gravity happens in minutes.

Denser particles move to the outer wall because the available inward force is insufficient for them at that radius; ω² replaces and multiplies g, so separation is fast.

Exercise 6.4 — Conical pendulum

Q13. A conical pendulum has a string of length 1.0 m making 30° with the vertical. Find its angular speed, period and the tension in the string for a bob of mass 0.2 kg.

Radius of the circle: r = L sinθ = 1.0 × 0.5 = 0.5 m Vertical: T cosθ = mg ⇒ T = mg/cosθ = 0.2(9.8)/0.866 = 2.26 N Horizontal: T sinθ = mrω² ⇒ ω² = g tanθ/(L sinθ) = g/(L cosθ) ω = √(9.8/(1.0 × 0.866)) = √11.32 = 3.36 rad s⁻¹ Period T_p = 2π/ω = 6.28/3.36 = 1.87 s

ω = 3.36 rad s⁻¹, period 1.87 s, tension 2.26 N

Q14. Derive the expression for the time period of a conical pendulum.

For a bob moving in a horizontal circle of radius r = L sinθ: Vertical equilibrium: T cosθ = mg ...(i) Centripetal: T sinθ = mrω² = m(L sinθ)ω² ...(ii) Dividing (ii) by (i): tanθ = (L sinθ)ω²/g ⇒ ω² = g/(L cosθ) ⇒ ω = √(g/(L cosθ)) Period: T_p = 2π/ω = 2π√(L cosθ/g)

T_p = 2π√(L cosθ/g), which reduces to the simple pendulum 2π√(L/g) as θ → 0.

Q15. Why does the period of a conical pendulum decrease as the angle θ increases?

From T_p = 2π√(L cosθ/g), as θ increases cosθ decreases, so the period decreases. Physically the bob circles a larger radius at a larger angle, requiring a larger centripetal force; the horizontal component of the tension grows, so the bob must move faster and complete the circle in less time.

Larger θ ⇒ smaller L cosθ ⇒ shorter period, because a wider cone needs a larger horizontal force and hence a larger ω.

Exercise 6.5 — Motion in a vertical circle

Q16. Find the minimum speed at the highest point and at the lowest point for a body to complete a vertical circle of radius 1.5 m.

At the top, the minimum condition is that gravity alone provides the centripetal force: mg = mv_top²/r ⇒ v_top = √(gr) = √(9.8 × 1.5) = √14.7 = 3.83 m s⁻¹ Energy conservation from bottom to top (height 2r): ½mv_b² = ½mv_t² + mg(2r) v_b² = v_t² + 4gr = gr + 4gr = 5gr v_b = √(5gr) = √(5 × 9.8 × 1.5) = √73.5 = 8.57 m s⁻¹

v_top,min = √(gr) = 3.8 m s⁻¹; v_bottom,min = √(5gr) = 8.6 m s⁻¹

Q17. A 0.5 kg ball is whirled in a vertical circle of radius 1 m at a constant speed of 5 m s⁻¹. Find the tension at the top and at the bottom.

At the top (both T and mg point down, toward the centre): T_top + mg = mv²/r T_top = mv²/r − mg = 0.5(25)/1 − 0.5(9.8) = 12.5 − 4.9 = 7.6 N At the bottom (T up toward the centre, mg down): T_bot − mg = mv²/r T_bot = mv²/r + mg = 12.5 + 4.9 = 17.4 N

T_top = 7.6 N, T_bot = 17.4 N — the string is most likely to break at the bottom

Q18. What happens if the speed at the highest point is less than √(gr)?

Then mg alone exceeds the centripetal force needed, so the body cannot stay on the circular path: the string goes slack (or the body leaves the track) and the motion becomes a projectile inside the circle. It will fall away from the circle before reaching the top.

The string slackens and the body leaves the circular path, moving as a projectile — the condition for completing the loop is v_top ≥ √(gr).

Q19. Show that the difference in tension between the lowest and highest points of a vertical circle is 6mg (for constant speed this is 2mv²/r; for a freely swinging body show 6mg).

For a body freely moving in the vertical circle (energy conserved, not constant speed):

At the bottom: T_b − mg = mv_b²/r At the top: T_t + mg = mv_t²/r Subtract: T_b − T_t = mv_b²/r − mv_t²/r + 2mg Energy: v_b² = v_t² + 4gr ⇒ (v_b² − v_t²)/r = 4g ⇒ T_b − T_t = 4mg + 2mg = 6mg

T_b − T_t = 6mg, the standard result — the tension at the bottom exceeds that at the top by six times the weight.

Exercise 6.6 — Banking of roads

Q20. Derive the expression tanθ = v²/(rg) for the banking angle of a road, and find θ for v = 30 m s⁻¹ and r = 200 m.

On a frictionless banked road the normal reaction N is perpendicular to the road: Vertical: N cosθ = mg Horizontal: N sinθ = mv²/r (centripetal) Dividing: tanθ = v²/(rg) tanθ = 900/(200 × 9.8) = 900/1960 = 0.459 θ = 24.7°

tanθ = v²/(rg); θ ≈ 25° for a 30 m s⁻¹ design speed on a 200 m radius

Q21. Why are roads banked? Give two advantages over an unbanked curve.

Banking tilts the surface so that the normal reaction itself has a horizontal component toward the centre. Advantages:

  • The centripetal force no longer depends entirely on friction, so the curve can be taken safely at higher speed — important on highways and racetracks.
  • Reduced reliance on friction means far less tyre wear and lower risk of skidding in rain or on ice.

The normal reaction supplies part (or all) of the centripetal force, allowing higher safe speeds and reducing the dependence on friction.

Q22. A curve of radius 80 m is banked at 15°. Find the maximum speed for which it is safe with μ = 0.3. (Use the friction-aided result.)

With friction assisting: v_max² = rg(μ + tanθ)/(1 − μ tanθ) = 80(9.8)(0.3 + 0.268)/(1 − 0.3 × 0.268) = 784(0.568)/(1 − 0.0804) = 445/0.9196 = 484 v_max = 22 m s⁻¹

v_max ≈ 22 m s⁻¹ (≈ 79 km h⁻¹); without banking the same friction would allow only √(0.3×80×9.8) = 15.3 m s⁻¹

Q23. What is the optimum (design) speed of a banked curve and why is no friction needed at that speed?

The optimum speed is v₀ = √(rg tanθ). At this speed the horizontal component of the normal reaction equals exactly the required centripetal force, so friction is neither needed nor called upon — the car neither tends to slide up nor down the bank. At lower speeds friction must act up the bank; at higher speeds, down the bank.

v₀ = √(rg tanθ); there the normal reaction alone provides mv²/r, so friction plays no part and tyre wear is minimal.

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Circular motion questions almost always reduce to “which force provides mv²/r?”. Decide that first and the algebra follows. The eight shapes below each begin from that question.

Q24. [Multi-step numerical] An 800 kg car takes a flat circular curve of radius 50 m on a road with μ = 0.6. Find the maximum safe speed and the maximum friction force available.

Friction provides the centripetal force: f_max = μmg = 0.6 × 800 × 9.8 = 4.7 × 10³ N μmg = mv²/r ⇒ v_max = √(μgr) = √(0.6 × 9.8 × 50) = √294 = 17 m s⁻¹ ≈ 62 km h⁻¹

Notice the mass cancels: a truck and a bicycle can take the same flat bend at the same speed, because both the friction available and the force required scale with mass. Doubling the radius raises the safe speed by only √2, and doubling μ does the same — that is why wet roads (μ ≈ 0.4) reduce the limit by only about 18%, but ice (μ ≈ 0.1) by more than half.

v_max = 17 m s⁻¹ (62 km h⁻¹) from μmg = mv²/r; friction available 4.7 kN, independent of mass in the final speed.

Q25. [Derivation] Derive v = √(rg tan θ) for the design speed of a banked frictionless road, and explain what happens below and above that speed.

Forces on the car: weight mg downward, normal reaction N perpendicular to the bank. Resolve N: vertical: N cos θ = mg (no vertical acceleration) horizontal: N sin θ = mv²/r (centripetal) Divide: tan θ = v²/(rg) ⇒ v = √(rg tan θ)

At exactly this speed no friction is needed. Below it, the car tends to slide down the bank, so friction acts up the slope; above it, the car tends to slide up, so friction acts down the slope. The banking angle therefore fixes a comfortable design speed, which is why mountain roads and racetracks are banked — and why the design speed does not depend on the mass of the vehicle.

tan θ = v²/(rg) from N cos θ = mg and N sin θ = mv²/r; at the design speed friction is zero, below it friction acts up-slope, above it down-slope.

Q26. [Graph/geometry reading] A conical pendulum has a string 1.0 m long making 30° with the vertical. Find the radius of the circle, the tension and the period.

Geometry: r = L sin θ = 1.0 × 0.500 = 0.50 m Vertical: T cos θ = mg ⇒ T = mg/cos30° = 1.155 mg Horizontal: T sin θ = mv²/r v² = rg tan θ = 0.50 × 9.8 × 0.577 = 2.83 ⇒ v = 1.68 m s⁻¹ T_period = 2πr/v = 2π(0.50)/1.68 = 1.87 s check: T = 2π√(L cos θ/g) = 2π√(0.866/9.8) = 1.87 s ✓

For a 0.2 kg bob the tension is 1.155 × 0.2 × 9.8 = 2.26 N. Two results are worth recognising: the period shortens as θ grows (unlike a simple pendulum), and the tension always exceeds the weight because the string must both support the bob and turn it.

r = 0.50 m, T = 1.155 mg, period 1.87 s (from 2π√(L cos θ/g)).

Q27. [Combined concept] A 0.20 kg ball on a 1.6 m string is whirled in a vertical circle. Find the minimum speed at the top for the string to stay taut, the corresponding speed at the bottom, and the tension at the bottom.

At the top, minimum case T = 0, so gravity alone provides the centripetal force: mg = mv_top²/r ⇒ v_top = √(gr) = √(9.8 × 1.6) = 3.96 m s⁻¹ Energy from bottom to top (height 2r = 3.2 m): ½v_b² = ½v_t² + g(2r) v_b = √(v_t² + 4gr) = √(15.7 + 62.7) = √78.4 = 8.85 m s⁻¹ Tension at the bottom: T − mg = mv_b²/r ⇒ T = m(g + v_b²/r) = 0.20(9.8 + 78.4/1.6) = 0.20(9.8 + 49) = 11.8 N

So T = 6mg at the bottom (not 3mg, because this is the minimum-speed case, where the top tension has just fallen to zero). The string is most likely to break at the bottom, not the top — the exact reverse of the usual guess.

v_top = 3.96 m s⁻¹, v_bottom = 8.85 m s⁻¹, T_bottom = 11.8 N ≈ 6mg.

Q28. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): A body in uniform circular motion has constant kinetic energy but changing momentum. Reason (R): Its speed is constant while its direction of motion changes continuously.

Both statements are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

KE = ½mv² — depends on the magnitude of velocity only ⇒ constant p = mv — a vector, whose direction changes every instant ⇒ changing Δp over one revolution = 0, but dp/dt ≠ 0 at every instant The change arises from the centripetal force: F = dp/dt = mv²/r toward the centre.

This is the cleanest example of the scalar/vector split in mechanics, and it explains why the centripetal force does no work: the force is always perpendicular to the displacement, so it changes momentum without changing energy.

Both true, R explains A: constant speed fixes KE, changing direction changes the vector p, which requires a perpendicular force.

Q29. [Experiment/observation table] A 50 g rubber stopper is whirled on a string of radius 0.80 m; 20 revolutions take 10.0 s. Find the period, the speed and the centripetal force, then state what is being measured.

Period T = 10.0/20 = 0.50 s Speed v = 2πr/T = 2π(0.80)/0.50 = 10.1 m s⁻¹ Force F = mv²/r = 0.050 × 101/0.80 = 6.3 N equivalently F = 4π²mr/T² = 4π²(0.050)(0.80)/0.25 = 6.3 N

The force is supplied by the tension in the string, which is measured by hanging known weights at the other end through a glass tube. Increasing the hanging weight until the radius stays steady at a fixed period is the standard verification of F = mv²/r, and it confirms both proportionalities: F ∝ m and F ∝ 1/T² at fixed r, and F ∝ r at fixed T.

T = 0.50 s, v = 10.1 m s⁻¹, F = 6.3 N supplied by the string tension — the experiment verifies F = 4π²mr/T².

Q30. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the centripetal acceleration of a satellite in a low orbit (r ≈ 6.6 × 10⁶ m, v ≈ 7.8 × 10³ m s⁻¹) and compare it with g. Then state the limiting cases of the flat-road cornering result v_max = √(μgr).

a = v²/r = (7.8 × 10³)²/6.6 × 10⁶ = 6.1 × 10⁷/6.6 × 10⁶ = 9.2 m s⁻² Compare: g at that altitude = 9.8 × (6.4/6.6)² = 9.2 m s⁻² ✓ The “centripetal” acceleration of an orbit IS gravity — that is what “orbit” means.

Limiting cases of v_max = √(μgr):

μ → 0 ⇒ v_max → 0 no friction, no flat-road cornering at any speed r → ∞ ⇒ v_max → ∞ a straight road needs no cornering force μ → ∞ ⇒ v_max → ∞ the ideal (impossible) infinite-grip tyre θ = 90° ⇒ v = √(rg tan θ) → ∞ vertical wall (wall-of-death stunt)

The μ → 0 case is exactly why the flat-road formula fails on ice and why banked curves are designed into roads: banking provides the centripetal force geometrically, without needing friction at all.

a ≈ 9.2 m s⁻², equal to local g (gravity is the centripetal force); as μ → 0 the flat-road maximum speed tends to zero, which is why banking exists.

Q31. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “A centrifugal force pushes the body outward in circular motion.”
(ii) “In a vertical circle the tension is greatest at the highest point.”
(iii) “Angular velocity is measured in m s⁻¹.”
(iv) “If the string of a whirling stone breaks, the stone flies radially outward.”

(i) FALSE in an inertial frame — there is no outward force; the body moves in a circle because the NET force (tension, friction, gravity) points inward. “Centrifugal” is only a bookkeeping pseudo-force inside a rotating frame. (ii) FALSE — tension is greatest at the LOWEST point (T = mg + mv²/r) and least at the top (T = mv²/r − mg, zero at the minimum speed). (iii) FALSE — angular velocity ω is rad s⁻¹ (or rev s⁻¹). m s⁻¹ measures the linear speed v, related by v = ωr. (iv) FALSE — it flies off along the TANGENT to the circle at the instant of breaking, because that is the direction of its velocity once the centripetal force vanishes.

All four errors come from the same root: treating the rotating frame as if it were inertial, or confusing the radial force with a radial velocity.

(i) no outward force in an inertial frame; (ii) tension is maximum at the bottom; (iii) ω is in rad s⁻¹; (iv) the stone leaves along the tangent.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. Can a body move in a circle with constant speed and still have an acceleration? Explain.

Yes. The speed is constant but the direction of the velocity changes continuously, so the velocity (a vector) changes and there is a centripetal acceleration v²/r directed toward the centre.

C2. Why is centripetal force not a new kind of force?

It is only a name for the net inward force needed for circular motion; whatever real force plays that role is the centripetal force — tension for a whirled stone, friction for a turning car, gravity for a satellite, electrostatic attraction for an electron in an atom.

C3. Define angular velocity and give its relation to linear velocity.

Angular velocity is the rate of change of angular displacement, ω = dθ/dt, in rad s⁻¹. Its relation to linear velocity is v = rω, where r is the perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation.

C4. Why does a cyclist lean inward when taking a turn?

Leaning tilts the normal reaction from the ground so that its horizontal component points toward the centre and supplies (or helps supply) the centripetal force, while the torque of the weight is balanced. Without leaning the friction alone would be insufficient and the cycle would skid outward.

C5. What is centrifugal force? Is it real?

It is a pseudo-force of magnitude mv²/r directed away from the centre, which appears only in a rotating (non-inertial) frame. In an inertial frame there is no outward force; the observed outward tendency is simply inertia — the body tends to move in a straight line while the frame turns.

C6. State the condition for a car to negotiate an unbanked curve without skidding.

The friction must be able to supply the centripetal force: μmg ≥ mv²/r, so v ≤ √(μrg). The maximum safe speed is √(μrg) and is independent of the mass of the car.

C7. Why is the tension in a vertical circle greatest at the lowest point?

At the bottom the tension must both support the weight and provide the centripetal force, T = mv²/r + mg, and the speed is also greatest there by energy conservation. At the top the weight itself helps: T = mv²/r − mg, so the tension is least.

C8. Why does a pilot feel heavier at the bottom of a loop?

The seat reaction must exceed the pilot's weight to provide the upward centripetal force at the bottom: N = mg + mv²/r. The pilot therefore feels an apparent weight larger than mg; at the top the seat pushes down and the apparent weight is mg − mv²/r.

C9. Why is a stone whirled in a vertical circle released tangentially when the string breaks?

Because the centripetal force disappears at the instant the string breaks. With no inward force, the stone continues with the velocity it had at that instant, and the velocity is always tangential to the circle. It then follows a projectile path.

C10. Distinguish between rotary and circular motion.

In circular motion the body as a whole moves along a circular path (a stone on a string, a car on a curve). In rotary motion the body spins about an axis through itself, so different points have different radii and speeds (a spinning top, a wheel about its axle).

C11. Why is the speed of a satellite independent of its mass?

Gravity supplies the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r. The mass m cancels, leaving v = √(GM/r), which depends only on the central mass and the orbital radius — so all satellites at the same height orbit at the same speed.

C12. What is the angle of banking? On what factors does it depend?

The angle the road surface makes with the horizontal, chosen so that the normal reaction can supply the centripetal force. From tanθ = v²/(rg) it depends on the design speed squared and inversely on the radius of the curve and g; it is independent of the vehicle's mass.

C13. Why does a body in a vertical circle not have uniform speed even if the string is pulled evenly?

Gravity has a tangential component along the circular path (mg sinφ), so the speed changes continuously: maximum at the bottom, minimum at the top. Uniform circular motion in a vertical plane is impossible without an external tangential drive, because gravity does work along the path.

C14. Give the dimensions of angular velocity, angular acceleration and centripetal acceleration.

ω = dθ/dt: [T⁻¹]; α = dω/dt: [T⁻²]; a_c = v²/r: [LT⁻²], the same as linear acceleration.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Derive the minimum speed required at the bottom of a vertical circle, using energy conservation, and state the condition at the top.

Condition at the top: gravity alone just provides the centripetal force mg = mv_t²/r ⇒ v_t² = gr Energy from bottom (height 0) to top (height 2r): ½mv_b² = ½mv_t² + mg(2r) v_b² = v_t² + 4gr = gr + 4gr = 5gr ⇒ v_b,min = √(5gr)

If v_b < √(5gr) the body fails to reach the top with v_t ≥ √(gr) and the string slackens.

v_top,min = √(gr), v_bottom,min = √(5gr); the square-root-five factor is the classic NEB result.

L2. [5] A car of 1200 kg takes a banked curve of radius 150 m at 25 m s⁻¹. Find the required banking angle for frictionless turning and the normal reaction.

tanθ = v²/(rg) = 625/(150 × 9.8) = 625/1470 = 0.425 θ = 23.0° N = mg/cosθ = 1200(9.8)/0.9205 = 12 780 N

θ ≈ 23°; N ≈ 1.28 × 10⁴ N, about 9% larger than the weight because the normal reaction also has the horizontal centripetal component.

L3. [5] Explain, with derivation, why a satellite in a circular orbit has a centripetal acceleration equal to g at that height.

Gravitational force = centripetal requirement: GMm/r² = mv²/r ⇒ a = v²/r = GM/r² But GM/r² is exactly the gravitational field strength at radius r: g(r) = GM/r² = g₀R²/r² (for height above the surface)

So the centripetal acceleration of a satellite is the local value of g; the satellite is in continuous free fall, which is why the astronauts feel weightless.

a_c = GM/r² = g at that height — orbital motion is free fall with the right tangential speed to miss the Earth.

L4. [4] A wheel of radius 0.4 m starts from rest and accelerates uniformly to 10 rad s⁻¹ in 2 s. Find α, the tangential and centripetal accelerations of a point on the rim at t = 2 s, and the resultant acceleration.

α = (10 − 0)/2 = 5 rad s⁻² a_t = rα = 0.4 × 5 = 2.0 m s⁻² a_r = rω² = 0.4 × 100 = 40 m s⁻² a = √(a_t² + a_r²) = √(4 + 1600) = 40.05 m s⁻²

α = 5 rad s⁻²; a_t = 2 m s⁻², a_r = 40 m s⁻², resultant ≈ 40 m s⁻² — at this speed the radial part completely dominates.

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 6

1. Radians. ω = 2πn requires n in revolutions per second; using rpm directly gives answers wrong by 60. Convert first, write the conversion in the answer.

2. Forgetting which force is centripetal. On the top of a vertical circle gravity and tension act in the same direction: T + mg = mv²/r. At the bottom they oppose: T − mg = mv²/r. Reversing the sign is the commonest error.

3. Using g instead of v²/r for the acceleration. In a vertical circle the acceleration is (v²/r) toward the centre, not g; g only enters through energy conservation and the vertical force balance.

4. Saying centripetal force does work. It is perpendicular to the velocity at every instant, so W = 0 and the speed in uniform circular motion is unchanged. Writing "work = Fs" for it is marked wrong.

5. Mass in the banking formula. tanθ = v²/(rg) and v_max = √(μrg) contain no m — mass cancels. Answering "depends on the mass of the car" loses the mark.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Draw the circle, mark the centre, draw the force arrows from the body toward the centre, then write ΣF_toward centre = mv²/r. One equation per direction; the direction statement earns its own mark.
