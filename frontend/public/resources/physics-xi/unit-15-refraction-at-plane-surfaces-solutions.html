Unit 15 — Refraction at Plane Surfaces

Physics XI (Phy. 101)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  4 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Four formulas cover the whole unit: n = sin i/sin r, n = c/v, the real/apparent depth ratio, and sin C = 1/n. Every exercise below is one of those four applied, with the two ray-diagram questions NEB asks most often.

How the unit is examined.

  • 2 marks — state the laws of refraction; define refractive index; state the conditions for total internal reflection; why does a pool look shallower than it is.
  • 4 marks — calculate n, or the critical angle, or the real/apparent depth; lateral shift numerical; explain an application of total internal reflection.
  • 5–6 marks — derive the relation between the refractive indices of two media; explain total internal reflection with a ray diagram and give two applications (optical fibre, mirage, prism periscope).

Rule that keeps losing students marks: the angle of refraction is measured from the normal, not the surface. Every diagram must show the normal, or the ray-diagram mark is lost even if the calculation is right.

Exercise 15.1 — Laws of refraction and refractive index

Q1. State the laws of refraction of light.

  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. For a given pair of media and a given colour of light, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant (Snell's law): sin i/sin r = ₁n₂ = constant.

Equivalently, the product n sin θ is constant across the boundary (n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r), which is the form in which the law is actually used in numericals.

Coplanarity plus Snell's law: sin i/sin r = n₂/n₁, or n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r.

Q2. Define the absolute refractive index of a medium and state its range of values.

The absolute refractive index of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to the speed of light in that medium:

n = c/v (≥ 1 always, since v ≤ c)

It can also be written n = sin i/sin r for light entering from air, or n = 1/sin C where C is the critical angle. Values: air 1.0003, water 1.33, glass 1.5, diamond 2.42.

n = c/v, dimensionless and always ≥ 1; a larger n means a denser (optically) medium and slower light.

Q3. A ray of light passes from air into water (n = 4/3) at an angle of incidence of 45°. Find the angle of refraction.

n = sin i/sin r ⇒ sin r = sin i/n sin r = sin45°/1.333 = 0.7071/1.333 = 0.530 r = sin⁻¹(0.530) = 32.0°

r ≈ 32° — the ray bends toward the normal on entering the denser medium

Q4. Light travels from glass (n = 1.5) into air at 30° to the normal. Find the angle of refraction, and state what happens if the angle of incidence becomes 50°.

At 30°: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r ⇒ 1.5 × 0.5 = 1 × sin r sin r = 0.75 ⇒ r = 48.6° Critical angle: sin C = 1/1.5 = 0.667 ⇒ C = 41.8° Since 50° > C, total internal reflection occurs — no refracted ray emerges.

r = 48.6° at 30° incidence; at 50° incidence the ray is totally internally reflected (because 50° > C = 41.8°).

Q5. Explain why a coin lying at the bottom of a pool appears raised.

Light from the coin travels upward through water and refracts away from the normal as it enters the air. To an observer above, these rays appear to come from a point higher than the coin. In the paraxial approximation the apparent depth is

apparent depth = real depth/n

For a pool 2 m deep with n = 1.33, the coin appears at 2/1.33 = 1.5 m — half a metre too shallow.

Rays from the coin bend away from the normal on leaving the water, so they appear to diverge from a virtual image above the coin; apparent depth = real depth/n.

Exercise 15.2 — Refractive index and the speed of light

Q6. The refractive index of glass is 1.5. Find the speed of light in glass and the refractive index of glass with respect to water (n_water = 4/3).

v = c/n = 3 × 10⁸/1.5 = 2.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ w_n_g = n_g/n_w = 1.5/1.333 = 1.125

v = 2.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; the refractive index of glass with respect to water is 1.125

Q7. Why does a medium with a higher refractive index bend light more?

Because the speed of light falls more in that medium. Light crossing a boundary must keep its frequency (the source's frequency is unchanged) but its wavelength and speed change; a smaller speed means a smaller wavelength in the medium. Refraction occurs to keep the wavefront continuous along the boundary, and the greater the change of speed, the greater the bending. Quantitatively, sin i/sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁.

Higher n ⇒ slower light ⇒ larger change of speed at the boundary ⇒ more bending; sin i/sin r = v₁/v₂.

Q8. A ray of light of wavelength 600 nm in air enters glass of n = 1.5. Find its speed, frequency and wavelength in glass.

f = c/λ_air = 3 × 10⁸/(600 × 10⁻⁹) = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz (unchanged in glass) v = c/n = 2.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ λ_glass = λ_air/n = 600/1.5 = 400 nm (check: v = fλ = 5 × 10¹⁴ × 400 × 10⁻⁹ = 2.0 × 10⁸ ✓)

v = 2.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; f = 5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz (unchanged); λ = 400 nm. Colour, which is set by frequency, does not change.

Exercise 15.3 — Real and apparent depth

Q9. Derive the relation apparent depth = real depth/n for an object viewed from above through a parallel-sided transparent medium.

Let an object O be at a real depth d below a plane surface. A ray from O strikes the surface at a point close to the normal and refracts into the air. For small angles (paraxial rays):

From the geometry of the two right triangles sharing the same surface distance x: tan i ≈ sin i ≈ x/real depth tan r ≈ sin r ≈ x/apparent depth Dividing: sin i/sin r = apparent depth/real depth... with i the angle in the medium and r in air: n = sin r/sin i (light goes from medium to air) ⇒ apparent depth = real depth/n

The derivation uses the paraxial approximation (small angles, looking nearly straight down); at oblique viewing the image is also displaced sideways and the expression is only approximate.

apparent depth = real depth/n, valid for near-normal viewing; the object appears raised by a fraction (1 − 1/n) of its real depth.

Q10. A glass slab of thickness 6 cm and n = 1.5 is placed over a coin. At what depth does the coin appear?

apparent depth = real depth/n = 6/1.5 = 4 cm

The coin appears 4 cm below the top surface — raised by 2 cm, i.e. by one third of the slab thickness (t(1 − 1/n) = 6 × 0.333 = 2 cm).

Q11. A water tank is 4 m deep. What is the apparent depth of its bottom when viewed from above? (n = 4/3) By how much does the bottom appear raised?

apparent depth = 4/(4/3) = 3 m raised by 4 − 3 = 1 m

It appears 3 m deep — a full metre shallower, which is why a swimming pool always looks deeper on entry than from the side.

Exercise 15.4 — Relation between refractive indices of two media

Q12. Show that ₁n₂ = 1/₂n₁ = n₂/n₁, and use it to find the refractive index of water with respect to glass (n_w = 1.33, n_g = 1.5).

Snell's law across a boundary: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r ⇒ sin i/sin r = n₂/n₁ = ₁n₂ Reversing the direction of the ray (reversibility): sin r/sin i = n₁/n₂ = ₂n₁ Hence ₁n₂ = 1/₂n₁ = n₂/n₁. w_n_g = n_g/n_w = 1.5/1.33 = 1.128 g_n_w = n_w/n_g = 0.887

₁n₂ = n₂/n₁ = 1/₂n₁; water with respect to glass = 1.128, glass with respect to water = 0.887.

Q13. Why is the refractive index of a medium with respect to air called its absolute refractive index, and why is it always greater than 1?

Because air's refractive index is very nearly 1 (1.0003) and light's reference speed is its speed in vacuum, so n_medium/n_air ≈ n_medium/vacuum = c/v. Since light is slowed by any material medium (v < c), the ratio c/v is always greater than 1. Only vacuum has exactly n = 1.

n = c/v with v ≤ c for all matter, so the absolute refractive index is ≥ 1, equality holding only for vacuum.

Exercise 15.5 — Lateral shift

Q14. Derive the expression for the lateral shift of a ray passing through a parallel-sided glass slab: shift = t sin(i − r)/cos r.

A ray enters a slab of thickness t at incidence i, refracts at r, and emerges parallel to its original direction but displaced sideways by a distance d (the perpendicular distance between the incident and emergent rays).

In the right triangle formed by the slab thickness t, the refracted ray and the normal, the path length of the ray inside the slab is L = t/cos r. The angle between the emergent ray and the original direction is (i − r). Therefore the perpendicular displacement: d = L sin(i − r) = t sin(i − r)/cos r

d = t sin(i − r)/cos r; the shift grows with thickness, with i and with n (since a larger n gives a smaller r).

Q15. A ray is incident at 45° on a glass slab 5 cm thick (n = 1.5). Find the lateral shift.

sin r = sin45°/1.5 = 0.7071/1.5 = 0.4714 ⇒ r = 28.1° i − r = 16.9° d = 5 × sin16.9°/cos28.1° = 5 × 0.2907/0.8816 = 5 × 0.3297 = 1.65 cm

Lateral shift ≈ 1.6 cm

Q16. What happens to a ray that passes through a parallel-sided slab at normal incidence? What is the net deviation for any other angle?

At normal incidence (i = 0) the ray enters undeviated, travels straight through and emerges undeviated, so the lateral shift is zero. For any other incidence the ray is deviated at the first surface and deviated back by the same amount at the second, so the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray: the net angular deviation is zero, but the ray is displaced sideways.

Zero deviation in every case; the only effect is a lateral shift, which is zero at normal incidence.

Q17. Why does a printed word seen through a glass slab appear raised but not otherwise distorted?

Each point of the word is viewed through the slab, so each appears raised by the apparent-depth effect (apparent depth = real depth/n). Since the slab has parallel faces and uniform thickness, every point is raised by the same amount and the emergent rays are parallel to the incident ones, so the letters keep their shape and size.

A uniform parallel slab raises every point equally; there is no magnification because the emergent rays stay parallel.

Exercise 15.6 — Total internal reflection and its applications

Q18. Define the critical angle and derive sin C = 1/n. State the two conditions for total internal reflection.

Critical angle: the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is exactly 90°.

Snell's law at the critical angle (medium → air): n sin C = 1 × sin 90° ⇒ sin C = 1/n

Conditions for TIR: (i) light must travel from a denser to a rarer medium; (ii) the angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.

sin C = 1/n; TIR needs denser → rarer travel with i > C. For glass C ≈ 42°, for water ≈ 49°, for diamond ≈ 24°.

Q19. Find the critical angle for glass (n = 1.5), water (n = 4/3) and diamond (n = 2.42).

Glass: sin C = 1/1.5 = 0.667 ⇒ C = 41.8° Water: sin C = 1/(4/3) = 0.75 ⇒ C = 48.6° Diamond: sin C = 1/2.42 = 0.413 ⇒ C = 24.4°

41.8°, 48.6° and 24.4° — the smaller the critical angle, the easier it is to trap light, which is why diamond sparkles.

Q20. Explain total internal reflection with a ray diagram in words, and explain why the reflected ray brightens as i approaches C.

As a ray in the denser medium approaches the boundary at increasing angles, refraction bends it further from the normal and a smaller fraction of the energy is transmitted; the reflected ray correspondingly brightens. At i = C the refracted ray grazes the surface (r = 90°) and carries almost no energy; for i > C no refracted ray exists at all and 100% of the energy is reflected back into the denser medium — total internal reflection.

Beyond the critical angle no refracted ray can satisfy Snell's law, so all the light is reflected; the approach to C shows the transmitted ray fading and the reflected ray brightening.

Q21. Explain the working of an optical fibre.

An optical fibre consists of a fine core of high refractive index surrounded by a cladding of lower refractive index. Light entering one end at a small angle to the axis strikes the core–cladding boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle, so it undergoes total internal reflection. Since no light is lost at each reflection (in an ideal fibre), the light is guided along the fibre, following bends, with very little attenuation — provided the bend radius is not so small that the angle falls below C.

Light is trapped by repeated total internal reflection at the core–cladding boundary; used for communication, endoscopy and sensors because there is almost no loss per reflection.

Q22. Explain the formation of a mirage.

On a hot day the air near the ground is much hotter and therefore less dense (lower n) than the air above. Light from the sky travelling downward bends gradually toward the normal in the denser upper air and away from the normal in the hotter lower layers, until at a shallow grazing angle it exceeds the critical angle and is totally internally reflected upward. The observer sees an image of the sky appearing to come from the ground, which looks like a pool of water.

Continuous refraction through layers of decreasing n, then total internal reflection in the hot air near the ground, redirects sky light into the eye — the "water" is an inverted image of the sky.

Q23. Explain how a right-angled prism is used to turn a light beam through 90° (prism periscope).

A right-angled isosceles glass prism (n = 1.5, C = 41.8°) is used with light entering one of the short faces at normal incidence, so it reaches the hypotenuse internally at 45°. Since 45° > 41.8°, total internal reflection occurs and the beam leaves through the other short face at 90° to its original direction. Two such prisms form a periscope or binocular erecting system.

The 45° incidence on the hypotenuse exceeds the glass critical angle, so TIR turns the beam through 90° with no silvering and almost no loss.

Q24. Why does a diamond sparkle more than glass of the same shape?

Diamond's refractive index is very high (2.42), so its critical angle is only 24.4° — a very wide range of internal angles is therefore totally internally reflected. Light entering the diamond bounces several times inside before emerging through the top facets, and because the facets are cut at specific angles it emerges in concentrated flashes. Diamond also has high dispersion, so the flashes are split into colours.

A small critical angle (24°) traps light through many internal reflections and the high dispersion splits it into colours — sparkle plus fire.

Additional problem types — the eight NEB question shapes

Refraction questions are ratio questions: μ = c/v = sin i/sin r = real depth/apparent depth = 1/sin C. The skill is choosing the right ratio and knowing which quantity does not change. The eight shapes follow.

Q25. [Multi-step numerical] A tank contains 12 cm of water (μ = 4/3) over a 6.0 cm thick glass slab (μ = 1.5). Find the apparent depth of the bottom as seen from directly above, and the total shift.

For each layer, apparent depth = real depth/μ: water: 12/(4/3) = 9.0 cm glass: 6.0/1.5 = 4.0 cm apparent depth = 9.0 + 4.0 = 13 cm real depth = 18 cm ⇒ shift = 18 − 13 = 5.0 cm Check by shifts: 12(1 − 3/4) + 6(1 − 2/3) = 3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0 cm ✓

Layers add in apparent depth, not in shift-per-unit-thickness, and each layer uses its own μ — the two common errors are to add the shifts as if the mediums were the same and to use a single combined μ. The observer sees the bottom 5 cm higher than it really is.

Apparent depth 13 cm from the surface, i.e. the bottom appears raised by 5.0 cm.

Q26. [Derivation] Derive the relation between real depth and apparent depth for an object viewed normally from above.

Let O be the object at real depth d, and O′ its image at apparent depth d′. A ray from O meets the surface at P with angle of incidence i in the medium and refracts into air at angle r. For near-normal viewing both angles are small: tan i ≈ sin i ≈ 1/μ × sin r … and geometry gives tan i = x/d, tan r = x/d′ (x = horizontal distance of P from the normal) ⇒ d′/d = tan i/tan r ≈ sin i/sin r = 1/μ d′ = d/μ

The derivation makes the small-angle assumption explicit: for oblique viewing the image is not simply raised but also displaced sideways, and the simple ratio fails. In the NEB examination the phrase “viewed from directly above” is the signal that d′ = d/μ applies.

d′ = d/μ from tan i/tan r ≈ sin i/sin r; the result holds only for near-normal viewing.

Q27. [Graph reading] A graph of sin i against sin r for a ray entering a medium is a straight line of slope 1.50. Find the refractive index, the speed of light in the medium, and the critical angle.

Snell's law: sin i = μ sin r, so slope = μ = 1.50 v = c/μ = 3.00 × 10⁸/1.50 = 2.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ sin C = 1/μ = 1/1.50 = 0.667 ⇒ C = 41.8°

The critical angle exists only for the denser-to-rarer direction: at i = C the refracted ray grazes the surface (r = 90°), and beyond C there is no refracted ray at all. The same μ gives both results, which is why one measurement of the slope settles the speed and the critical angle in one go.

μ = 1.50 from the slope; v = 2.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; C = 41.8°.

Q28. [Combined concept] An optical fibre has core μ = 1.50 and cladding μ = 1.45. Find the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary and the maximum angle at which light can enter the fibre face and still be guided.

At the core–cladding boundary (core is denser): sin C = n_clad/n_core = 1.45/1.50 = 0.967 ⇒ C = 75.1° Acceptance angle at the end face (air to core): n₀ sin θ₀ = √(n_core² − n_clad²) sin θ₀ = √(2.250 − 2.1025) = √0.1475 = 0.384 θ₀ = 22.6°

So the fibre accepts a cone of half-angle 22.6° — the numerical aperture is 0.384. Light entering within that cone strikes the core-cladding wall at more than 75.1° and is totally internally reflected along the fibre. Increase the core–cladding difference and the acceptance cone widens, which is why multimode fibres use a larger step in μ but lose more light per metre.

C = 75.1°; acceptance half-angle 22.6° (numerical aperture 0.384).

Q29. [Assertion–reason] Assertion (A): A coin at the bottom of a tank of water appears raised. Reason (R): Rays from the coin bend away from the normal as they leave the water, so they appear to come from a point nearer the surface.

Both statements are true and R explains A.

Water (μ = 4/3) to air: the ray bends AWAY from the normal sin r = μ sin i > sin i ⇒ r > i Back-extending the emergent ray meets the vertical through the coin above the true coin: apparent depth = real depth/μ = 30/1.333 = 22.5 cm for a 30 cm tank the coin looks 7.5 cm higher than it is

The same reasoning explains why a pool looks shallower than it is (and why it is dangerous to dive into a pool whose depth you judged by eye), why a stick in water looks bent at the surface, and why the underwater part of a fish looks closer to the surface than the fish's real position.

Both true, R explains A: refraction away from the normal on emerging makes the coin appear raised by real depth × (1 − 1/μ).

Q31. [Estimation and limiting case] Estimate the critical angle for water (μ = 4/3) and for diamond (μ = 2.42), and state the limiting cases i → C and i → 0.

water: sin C = 3/4 = 0.750 ⇒ C = 48.6° diamond: sin C = 1/2.42 = 0.413 ⇒ C = 24.4° Compare glass (μ = 1.5): C = 41.8°, and air (μ ≈ 1): no critical angle at all.

Limiting cases:

i → 0 r → 0: no bending, the ray passes straight through (normal incidence) i → C r → 90°: the refracted ray grazes the surface — the onset of TIR i > C no refracted ray exists; all the light is reflected internally μ₂ > μ₁ C does not exist: light bends TOWARDS the normal and cannot be trapped

Diamond's small critical angle (24.4°) is why it sparkles: light entering the stone is trapped and bounces many times before escaping through a facet, and the wide angle between the facets cuts it into brilliant flashes. Water's larger 48.6° is why a fish's window of the sky above is a narrow cone.

C = 48.6° for water and 24.4° for diamond; at i → C the ray grazes, at i → 0 there is no deviation, and TIR is impossible going into a denser medium.

Q32. [Error-hunting] Correct each false statement.
(i) “Refractive index has the unit m s⁻¹.”
(ii) “Total internal reflection occurs when light passes from a rarer to a denser medium.”
(iii) “When light enters glass, its frequency changes.”
(iv) “A ray incident normally on a glass slab is deviated.”

(i) FALSE — μ = c/v is a ratio of two speeds, so it is dimensionless (1.5, not 1.5 m s⁻¹). (ii) FALSE — TIR needs the DENSER-to-RARER direction (glass to air), otherwise there is no angle for which r reaches 90°. (iii) FALSE — frequency is set by the source and does not change on refraction; it is the WAVELENGTH (and speed) that change: λ_glass = λ_air/μ. Colour, which depends on frequency, therefore does not change. (iv) FALSE — a ray along the normal has i = 0, so r = 0: it passes straight through with no deviation (because sin i = μ sin r gives r = 0).

Statement (iii) is the one that costs marks in the dispersion chapter later: since frequency is constant, a single colour cannot be split by refraction — only the different μ values separate the colours.

(i) μ is dimensionless; (ii) denser to rarer; (iii) frequency is unchanged, λ and v change; (iv) normal incidence is undeviated.

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. State Snell's law.

For a given pair of media and a given colour, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant: sin i/sin r = n₂/n₁, the incident ray, refracted ray and normal being coplanar.

C2. Define refractive index in two ways.

Absolutely: n = c/v, the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to that in the medium. Relative to another medium: ₁n₂ = sin i/sin r = v₁/v₂, the ratio of the speeds and the inverse of the sine ratio across that boundary.

C3. State the conditions for total internal reflection.

(i) The light must be travelling from an optically denser medium to a rarer one. (ii) The angle of incidence in the denser medium must be greater than the critical angle, where sin C = 1/n. Both conditions are required.

C4. Define the critical angle.

The angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the refracted ray grazes the boundary at 90° in the rarer medium, given by sin C = 1/n. Beyond it no refracted ray exists.

C5. Why does light bend when it enters a new medium?

Because its speed changes. The frequency stays the same, so the wavelength adjusts to v/f; the wavefronts must stay continuous along the boundary, and this forces the direction of travel to change. The greater the change in speed, the greater the bending.

C6. Why does a stick held obliquely in water appear bent at the surface?

Light from the submerged part bends away from the normal on leaving the water, so each point of the stick appears displaced upward from its real position. The part above the water is seen directly, so the two halves seem to meet at an angle — the stick looks bent at the water line.

C7. Why does a swimming pool look shallower than it is?

Because of refraction: rays from the bottom bend away from the normal on leaving the water and appear to come from a shallower point. Apparent depth = real depth/n, so with n = 1.33 a 4 m pool looks about 3 m deep.

C8. What is lateral shift and on what does it depend?

The perpendicular displacement between the incident and emergent rays after passing through a parallel-sided slab: d = t sin(i − r)/cos r. It increases with the slab thickness t, with the angle of incidence i, and with the refractive index n; it is zero at normal incidence.

C9. Give two applications of total internal reflection.

Optical fibres for communication and endoscopy (light guided by repeated TIR), and the prism periscope or binocular erecting prism (TIR at the hypotenuse turns the beam). Other examples: mirage, diamond sparkle, and the "light pipe" in decorative fountains.

C10. Why is a prism periscope preferred to a mirror periscope?

Because total internal reflection reflects essentially 100% of the light with no metallic coating to tarnish or absorb. A silvered mirror loses several percent at each reflection and its coating can degrade.

C11. Why is the refractive index of a medium quoted with respect to a particular wavelength?

Because the speed of light in the medium (and hence n) depends on wavelength — dispersion. Values such as n = 1.5 for glass refer to yellow sodium light unless otherwise stated; violet light has a slightly larger n than red in the same glass.

C12. Does the frequency of light change when it enters a new medium? Explain.

No. Frequency is set by the source and is the number of wavefronts crossing a point per second; the boundary cannot create or destroy wavefronts. Speed and wavelength change (v = c/n, λ = λ_air/n), but f remains constant, which is why colour is unchanged.

C13. What is meant by an optically denser medium?

A medium with a larger refractive index, in which light travels more slowly. "Denser" here is an optical statement, not a statement about mass density — alcohol (n = 1.36) is optically denser than some oils despite lower mass density.

C14. Why does a drop of water on a page make the print look larger and brighter?

The drop is a small plano-convex lens: light from the print is refracted at the curved surface and magnified before reaching the eye, so the lettering appears enlarged. The brightness increases because the drop also collects more light and reduces diffused scattering from the paper.

C15. Why is the Sun visible a few minutes before actual sunrise and after sunset?

Because the atmosphere is a refracting medium of decreasing density with height; the Sun's rays bend gradually downward as they travel through it, so the Sun appears above the horizon before it geometrically is, and remains visible for a few minutes after it has set.

Long-answer set — 4 to 6 marks, fully worked

L1. [6] Explain total internal reflection with a diagram in words, derive sin C = 1/n, and show why 45° incidence in a glass prism causes TIR.

As i increases inside the denser medium, the refracted ray bends further from the normal and the transmitted intensity falls; at i = C the refracted ray is along the boundary (r = 90°), and for i > C Snell's law has no real solution for r, so all the light is reflected internally.

At i = C: n sin C = 1 · sin 90° = 1 ⇒ sin C = 1/n For glass n = 1.5: C = sin⁻¹(0.667) = 41.8° In a right-angled isosceles prism the ray strikes the hypotenuse at 45° 45° > 41.8° ⇒ total internal reflection, the beam is turned by 90°.

sin C = 1/n; glass C = 41.8° < 45°, so the hypotenuse of a right-angled prism always reflects totally — the basis of the prism periscope and binocular erecting system.

L2. [5] A ray of light strikes the surface of water at 40°. The resulting angle of refraction of the ray in the water is 28.9°. Find n_water from these data and calculate the speed of light in water.

n = sin i/sin r = sin40°/sin28.9° = 0.6428/0.4830 = 1.331 v = c/n = 3 × 10⁸/1.331 = 2.25 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹

n = 1.33; v = 2.25 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ — the standard values for water.

L3. [5] A glass slab of thickness 8 cm and n = 1.5 is placed in air. A ray strikes its top surface at 50°. Find the angle of refraction at the first surface, the angle of incidence on the second surface, the emergent angle, and the lateral shift.

1st surface: sin r = sin50°/1.5 = 0.766/1.5 = 0.511 ⇒ r = 30.7° 2nd surface: the faces are parallel, so the normal is parallel and the incidence angle there is equal to r = 30.7° (alternate angles) Emergent angle: sin e = n sin(30.7°) = 1.5 × 0.511 = 0.766 ⇒ e = 50° (same as i) Lateral shift: d = t sin(i − r)/cos r = 8 × sin(50 − 30.7)/cos30.7 = 8 × 0.3305/0.8601 = 8 × 0.3843 = 3.07 cm

r = 30.7°, incidence at the second surface 30.7°, emergence 50°, lateral shift ≈ 3.1 cm. The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray.

L4. [4] A tank contains water (n = 4/3) to a depth of 2 m and a layer of oil (n = 1.5) 1 m deep on top. At what depth does the bottom of the tank appear when viewed from above?

Apparent depth is the sum of the apparent depths of each layer: oil: 1/1.5 = 0.667 m water: 2/(4/3) = 1.500 m total apparent depth = 2.167 m

The bottom appears about 2.17 m below the surface instead of 3 m — each layer raises the image in proportion to (1 − 1/n).

Apparent depth ≈ 2.2 m (raised by about 0.83 m from the true 3 m).

The five traps that cost the most marks in Unit 15

1. Angles from the surface instead of the normal. Every refraction formula uses the angle with the normal. A ray at 40° to the surface is at 50° incidence.

2. Using n < 1 or sin r > 1. If a calculation gives sin r > 1, the ray is undergoing total internal reflection, not refraction. Recognise it and say so instead of forcing a number.

3. Inverting the refractive index. Going from air to glass, sin r = sin i/n. Going from glass to air, sin r = n sin i. Decide the direction first and write it down.

4. Apparent depth multiplied instead of divided. Apparent = real/n; a denser medium raises the object, so apparent depth must be smaller.

5. Saying frequency changes with medium. Frequency is fixed by the source; only v and λ change. Colour is set by frequency.

Answer-writing habit for this unit. Draw the boundary, the normal and both rays; label i and r between ray and normal; state which medium is denser before substituting; finish with the direction of bending ("toward/away from the normal").
