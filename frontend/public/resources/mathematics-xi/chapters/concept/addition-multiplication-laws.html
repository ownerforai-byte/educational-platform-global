5.3 · Addition, Multiplication and Independence — two-mark conceptual answers

Topics Addition law · Multiplication law · Independence · Conditional probability

Each question is the two-mark kind NEB asks at the start of the paper; the model answer is one or two precise sentences.

Addition law 2 marks

C1. State the addition law of probability. [2]

Ans. P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B), because the outcomes in both events are counted twice in the sum.

Addition law 2 marks

C2. When does the addition law reduce to P(A) + P(B)? [2]

Ans. When A and B are mutually exclusive, so P(A ∩ B) = 0 and the events cannot happen together.

Multiplication law 2 marks

C3. State the multiplication law for conditional probability. [2]

Ans. P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B | A), provided P(A) ≠ 0; this is just the definition of P(B | A) rearranged.

Independence 2 marks

C4. When is P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B)? [2]

Ans. Exactly when A and B are independent, so the occurrence of one does not change the chance of the other.

Independence 2 marks

C5. Can two mutually exclusive events be independent? [2]

Ans. No, unless one of them has probability 0: exclusivity makes P(A ∩ B) = 0, while independence needs it to equal P(A)P(B) > 0 in general.

Conditional probability 2 marks

C6. What is P(A | B) when A is a subset of B? [2]

Ans. It equals P(A)/P(B), because whenever B happens A also happens, so the overlap is all of A.
