Unit 7 — Computational Methods or Mechanics

Mathematics XI (Mat. 007)  •  NEB Grade 11  •  12 teaching hours  •  Exercise solutions + conceptual bank

Two optional halves of one unit. The computational half is pure procedure — bisection, Newton-Raphson, trapezoidal, Simpson's — and rewards a clean table more than cleverness. The mechanics half is force resolution and uniform acceleration, the same problems physics asks but marked as mathematics.

What is examined.

  • 2 marks — one iteration of a method, a resultant force, or one kinematics substitution.
  • 4 marks — a two-iteration bisection or Newton-Raphson, or a trapezoidal/Simpson evaluation with n = 4.
  • 5–6 marks — a fully tabulated numerical integration compared against the exact value, or a statics/dynamics problem in two stages.

Marks are concentrated in the table: correct x-values, correct f(x)-values, correct weights (1-4-2-4-…-1 for Simpson, 1-2-2-…-1 for trapezoidal), correct h. Write h explicitly before the table.

Exercise 7.2 — Newton-Raphson method

The iteration. xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f′(xn). It converges much faster than bisection but fails when f′(xn) = 0 or the starting guess is far from the root.

Q1. Using Newton-Raphson with x₀ = 2, find √5 correct to three decimal places.

The root satisfies f(x) = x² − 5 = 0, f′(x) = 2x.

x₁ = 2 − (4 − 5)/(2·2) = 2 + 1/4 = 2.25 x₂ = 2.25 − (5.0625 − 5)/4.5 = 2.25 − 0.01389 = 2.23611 x₃ = 2.23611 − (5.00019 − 5)/4.47222 ≈ 2.23607

√5 ≈ 2.236

Q2. Use Newton-Raphson twice on x³ − 2x − 5 = 0 starting from x₀ = 2.

f(x) = x³ − 2x − 5, f′(x) = 3x² − 2 x₁ = 2 − (8 − 4 − 5)/(12 − 2) = 2 − (−1/10) = 2.1 x₂ = 2.1 − (9.261 − 4.2 − 5)/(13.23 − 2) = 2.1 − 0.061/11.23 ≈ 2.0946

x₁ = 2.1, x₂ ≈ 2.0946 (true root 2.09455…)

Q3. Find a root of x² − 7 = 0 by Newton's method from x₀ = 3, correct to 4 decimals.

x₁ = 3 − (9 − 7)/6 = 3 − 0.3333 = 2.6667 x₂ = 2.6667 − (7.1111 − 7)/5.3333 = 2.6667 − 0.0208 = 2.6458 x₃ = 2.6458 − (6.9996 − 7)/5.2916 ≈ 2.6458

√7 ≈ 2.6458

Q4. Why does Newton-Raphson fail for f(x) = x³ − 3x + 2 starting at x₀ = 1?

f′(x) = 3x² − 3 ⇒ f′(1) = 0

The iteration divides by f′(x₀), which is zero, so the formula is undefined. (Here x = 1 is a double root, where the tangent is horizontal.)

Because f′(x₀) = 0 — Newton-Raphson requires a non-zero derivative at the current estimate.

Exercise 7.4 — Simpson's one-third rule

The rule. For even n, ∫ab f(x)dx ≈ (h/3)[f₀ + 4(f₁ + f₃ + …) + 2(f₂ + f₄ + …) + fn]. Odd-indexed points get weight 4, even interior points weight 2. n must be even — a question with n = 5 is a trick: subdivide into more intervals or state that Simpson does not apply.

Q1. Evaluate ∫₀¹ x² dx by Simpson's rule with n = 4.

h = 0.25 S = (h/3)[f₀ + 4(f₁ + f₃) + 2f₂ + f₄] = (0.25/3)[0 + 4(0.0625 + 0.5625) + 2(0.25) + 1] = 0.08333[4(0.625) + 0.5 + 1] = 0.08333[2.5 + 1.5] = 0.08333(4) = 1/3

S = 1/3 — exact, because Simpson is exact for polynomials up to degree 3

Q2. Evaluate ∫₀¹ dx/(1 + x²) by Simpson's rule with n = 4.

h = 0.25, f values as in 7.3 Q2 S = 0.08333[1 + 4(0.9412 + 0.6400) + 2(0.8000) + 0.5] = 0.08333[1 + 6.3248 + 1.6 + 0.5] = 0.08333(9.4248) = 0.7854

Exact = π/4 ≈ 0.785398 — agreement to four decimals.

S ≈ 0.7854

Q3. Evaluate ∫₁² dx/x by Simpson's rule with n = 4.

h = 0.25 S = 0.08333[1 + 4(0.8000 + 0.5714) + 2(0.6667) + 0.5] = 0.08333[1.5 + 5.4856 + 1.3334] = 0.08333(8.3190) = 0.6933

Exact: ln 2 = 0.6931.

Q4. Evaluate ∫₀² x³ dx by Simpson's rule with n = 4 and verify the result.

h = 0.5 x: 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 f = x³: 0, 0.125, 1, 3.375, 8 S = (0.5/3)[0 + 4(0.125 + 3.375) + 2(1) + 8] = 0.16667[14 + 2 + 8] = 0.16667(24) = 4

Exercise 7.5 — Statics: resultant and resolution

Q1. Two forces of 3 N and 4 N act at right angles. Find their resultant and its direction.

R = √(3² + 4²) = 5 N tanθ = 4/3 ⇒ θ = 53.13° from the 3 N force

R = 5 N at 53.13° to the 3 N force

Q2. Two forces 5 N and 8 N act at 60° to each other. Find the resultant.

Parallelogram law: R = √(P² + Q² + 2PQcosθ)

R = √(25 + 64 + 2(5)(8)(1/2)) = √(25 + 64 + 40) = √129 ≈ 11.36 N

R ≈ 11.36 N

Q3. Resolve a force of 10 N acting at 30° to the horizontal into horizontal and vertical components.

Fx = 10 cos30° = 10(√3/2) ≈ 8.66 N Fy = 10 sin30° = 10(1/2) = 5 N

8.66 N horizontal, 5 N vertical

Q4. Two equal forces P act at 120°. Show that their resultant equals P.

R = √(P² + P² + 2P²cos120°) cos120° = −1/2 R = √(2P² − P²) = √P² = P

R = P  (proved) — a classic result worth remembering

Q5. The resultant of two perpendicular forces is 13 N and one force is 5 N. Find the other force and the angle the resultant makes with it.

13² = 5² + Q² ⇒ Q² = 169 − 25 = 144 ⇒ Q = 12 N tanθ = 5/12 ⇒ θ = tan⁻¹(5/12) ≈ 22.62° from the 12 N force

Q = 12 N; resultant at ≈ 22.6° to the 12 N force

Q6. Three forces of 3 N, 4 N and 5 N act at a point. Under what arrangement is the system in equilibrium?

3 N and 4 N perpendicular have resultant 5 N. If the given 5 N force acts in the exactly opposite direction to that resultant, the three forces cancel.

Equilibrium when the 5 N force acts opposite to the resultant of the 3 N and 4 N forces (which has magnitude 5 N).

Exercise 7.6 — Dynamics: uniform acceleration

The three equations. v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as. Choose by what is missing: no s ⇒ first; no v ⇒ second; no t ⇒ third.

Q1. A body starts with 5 m/s and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 4 s. Find its final velocity and distance travelled.

v = u + at = 5 + 2(4) = 13 m/s s = ut + ½at² = 5(4) + ½(2)(16) = 20 + 16 = 36 m

v = 13 m/s, s = 36 m

Q2. A body starts from rest with acceleration 4 m/s² for 5 s. Find v and s.

v = 0 + 4(5) = 20 m/s s = 0 + ½(4)(25) = 50 m

v = 20 m/s, s = 50 m

Q3. A stone is dropped from a height of 45 m. Taking g = 10 m/s², find the time to reach the ground and the striking speed.

s = ut + ½gt² with u = 0: 45 = ½(10)t² ⇒ t² = 9 ⇒ t = 3 s v = gt = 10(3) = 30 m/s

t = 3 s, v = 30 m/s

Q4. A car moving at 20 m/s stops in 50 m. Find the retardation and the time taken.

v² = u² + 2as ⇒ 0 = 400 + 2a(50) ⇒ a = −4 m/s² v = u + at ⇒ 0 = 20 − 4t ⇒ t = 5 s

Retardation 4 m/s², time 5 s

Q5. A body slides down a smooth plane inclined at 30°. Taking g = 9.8 m/s², find its acceleration and the distance covered in 2 s from rest.

a = g sinθ = 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 m/s² s = ½at² = ½(4.9)(4) = 9.8 m

a = 4.9 m/s², s = 9.8 m

Q6. A body starts from rest with acceleration 2 m/s². Find the distance covered in the 3rd second.

Distance in the nth second: sn = u + (a/2)(2n − 1).

s₃ = 0 + (2/2)(2·3 − 1) = 1(5) = 5 m

Check by totals: s(3 s) = 9 m, s(2 s) = 4 m, difference 5 m ✓

5 m

Conceptual bank — 2-mark questions with model answers

C1. State the condition the bisection method needs before it can start.

A continuous f and an interval [a, b] with f(a) and f(b) of opposite signs, so that by the intermediate value theorem at least one root lies inside.

C2. Why does bisection always converge?

Each iteration halves the bracketing interval while keeping a sign change, so the interval width (b−a)/2ⁿ → 0. A root is trapped in a shrinking interval, hence the approximations converge.

C3. Why is Newton-Raphson faster than bisection?

Bisection halves the error each step (linear, error ×½); Newton uses the tangent and roughly squares the accuracy (quadratic convergence), typically gaining two correct decimals per iteration.

C4. Write the Newton-Raphson iteration formula.

xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f′(xn), provided f′(xn) ≠ 0.

C5. When does Newton-Raphson break down?

When f′(xn) = 0 (horizontal tangent), when the starting guess is far from the root, or when the iterates cycle — bisection is safer but slower.

C6. Why must the number of intervals n be even in Simpson's one-third rule?

The rule is built by pairing consecutive strips with weights 1-4-1; a pair needs two strips, so n must be a multiple of 2. With odd n the last strip cannot be paired and the formula does not apply.

C7. Which is more accurate for the same n — trapezoidal or Simpson's rule?

Simpson's. Trapezoidal is exact only for straight lines (error O(h²)); Simpson's is exact for cubics (error O(h⁴)), so it is dramatically better for curved integrands.

C8. Define the step size h in a numerical integration.

h = (b − a)/n, the width of each subinterval when [a,b] is divided into n equal parts.

C9. For a curve that is concave upward, does the trapezoidal rule over- or under-estimate the integral?

It over-estimates: each trapezium lies above the curve when the curve is convex (concave up), so the sum of trapezium areas exceeds the true area.

C10. Define the resultant of a system of forces.

The single force that produces the same effect on a body as the whole system — same translation and same rotation tendency.

C11. State the parallelogram law of forces.

If two forces acting at a point are represented in magnitude and direction by the adjacent sides of a parallelogram, their resultant is the diagonal through that point, with R = √(P² + Q² + 2PQcosθ).

C12. When is a particle acted on by several forces in equilibrium?

When the vector sum of the forces is zero — equivalently, the resultant has zero magnitude (the force polygon closes).

C13. Write the three equations of motion under uniform acceleration.

v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as. They hold only for constant acceleration.

C14. What is retardation, and how does it appear in the equations?

Retardation is negative acceleration (deceleration); a is taken with a minus sign, e.g. a = −4 m/s².

C15. Give the formula for the distance covered in the nth second.

sn = u + (a/2)(2n − 1), obtained as s(n) − s(n−1).

C16. What acceleration does a body of mass m experience sliding down a smooth incline of angle θ?

a = g sinθ down the plane (the normal reaction g cosθ has no component along the plane, and the plane is smooth so there is no friction).

C17. What is a numerical root of an equation?

A value of x (found by iteration, to a stated accuracy) at which f(x) ≈ 0. Numerical methods are used when the exact algebraic solution is unavailable or awkward.

C18. How many bisection steps are needed to reach an error below a given bound?

After n steps the interval length is (b − a)/2ⁿ, so n must satisfy (b − a)/2ⁿ < ε. Each step halves the bracket, giving a guaranteed but slow convergence.

C19. Why does Newton–Raphson need a good initial approximation?

The method uses the tangent at the current point; near a root the tangent points at it, but far away it can overshoot, oscillate or diverge, especially where f′ is small.

C20. Compare the order of convergence of bisection and Newton–Raphson.

Bisection is linear (error roughly halves each step); Newton–Raphson is quadratic when it converges, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles. Newton is faster but less reliable.

C21. What is the geometric meaning of the trapezoidal rule?

The area under the curve is approximated by the sum of trapeziums whose parallel sides are the ordinates and whose width is h.

C22. Why is Simpson's rule called the one-third rule?

Because the combined weight of each pair of strips is h/3 — the formula is (h/3)[(first + last) + 4(sum of odd) + 2(sum of even)].

C23. What restriction does Simpson's rule place on the number of intervals?

n must be even, so that the ordinates pair up into parabolic arcs. With an odd n the last interval must be handled separately.

C24. Which is more accurate, and why?

Simpson's rule, because it fits parabolas (quadratic through three points) rather than straight lines, so its error falls far faster as h shrinks.

C25. Define numerical integration and say when it is needed.

It approximates ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx using sampled ordinates. It is needed when f has no elementary antiderivative or exists only as a table of values.

C26. What is a transcendental equation? Give an example.

An equation mixing algebraic and non-algebraic functions, e.g. cos x = x or x eˣ = 1. Such equations have no closed algebraic solution, so numerical methods are used.

C27. If f(a)f(b) > 0, is it certain that no root lies in [a, b]?

No. An even number of roots (or a root where f does not change sign, as in a tangency) can hide in the interval. A sign change guarantees a root, but its absence does not rule roots out.

C28. Why does the bisection method rely on continuity?

The existence of a root follows from the intermediate value theorem, which needs f continuous on [a, b]. Without continuity a sign change proves nothing.

C29. Distinguish statics from dynamics.

Statics deals with bodies at rest or in equilibrium (net force and net moment both zero); dynamics deals with bodies in motion and the forces producing it.

C30. State the conditions for equilibrium of a body.

ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and the net moment about any point is zero. Three non-parallel forces in equilibrium meet at a point.

C31. Distinguish distance from displacement.

Distance is the total path length (a scalar, never decreasing); displacement is the straight-line change of position (a vector, which can decrease to zero).

C32. What is uniform acceleration and what does it preserve?

Acceleration of constant magnitude and direction, so velocity changes linearly with time and the standard equations v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as apply.

C33. State the sign convention used in kinematics.

Pick one direction as positive; then velocity, acceleration and displacement along the opposite direction all carry a negative sign. Mixing signs is the commonest source of wrong answers.

C34. On a smooth incline of angle θ, how does the acceleration depend on θ?

a = g sin θ. It increases with the inclination, since the component of gravity along the plane grows; at θ = 90° it equals free fall.

C35. What happens to the range if the launch speed doubles?

Horizontal range R = u²sin2θ/g is proportional to u², so doubling u quadruples the range for the same angle.

C36. Why is the error in the trapezoidal rule usually one-sided?

Straight chords lie wholly above the curve for convex (concave-up) functions and wholly below for concave-down ones, so the approximation is consistently an over- or under-estimate rather than random.

Trap 1 — starting bisection without a sign change. If f(a) and f(b) have the same sign there may be no root, or two roots, in the interval; the method's guarantee is lost.

Trap 2 — using Simpson with odd n. A 5-interval Simpson question is deliberately wrong; n must be even. Use n = 4 or 6, or switch to trapezoidal.

Trap 3 — wrong weights. Trapezoidal: 1-2-2-…-2-1. Simpson: 1-4-2-4-…-1. Swapping them is the single most common error in this unit.

Trap 4 — rounding too early. Keep four decimals in intermediate f(x) values; rounding to one decimal destroys the accuracy claim.

Trap 5 — using v² = u² + 2as with changing acceleration. These equations are valid only while a is constant; for two stages, solve each stage separately.

Trap 6 — sign of g. Take upward positive and g negative, or downward positive and g positive — but keep the choice consistent through the whole solution.

Trap 7 — reporting a resultant without a direction. A force has magnitude and direction; a numerical answer alone is incomplete.

Table-first habit. In every numerical method question, draw the table before computing anything else: x-row, f(x)-row, then the weighted sum. This earns the method marks even if the final arithmetic slips, and it makes errors visible.
