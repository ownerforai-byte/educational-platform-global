Mirror Formula

#Concept
1A spherical reflecting surface acts as a spherical mirror. For paraxial rays, a concave mirror converges parallel rays to a real focus; a convex mirror makes them diverge from a virtual focus.
2The centre of curvature C is distance R from pole P; paraxial focus F is halfway between, so f=R/2. Wide-angle marginal rays do not meet this approximation exactly.
3Using Cartesian distances positive to the right, a concave mirror has f=−15 cm. For u=−30 cm, 1/v=1/f−1/u=−1/15+1/30=−1/30, so v=−30 cm and m=−v/u=−1.
4Construct an image with a ray parallel to the principal axis (reflected through F for a concave mirror) and a ray through C (retraces itself). Their intersection locates a real image; backward extensions locate a virtual image.
5Spherical aberration occurs when marginal rays focus differently from paraxial rays. A smaller aperture reduces it by excluding marginal rays.

Key Points

Point
Keep mirror signs consistent with the stated convention.
R is measured from pole to centre of curvature.
Aperture reduction suppresses marginal-ray aberration.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not substitute the thin-lens equation for the mirror equation.
f=R/2 assumes a spherical reflecting surface and small angles.

Summary: Curved-mirror image formation follows reflection geometry. The paraxial focus lies halfway to the centre of curvature, and signed equations agree with ray constructions.

Real and Virtual Images

#Concept
1A real image forms where reflected rays physically converge. It can be projected onto a screen and is commonly inverted for a single concave-mirror image of a real object beyond F.
2A virtual image forms where reflected rays only appear to originate when extended backward. It cannot be projected on a screen; a plane mirror forms an upright virtual image behind the mirror.
3For a concave mirror, an object beyond F generally gives a real image; an object between pole and F gives a virtual, upright, magnified image. A convex mirror gives a virtual, upright, diminished image for a real object.
4Ray test: follow reflected rays forward. If they meet, the image is real; if only backward extensions meet, it is virtual. This geometric test is more reliable than judging whether the image seems behind the mirror.
5Example: a shaving mirror used close to the face has the face inside the concave mirror’s focal length, producing an upright virtual magnified image.

Key Points

Point
Real rays meet; virtual rays do not.
Object position controls mirror image type.
A screen test distinguishes a real image.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Virtual describes ray geometry, not image brightness.
State the equation’s sign convention when giving image distances.

Summary: Ray geometry defines image type: physical convergence forms a real image, while backward extensions form a virtual image. This predicts screen projection and orientation.

Academic Extension: Evidence, Conditions & Open Questions

Topic bridge — Reflection At Curved Mirror. Use this extension to connect the topic’s core material with deeper academic inquiry: state a claim, name the conditions under which it holds, and distinguish established knowledge from an open research or interpretive question.

Established academic fact

Ray optics predicts reflection, image formation and refraction well when apertures and objects are much larger than the wavelength. The wave model explains interference and diffraction that ray diagrams cannot.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

A thin-lens or mirror equation assumes ideal geometry and a paraxial approximation (rays close to the principal axis and making small angles). Real aberrations and finite apertures limit the ideal image.

Open research question — What is dark matter made of?

Astronomical observations provide strong evidence for unseen mass through gravitational effects, but its particle identity has not been established. Researchers test candidate explanations by looking for distinct signals; an open question about the cosmos does not make established classroom models false.
