Laws of Refraction and Refractive Index

#Concept
1**Snell's law (First law of refraction):** When light passes obliquely from one medium to another, $\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$, where $i$ is the angle of incidence, $r$ is the angle of refraction, and $n_1$, $n_2$ are the refractive indices of the first and second media respectively. This law is the basis of all refraction phenomena.
2**Refractive index definition:** $n = \frac{c}{v}$, where $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\,\text{m/s}$ is the speed of light in vacuum and $v$ is its speed in the medium. Since $v < c$ always, $n > 1$ for all real materials. Higher $n$ means light travels slower in that medium.
3**Relative refractive index:** $n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$. This is the refractive index of medium 2 as seen from medium 1. When light enters a denser medium ($n_2 > n_1$), it bends TOWARD the normal. When entering a rarer medium, it bends AWAY from the normal.
4**Laws of reflection (concurrent with refraction):** The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection ($i = r$). These laws apply at both the reflecting and refracting surfaces.
5**Dispersion of light:** White light splits into its constituent colors when refracted because different wavelengths travel at different speeds in a medium. Red (long λ) has $n_{red} < n_{blue}$ (short λ). This is why prisms disperse white light into a spectrum.
6**Rainbow formation (primary rainbow):** Water droplets act as spherical prisms. Light enters, refracts, reflects once inside the droplet, then refracts again on exit. Red emerges at about 42° and violet at about 40° from the backscattering direction. The secondary rainbow (24°) forms from two internal reflections.
7**Dispersive power of a material:** $\omega = \frac{n_F - n_C}{n_D}$, where $n_F$, $n_C$, $n_D$ are the refractive indices for blue (Fraunhofer F), red (Fraunhofer C), and yellow (Fraunhofer D, sodium D line) light respectively. Higher ω means more dispersion.
8**Abbe number:** $V_D = \frac{n_D - 1}{n_F - n_C}$. This is the reciprocal of dispersive power, scaled and shifted. Gases have $V_D \gg 1$ (negligible dispersion). Crown glass has $V_D \approx 60$, flint glass $V_D \approx 30$.
9**Limiting cases of Snell's law:** When $i = 0°$: $r = 0°$ (normal incidence, no bending). When $n_2 \ll n_1$: the ray undergoes total internal reflection if $i > 90°$, which is impossible, so this explains why very high-index materials can guide light.
10**Critical angle derivation:** At the critical angle, $r = 90°$, so $\sin i_c = \frac{1}{n_2/n_1} = \frac{n_1}{n_2}$. In terms of refractive indices: $\sin i_c = \frac{n_{rarer}}{n_{denser}}$. For glass ($n = 1.52$): $i_c = \arcsin(1/1.52) \approx 41.1°$.
11**Total Internal Reflection (TIR):** When $i > i_c$, no refracted ray exists. 100% of the light is reflected back into the denser medium. This is COMPLETELY different from ordinary reflection at a surface where typically 85-95% is reflected.
12**NEB exam focus:** Apply Snell's law to find angles of incidence and refraction. Calculate refractive indices from speed of light data. Understand the conditions for TIR and calculate critical angles. Draw accurate ray diagrams showing refraction at plane surfaces.
13**Evanescent wave and frustrated TIR:** Just below the critical angle, an evanescent wave penetrates the rarer medium and decays exponentially. If another medium is brought within ~1 wavelength, this wave can transfer energy — this is the principle behind atomic force microscopy and photon tunneling sensors.
14**Practical applications — prisms:** At least 40° apex angle prisms rely on TIR (as in Porro prisms and binocular roof prisms). Right-angle prisms use TIR at two internal surfaces. This provides 100% reflection without coating, unlike mirrors which lose 10-15% per reflection.
15**Astronomical applications — refractive index of air:** Even air has $n \approx 1.0003$ at STP. This causes atmospheric refraction, making stars appear ~0.5° higher than their true positions. This effect must be corrected in precise astronomical measurements.
16**Biological applications — eye lens:** The human crystalline lens has varying refractive index from center to periphery and from front to back, allowing it to focus light onto the retina for all distances. The cornea contributes ~70% of the eye's refractive power ($n_{cornea} \approx 1.376$).
17**Wave optical interpretation:** The phase change upon reflection depends on the relative indices. When reflecting off a medium with higher $n$, there is a 180° phase shift (equivalent to a path difference of λ/2). This determines whether thin film interference is constructive or destructive.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Snell's law is $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$ with no mention of which is larger. ✅ Snell's law shows that when $n_2 > n_1$: $\sin\theta_2 < \sin\theta_1$, so $r < i$ (ray bends toward normal). When $n_2 < n_1$: $r > i$ (ray bends away from normal). The direction of bending is determined by the relative densities.
❌ The refractive index $n$ can be less than 1. ✅ For real materials at optical frequencies, $n > 1$ because $v < c$. In some special cases (metamaterials at microwave frequencies), engineered materials can have $n < 1$, but this doesn't apply to standard optical media.
❌ A larger refractive index means faster light in that medium. ✅ LARGER refractive index means SLOWER light. The relationship is $n = c/v$: larger $n$ means smaller $v$. Diamond ($n \approx 2.4$) is optically the fastest glass.
❌ The critical angle formula is $\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_1}{n_2}$. ✅ The correct formula is $\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$ where $n_1 > n_2$ (denser to rarer). Using $\frac{n_1}{n_2} \approx \frac{1.52}{1} = 1.52 > 1$ would give $\sin\theta_c > 1$, which is impossible for real angles.
❌ Total internal reflection means all light is absorbed and disappears. ✅ TIR means ALL light is REFLECTED back. No transmission occurs, but the light energy is preserved in the reflected beam.
❌ Dispersion only happens with prisms. ✅ Dispersion occurs at EVERY refracting surface. Prisms make dispersion visible because they have large apex angles, but rainbows form from millions of tiny atmospheric droplets acting as prisms.
❌ The angle of refraction is always greater than the angle of incidence. ✅ Refraction angle is SMALLER than incidence angle when entering denser medium, and LARGER when entering rarer medium. The ray bends toward normal in denser medium, away in rarer medium.

Laws of Refraction: Refractive Index

#Concept
1Refraction: Bending of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another due to change in speed of light in different media.
2Laws of Refraction (Snell's Laws): (1) Incident ray, refracted ray, and normal at point of incidence all lie in the same plane. (2) The ratio of sine of angle of incidence to sine of angle of refraction is constant for a given pair of media: sin i / sin r = constant = ₂₁μ (refractive index of second medium w.r.t. first).
3Refractive Index: ₂₁μ = v₁/v₂ = sin i / sin r. Absolute refractive index μ = c/v (ratio of speed in vacuum to speed in medium). μ ≥ 1, μ_vacuum = 1, μ_air ≈ 1.0003, μ_water ≈ 1.33, μ_glass ≈ 1.5, μ_diamond ≈ 2.42.
4Optical Density: Medium with higher refractive index is optically denser. Light bends towards normal when entering denser medium, away from normal when entering rarer medium.
5Lateral Shift: When light passes through a parallel-sided glass slab, emergent ray is parallel to incident ray but laterally displaced. Shift S = t·sin(i-r)/cos r where t = thickness.
6Real and Apparent Depth: Object in denser medium viewed from rarer appears nearer: apparent depth = real depth / μ. Conversely, object in rarer viewed from denser appears farther: apparent depth = μ × real depth.
7Refraction Through Multiple Media: sin i / sin r = constant at each interface. For parallel interfaces, emergent ray direction depends only on first and last medium: sin i₁ / sin r_final = μ_final/μ_initial.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing bending direction: Towards normal when entering denser (slower) medium; away when entering rarer.
Thinking apparent depth formula works both ways: d_app = d_real/μ only when viewing from rarer medium.
Believing frequency changes in refraction: Frequency stays constant; speed and wavelength change.
Confusing refractive index μ with relative index ₂₁μ.
Forgetting incident ray, refracted ray, normal are coplanar (first law).

Summary: Refraction is bending of light at medium interface due to speed change. Snell's law: sin i/sin r = μ. Absolute index μ = c/v. Light bends towards normal in denser medium. Parallel slab produces lateral shift. Apparent depth = real depth/μ (viewing from rarer).

Laws of Refraction

#Concept
1**Refraction.** Light bends while crossing obliquely between media of different optical density because its speed changes; it bends towards the normal entering a denser medium and away from it leaving.
2**Snell's law.** For a ray crossing the boundary, n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r, with the incident ray, refracted ray and normal all in one plane and sin i/sin r constant for the pair of media.
3**Refractive index.** The absolute index of a medium is n = c/v; the relative index of medium 2 w.r.t. 1 is n₂₁ = v₁/v₂ = sin i/sin r = 1/n₁₂.
4**Apparent depth.** Viewed normally, a depth t appears as t/n; the normal shift is t(1 − 1/n) — the standard numerical on river depth and pooled objects.
5**Dispersion's origin.** Because v depends on wavelength, n_violet > n_red; white light refracting at a surface separates colour — the seed of the prism spectrum.

Lateral Shift

#Concept
1**Lateral Shift:** This topic covers the fundamental concepts of lateral shift in the physics curriculum.
2Understanding lateral shift is essential for solving problems and building higher-level mathematical skills.
3Key definitions and theorems related to lateral shift should be memorized and practiced regularly.
4**Definition:** When a ray of light passes through a parallel-sided transparent slab (like a glass slab), it emerges parallel to the original direction but displaced sideways. This perpendicular displacement is called the lateral shift (or lateral displacement).
5**Formula:** $d = t \cdot \frac{\sin(i - r)}{\cos r}$, where $t$ is the thickness of the slab, $i$ is the angle of incidence, and $r$ is the angle of refraction.
6**Derivation:** Inside the slab, the ray travels at angle $r$ to the normal. The horizontal distance traveled inside the slab is $t/\cos r$. The lateral shift is this distance multiplied by $\sin(i - r)$: $d = (t/\cos r) \cdot \sin(i - r)$.
7**Special case — normal incidence:** When $i = 0°$, $d = 0$. The ray passes straight through without any lateral shift. This is consistent with Snell's law: $r = 0$ when $i = 0$.
8**Dependence on thickness:** The lateral shift is directly proportional to the slab thickness $t$. A thicker slab produces a larger shift. Doubling the thickness doubles the shift.
9**Dependence on angle of incidence:** As $i$ increases from 0°, the lateral shift increases. At grazing incidence ($i \to 90°$), the shift approaches the thickness $t$ (the ray travels almost parallel to the slab faces).
10**Dependence on refractive index:** For a fixed angle of incidence, a higher refractive index means a larger refraction angle $r$ is smaller, so $(i - r)$ is larger, and the shift is larger. Denser materials produce more lateral shift.
11**No angular deviation:** The emergent ray is PARALLEL to the incident ray. The slab causes a lateral displacement but no change in direction. This is because the two refractions (entry and exit) cancel each other's angular effect.
12**Apparent shift of objects:** When you look through a glass window at an angle, objects appear slightly displaced from their true positions. This is due to lateral shift. The effect is small for thin windows but noticeable with thick glass blocks.
13**Multiple slabs:** If light passes through multiple parallel slabs of different materials, the total lateral shift is the sum of individual shifts: $d_{total} = \sum t_k \cdot \frac{\sin(i_k - r_k)}{\cos r_k}$, where each $i_k$ and $r_k$ is determined by Snell's law at each interface.
14**NEB exam focus:** Derive the lateral shift formula. Calculate the shift for given values of $t$, $i$, and $n$. Explain why the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray. Draw ray diagrams showing the shift.
15**Numerical example:** A glass slab ($n = 1.5$) of thickness 10 cm is illuminated by a ray at $i = 60°$. First find $r$: $\sin r = \sin 60°/1.5 = 0.866/1.5 = 0.5774$, so $r = 35.3°$. Then $d = 10 \cdot \frac{\sin(60° - 35.3°)}{\cos 35.3°} = 10 \cdot \frac{\sin 24.7°}{0.816} = 10 \cdot \frac{0.418}{0.816} = 5.12$ cm.
16**Why the emergent ray is parallel:** At the first interface, the ray bends toward the normal by angle $(i - r)$. At the second interface, it bends away from the normal by the same angle $(i - r)$ because the surfaces are parallel and the geometry is symmetric. Net angular change = 0.
17**Application — periscopes and binoculars:** Parallel-sided glass blocks are used in optical instruments to displace the light path without changing its direction. This allows compact instrument design while maintaining the line of sight.
18**Connection to prism:** A prism has non-parallel faces, so the emergent ray is NOT parallel to the incident ray — it is deviated by angle $\delta = i + e - A$. The lateral shift formula does not apply to prisms.

Practice with multiple examples to solidify your understanding.
❌ The emergent ray is deviated from the original direction. ✅ The emergent ray is PARALLEL to the incident ray. A parallel-sided slab causes lateral shift but NO angular deviation.
❌ Lateral shift depends on the refractive index of the slab material only. ✅ Lateral shift depends on ALL THREE: thickness $t$, angle of incidence $i$, AND refractive index $n$ (through the refraction angle $r$).
❌ At normal incidence, there is a lateral shift. ✅ At normal incidence ($i = 0°$), the lateral shift is ZERO. The ray passes straight through without any displacement.

Relation Between Refractive Indices

#Concept
1**Absolute refractive index:** The refractive index of a medium measured relative to vacuum (or air, approximately). $n = \frac{c}{v}$, where $c = 3.00 \times 10^8$ m/s is the speed of light in vacuum and $v$ is the speed in the medium.
2**Relative refractive index $n_{21}$:** When light passes from medium 1 to medium 2, the relative refractive index is $n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$. It tells how much slower (or faster) light travels in medium 2 compared to medium 1.
3**Snell's law with relative index:** $n_{21} = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}$, where $i$ is the angle of incidence in medium 1 and $r$ is the angle of refraction in medium 2. This is the original form of Snell's law.
4**Reciprocity relation:** $n_{12} = \frac{1}{n_{21}}$. The refractive index of medium 1 relative to medium 2 is the reciprocal of the refractive index of medium 2 relative to medium 1. This follows directly from $n_{12} = n_1/n_2$.
5**Multiplicative chain rule:** For light passing through multiple media: $n_{31} = n_{32} \times n_{21}$. For example, air → water → glass: $n_{glass/air} = n_{glass/water} \times n_{water/air}$. This is useful for calculating the overall deviation through a stack of materials.
6**Speed of light in different media:** $v = c/n$. In water ($n = 1.33$): $v = 2.26 \times 10^8$ m/s. In glass ($n = 1.52$): $v = 1.97 \times 10^8$ m/s. In diamond ($n = 2.42$): $v = 1.24 \times 10^8$ m/s. Light slows down in denser media.
7**Wavelength changes, frequency stays constant:** When light enters a medium, its speed changes but its frequency remains the same (determined by the source). Therefore: $\lambda_{medium} = \frac{\lambda_{vacuum}}{n}$. The wavelength shortens in denser media.
8**Refractive index and optical density:** A medium with higher $n$ is optically denser. Light travels slower in optically denser media. Note: optical density is NOT the same as mass density (e.g., oil has lower mass density than water but can have a higher refractive index).
9**Practical example — diving board apparent depth:** A diver looking down at a pool floor sees it at apparent depth $d' = d/n$, where $d$ is the real depth. For water ($n = 1.33$), a 3 m deep pool appears to be $3/1.33 = 2.25$ m deep.
10**NEB exam focus:** Distinguish between absolute and relative refractive index. Derive $n_{31} = n_{32} \times n_{21}$. Solve problems involving light passing through multiple media. Calculate apparent depth and real depth.
11**Why relative index matters:** In compound lens systems (camera lenses, microscopes), light passes through multiple glass-air interfaces. The relative refractive index at each interface determines the bending angle and overall focal length.
12**Refractive index and color:** The refractive index depends on wavelength (dispersion). Blue light has a slightly higher $n$ than red light in most glasses. This causes chromatic aberration in lenses — different colors focus at different points.
13**Fresnel equations connection:** The reflection coefficient at an interface depends on the relative refractive index. For normal incidence: $R = \left(\frac{n_2 - n_1}{n_2 + n_1}\right)^2$. At an air-glass interface ($n_1 = 1, n_2 = 1.52$): $R = (0.52/2.52)^2 = 0.043$ (4.3% reflection).
14**Index matching:** When two media have the same refractive index ($n_1 = n_2$), there is no refraction at the interface — light passes straight through as if the boundary doesn't exist. This principle is used in immersion oil microscopy to increase resolution.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ The refractive index can be less than 1. ✅ For normal materials, $n \geq 1$ because light travels slowest in vacuum. However, in specialized materials (plasmas, metamaterials), the phase velocity can exceed $c$, giving $n < 1$ — but information still travels at $v \leq c$.
❌ A higher refractive index means a denser material (more mass per volume). ✅ Optical density and mass density are different. Oil ($\rho \approx 0.9$ g/cm³) can have $n \approx 1.47$, while water ($\rho = 1.0$ g/cm³) has $n = 1.33$. Oil is optically denser but mass-wise lighter.
❌ $n_{21} = n_2 - n_1$. ✅ The relative refractive index is a RATIO, not a difference: $n_{21} = n_2/n_1$. This is crucial for correct application of Snell's law.
❌ The frequency of light changes when it enters a new medium. ✅ The FREQUENCY remains constant. Only the speed and wavelength change. $f = c/\lambda_{vacuum} = v/\lambda_{medium}$.
❌ Light always bends toward the normal when entering a new medium. ✅ Light bends TOWARD the normal only when entering a denser medium ($n_2 > n_1$). When entering a rarer medium ($n_2 < n_1$), it bends AWAY from the normal.
❌ The chain rule $n_{31} = n_{32} \times n_{21}$ only works for three media. ✅ The chain rule generalizes to any number of media: $n_{nm} = n_{n,n-1} \times n_{n-1,n-2} \times \cdots \times n_{21}$.
❌ The refractive index is the same for all colors of light. ✅ The refractive index decreases with increasing wavelength (normal dispersion). Blue light ($\lambda \approx 450$ nm) has a higher $n$ than red light ($\lambda \approx 700$ nm) in most glasses.

Total Internal Reflection

#Concept
1**Total Internal Reflection:** This topic covers the fundamental concepts of total internal reflection in the physics curriculum.
2Understanding total internal reflection is essential for solving problems and building higher-level mathematical skills.
3Key definitions and theorems related to total internal reflection should be memorized and practiced regularly.
4**Definition:** Total internal reflection (TIR) occurs when a ray of light traveling in a denser medium strikes the boundary with a rarer medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle. All the light is reflected back into the denser medium — no refraction occurs.
5**Two essential conditions:** (1) Light must travel from a DENSER medium to a RARER medium ($n_1 > n_2$). (2) The angle of incidence must EXCEED the critical angle ($i > \theta_c$).
6**Critical angle formula:** $\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$, where $n_1$ is the refractive index of the denser medium and $n_2$ is the refractive index of the rarer medium. At the critical angle, the refracted ray travels along the boundary ($r = 90°$).
7**Critical angle for common materials:** Glass-to-air ($n = 1.52$): $\theta_c = \arcsin(1/1.52) = 41.1°$. Water-to-air ($n = 1.33$): $\theta_c = \arcsin(1/1.33) = 48.8°$. Diamond-to-air ($n = 2.42$): $\theta_c = \arcsin(1/2.42) = 24.4°$.
8**What happens at different angles:** If $i < \theta_c$: partial reflection + refraction (most light transmits). If $i = \theta_c$: refraction at 90° (along the boundary). If $i > \theta_c$: TOTAL internal reflection (100% reflection, no transmission).
9**Why TIR is otal:** Unlike mirror reflection (which absorbs 5–15% of light), TIR reflects 100% of the light. This makes it far more efficient than metallic mirrors for bending light paths in optical instruments.
10**Optical fibre principle:** An optical fibre consists of a core (high $n$) surrounded by cladding (lower $n$). Light entering the core at angles greater than the critical angle undergoes repeated TIR, traveling kilometers with minimal loss. This is the basis of modern telecommunications.
11**Diamond sparkle:** Diamond's high refractive index ($n = 2.42$) gives a very small critical angle (24.4°). Light entering a well-cut diamond undergoes multiple TIR events before exiting, creating the characteristic brilliant flashes of color (fire).
12**Mirage phenomenon:** On a hot road, air near the surface is hotter and less dense (lower $n$) than air above. Light from the sky bends away from the normal as it passes through increasingly rarer layers. At a certain height, the angle exceeds the critical angle and TIR occurs, creating the illusion of water on the road.
13**Prism binoculars:** Porro prisms in binoculars use TIR at the hypotenuse face (glass-to-air at 45°, which exceeds the critical angle of ~42° for glass). This provides reflection without the need for a silvered coating — brighter images with no degradation.
14**NEB exam focus:** State the two conditions for TIR. Derive the critical angle formula. Calculate critical angles for common material pairs. Explain optical fibres and mirages using TIR. Draw ray diagrams showing TIR in prisms and fibres.
15**Fibre optic numbers — acceptance angle:** The maximum angle at which light can enter an optical fibre and still undergo TIR is called the acceptance angle. $\sin\theta_a = \sqrt{n_{core}^2 - n_{clad}^2}$. For typical fibres ($n_{core} = 1.48$, $n_{clad} = 1.46$): $\theta_a \approx 16°$.
16**Optical fibre types:** (1) Step-index fibre: core and cladding have uniform but different indices. (2) Graded-index fibre: the core's index gradually decreases from center to edge, causing light to follow curved paths and reducing modal dispersion.
17**Applications of TIR:** (1) Optical fibres for communication and endoscopy. (2) Prism reflectors in binoculars and periscopes. (3) Diamond cutting for maximum brilliance. (4) Automotive rain sensors (TIR is disrupted by water droplets on the windshield). (5) Fiber-optic sensors for temperature and pressure.
18**Partial reflection at TIR:** In reality, at angles just above the critical angle, a small evanescent wave penetrates into the rarer medium (decaying exponentially over a distance of about one wavelength). This is the principle behind frustrated TIR and near-field optical microscopy.
19**Energy conservation in TIR:** Since no light is transmitted, all the incident energy is reflected. The intensity of the reflected ray equals the intensity of the incident ray (ignoring absorption in the medium). This is why TIR prisms are preferred over silvered mirrors in precision optics.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Carefully distinguish between similar-looking concepts in total internal reflection.
Pay attention to the conditions under which formulas and theorems apply.
Practice with multiple examples to solidify your understanding.
❌ TIR can occur when light goes from rarer to denser medium. ✅ TIR ONLY occurs when light travels from DENSER to RARER medium ($n_1 > n_2$). From rarer to denser, the ray always bends toward the normal and always enters the second medium.
❌ The critical angle exists for any pair of media. ✅ The critical angle only exists when $n_1 > n_2$. If light goes from air to glass ($n_1 < n_2$), there is no critical angle — refraction always occurs.
❌ At the critical angle, all light is reflected. ✅ At the critical angle ($i = \theta_c$), the refracted ray travels ALONG the boundary ($r = 90°$). TIR begins only when $i > \theta_c$, not at $i = \theta_c$.
❌ TIR produces a dimmer reflection than a mirror. ✅ TIR reflects 100% of the light — it is BRIGHTER than any metallic mirror, which typically absorbs 5–15% of incident light.
❌ Optical fibres work because light is "trapped" by the cladding. ✅ Optical fibres work because of TIR at the core-cladding interface. The cladding has a LOWER refractive index than the core, creating the condition for TIR.
❌ The critical angle for diamond is about 42°. ✅ Diamond's critical angle is about 24.4° (much smaller than glass's 42°). This small critical angle is what makes diamonds so brilliant — light undergoes multiple TIR events before exiting.
❌ TIR can occur at any interface, including solid-solid interfaces. ✅ TIR requires a boundary between two media with DIFFERENT refractive indices. It can occur at any such boundary (glass-air, water-glass, fibre core-cladding) as long as light travels from the higher-index to the lower-index medium.

Established academic fact

Ray optics predicts reflection, image formation and refraction well when apertures and objects are much larger than the wavelength. The wave model explains interference and diffraction that ray diagrams cannot.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

A thin-lens or mirror equation assumes ideal geometry and a paraxial approximation (rays close to the principal axis and making small angles). Real aberrations and finite apertures limit the ideal image.

Open research question — What is dark matter made of?

Astronomical observations provide strong evidence for unseen mass through gravitational effects, but its particle identity has not been established.
