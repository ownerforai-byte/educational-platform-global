Dalton's Atomic Theory and its Postulates

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning and background
2John Dalton (1803–1808, *A New System of Chemical Philosophy*) proposed the first quantitative atomic theory. Its purpose was to explain the experimentally discovered laws of chemical combination (conservation of mass, definite proportions, multiple proportions) by assuming that matter is made of discrete, indestructible particles called **atoms**. The theory marks the birth of modern chemistry because it turned qualitative ideas about matter into a testable, quantitative model.
3### 2. Postulates (state them in order)
4• Matter is composed of extremely small, indivisible particles called atoms.
5• Atoms of the same element are identical in mass, size and chemical properties; atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.
6• Atoms cannot be created, destroyed or divided by chemical reactions — a chemical change only rearranges atoms (this explains the conservation of mass).
7• Atoms of different elements combine in fixed, simple whole-number ratios to form compounds (this explains the law of definite proportions and the law of multiple proportions).
8• The atom is the smallest particle that can take part in a chemical reaction; in a compound, atoms are held together by chemical attraction.
9• Atoms of one element cannot be converted into atoms of another element by ordinary chemical means.
10### 3. Successes (why it was accepted)
11It explained the law of conservation of mass, the law of definite (constant) proportions and the law of multiple proportions by pure logic, provided the basis for balancing equations and hence for all stoichiometric calculation, introduced the idea of atomic (relative) masses, and prepared the ground for the mole concept and Avogadro's molecular hypothesis.
12### 4. Applications
13• Quantitative chemistry: balanced equations, mole calculations, limiting-reactant and yield problems all rest on atoms being conserved and combining in fixed ratios.
14• Determination of atomic and molecular masses; formulation of the atomic mass unit and relative atomic mass scale.
15• Basis of the modern atomic model (with the modifications listed below), of stoichiometry-based industry (fertilisers, metallurgy, cement, pharmaceuticals) and of chemical analysis by mass balance.
16### 5. Limitations and modifications (exam-critical)
171. **Atoms are divisible:** the discovery of the electron (Thomson, 1897), proton (Rutherford, 1919) and neutron (Chadwick, 1932) showed that atoms contain subatomic particles and can be split.
182. **Not all atoms of an element are identical:** isotopes (¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C; ³⁵Cl, ³⁷Cl) have the same chemical properties but different masses.
193. **Atoms of different elements can have the same mass:** isobars such as ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca.
204. **Atoms of one element can be changed into another:** nuclear reactions and transmutation (¹⁴N + α → ¹⁷O + p; radioactivity, fission and fusion) achieve exactly what Dalton denied.
215. **Mass is not strictly conserved** in nuclear processes — the mass defect appears as energy according to E = mc², so the conservation-of-mass postulate is only valid for ordinary chemical reactions.
226. **It did not explain** the nature of the force holding atoms together (chemical bonding), the structure of the atom, the law of gaseous volumes and the law of reciprocal proportions; it also could not account for allotropes, giant molecules or the existence of diatomic element molecules (H₂, O₂, N₂) until Avogadro's molecular concept.
23**Summing up:** Dalton's core idea — that matter is atomic and that reactions rearrange atoms — is still valid; his absolute statements about indivisibility, identical masses and non-convertibility are the parts that modern chemistry and physics have modified.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Dalton's atomic theory is completely wrong today. ✅ Its core (matter is atomic and reactions only rearrange atoms) is fully valid; only its absolute claims about indivisibility, identical masses and non-convertibility have been modified.
❌ Atoms of the same element always have the same mass. ✅ Isotopes such as ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl differ in mass while remaining chemically similar.
❌ Dalton explained the law of gaseous volumes. ✅ That was Gay-Lussac (1809), and Avogadro's hypothesis was needed to interpret it — Dalton's theory by itself could not accommodate it.
❌ Nuclear reactions obey Dalton's postulates. ✅ They violate them: atoms are split, elements change and mass is converted into energy.
❌ Dalton's theory explained chemical bonding. ✅ It assumed atoms are held together by 'chemical attraction' without explaining its nature.

Law of Conservation of Mass — Statement, Verification and Applications

#Concept
1### Focus of this tab
2The first law of chemical combination in depth — statement, historical verification, microscopic explanation and its one important limitation.
3### Statement and history
4'In a chemical reaction, matter can neither be created nor destroyed; the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.' Established by Antoine Lavoisier (1774) from careful weighings — e.g. mercury calx (HgO) heated in a sealed retort gave mercury and oxygen with no loss of mass — the work that made chemistry quantitative.
5### Experimental verification (Landolt, 1893)
6Landolt placed two reacting solutions (AgNO₃ and NaCl) in separate limbs of an H-shaped sealed tube, weighed it, inverted to mix, waited for complete precipitation (AgCl), and reweighed — mass unchanged within 1 part in 10⁷. Modern verification uses any precipitation, neutralisation or redox reaction carried out in a sealed system.
7### Microscopic explanation
8A reaction only rearranges atoms: bonds break and form, but no atom disappears or appears (Dalton's third postulate). The equation must therefore be balanced — atom counts, and hence masses, are equal on both sides. This is why balancing is the first step of every stoichiometric calculation.
9### What must be included in the weighing
10All gaseous reactants/products (CO₂ escaping an open flask breaks the balance), the solvent, the precipitate, and the container. The classic school error: burning magnesium on an open balance shows a mass *gain* — oxygen has entered; in a sealed flask the mass is constant.
11### Limitation (important)
12The law holds for **chemical** reactions only. In nuclear reactions (fission, fusion, radioactivity) a measurable mass defect converts to energy: E = Δmc² — mass and energy are jointly conserved. Even in chemistry the energy change has a mass equivalent, but it is ≈10⁻¹⁰ of the reacting mass, far below weighing precision.
13### Applications
14Balancing equations; material balances in industry (input = output accounting for ammonia, cement, steel plants); gravimetric analysis; detecting loss of product (leaks, volatilisation); and every 'how much product' calculation in stoichiometry.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Magnesium burning in air contradicts the law because the ash is heavier. ✅ The extra mass is the oxygen that combined; in a closed system the total is constant.
❌ The law applies to nuclear reactions. ✅ It is limited to chemical reactions; nuclear processes show mass–energy equivalence.
❌ A balanced equation conserves molecules. ✅ It conserves atoms and mass; molecular counts may change (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: 4 molecules → 2).

Law of Definite (Constant) Proportions — Statement, Examples and Limitations

#Concept
1### Focus of this tab
2The second law of chemical combination: Proust's law of definite proportions with its classic percentage data, the calculations it supports, and the compounds that break it.
3### Statement and history
4'A chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed proportion by mass, regardless of its source or method of preparation.' Established by Joseph Proust (1799) against Berthollet, who believed composition could vary continuously with conditions.
5### The classic data
6• Water: from river, sea, rain or synthesised from H₂ + O₂ — H : O = 1 : 8 by mass (11.1% H, 88.9% O).
7• Carbon dioxide: 27.27% C, 72.73% O (C : O = 3 : 8) whether from burning carbon, respiration or limestone decomposition.
8• Copper oxide: 79.9% Cu, 20.1% O whether prepared by heating copper in air or reducing CuO in hydrogen.
9### Why it holds
10A pure compound has a fixed formula (H₂O, CO₂, CuO): fixed atom counts and fixed atomic masses give a fixed mass ratio — a direct consequence of Dalton's theory (the law was actually strong evidence *for* atoms).
11### Calculations it supports
12• Percentage composition: % element = (mass of element in formula ÷ molar mass) × 100.
13• Mass of element in a given sample: sample mass × (%/100) — e.g. oxygen in 90 g of water = 90 × (8/9) = 80 g.
14• Purity analysis: a brass sample or an ore is assayed by comparing its metal percentage with the theoretical value for the pure compound.
15### Limitations (where the law fails)
161. **Non-stoichiometric (berthollide) compounds:** Fe₀.₉₃O, FeS₁₋ₓ, Cu₂₋ₓO — composition varies within a small range; the law needs a true formula compound.
172. **Isotopic variation:** D₂O (heavy water) has H : O = 2 : 8, not 1 : 8 — the same 'compound' differs when isotopes change.
183. **Mixtures, solutions and alloys** vary continuously in composition — the law is for pure compounds only.
194. Polymers and associated molecules with variable chain length have no single fixed composition.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ The law means elements always combine in the same proportion. ✅ It applies to a given *compound*; the same two elements may form several compounds (CO, CO₂) with different ratios — that is multiple proportions.
❌ Heavy water is a counterexample to chemistry being consistent. ✅ It shows the law depends on the isotope chosen — D₂O and H₂O are different compounds.
❌ The law applies to brass or steel. ✅ Alloys are mixtures with variable composition; only pure compounds are covered.

Laws of Stoichiometry

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning
2Stoichiometry is the study of the quantitative relations between the amounts of reactants and products in a chemical reaction, derived from the balance of atoms. The **laws of chemical combination** (laws of stoichiometry) are the experimental generalisations on which those calculations rest: conservation of mass, definite proportions, multiple proportions, reciprocal proportions and gaseous volumes (with Avogadro's law completing the set).
3### 2. Law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier, 1774)
4In a chemical reaction matter is neither created nor destroyed: the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products. Verified by Landolt in sealed tubes (and by many reactions, e.g. AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃). It follows from atoms being merely rearranged; gases, precipitates and the mass of the container must all be accounted for. This law is the reason every chemical equation must be balanced.
5### 3. Law of definite (constant) proportions (Proust, 1799)
6A given chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed ratio by mass, no matter how it was prepared, its source or its purity. Water obtained from any source has H : O = 1 : 8 by mass; CO₂ always has C : O = 3 : 8. It is a consequence of a fixed number of atoms in the formula unit.
7### 4. Law of multiple proportions (Dalton, 1803)
8If two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in the ratio of small whole numbers. Examples: CO and CO₂ (fixed 12 g C combines with 16 g and 32 g O → 1 : 2); H₂O and H₂O₂ (fixed 2 g H with 16 g and 32 g O → 1 : 2); NO, N₂O, NO₂, N₂O₅ with a fixed mass of N give oxygen masses in 1 : 2 : 4 : 5. This law gave the strongest support for the atomic theory.
9### 5. Law of reciprocal (equivalent) proportions (Richter, 1792)
10The masses of two elements that combine separately with a fixed mass of a third element are in a simple ratio to the masses in which they combine with each other. Example with hydrogen as the reference: H : O = 1 : 8 in water and H : C = 1 : 3 in methane; therefore in carbon dioxide C : O = 3 : 8, exactly the simple ratio predicted — the law that led to the concept of equivalent mass.
11### 6. Law of gaseous volumes (Gay-Lussac, 1809)
12When gases react, they do so in volumes that bear a simple whole-number ratio to one another and to the volumes of gaseous products, at the same temperature and pressure: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (1 : 1 : 2); N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (1 : 3 : 2). This law inspired Avogadro's hypothesis — equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules — and is used for all volume–volume stoichiometry.
13### 7. Meaning and applications
14The laws convert qualitative chemistry into arithmetic: balancing equations (conservation of mass and atoms), finding percentage composition (definite proportions), deciding formulas of oxides and hydrides (multiple and reciprocal proportions), and performing gas-volume calculations in reactions (gaseous volumes with Avogadro's law). Practical uses: industrial material balances for ammonia, sulphuric acid and cement plants, analysis of ores and alloys, calculation of reactant quantities and costs, fuel combustion and pollution control, and every titration or gravimetric analysis performed in a laboratory.
15### 8. Limitations
16• The law of conservation of mass fails in nuclear reactions, where a small mass defect appears as energy (E = mc²); even chemically it needs all gaseous products to be captured.
17• The law of definite proportions does not hold for non-stoichiometric (berthollide) compounds such as Fe₀.₉₃O or the iron sulphides, where composition varies within a range; isotopic substitution (D₂O vs H₂O) also changes the mass ratio.
18• These laws apply to pure compounds and chemical reactions, never to mixtures, solutions or alloys, whose composition can be varied continuously.
19• The law of multiple proportions requires very accurate analysis — small errors in percentage composition hide or create apparent ratios; it is a description, not an explanation of atomic structure.
20• Reciprocal proportions and gaseous-volume laws demand accurate measurement; the volume law is strictly valid only for gases behaving ideally at the same T and P, and it fails for dissociating or associating vapours (PCl₅, NH₄Cl).
21• None of the laws says anything about reaction rate, mechanism, equilibrium or side reactions — they give only the mass/volume bookkeeping of a completed reaction.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ The laws of stoichiometry explain why reactions occur. ✅ They describe the quantitative relationships; the reason lies in atomic structure and bonding.
❌ Definite proportions holds for every compound. ✅ Non-stoichiometric compounds (Fe₀.₉₃O, FeS₁₊ₓ) violate it, and isotopic variants change the mass ratio.
❌ Multiple proportions and reciprocal proportions are the same law. ✅ Multiple proportions compares two compounds of the same two elements; reciprocal proportions compares two compounds sharing a third element.
❌ Gay-Lussac's volume law needs no conditions. ✅ Volumes must be measured at the same temperature and pressure, and the gases must behave ideally.
❌ Conservation of mass means mass is always conserved everywhere. ✅ It holds for chemical reactions but not for nuclear reactions (mass–energy equivalence).

Avogadro's Law and its Deductions (Molecular Mass, Vapour Density, Volume and Number of Particles)

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning and statement
2Avogadro's law (Amedeo Avogadro, 1811, published as a hypothesis) states: equal volumes of all gases, under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. Hence for a gas $V\propto n$ at constant T and P, and volume ratio = mole ratio. One mole of any gas contains the Avogadro number N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules and occupies a molar volume of 22.4 dm³ (22.4 L) at STP (273.15 K, 1 atm) or ≈24 L at RTP. The law resolved the confusion between atoms and molecules that had blocked Dalton's theory and Gay-Lussac's volume results.
3### 2. Deduction 1 — molecular mass and vapour density
4**Vapour density (VD)** of a gas is the ratio of the mass of a certain volume of the gas to the mass of the same volume of hydrogen under identical conditions. Because equal volumes contain equal numbers of molecules, masses are in the ratio of molecular masses, and with hydrogen as the standard (M = 2): $VD=\frac{M}{2}$, so **molecular mass = 2 × vapour density**. Example: VD of CO₂ = 22 → M = 44 g mol⁻¹. (With air as reference, M = 28.94 × relative density.)
5### 3. Deduction 2 — molecular mass and volume of gas
6Since 1 mol of any gas occupies 22.4 L at STP, the mass of 22.4 L of a gas at STP equals its molar mass in grams. Hence $n=\frac{V\ (\text{at STP})}{22.4\ \text{L mol}^{-1}}$ and $M=\frac{\text{mass of the gas}}{\text{volume at STP}}\times22.4$. Conversely, the volume occupied by a known mass m is $V=\frac{m}{M}\times22.4$ L at STP. This is the basis of all gas-volume stoichiometry.
7### 4. Deduction 3 — molecular mass and number of particles
8One mole contains N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ particles, so $n=\frac{N}{N_{A}}$, the mass of one molecule $=\frac{M}{N_{A}}$ (e.g. 3.0 × 10⁻²³ g for water), and the volume of one molecule follows from the molar volume. Gram-molecular mass is the mass in grams numerically equal to the molecular mass, and it always contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules (gram-atomic mass contains the same number of atoms).
9### 5. Applications
10• **Atomicity of gases:** comparing volumes of a gas with the volumes of its elements (e.g. 2 volumes H₂ + 1 volume O₂ → 2 volumes H₂O vapour) shows that H₂, O₂ and N₂ are diatomic while O₃ and P₄ are polyatomic.
11• **Molecular mass of gases and volatile liquids:** by vapour density (M = 2 × VD) or by Victor Meyer's and Dumas' methods, and by the limiting-density/effusion methods.
12• **Molar-volume stoichiometry:** volume–volume and mass–volume calculations for gases at STP; determination of the molar volume of a gas and of Avogadro's constant.
13• **Establishing formulas:** Avogadro's law plus Gay-Lussac's volume law fixed the formulas H₂O, NH₃, CO₂ and HCl (which Dalton's theory alone could not).
14### 6. Limitations
15• Avogadro's law applies to **gases only** — it says nothing about the volume of liquids or solids, whose molar volumes differ greatly.
16• It assumes ideal behaviour; real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature, so the simple 22.4 L molar volume is approximate except near STP (for NH₃ or CO₂ it can be in error by a few per cent).
17• It fails for gases that **dissociate or associate** in the vapour state: PCl₅, N₂O₄ and NH₄Cl give anomalously high or low vapour densities, and a correction factor (degree of dissociation/association) is needed.
18• It provides no information about the size, shape or mass of individual molecules; 'equal numbers of molecules' says nothing about molecular structure.
19• The law was ignored for nearly half a century because Berzelius and Dalton did not accept the atom–molecule distinction — a reminder that it depends on the concept of a molecule, not merely on measurement.
20• The molar volume figures are defined at specified T and P (22.4 L at STP, 24 L at RTP), so results must always state the conditions.
21**Fact.** 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ entities (Avogadro constant, Nₐ). Molar mass (M) is g·mol⁻¹; for a compound M = Σ(atomic masses).
22**Fact.** n (mol) = mass/M = volume(ideal gas, STP)/22.4 L = N/Nₐ.
23**Fact.** Limiting reactant is the one consumed first; % yield = (experimental/theoretical)×100.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Equal volumes of any two gases have equal masses. ✅ They have equal *numbers of molecules*; masses differ because molecular masses differ.
❌ Vapour density is the same as density. ✅ Vapour density is a *relative* quantity measured with respect to hydrogen (or air); density is an absolute mass per unit volume.
❌ 1 mole of every gas occupies 22.4 L under all conditions. ✅ Only at STP (273.15 K, 1 atm); at other conditions use PV = nRT.
❌ Molecular mass equals vapour density. ✅ Molecular mass = 2 × vapour density (for the hydrogen reference).
❌ Avogadro's law can be applied to a dissociating gas without correction. ✅ Dissociation/association changes the number of particles, so an apparent (abnormal) vapour density results.

Law of Multiple Proportions — Dalton's Law with Worked Data

#Concept
1### Focus of this tab
2Dalton's own law: the full statement, the standard data sets (CO/CO₂, H₂O/H₂O₂, nitrogen oxides) and the method of showing whole-number ratios — the NEB calculation pattern.
3### Statement and history
4'When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in the ratio of small whole numbers.' Proposed by John Dalton (1803) — in fact the law is the strongest historical evidence for his atomic theory, since whole-number ratios are what whole atoms give.
5### Standard data sets
6• **Carbon oxides:** in CO, 12 g C combines with 16 g O; in CO₂, 12 g C with 32 g O. Fixed carbon → oxygen ratio 16 : 32 = 1 : 2.
7• **Hydrogen oxides:** H₂O (2 g H : 16 g O) and H₂O₂ (2 g H : 32 g O) → 1 : 2.
8• **Sulphur oxides:** SO₂ (32 g S : 32 g O) and SO₃ (32 g S : 48 g O) → 2 : 3.
9• **Nitrogen oxides:** N₂O, NO, N₂O₃, NO₂, N₂O₅ — with a fixed mass of nitrogen, oxygen masses stand in 1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5.
10• **Iron chlorides:** FeCl₂ and FeCl₃ — chlorine ratio 2 : 3 for fixed iron.
11### The calculation method (do it in this order)
12(1) Choose a fixed mass of one element (usually 1 g or the atomic mass). (2) Compute the mass of the other element combined with that fixed mass in each compound (divide/multiply proportionally). (3) Divide the masses by the smallest to get the simple ratio; multiply up if fractions appear.
13### Why it works and where it stops
14Atoms combine as whole units: one C with one or two O atoms — hence 1 : 2. The law needs accurate analysis (small errors destroy the whole-number pattern), applies to *pure* compounds of the same two elements, and says nothing about structure or bonding.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ The law compares masses of both elements. ✅ One element's mass is held fixed; the other's varies in whole-number ratios.
❌ CO and CO₂ show definite proportions. ✅ Each compound separately follows definite proportions; the *pair* demonstrates multiple proportions.
❌ Any ratio qualifies as 'small whole number'. ✅ It must be genuinely integral (1 : 2, 2 : 3); 1 : 1.49 signals analytical error.

Law of Reciprocal (Equivalent) Proportions — Richter's Law

#Concept
1### Focus of this tab
2The law of reciprocal proportions (Richter, 1792; also called the law of equivalent proportions) — statement, the reference-element method, worked verification and its link to equivalent masses.
3### Statement
4'The masses of two elements which combine separately with a fixed mass of a third element are either the same as, or simple multiples of, the masses in which they combine with each other.' It is the law that connects compounds sharing a common third element.
5### The standard verification (H as reference)
6• Water H₂O: H : O = 1 : 8.
7• Methane CH₄: H : C = 1 : 3, i.e. C : H = 3 : 1 — 3 g C combines with 1 g H.
8• Carbon dioxide CO₂: C : O = 12 : 32 = 3 : 8.
9Check: with a fixed mass of H (1 g), the masses of C and O that combine are 3 g and 8 g. The ratio C : O = 3 : 8 — exactly the ratio in which C and O combine in CO₂. The law is verified.
10### Second pattern (oxygen as reference)
11In SO₂, S : O = 32 : 32 = 1 : 1; in H₂O, H : O = 1 : 8. With a fixed mass of O (say 8 g), H = 1 g and S = 8 g combine. In H₂S the actual ratio is H : S = 1 : 16 = 1 : 16, which is a simple multiple (×2) of the predicted 8 : 1 → the law holds in 'simple multiple' form.
12### Connection to equivalent masses
13The 'masses combining with a fixed mass of the reference element' are precisely the **equivalent masses** (H = 1.008, O = 8, C = 3 when valency 4 is chosen...). The law is the logical basis of the equivalent-mass concept and of the classical method of determining equivalent masses by analysis.
14### Limitations
15It applies to pure compounds and exact analyses; like multiple proportions it fails for berthollides; and it requires the three elements actually to form the relevant binary compounds (e.g. some noble gases form none). It is also the hardest of the laws to verify experimentally because the data must be very accurate.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ This law compares two compounds of the same two elements. ✅ That is multiple proportions; reciprocal proportions links three elements through two compounds.
❌ The ratio must be identical. ✅ It may be the same or a *simple multiple* — the 'simple multiple' clause is part of the statement.
❌ The reference element must be hydrogen. ✅ Any common third element works; hydrogen and oxygen are just the textbook choices.

Mole and its Relation with Mass, Volume and Number of Particles

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of the mole
2A mole is the SI unit of amount of substance. It is the amount of substance that contains exactly the same number of elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, formula units) as there are atoms in 12 g of carbon-12, that is **6.022 × 10²³** entities — the Avogadro number N_A. The mole is simply a counting unit, like 'dozen' or 'score', but on a scale suited to atoms and molecules: it links the invisible particle world with weighable masses.
3### 2. Relation with mass
4$n=\frac{m}{M}$, where m is the mass in grams and M the molar mass (g mol⁻¹). Molar mass = relative atomic/molecular mass expressed in g mol⁻¹: 1 atom of carbon has a mass of 12 u (1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1/12 the mass of one ¹²C atom), while 1 mole of carbon atoms has a mass of 12 g. Gram-atomic mass and gram-molecular mass are the mass of one mole of atoms and of molecules respectively. Example: 44 g of CO₂ = 1 mol = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules.
5### 3. Relation with number of particles
6$n=\frac{N}{N_{A}}$; N = n × N_A. The number of *atoms* must be counted with care: 1 mol of H₂SO₄ contains 2 mol H atoms, 1 mol S, 4 mol O (7 mol of atoms in total, 6.022 × 10²³ H₂SO₄ molecules), while 1 mol of H₂ contains 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ atoms. Ions, electrons and formula units can also be counted in moles (1 mol of NaCl gives 1 mol Na⁺ + 1 mol Cl⁻).
7### 4. Relation with volume (gases)
8For gases the mole relates to volume through Avogadro's law: 1 mol of any gas at STP (273.15 K, 1 atm) occupies 22.4 L, so $n=\frac{V\ (\text{at STP})}{22.4}$; at RTP (25 °C, 1 bar) the molar volume is ≈24.0 L. For any other conditions the ideal gas equation is used: $n=\frac{PV}{RT}$. Concentration extends the idea to solutions: $n=M_{\text{conc}}\times V_{\text{L}}$ (molarity × volume in litres).
9### 5. Applications
10• Core of every stoichiometric calculation: converting masses, gas volumes and numbers of particles into moles and back, so that the mole ratio of a balanced equation can be applied.
11• Determination of relative atomic and molecular masses, molar volumes, vapour densities and Avogadro's constant; standardisation of solutions and titrations.
12• Industry and pharmacy: calculating exact reactant quantities and costs for ammonia, sulphuric acid, cement or drug manufacture; agriculture: fertiliser dosage; environment: calculating CO₂ (44 g mol⁻¹) and pollutant emissions per tonne of fuel; medicine: expressing doses and concentrations in moles.
13### 6. Limitations
14• The mole counts *specified entities*: '1 mol of oxygen' is ambiguous (O atoms or O₂ molecules?), so the entity must always be named; the mole is meaningless without it.
15• Since the 2019 SI revision N_A is a defined number (6.02214076 × 10²³) rather than being tied to 12 g of ¹²C, so the earlier 'gram-atom' style definitions are outdated.
16• Molar masses are based on **standard atomic weights**, which are average values for the natural isotopic composition and therefore carry an uncertainty (and vary slightly for elements such as boron, lithium and lead whose isotopic abundances differ with source). Exact work needs isotope-specific molar masses.
17• The relation n = V/22.4 L holds only for gases at STP that behave ideally; for real gases and for other conditions PV = nRT must be used, and for associating gases the particle count differs from the formula count.
18• The mole expresses *amount*, not concentration — molarity, molality and mole fraction are different measures; molarity in particular is temperature-dependent because volume changes with temperature.
19• Mole-based stoichiometry cannot predict how fast a reaction goes, in which direction it proceeds, or what yield it gives; those come from kinetics, thermodynamics and equilibrium.
20**Fact.** 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ entities (Avogadro constant, Nₐ). Molar mass (M) is g·mol⁻¹; for a compound M = Σ(atomic masses).
21**Fact.** n (mol) = mass/M = volume(ideal gas, STP)/22.4 L = N/Nₐ.
22**Fact.** Limiting reactant is the one consumed first; % yield = (experimental/theoretical)×100.

Calculations Based on Mole Concept

#Concept
1Mole Concept Foundation: One mole contains exactly 6.022 × 10²³ entities (Avogadro's number Nₐ). The molar mass M (g/mol) of any substance equals its atomic or molecular mass numerically. This bridges the microscopic (atoms/molecules) and macroscopic (grams) worlds.
2Mass-to-Moles Conversion: n = m/M where n = moles, m = mass in grams, M = molar mass in g/mol. Example: 36 g water (M = 18 g/mol) → n = 36/18 = 2 mol. This is the most fundamental stoichiometric calculation.
3Moles-to-Particles Conversion: Number of particles N = n × Nₐ. Example: 0.5 mol CO₂ contains 0.5 × 6.022×10²³ = 3.011×10²³ molecules. Reverse: n = N/Nₐ when given particle count.
4Molar Volume at STP: One mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at STP (0°C, 1 atm). n = V/22.4 for gases at STP. Example: 5.6 L O₂ at STP → n = 5.6/22.4 = 0.25 mol. Non-STP conditions require PV = nRT.
5Percentage Composition: % of element = (mass of element in 1 mol compound / molar mass of compound) × 100. Example: In H₂O, %H = (2×1.008/18.015)×100 = 11.19%, %O = (16.00/18.015)×100 = 88.81%. Used to determine empirical formulas from experimental data.
6Empirical Formula Determination: Step 1: Convert % composition to grams (assume 100 g sample). Step 2: Convert grams to moles for each element. Step 3: Divide all mole values by smallest mole value. Step 4: Multiply by integer if ratios are not whole numbers. Result gives simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
7Molecular Formula from Empirical: Molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ where n = molecular mass / empirical formula mass. Example: Empirical CH₂O (mass 30), molecular mass 180 → n = 180/30 = 6 → molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose).
8Stoichiometric Calculations from Balanced Equations: Coefficients give mole ratios. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: 2 mol H₂ reacts with 1 mol O₂ to produce 2 mol H₂O. Convert given quantity to moles → use ratio → convert back to desired units.
9Mass-Mass Stoichiometry: Given mass of reactant A, find mass of product B. Steps: mass A → moles A (÷M_A) → moles B (×ratio) → mass B (×M_B). Example: How much CO₂ from 100 g CaCO₃? CaCO₃→CaO+CO₂. n(CaCO₃)=100/100=1 mol → n(CO₂)=1 mol → m=44 g.
10Mass-Volume Stoichiometry (Gases): For reactions involving gases, use molar volume. Example: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. From 6.5 g Zn (0.1 mol), H₂ produced = 0.1 mol = 0.1×22.4 = 2.24 L at STP.
11Solution Stoichiometry: Molarity M = n/V(L). n = M × V. Use in titration calculations: M₁V₁/n₁ = M₂V₂/n₂ where n₁,n₂ are coefficients. Example: 25 mL 0.1M NaOH neutralizes how many mL of 0.05M H₂SO₄? Ratio 2:1 → V = (0.1×25×1)/(0.05×2) = 25 mL.
12Limiting Reagent Identification: Calculate moles of each reactant. Divide by coefficient in balanced equation. Smallest value identifies limiting reagent. All product calculations based on limiting reagent only. Excess reagent remains partially unreacted.
13Theoretical Yield Calculation: Maximum possible product based on limiting reagent and stoichiometry. Actual yield is always less due to incomplete reactions, side reactions, and losses during purification.
14Percentage Yield: % yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100. Industrial processes aim for >90%. Low yields indicate inefficiency, side reactions, or equilibrium limitations. NEB frequently asks numerical problems combining limiting reagent and % yield.
15Percentage Purity: % purity = (mass of pure substance / mass of impure sample) × 100. Impure samples require correction before stoichiometric calculations. Example: 80% pure limestone means only 80 g CaCO₃ per 100 g sample participates in reaction.

Key Points

Point
n = m/M is the gateway to all stoichiometric calculations.
Always convert to moles before applying mole ratios from balanced equations.
22.4 L/mol applies only to ideal gases at STP.
Empirical formula = simplest ratio; molecular formula = actual atom count.
Limiting reagent determines maximum product; excess reagent is leftover.
% yield compares actual vs theoretical; always ≤ 100%.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing molecular mass with molar mass: Molecular mass is dimensionless (relative); molar mass has units g/mol. Numerically equal but dimensionally different.
Using 22.4 L for non-STP conditions: Only valid at 0°C and 1 atm. Room temperature (25°C) gives ~24.5 L/mol.
Forgetting diatomic elements: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ exist as molecules. Atomic mass ≠ molecular mass for these.
Mixing up empirical and molecular formulas: Empirical is simplest ratio; molecular is actual. They can be identical (H₂O) or different (C₆H₁₂O₆ vs CH₂O).
Assuming 100% yield in calculations: Theoretical yield is maximum possible; actual is always less. Never use actual yield to predict reactant needs.

Limiting Reactant and Excess Reactant

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning
2The **limiting reactant (limiting reagent)** is the reactant completely consumed in a reaction, and it therefore determines the maximum amount of product that can form (the theoretical yield). The other reactant, part of which is left over when the reaction stops, is the **excess reactant**. The idea is needed whenever the reactants are not mixed in exactly the stoichiometric ratio of the balanced equation.
3### 2. How to identify the limiting reactant
4Method 1 (standard): convert the given masses/volumes of all reactants into moles, divide each by its stoichiometric coefficient, and the smallest quotient identifies the limiting reactant. Method 2: compare the moles *required* (from the mole ratio) with the moles *available*; the reactant for which the requirement exceeds the supply is limiting.
5### 3. Worked example — ammonia synthesis
6N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. With 2 mol N₂ and 3 mol H₂: for N₂ the quotient is 2/1 = 2, for H₂ it is 3/3 = 1 → **H₂ is limiting**. Hydrogen needs only 1 mol N₂, so 1 mol N₂ stays unused (excess), and NH₃ formed = (2/3) × 3 = 2 mol = 34 g.
7### 4. Worked example — combustion (mass basis)
8CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O, with 8 g CH₄ (0.5 mol) and 24 g O₂ (0.75 mol): quotients 0.5 for CH₄ and 0.75/2 = 0.375 for O₂ → **O₂ is limiting**. It consumes 0.375 mol CH₄, so 0.125 mol CH₄ (2 g) remains excess and 0.375 mol CO₂ (16.5 g) is formed.
9### 5. Applications
10• Laboratory and industry: fixing reactant quantities for a target product mass, material balances in the Haber process, contact process and lime kiln, and choosing which reactant to use in excess on the basis of cost, hazards and equilibrium considerations.
11• Combustion engineering: excess air is supplied to burn fuel completely (avoiding CO and soot), while excessive excess air wastes heat and promotes NOₓ formation.
12• Pharmaceutical and fine-chemical synthesis: the cheaper or more easily handled reagent is taken in excess to drive the expensive intermediate to completion, and by-product amounts are predicted from the limiting reagent.
13• Analytical and environmental work: computing the maximum pollutant that can be produced, and in titrations, where the equivalence point marks the stoichiometric balance of acid and base.
14### 6. Meaning of 'extent of reaction'
15The limiting reactant fixes how far the reaction can proceed: while it remains, the reaction continues; once it is consumed, a simple irreversible reaction stops. This is why the theoretical yield is always calculated from the limiting reactant, and why excess reactant is added deliberately in practice.
16### 7. Limitations
17• The concept requires a known single reaction with fixed stoichiometry; with side reactions, reversibility or equilibrium the idea must be replaced by the equilibrium constant, conversion and selectivity.
18• Comparison must be made in **moles**; comparing masses directly (5 g H₂ with 5 g O₂) leads to wrong conclusions.
19• In a kinetically controlled reaction the slowest step may govern the rate even though the reagent is stoichiometrically in excess — the limiting reagent is not automatically the rate-determining species.
20• When a reactant acts as the solvent or medium (water, large excess of acid), the concept loses meaning.
21• The yield computed from the limiting reactant is an upper limit only; actual yields are lower because of incomplete reaction, side products and handling losses.
22Theoretical yield is always calculated from the limiting reactant, never from the excess reactant.
23Percentage yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100; actual yield is always less than theoretical.
24In industrial processes, the cheaper reactant is usually taken in excess to maximize conversion of the expensive reactant.

Point

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ The reactant present in the smallest mass is limiting. ✅ The limiting reactant is found in *moles* divided by the stoichiometric coefficient, not by mass.
❌ The limiting reactant is the one that runs out last. ✅ It is used up first and stops the reaction; the other reactant is in excess.
❌ Excess reactant also becomes product. ✅ Only the consumed part reacts; the remainder is unreacted excess.
❌ Every reaction has a limiting reactant. ✅ Only when the reactants are not in the exact stoichiometric mole ratio.
❌ Limiting-reactant reasoning gives the actual yield of a reversible reaction. ✅ It gives the maximum possible yield; the equilibrium position decides the actual extent.
❌ Assume 100% yield in calculations → ✅ Theoretical yield is the maximum possible; actual yield accounts for losses and incomplete reactions.

Verifying Whole-Number Ratios from Experimental Data

#Concept
1### Focus of this tab
2The computational skill behind all the combination laws: converting raw percentage/mass data into whole-number ratios — the shared method of definite, multiple and reciprocal proportions, and of empirical-formula work.
3### The standard five-step method
4(1) Take the percentage of each element as mass in 100 g (or use the given masses). (2) Divide each mass by the atomic mass of that element → moles. (3) Divide all values by the smallest. (4) If the ratios are still fractional (e.g. 1 : 1.5, 1 : 1.33, 1 : 1.67), multiply the whole set by 2, 3 or 4 respectively to clear fractions. (5) Interpret: the whole-number ratio is the atomic ratio in the compound.
5### Worked pattern 1 — a binary oxide
6An oxide contains 60% metal and 40% oxygen (atomic mass of metal = 48). Metal = 60/48 = 1.25; O = 40/16 = 2.5. Divide by 1.25 → M : O = 1 : 2 → formula MO₂. (With 70% metal it would be 1.46 : 1.875 → 1 : 1.28… → careful: re-check data, since real ratios must resolve.)
7### Worked pattern 2 — two oxides checked against multiple proportions
8Oxide A: 46.7% N; oxide B: 30.4% N. Per gram of nitrogen: A has 46.7/53.3 = 0.876 g O; B has 30.4/69.6 = 0.437 g O. Ratio 0.876 : 0.437 = 2 : 1 — a whole-number ratio, so the data obey the law of multiple proportions (NO₂-type and NO-type oxides).
9### Recognising the intended multiplier
10• ×2 for halves: 1 : 1.5, 1 : 2.5.
11• ×3 for thirds: 1 : 1.33, 1 : 1.67, 1 : 2.33.
12• ×4 for quarters: 1 : 1.25, 1 : 1.75.
13### Error awareness (limitations of the method)
14Real analytical data carry errors, so ratios like 1 : 1.48 may 'want' to be 1 : 1.5 while 1 : 1.35 may be measurement noise around 1 : 1.33. Judgement (and significant figures) decide; non-stoichiometric compounds (Fe₀.₉₃O) will never resolve exactly, and the method assumes the compound is a true formula compound.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Any near-integer ratio may be rounded freely. ✅ Round only with analytical justification; 1 : 1.9 is not 1 : 2 in a berthollide.
❌ The ratio is of masses. ✅ After dividing by atomic masses, the ratio is of *atoms* (moles), not grams.
❌ Multiplying changes the formula. ✅ Scaling the whole ratio set preserves it (2 : 4 is the same as 1 : 2).

Theoretical Yield, Experimental Yield and Percentage Yield

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of the three quantities
2**Theoretical yield** is the maximum amount of product calculated from the balanced equation using the limiting reactant (assuming complete reaction and no losses). **Experimental (actual) yield** is the amount of pure product actually isolated in the experiment. **Percentage yield** compares them: $\%\text{yield}=\frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}}\times100$. Related terms: conversion (fraction of reactant consumed), selectivity (fraction converted to the desired product) and atom economy (mass of desired product ÷ mass of all reactants × 100).
3### 2. Why the actual yield is lower than the theoretical yield
4• The reaction is **incomplete** — the limiting reactant is not fully consumed in the time allowed.
5• The reaction is **reversible**, so it stops at an equilibrium position (this is exactly the case in the Haber process).
6• **Side reactions** produce by-products (organic syntheses especially).
7• **Mechanical losses** during filtration, decantation, transfer, crystallisation, evaporation and drying, plus product remaining dissolved in the mother liquor (solubility loss).
8• Product may be **volatile, thermally unstable or hygroscopic**, and impurities may interfere with the reaction.
9• Weighing, reading and calculation errors of the experimenter.
10A yield greater than 100% always indicates experimental error: the product is wet or impure, or unreacted starting material has been weighed as product.
11### 3. Worked examples
12**Example 1:** CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. From 100 g CaCO₃ the theoretical yield of CaO is 56 g; if 47.6 g is obtained, % yield = 47.6/56 × 100 = 85%.
13**Example 2:** C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH. 7.0 g of ethene (0.25 mol) should give 0.25 mol = 11.5 g of ethanol; an actual yield of 9.2 g gives 80%.
14**Example 3:** Aspirin synthesis: 5.0 g of salicylic acid (M = 138) gives a theoretical 6.5 g of aspirin (M = 180, 1:1 mole ratio); a student isolating 5.2 g reports (5.2/6.5) × 100 = 80%.
15### 4. Meaning and use of atom economy
16Percentage yield measures how much of the *intended* product is recovered, whereas atom economy measures how much of the *reactant mass* ends up in the desired product (substitution and elimination reactions with by-products have poor atom economy). Green chemistry prefers high atom economy because it minimises waste, an idea quantified by the E-factor (mass of waste ÷ mass of product). Both concepts are needed to judge whether a process is efficient and environmentally acceptable.
17### 5. Applications
18• Industrial economics: yield determines raw-material cost, plant capacity and profitability. In the Haber process only about 15% of the gas reacts per pass, so unreacted gas is recycled to raise the overall yield; the contact process similarly recycles SO₂.
19• Laboratory synthesis (aspirin, soap, esters, dyes, polymers): the percentage yield is the standard measure of how successful a procedure was, and it is compared with literature values.
20• Green chemistry: atom economy and the E-factor (waste ÷ product) supplement % yield to judge environmental performance — a high-yield reaction with a poor atom economy still generates much waste.
21• Pharmaceutical and food quality control: yield data with assays (purity) are used for batch release, cost estimation and process optimisation, and yield losses indicate equipment or procedural faults.
22### 6. Limitations
23• Percentage yield does not measure **purity** — a 100% yield can be an impure or wet product; a separate assay (%, melting point, chromatography) is needed.
24• The theoretical value is only as good as its assumptions: the correct limiting reactant, the correct balanced equation and a single principal reaction must all be established first.
25• A value greater than 100% is always an error (impure/wet product, unreacted starting material, weighing mistake) and should never be reported as a good result.
26• For reversible reactions the theoretical yield calculated from stoichiometry is not attainable at all; the equilibrium yield depends on K, temperature, pressure and recycling — the practical yield must be measured.
27• Percentage yield says nothing about energy consumption, solvent use, hazards or waste, so it cannot by itself establish that a process is efficient or green; and it cannot be predicted theoretically — it must be determined experimentally.

❌ / ✅
❌ A 100% yield means a pure product. ✅ Purity is a separate property; yields above 100% are the usual sign of an impure or wet product.
❌ Percentage yield is calculated from the reactant taken in excess. ✅ It is calculated from the limiting reactant, which fixes the theoretical yield.

Calculation of Empirical and Molecular Formula from Percentage Composition

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of the two formulas
2The **empirical formula** gives the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element present in a compound (CH₂O for glucose). The **molecular formula** gives the actual number of each kind of atom in one molecule (C₆H₁₂O₆ for glucose). They are related by an integer n: molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₙ, where $n=\frac{\text{molecular mass}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}$, always a whole number (1, 2, 3 …).
3### 2. Standard procedure from percentage composition
4(1) Take the percentage of each element as its mass in 100 g of the compound. (2) Divide each mass by the atomic mass of that element to obtain the number of moles. (3) Divide all the mole values by the smallest one. (4) If the ratios are not whole numbers, multiply them by a suitable small integer (2, 3, …) to get the simplest whole-number ratio — this is the empirical formula. (5) Calculate the empirical formula mass and use n = molecular mass/empirical formula mass to write the molecular formula.
5### 3. Worked example (percentage composition)
6A compound contains 40% C, 6.67% H and 53.33% O with molecular mass 180. Moles: C = 40/12 = 3.33, H = 6.67/1 = 6.67, O = 53.33/16 = 3.33. Divide by 3.33 → C : H : O = 1 : 2 : 1 → empirical formula CH₂O, formula mass 30. n = 180/30 = 6, so the molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose).
7### 4. Combustion analysis (organic compounds)
8An organic compound is burnt completely in excess oxygen; the CO₂ absorbed in KOH solution gives the mass of carbon (mass C = mass CO₂ × 12/44) and the H₂O absorbed in anhydrous CaCl₂ gives the mass of hydrogen (mass H = mass H₂O × 2/18). Any remaining mass is usually oxygen determined **by difference**. The masses of C and H are then converted to moles exactly as above, the formula is determined, and the molecular mass (from vapour density, M = 2 × VD) gives n.
9### 5. Related calculations
10• **Percentage composition from a formula** (the reverse calculation): % element = (n × atomic mass of element ÷ molar mass of compound) × 100.
11• **Hydrated salts:** the water of crystallisation is found from the mass lost on heating and is included in the ratio (e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O).
12• **Equivalent/mole-ratio problems:** reacting masses or volumes are converted to moles and reduced to the simplest ratio to obtain a formula (e.g. determining x in FeₓOᵧ from oxidation data).
13### 6. Applications
14• Identification and characterisation of unknown organic and inorganic compounds in research and quality control.
15• Pharmaceutical analysis (confirming that a drug matches its declared formula) and forensic/clinical analysis of unknown samples.
16• Fuel, polymer, fertiliser and cement analysis, where the elemental composition fixes properties such as calorific value, degree of unsaturation or nutrient content.
17• Foundation of instrumental methods — mass spectrometry gives the molecular mass, so an empirical formula from combustion analysis plus an MS molecular ion gives the molecular formula directly.
18### 7. Limitations
19• Rounding is dangerous: if the smallest quotient gives ratios near 0.5 (e.g. 1 : 1.5), the whole set must be multiplied by 2 (or 3 for 1 : 1.33 : 1.67) — stopping early gives the wrong formula.
20• The result depends entirely on the accuracy of the percentage analysis. Errors propagate, especially for the element found 'by difference' (usually oxygen), which accumulates the errors of all the others.
21• The empirical formula does **not** identify the compound. Many different compounds share one empirical formula — CH₂O represents formaldehyde, acetic acid, lactic acid and glucose — so the molecular mass (or mass-spectrometric data) is essential to fix the molecular formula.
22• Molecular masses obtained from vapour density assume ideal-gas behaviour; association or dissociation (carboxylic acids, PCl₅, N₂O₄) gives an apparent molecular mass and hence a wrong n.
23• The method suits pure, well-defined compounds. For non-stoichiometric compounds, polymers with a distribution of chain lengths, hydrates of variable composition and mixtures, no single empirical formula exists.
24• Combustion analysis cannot distinguish elements present in small amounts (nitrogen, halogens, sulfur) unless special absorption trains or instrumental methods (CHN analyser) are used, and hygroscopic or volatile samples give poor data.
25**Fact.** 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ entities (Avogadro constant, Nₐ). Molar mass (M) is g·mol⁻¹; for a compound M = Σ(atomic masses).
26**Fact.** n (mol) = mass/M = volume(ideal gas, STP)/22.4 L = N/Nₐ.
27**Fact.** Limiting reactant is the one consumed first; % yield = (experimental/theoretical)×100.

Established academic fact

A balanced chemical equation conserves atoms and charge. Its coefficients give mole ratios, not mass ratios; the limiting reactant sets the maximum theoretical amount of product.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

Theoretical yield assumes the stated reaction and complete conversion. Actual yield may be lower because of incomplete reaction, side reactions or collection losses; oxidation states are formal electron-accounting values, not always literal atomic charges.

Open research question — How chemistry led to the first living systems

Life’s existence and subsequent evolution are established, but the detailed sequence by which non-living chemistry produced self-sustaining living systems remains unresolved. Researchers investigate plausible molecules and environments without treating any single pathway as proven.
