Unit 20: Electric Field

Physics XI | NEB (Grade 11) | Phy. 101  •  Content area: Electric & Magnetism

The electric field turns a force law into a description of the space around a charge: it says what force a test charge would feel at every point, whether or not one is actually there.

What the model asserts

  1. The electric field is force per unit positive test charge, E = F/q, directed along the force on a positive test charge.
  2. A point charge produces E = kq/r^2 directed radially, and superposition means the total field is the vector sum of the individual fields.
  3. Field lines are a visualisation, not a count of anything: their density represents field strength, they start on positive charge and end on negative charge, and they never cross.
  4. The field inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is zero; the field of an infinite non-conducting sheet is sigma/(2 epsilon0), and of a conducting sheet sigma/epsilon0.
  5. Because F = qE for any charge, the field can be treated as an independent variable - the reason Gauss's law is so powerful.
  6. A moving charge creates a magnetic field as well, so the electric and magnetic fields of a charge are two aspects of a single electromagnetic field.
  7. Field lines are bookkeeping: the number crossing a surface is proportional to the flux, so a denser bundle means a stronger field, even though the lines themselves have no physical substance.

Conditions, assumptions and limits

  • Closed-form field results exist only where symmetry is high enough - spherical, cylindrical or planar; any other distribution requires integration.
  • Electrostatic fields are conservative, so the work around a closed path vanishes and a scalar potential can be used instead.
  • In a dielectric the field is reduced by the relative permittivity, and polarisation P must be introduced; writing E = F/q with a 'free' charge only is wrong inside matter.
  • Electrostatic reasoning does not extend to time-varying fields: Maxwell's displacement current means a changing electric field generates a magnetic field.
  • Superposition is a statement about linear response, and real dielectrics are generally not linear, so polarisation and non-linear media need their own treatment.

Common misconceptions — 3 to unlearn

“Electric field lines are tubes of force carrying energy, so the number of lines meeting a surface tells you how much energy is there.”

Why it fails: Field lines are a bookkeeping device, not physical threads. Their number is arbitrary and can be rescaled; what they represent is the direction of the field and, through their density, the strength of the field.

Correct: A field line is drawn tangent to E at every point, and the number of lines crossing a surface is proportional to the flux through it, not to energy. The energy density of an electric field is (1/2) epsilon0 E^2, which depends on the field strength, not on any choice of line density.

“Inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium the electric field is zero, so a charged conductor has no field at all.”

Why it fails: The field is zero only inside the material of the conductor. Outside the conductor the field is not zero, and for a charged conductor it extends into the surrounding space, falling off as 1/r^2 for a sphere.

Correct: Free charges in a conductor move until the net force on them vanishes, so E = 0 within the material. The field outside the surface is perpendicular to it and is given by E = sigma / epsilon0 just outside, and by the equivalent point charge at the centre for a spherical conductor.

“An uncharged object is never affected by a charged one.”

Why it fails: Charged objects polarise neutral ones: the charges in the neutral body redistribute, so it experiences a net attraction. This is why a charged rod picks up bits of paper.

Correct: In a neutral conductor the free electrons move slightly towards or away from the charged rod, producing an induced dipole and a net attraction that survives even when the rod is subsequently removed. The effect is much weaker than the force between two charged objects, but it is real and it is how electrophoresis and electrostatic dust collection work.

Workbook — 3 questions with answers

numerical

Find the electric field at a point 10 cm from a point charge of 5.0 microcoulomb in air, and the force on an electron placed there.

Answer: E = k q / r^2 = 8.99 x 10^9 x 5.0 x 10^-6 / (0.10)^2 = 8.99 x 10^9 x 5.0 x 10^-6 / 0.01 = 4.5 x 10^6 V/m, directed radially outward. The force on the electron is F = q E = 1.602 x 10^-19 x 4.5 x 10^6 = 7.2 x 10^-13 N, directed opposite to the field (inwards), because the electron is negatively charged.

derivation

Derive Gauss's law, integral form, from the fact that the flux of the field of a point charge through any closed surface is independent of the shape of that surface.

Answer: For a point charge q, the field at distance r is E = q / (4 pi epsilon0 r^2). The flux through a closed surface S is the integral of E . dA; a spherical surface of radius r gives E x 4 pi r^2 = q / epsilon0. Because the field of a point charge is radial and its magnitude falls as 1/r^2, the same flux results for any closed surface enclosing the charge, and zero for a surface that does not. For a distribution of charges the total flux is the sum of the contributions, so the integral form is integral(S) E . dA = (1 / epsilon0) sum q_enclosed, or equivalently the differential form div E = rho / epsilon0. Superposition makes the result exact for any charge distribution, which is what makes Gauss's law the tool of choice for symmetric problems.

reasoning

A charged glass rod is brought near, but not touching, a light metal sphere suspended on a thread. The sphere is attracted to the rod. Explain this, and predict what happens if the rod is touched to the sphere and then removed while the sphere is still close to the rod.

Answer: The rod polarises the sphere: the free electrons in the metal shift slightly towards or away from the rod, so the near side of the sphere carries charge of opposite sign to the rod and the far side carries like charge. The nearer opposite charge is closer to the rod than the farther like charge, so the attraction outweighs the repulsion and the net force is towards the rod. On touching, charge flows between the rod and the sphere until they are at the same potential, and the sphere is left with a net charge of the same sign as the rod. When the rod is then removed, that net charge spreads uniformly over the sphere, and the force becomes repulsive. The attraction before contact is due to induction; the repulsion after contact is due to the transferred charge.

Open questions and mysteries

How does a charge act on matter that is not charged?

Under active scrutiny

The field picture handles it through polarisation; whether the field itself is physical or a bookkeeping device is a deeper interpretational question.

Can a static field be completely screened in any geometry?

Open question

Only conductors screen, and the shape of the screening conductor matters; a fully screened, shape-independent result is not generally available.

Inquiry prompts

  2. Show that two infinite sheets carrying opposite uniform charges produce a uniform field between them and zero outside.
  3. Plot the field of an electric dipole along its axis and compare with the far-field 1/r^3 law.
