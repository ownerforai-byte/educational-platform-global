Unit 25: Solids

Physics XI | NEB (Grade 11) | Phy. 101  •  Content area: Modern Physics

Solid-state physics explains why metals conduct and insulators do not, why semiconductors conduct better when heated and metals worse, and why resistance can vanish altogether below a critical temperature.

What the model asserts

  1. In a solid, allowed energy levels broaden into bands; overlapping bands make a metal, while a gap between the valence and conduction bands makes a semiconductor or an insulator.
  2. In a metal the conductivity falls as temperature rises because more scattering; in a semiconductor it rises because more carriers are excited across the gap.
  3. The Fermi level sits at the top of the occupied states at 0 K in a metal, and the Fermi energy of copper is about 7 eV.
  4. Carrier concentration in a semiconductor follows n proportional to T^(3/2) exp(-Eg/2kT), so the temperature dependence is exponential and steep.
  5. Doping shifts the Fermi level and produces n-type or p-type material; the band gap itself is unchanged by doping.
  6. Below the critical temperature a superconductor has exactly zero resistance and expels magnetic fields (the Meissner effect); BCS theory explains conventional superconductors through Cooper pairs.
  7. The band structure is one object that explains whether a solid conducts, whether it is a semiconductor, and whether it becomes superconducting when it gets cold.

Conditions, assumptions and limits

  • Band theory assumes a periodic potential, so it describes crystals well and amorphous or strongly disordered materials poorly.
  • BCS theory accounts for conventional phonon-mediated superconductors but not the high-temperature cuprates, for which a complete microscopic understanding is still outstanding.
  • Superconductivity requires all three of T below T_c, current density below the critical value, and field below the critical field.
  • The temperature dependence of a semiconductor is exponential only in the extrinsic or intrinsic regimes described by the model; heavy doping and high fields break that description.
  • The nearly-free-electron model treats the ions as a periodic potential and the electrons as free particles; it breaks down for narrow bands, strong correlations and the high-temperature cuprates.

Common misconceptions — 3 to unlearn

“An insulator has no electrons, while a metal has free electrons that move freely through the lattice.”

Why it fails: Every solid has electrons. The difference is the band structure: in a metal the valence band and the conduction band overlap, whereas in an insulator a band gap separates a full valence band from an empty conduction band.

Correct: An insulator has as many electrons as a metal; they are simply bound in a full valence band, and the energy needed to reach the conduction band (the band gap) is far too large for thermal energy at ordinary temperatures. A semiconductor has a small gap (about 1.12 eV for silicon), so some electrons are thermally excited across it, which is why its conductivity rises with temperature.

“Doping adds free charge carriers to a semiconductor.”

Why it fails: Doping does not add carriers directly; it adds impurity levels close to the band edges, and the carriers appear when those levels are ionised, by thermal energy in n-type material or by the release of bound electrons in p-type.

Correct: In n-type silicon, a group V donor (for example phosphorus) contributes a fifth valence electron which is only weakly bound and is easily promoted into the conduction band, leaving a fixed positive ion behind. In p-type material, a group III acceptor (for example boron) has one fewer valence electron, so an electron is drawn from the valence band, leaving a mobile hole. The Fermi level shifts into the conduction band for n-type and into the valence band for p-type.

“The Fermi level is the highest energy level occupied by electrons in the metal.”

Why it fails: That is true only at 0 K. At any temperature above absolute zero, states above the Fermi level are occupied with a probability given by the Fermi-Dirac distribution, and the occupancy at the Fermi level itself is one half.

Correct: At T = 0 K the Fermi level is the highest occupied state. At finite T, f(E) = 1/(exp((E - E_F)/kT) + 1), so f(E_F) = 1/2 and the occupancy falls away over an energy range of a few kT around E_F. For copper, E_F is about 7 eV while kT at room temperature is only about 0.026 eV, which is why the everyday description of a metal as a filled band with a sharp edge is a good approximation, whereas for a semiconductor with a 1.12 eV gap the same approximation fails completely.

Workbook — 3 questions with answers

numerical

Silicon has a band gap of 1.12 eV. Find the wavelength of the longest-wavelength photon that can excite a valence electron across the gap, and state whether visible light can do this.

Answer: The threshold photon energy is E = 1.12 eV. Using E = hc/lambda with hc = 1240 eV nm, lambda = 1240 / 1.12 = 1107 nm = 1.11 x 10^-6 m, in the near infrared. Visible light (about 380 to 750 nm) has photon energies of 3.3 to 1.65 eV, all above the 1.12 eV gap, so visible photons can excite electrons across the silicon gap - which is exactly why silicon solar cells respond to visible light and not to the infrared beyond 1.11 microns.

derivation

Derive the temperature dependence of the intrinsic carrier concentration in a semiconductor, n = n_i = sqrt(N_C N_V) exp(-E_g / 2 k T), and explain the opposite trends for a metal and a semiconductor.

Answer: In an intrinsic semiconductor the number of thermally excited electrons equals the number of holes, n = p = n_i, and the mass action law np = n_i^2 holds. The effective densities of states in the conduction and valence bands are N_C = 2(2 pi m_e k T / h^2)^(3/2) and N_V = 2(2 pi m_h k T / h^2)^(3/2), each proportional to T^(3/2). The probability of an electron occupying a state just above the conduction band edge is exp(-E_g / 2 kT) by Boltzmann statistics, so n = sqrt(N_C N_V) exp(-E_g / 2 kT), which is proportional to T^(3/2) exp(-E_g / 2kT). The exponential factor dominates for a small band gap, so n rises steeply with temperature. In a metal the relevant electrons are already in the conduction band, and the effect of heating is to increase the lattice vibrations, so the mean free path and the conductivity fall. Hence the resistance of a metal rises with temperature while that of a semiconductor falls.

reasoning

A superconductor has exactly zero resistance below a critical temperature. Explain what the Meissner effect adds to this statement, and why zero resistance alone is not enough to identify a superconductor.

Answer: Zero resistance says only that a current, once established, persists without decay. The Meissner effect says that when a material is cooled below its critical temperature it expels magnetic flux from its interior, so the field inside becomes zero except in a penetration depth of about 100 nm at the surface. This is a distinct thermodynamic property: a perfect conductor with zero resistance that was cooled in a field would keep that field trapped, while a superconductor expels it. Zero resistance is also not unique: a perfect conductor cooled in a field behaves the same way, and a low-resistance material such as copper at liquid-helium temperature has a resistance far below any measurement. Superconductivity is therefore identified by the combination of zero resistance and flux expulsion below a critical temperature, together with the critical field, the critical current and the critical temperature as separate limits.
