Unit 15: Refraction at Plane Surfaces

Physics XI | NEB (Grade 11) | Phy. 101  •  Content area: Waves & Optics

Refraction at a plane boundary is Snell's law, but the interesting physics is in the consequences: the lateral shift through a slab, the shallower apparent depth of a pool, and total internal reflection, which is the whole basis of fibre optics.

What the model asserts

  1. Snell's law reads n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2), with n = c/v the refractive index; the frequency of light is unchanged at the boundary while its speed and wavelength change.
  2. A plane-parallel slab displaces the emergent ray sideways while keeping it parallel to the incident ray; the displacement grows with thickness and with obliquity.
  3. Total internal reflection requires n1 > n2 and an incidence angle above the critical angle theta_c = arcsin(n2/n1), about 41.1 degrees for crown glass (n = 1.52) against air.
  4. Viewed near-normally, an object in water appears shallower by a factor of about n, so a 1.5 m depth reads as about 1.1 m.
  5. Optical fibres work by repeated total internal reflection; the acceptance angle is fixed by the numerical aperture of the core and cladding.
  6. In the wave picture refraction follows from phase continuity at the boundary: the phase must match across it, which is Snell's law in a different costume.
  7. Snell's law is a statement about phase continuity across a boundary: the change of direction is what keeps the wavefronts in step, and the same law governs light as a wave and as a quantum field.

Conditions, assumptions and limits

  • Snell's law assumes an isotropic, homogeneous, stationary, non-absorbing medium and a smooth interface; rough or moving boundaries change the result.
  • Near the critical angle the transmitted ray grazes the surface and the reflection coefficient approaches one.
  • The simple apparent-depth rule (divide by n) holds only for near-normal viewing; at oblique angles the correct factor is cos(theta')/cos(theta).
  • Dispersion - the wavelength dependence of n - is neglected in the basic model but must be included whenever white light is involved.
  • Fermat's principle fixes the path of least time rather than least distance, and that distinction is why a refracted ray bends towards the normal in a denser medium.

Open questions and mysteries

Can an image be formed that is not limited by the diffraction limit?

Frontier

Superlenses using negative-index materials beat the diffraction limit at a single frequency; extending the idea to a usable bandwidth remains an open and difficult problem.

Why does light slow down in a medium without losing energy?

Under active scrutiny

The classical account through the material's dielectric response is standard, while the fully microscopic picture of coherent forward scattering is subtler.

Inquiry prompts

  1. Compute the lateral shift of a ray through a glass slab and verify the prediction with a pencil, a glass block and a sheet of paper.
  2. Design an optical fibre for a given numerical aperture and explain the role of the cladding.
  3. Explain why a swimming pool looks shallower at the far end, and derive the apparent depth for oblique viewing.
