Electric Field Due to Point Charges and Field Lines

#Concept
1**Electric Field Concept & Definition:** An electric field $\vec{E}$ is an intrinsic vector property of space surrounding any charged distribution. It is defined quantitatively as the electrostatic force experienced per unit positive infinitesimal test charge placed at that location: $\vec{E} = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}$. The limiting operation $q_0 \to 0$ ensures the test charge does not disturb the source charge configuration. The SI unit of electric field is Newton per Coulomb ($\text{N/C}$) or equivalently Volt per meter ($\text{V/m}$), with dimensional formula $[M L T^{-3} I^{-1}]$.
2**Electric Field due to an Isolated Point Charge:** Applying Coulomb's law to a source charge $q$ at position vector $\vec{r}'$ and test charge $q_0$ at field point $\vec{r}$, the electric field is: $\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^3} (\vec{r} - \vec{r}') = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2}\hat{r}$. The electric field radiates spherically outward in all directions for a positive source charge ($q > 0$) and converges radially inward for a negative source charge ($q < 0$), exhibiting strict inverse-square attenuation with radial distance $r$.
3**Superposition Principle for Discrete & Continuous Charges:** The net electric field created by an ensemble of $N$ discrete point charges is the vector sum of individual fields: $\vec{E}_{net} = \sum_{i=1}^N \vec{E}_i = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{q_i}{r_i^2}\hat{r}_i$. For continuous charge distributions, summation transitions to volume, surface, or linear integrals: $\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{dq}{r^2}\hat{r}$, where $dq = \lambda dl$ (linear charge density), $dq = \sigma dA$ (surface charge density), or $dq = \rho dV$ (volume charge density).
4**Properties of Electric Field Lines:** Introduced by Michael Faraday, electric field lines (lines of force) provide an intuitive geometric mapping of electrostatic vector fields: (1) Lines originate perpendicularly on positive charges and terminate on negative charges (or extend to infinity); (2) The tangent to a field line at any point indicates the direction of $\vec{E}$ at that point; (3) Two field lines can NEVER cross, because intersecting lines would imply two distinct field vectors at the same spatial coordinate; (4) The local areal density of lines is directly proportional to field magnitude $E$; (5) Electrostatic field lines never form closed loops, reflecting the conservative nature of electrostatic forces ($\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r} = 0$).
5**Electric Dipole & Dipole Moment:** An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite point charges $\pm q$ separated by a small distance $2a$. The electric dipole moment vector is defined as $\vec{p} = q(2\vec{a})$, directed by convention from the negative charge $-q$ to positive charge $+q$, with SI unit $\text{C}\cdot\text{m}$. In an external electric field $\vec{E}$, a dipole experiences a net aligning torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$ and possesses electrostatic potential energy $U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E}$. In a non-uniform field, it experiences both torque and a net translational force $\vec{F} = (\vec{p} \cdot \vec{\nabla})\vec{E}$.
6**Electric Field of a Dipole at Axial & Equatorial Points:** For a dipole of moment $p = 2qa$ at distances $r \gg a$: (1) **Axial point (End-on position):** $\vec{E}_{axial} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2\vec{p}}{r^3}$, parallel to $\vec{p}$; (2) **Equatorial point (Broadside-on position):** $\vec{E}_{equatorial} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\vec{p}}{r^3}$, antiparallel to $\vec{p}$. Notice that for any dipole, $E \propto 1/r^3$ (faster fall-off than a point charge's $1/r^2$), and $E_{axial} = 2 E_{equatorial}$ at the same radial distance $r$.

Key Points

Point
Electric field intensity is force per unit positive test charge ($E = F/q_0$) in N/C or V/m.
Point charge field obeys inverse square law ($E \propto 1/r^2$); short dipole field obeys inverse cube law ($E \propto 1/r^3$).
Field lines point outward from positive charges and inward toward negative charges, never intersecting.
Axial field of a short dipole is exactly twice the magnitude of equatorial field at equal distance ($E_{axial} = 2 E_{equatorial}$).
In a uniform electric field, an electric dipole experiences zero net force but a restoring torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing field line direction with trajectory of a moving charge: An electric field line represents the direction of electrostatic force on a stationary positive charge, NOT the trajectory of a moving charge. A moving charge accelerates along $\vec{F} = q\vec{E}$, so velocity and field vectors generally diverge due to inertia.
Assuming field lines can intersect: If two field lines crossed, a test charge at the intersection would experience two distinct electrostatic forces in different directions simultaneously, which violates the uniqueness theorem of vector fields.
Confusing the radial fall-off of dipoles versus point charges: Point charge field scales as $1/r^2$, whereas an electric dipole field decays as $1/r^3$ because the positive and negative charges largely cancel each other out at long distances ($r \gg a$).

Summary: The electric field E is a vector field describing electrostatic force per unit test charge (E = F/q₀). Point charges produce radially symmetric fields falling off as 1/r², mapped visually by continuous non-intersecting field lines originating on positive and terminating on negative charges. Electric dipoles (p = 2qa) generate fields decaying as 1/r³, with axial field intensity twice that of the equatorial position at the same large distance, experiencing aligning torque τ = p × E in an external field.

Electric Field Due to Point Charges; Field Lines

#Concept
1Electric Field Definition: The electric field $\vec{E}$ at a point is the force $\vec{F}$ experienced per unit positive test charge $q_0$ placed at that point: $\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q_0$. It is a vector quantity with SI unit N/C (equivalently V/m).
2Field of a Point Charge: By Coulomb's law, the field due to a point charge $q$ at distance $r$ is $\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{r} = k\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{r}$, directed radially outward for positive $q$ and inward for negative $q$. $k = 8.99 \times 10^9\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
3Field Lines: Imaginary curves whose tangent gives field direction; density of lines represents field strength. Lines start on positive charges, end on negative charges, never cross, never form closed loops in electrostatics.
4Superposition Principle: The net field from multiple charges is the vector sum of individual fields: $\vec{E}_\text{net} = \sum_i \vec{E}_i$. This follows from linearity of Coulomb's law.
5Uniform Field: Between two parallel oppositely charged plates, the field is uniform: $E = V/d$ or $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (for infinite plates). A charge experiences constant force everywhere between plates.
6Dipole Field: For an electric dipole ($+q, -q$ separated by $2a$), axial field: $E_\text{axial} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2pr}{(r^2-a^2)^2} \approx \frac{2kp}{r^3}$ (for $r \gg a$). Equatorial field: $E_\text{eq} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}} \approx \frac{kp}{r^3}$. Dipole moment $p = q(2a)$.
7Field Inside Conductor: At electrostatic equilibrium, $\vec{E} = 0$ inside a conductor. All excess charge resides on the surface. The surface is an equipotential.

Key Points

Point
Electric field $\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q_0$ — force per unit positive test charge.
Point charge field: $E = kq/r^2$, radially outward (+) or inward (-).
Field lines: start (+), end (-), never cross, density = strength.
Superposition: net field is vector sum of individual fields.
Uniform field between parallel plates: $E = V/d$.
Inside conductor at equilibrium: $\vec{E} = 0$.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing field direction for positive vs negative test charge: Field points in force direction on + charge, opposite on - charge.
Thinking field lines are real physical lines: They are imaginary visualization tools; the field exists continuously in space.
Assuming field strength is constant near a point charge: $E \propto 1/r^2$ — it decreases rapidly with distance.
Adding field magnitudes instead of vectors: Electric field is a vector; use vector addition for multiple charges.
Believing field exists inside a charged conductor: At electrostatic equilibrium, E = 0 inside conductor material.

Summary: The electric field $\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q_0$ describes force per unit positive test charge. A point charge produces $E = kq/r^2$ radially. Field lines visualize field (start +, end -, never cross). Superposition gives net field from multiple charges. Field is uniform between parallel plates ($E = V/d$) and zero inside a conductor at equilibrium.

Gauss's Law and Electric Flux

#Concept
1**Electric Flux Concept & Definition:** Electric flux $\Phi_E$ is a macroscopic scalar measure of the total number of electric field lines passing perpendicularly through a given geometric surface. For an infinitesimal surface area vector $d\vec{A} = dA\,\hat{n}$ (where $\hat{n}$ is the unit normal pointing outward for a closed surface), the elemental flux is $d\Phi_E = \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = E\,dA\,\cos\theta$. The total electric flux through an arbitrary finite surface $S$ is given by the surface integral: $\Phi_E = \iint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A}$. The SI unit of electric flux is $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$ or $\text{V}\cdot\text{m}$, with dimensions $[M L^3 T^{-3} I^{-1}]$.
2**Gauss's Law in Electrostatics:** Formulated by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1835, Gauss's law states that the net outward electric flux through any arbitrary closed imaginary surface (known as a Gaussian surface) is directly proportional to the total net electric charge enclosed by that surface: $\Phi_{net} = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{enclosed}}{\varepsilon_0}$. Gauss's law is one of Maxwell's four fundamental equations of classical electrodynamics, valid for any static or moving charge distribution and any choice of closed surface.
3**Proof of Gauss's Law for a Point Charge:** Consider an isolated positive point charge $+q$ enclosed by a concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$. Due to spherical symmetry, the electric field has constant radial magnitude $E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$ and is everywhere parallel to the outward area vector $d\vec{A}$ ($\theta = 0^\circ$). The total flux is: $\oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \oint E\,dA = E \oint dA = \left(\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\right)(4\pi r^2) = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$. By superposition, this result holds for any arbitrary shape and any number of enclosed discrete charges.
4**Physical Implications of Gauss's Law:** (1) Charges located strictly outside the Gaussian surface contribute zero net flux through the closed surface, as every entering field line must exit elsewhere; (2) The total flux depends only on the net interior charge $Q_{enc} = \sum q_i$ and the medium permittivity $\varepsilon_0$, independent of the size, shape, or position of charges inside; (3) While the total flux depends only on enclosed charge, the electric field $\vec{E}$ at any specific point on the surface is the resultant field produced by ALL charges (both inside and outside).
5**Gauss's Law in Differential Form (Maxwell's 1st Equation):** Applying the Gauss Divergence Theorem to convert the surface integral to a volume integral yields: $\oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \iiint_V (\vec{\nabla} \cdot \vec{E})\,dV = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0} \iiint_V \rho\,dV$. Equating integrands for an arbitrary infinitesimal volume gives the differential form: $\vec{\nabla} \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$, stating that electric field divergence at any spatial point is directly proportional to local volume charge density $\rho$.
6**Selection Criteria for Gaussian Surfaces:** Gauss's law is always fundamentally true, but it is practically useful for calculating electric fields only when the charge configuration exhibits high geometric symmetry (spherical, cylindrical, or planar). A Gaussian surface must satisfy: (1) $\vec{E}$ is either perpendicular or parallel to the surface; (2) $|\vec{E}|$ is constant over the portions of the surface where flux is non-zero; (3) The surface passes through the point where the field is to be evaluated.

Key Points

Point
Electric flux is the surface integral of normal electric field: $\Phi_E = \int \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A}$.
Total electric flux through any closed Gaussian surface equals $Q_{enc} / \varepsilon_0$, independent of surface shape.
External charges contribute zero net flux to a closed Gaussian surface.
Gauss's law is valid only because Coulomb's law is an exact inverse-square law ($E \propto 1/r^2$).
In differential form, $\vec{\nabla} \cdot \vec{E} = \rho / \varepsilon_0$, representing Maxwell's first equation.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Thinking electric flux requires an actual physical fluid to flow: Electric flux is purely a geometric measure of field line penetration across a surface, not a transfer of physical mass or fluid.
Believing that zero net flux implies zero electric field everywhere: If a Gaussian surface encloses zero net charge, $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = 0$, but the electric field $\vec{E}$ at points on the surface need not be zero (e.g., an uncharged sphere placed in a uniform external field has entering flux equal to exiting flux).
Confusing the field $\vec{E}$ in Gauss's law with the field produced only by enclosed charges: $\vec{E}$ in the integral $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A}$ is the total resultant electric field produced by ALL charges in the universe, whereas the right-hand side $Q_{enc}$ counts strictly enclosed charges.

Summary: Electric flux Φ_E quantifies the perpendicular penetration of electric field lines across a surface (Φ_E = ∬ E · dA). Gauss's Law states that total net outward electric flux through any arbitrary closed Gaussian surface equals the total enclosed charge divided by vacuum permittivity (∮ E · dA = Q_enc / ε₀). While flux depends exclusively on enclosed charges, local surface field E reflects all charges. In differential form, ∇ · E = ρ / ε₀, serving as Maxwell's first equation.

Total electric flux through any closed Gaussian surface equals $Q_{enc} / \varepsilon_0$, independent of surface shape.
External charges contribute zero net flux to a closed Gaussian surface.
Gauss's law is valid only because Coulomb's law is an exact inverse-square law ($E \propto 1/r^2$).
In differential form, $\vec{\nabla} \cdot \vec{E} = \rho / \varepsilon_0$, representing Maxwell's first equation.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Thinking electric flux requires an actual physical fluid to flow: Electric flux is purely a geometric measure of field line penetration across a surface, not a transfer of physical mass or fluid.
Believing that zero net flux implies zero electric field everywhere: If a Gaussian surface encloses zero net charge, $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = 0$, but the electric field $\vec{E}$ at points on the surface need not be zero (e.g., an uncharged sphere placed in a uniform external field has entering flux equal to exiting flux).
Confusing the field $\vec{E}$ in Gauss's law with the field produced only by enclosed charges: $\vec{E}$ in the integral $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A}$ is the total resultant electric field produced by ALL charges in the universe, whereas the right-hand side $Q_{enc}$ counts strictly enclosed charges.

Summary: Electric flux Φ_E quantifies the perpendicular penetration of electric field lines across a surface (Φ_E = ∬ E · dA). Gauss's Law states that total net outward electric flux through any arbitrary closed Gaussian surface equals the total enclosed charge divided by vacuum permittivity (∮ E · dA = Q_enc / ε₀). While flux depends exclusively on enclosed charges, local surface field E reflects all charges. In differential form, ∇ · E = ρ / ε₀, serving as Maxwell's first equation.

Applications of Gauss's Law

#Concept
1**Overview of Gauss's Law Applications:** Gauss's law provides an elegant and straightforward analytical method for determining electric fields produced by symmetric charge distributions where direct Coulomb integration is cumbersome. The three standard canonical geometries required by the NEB curriculum are: (1) an infinitely long straight line of charge (cylindrical symmetry); (2) an infinite uniformly charged plane sheet (planar symmetry); and (3) a uniformly charged spherical shell or conducting sphere (spherical symmetry).
2**Electric Field of an Infinitely Long Straight Wire (Line Charge):** Consider a straight thin wire of infinite length having uniform linear charge density $\lambda$ ($\text{C/m}$). By cylindrical symmetry, the electric field is purely radial, pointing perpendicular to the wire. Construct a coaxial Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$. The electric flux through the two circular flat end caps is zero because $\vec{E} \perp d\vec{A}$ ($\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = 0$). Through the curved cylindrical surface of area $2\pi r L$, $\vec{E}$ is parallel to $d\vec{A}$ everywhere and constant in magnitude: $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = E (2\pi r L)$. Enclosed charge is $Q_{enc} = \lambda L$. Applying Gauss's law: $E(2\pi r L) = \frac{\lambda L}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}$. The field decays inversely with distance ($E \propto 1/r$).
3**Electric Field due to an Infinite Uniform Plane Sheet of Charge:** Consider an infinite non-conducting thin plane sheet carrying uniform surface charge density $\sigma$ ($\text{C/m}^2$). By symmetry, $\vec{E}$ is everywhere perpendicular to the sheet, directed away from both faces if $\sigma > 0$. Construct a Gaussian pillbox (a cylinder of cross-sectional area $A$ piercing perpendicular to the sheet with ends parallel to it). Flux through the curved surface is zero as $\vec{E}$ is parallel to the surface. Flux through the two end caps is $\Phi = E A + E A = 2 E A$. Enclosed charge is $Q_{enc} = \sigma A$. By Gauss's law: $2 E A = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$. Crucially, the electric field is completely independent of distance $r$ from the sheet, forming a perfectly uniform field.
4**Electric Field of a Charged Conducting Plate & Between Parallel Plates:** For a conducting plate of finite thickness, mobile free electrons ensure that the internal electric field is strictly zero ($E_{inside} = 0$). The charge redistributes onto both outer faces with surface density $\sigma$. Constructing a pillbox with one face inside the conductor and one outside yields $E A = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$ (twice that of an isolated non-conducting sheet). For two oppositely charged parallel conducting plates ($\pm \sigma$), fields add constructively between plates: $E_{between} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$, while cancelling outside: $E_{outside} = 0$.
5**Electric Field due to a Uniformly Charged Thin Spherical Shell:** Consider a thin spherical shell of radius $R$ carrying total charge $Q$ distributed uniformly with surface density $\sigma = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2}$. Construct a concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$: (1) **Outside the shell ($r > R$):** $\oint E\,dA = E(4\pi r^2) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2} = \frac{\sigma R^2}{\varepsilon_0 r^2}$. The shell acts as if all its charge were concentrated at the geometric center; (2) **On the surface ($r = R$):** $E_{surface} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{R^2} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$ (maximum magnitude); (3) **Inside the shell ($r < R$):** The Gaussian surface encloses zero charge ($Q_{enc} = 0$), so $E(4\pi r^2) = 0 \implies E_{inside} = 0$.
6**Electric Field of a Uniformly Charged Non-Conducting Solid Sphere:** For an insulating solid sphere of radius $R$ with uniform volume charge density $\rho = \frac{Q}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3}$: (1) **Outside ($r \ge R$):** $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2}$; (2) **Inside ($r < R$):** A concentric Gaussian sphere of radius $r$ encloses charge $Q_{enc} = \rho \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right) = Q \left(\frac{r^3}{R^3}\right)$. Applying Gauss's law: $E(4\pi r^2) = \frac{Q r^3}{\varepsilon_0 R^3} \implies E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q r}{R^3} = \frac{\rho r}{3\varepsilon_0}$. Thus, the electric field increases linearly with radial distance $r$ from zero at the center up to maximum at the surface ($E \propto r$ for $r \le R$), then decays as $1/r^2$ for $r > R$.

Key Points

Point
Line charge field decreases inversely with radial distance: $E \propto 1/r$.
Infinite sheet field is completely uniform and distance-independent: $E = \sigma / (2\varepsilon_0)$.
Inside any hollow conductor or spherical shell in electrostatic equilibrium, $E = 0$ (electrostatic shielding).
Outside a spherical charge distribution, the field behaves as if all charge is concentrated at the center ($E \propto 1/r^2$).
Inside a uniformly charged solid dielectric sphere, field increases linearly from center to surface ($E \propto r$).

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing field of a non-conducting infinite sheet with a conducting plate: An isolated non-conducting sheet has $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ on both sides because charge is embedded in a single layer. A conducting plate has charge on two separate faces; just outside any conducting surface, the boundary condition gives $E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$.
Assuming field inside a charged sphere is always zero: Inside a hollow shell or conductor, $E = 0$. However, inside a uniformly charged *non-conducting solid sphere*, the field increases linearly with radius: $E = \frac{\rho r}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{k Q r}{R^3}$.
Neglecting Gaussian end-caps in line charge: Students often forget to justify why the two flat circular ends of a Gaussian cylinder contribute zero flux (because $\vec{E} \perp d\vec{A}$, making $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = 0$).

Summary: Gauss's law enables straightforward derivation of electric fields for symmetric charge systems: an infinite line charge yields E = λ / (2πε₀r) showing inverse distance decay; an infinite planar sheet produces a uniform, distance-independent field E = σ / (2ε₀); and a spherical shell creates an inverse-square field externally (E = Q / (4πε₀r²)) while maintaining strictly zero internal field (E = 0), establishing electrostatic shielding.

Academic Extension: Evidence, Conditions & Open Questions

Established academic fact

Coulomb’s law models the force between point charges; superposition lets the vector contributions from several charges be added. In circuit problems, charge and energy conservation support Kirchhoff’s rules.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

Ohm’s law V = IR is a useful relation for ohmic materials over conditions where resistance is approximately constant. It is not universal: temperature changes and components such as diodes can produce non-ohmic behaviour.

Open research question — What is dark matter made of?

Astronomical evidence suggests that most matter does not interact strongly with light, yet its gravitational effects are measurable. Whether dark matter consists of new particles—and how those particles interact with ordinary matter—remains open; the point-charge electrostatic model remains reliable in its own domain.
