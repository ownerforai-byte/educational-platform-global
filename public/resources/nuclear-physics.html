Nucleus: Discovery, Density and Composition

#Concept
1**Atomic mass unit (u):** $1\\\\,\ ext{u} = 1.660539\ imes 10^{-27}\\\\,\ ext{kg}$. Defined as exactly $1/12$ the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state. All nuclear masses are expressed in u for convenience.
2**Exact mass vs mass number:** The mass number $A$ is the integer count of nucleons. The exact mass in u differs slightly from $A$ due to mass defect. For example, $^{12}\ ext{C}$ has exact mass $12\\\\,\ ext{u}$ by definition, but $^{1}\ ext{H}$ has exact mass $1.007825\\\\,\ ext{u}$, not $1\\\\,\ ext{u}$.
3**Isotopes:** atoms of the same element ($Z$ same) with different mass numbers ($A$ different). They have identical chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different nuclear masses, stabilities, and decay modes. Example: $^{35}\ ext{Cl}$ ($75.78\\\\%$) and $^{37}\ ext{Cl}$ ($24.22\\\\%$) are chlorine isotopes.
4**Mass defect $\\\\Delta m$:** $\\\\Delta m = Zm_p + Nm_n - M_{\ ext{nucleus}}$, where $m_p \\\\approx 1.007276\\\\,\ ext{u}$ is the proton mass, $m_n \\\\approx 1.008665\\\\,\ ext{u}$ is the neutron mass, and $M_{\ ext{nucleus}}$ is the nuclear mass in u. The mass defect is always positive - the nucleus is lighter than its separated parts.
5**Packing fraction:** $f = (M - A)/A$, where $M$ is the exact atomic mass in u and $A$ is the mass number. $f$ measures the fractional deviation from $A$. Negative $f$ (mass less than mass number) indicates strong binding; positive $f$ indicates weaker binding. $^{56}\ ext{Fe}$ has the most negative $f$, making it the most stable nuclide.
6**Binding energy:** $BE = \\\\Delta m c^2$. The energy equivalent of the mass defect. In MeV, $BE\ ext{(MeV)} \\\\approx 931.494 \ imes \\\\Delta m\ ext{(u)}$. For $^{56}\ ext{Fe}$, $BE/A \\\\approx 8.8\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$, the peak of the binding-energy curve.
7**Binding energy per nucleon:** $BE/A$. Rises from $\\\\sim 1\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$ for light hydrogen to $\\\\sim 8.8\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$ near iron ($A \\\\approx 56$), then slowly declines to $\\\\sim 7.6\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$ for uranium. This curve explains why light nuclei release energy via fusion and heavy nuclei via fission.
8**Carbon-14 dating:** $^{14}\ ext{C}$ is a radioactive isotope with half-life $5730\\\\,\ ext{years}$. While alive, living organisms maintain $^{14}\ ext{C}/^{12}\ ext{C} \\\\approx 1.1\ imes 10^{-12}$. After death, $^{14}\ ext{C}$ decays via $\eta^-$; measuring the remaining $^{14}\ ext{C}$ gives the time since death (archaeological dating up to $\\\\sim 50\\\\,\ ext{kyr}$).
9**Isotopic abundance:** The natural abundance of each isotope (given as a percentage) determines the standard atomic weight listed in the periodic table. For chlorine, $\ar{A} = 0.7578 \ imes 34.96885 + 0.2422 \ imes 36.96590 \\\\approx 35.45\\\\,\ ext{u}$, matching the tabulated value.
10**Mass spectrometry:** A device that separates ions by their mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$). Ions are accelerated through electric fields and deflected by magnetic fields; lighter ions deflect more. The resulting spectrum shows peaks at different $m/z$ values corresponding to different isotopes, with peak heights proportional to abundance.
11**Stable vs. radioactive isotopes:** Only $\\\\sim 254$ isotopes are stable; over 3000 are known to be radioactive. Stable isotopes have balanced neutron-proton ratios; too many or too few neutrons leads to instability and radioactive decay.
12**Nuclide notation:** A nuclide is written as $^{A}_{Z}\ ext{X}$, where $A$ is the mass number (protons + neutrons), $Z$ is the atomic number (protons), and $\ ext{X}$ is the element symbol. Example: $^{238}_{92}\ ext{U}$ has 92 protons and $238 - 92 = 146$ neutrons.
13**Rutherford's gold foil experiment (1911):** α particles (⁴He²⁺) were fired at a thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, but some were scattered at large angles. Rutherford concluded atoms have a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the center.
14**Unexpected result:** Rutherford expected, based on J.J. Thomson's 'plum pudding' model, that α particles would experience only slight deflection. The large-angle scattering (some α particles bounced back nearly 180°) proved the positive charge (and most of the mass) was concentrated in a tiny, dense region — the nucleus.
15**Size of nucleus:** The nucleus is roughly 10⁴ times smaller in radius than the atom. For a gold atom (radius ∼125 pm), the nucleus radius is ∼7 fm (femtometers = 10⁻¹⁵ m). The nucleus occupies a volume of about 10⁻¹² of the atom's volume.
16**Nuclear density:** The density of nuclear matter is nearly constant for all nuclei. This can be derived from R = R₀A^(1/3), where A is the mass number. Since m_nucleus ≈ Am_u (atomic mass unit), density ρ = M_nucleus/V_nucleus = Am_u / (4/3 π R₀³A) = 3m_u/(4πR₀³). The A's cancel out!
17**Nuclear density value:** Using R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm and m_u ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, the nuclear density ρ_nuc ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³. This is about 2.3 × 10¹⁴ times denser than ordinary matter — a sugar-cube-sized amount of neutron star material would weigh about 100 million tons on Earth.
18**Mass number (A):** A = Z + N, the total number of protons (Z) plus neutrons (N) in the nucleus. Written as a superscript before the element name: ⁵⁶Fe, ²³⁵U. It approximates the atomic mass in atomic mass units (though the actual mass is slightly less due to mass defect).
19**Atomic number (Z):** The number of protons in the nucleus, which determines the chemical element. All atoms of hydrogen have Z = 1, all helium atoms have Z = 2, all carbon atoms have Z = 6. Z also equals the number of orbiting electrons in a neutral atom.
20**Neutron number (N):** N = A − Z, the number of neutrons in the nucleus. Neutrons contribute to the strong nuclear force that holds the nucleus together but do not affect the chemical properties of the element. They also add mass without adding Coulomb repulsion.
21**Isotopes:** Atoms of the same element (same Z) but different neutron numbers (different N, different A). Example: hydrogen has three isotopes — protium (¹H, N=0), deuterium (²H, N=1), and tritium (³H, N=2). They have the same chemistry but different nuclear properties.
22**Nuclear radius formula:** R = R₀A^(1/3), where R₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m is a constant. This shows that nuclear radius scales as the cube root of mass number — a consequence of the constant density. The nuclear force is short-range (1-2 fm), so adding a nucleon adds it to the surface.
23**Rutherford scattering formula:** The number of α particles scattered at angle θ is proportional to 1/sin⁴(θ/2). This sharp angular dependence confirmed the concentrated-charge, small-radius nature of the nucleus. The formula matched Rutherford's theoretical predictions precisely.
24**Discovery of neutron (1932):** James Chadwick discovered the neutron by bombarding beryllium with α particles. The neutral, massive particles (neutrons) could knock out protons from paraffin wax. This confirmed the proton-neutron model of the nucleus.
25**Nuclear composition:** Nuclei contain Z protons (positively charged) and N neutrons (neutral), held together by the strong nuclear force. The strong force is short-range (fewer than 2-3 fm) but extremely powerful — overcoming the electrostatic repulsion between protons.
26**Density calculation example:** For a carbon-12 nucleus (A = 12): R = 1.2 × 12^(1/3) = 1.2 × 2.29 = 2.75 fm. Mass = 12 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.99 × 10⁻²⁶ kg. Volume = 4/3 π(2.75 × 10⁻¹⁵)³ = 8.72 × 10⁻⁴⁴ m³. Density = 1.99×10⁻²⁶/8.72×10⁻⁴⁴ = 2.28 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³. Same as uranium — confirming constant density.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Misconception - Mass number $A$ is an integer count of nucleons; exact atomic mass in u is not exactly $A$. The difference is the mass defect, which is small but measurable. Mass spectrometry separates isotopes by their exact masses, not their mass numbers.
Misconception - Packing fraction $f = (M - A)/A$ can be negative because the bound nucleus weighs less than its constituent nucleons. Students expect $M > A$ by adding individual proton and neutron masses, but binding energy release makes $M < Zm_p + Nm_n$.
Misconception - Isotopes share the same $Z$ but have different $A$; isobars share the same $A$ but have different $Z$; isotones share the same $N$ but have different $A$. These three distinctions are common exam distractors.
Misconception - Standard atomic weight (the periodic table value) is the weighted-average of all naturally occurring isotopic masses, not the mass of any single atom. A single chlorine atom has mass $\\\sim 35\\\, ext{u}$ or $\\\sim 37\\\, ext{u}$, never $35.45\\\, ext{u}$.
Misconception - Binding energy per nucleon $BE/A$ peaks at iron ($A \\\\sim 56$) at $\\\sim 8.8\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$, while uranium has $BE/A \\\\approx 7.6\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$. Fission energy release depends on the difference between parent and daughter nuclei, not a fixed value.
Misconception - $1\\\\,\ ext{u}$ of mass defect is equivalent to $931.5\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$ of energy. This conversion factor is essential for all nuclear binding-energy calculations; omitting it produces results that are off by a factor of $\\\sim 10^8$.
Misconception - In $eta^-$ decay, a neutron converts to a proton, electron, and antineutrino: $n \ ightarrow p + e^- + \ar{\ u}_e$. The emitted electron originates from the nucleus, not from the orbital electron cloud.
Misconception - Half-life is the time required for a radioactive sample to decay to half its original amount. After $n$ half-lives, the fraction remaining is $(1/2)^n$, not $1/(2n)$.
The nucleus is NOT the same as the atom — the nucleus is the tiny dense central part (protons and neutrons), while the atom includes the orbiting electrons. A nucleus is about 10,000 times smaller in radius than the atom it belongs to.
Nuclear density is NOT the same as atomic density. Atomic density (mass/volume of the entire atom) is about 10³ kg/m³ (like water or solid materials). Nuclear density (mass/volume of just the nucleus) is about 10¹⁷ kg/m³ — unimaginably denser.
Mass number A approximates atomic mass in atomic mass units, but is NOT exactly equal. For example, ¹²C has A = 12 and atomic mass exactly 12 u (by definition), but most nuclei have actual mass slightly less than A due to binding energy (mass defect).
The nuclear radius formula R = R₀A^(1/3) shows that doubling the mass number (e.g., from helium A=4 to beryllium A=8) does NOT double the radius — it increases it by 2^(1/3) ≈ 1.26. This cubic-root scaling confirms the constant density hypothesis.
Atomic number Z determines the element, but ISOTOPES of the same element have the same Z but different A. You cannot tell isotopes apart chemically, but they differ greatly in their nuclear stability (e.g., ¹H is stable; ³H is radioactive with a 12-year half-life).

Nucleus: Discovery of Nucleus

#Concept
1Atomic Models Timeline: Dalton's solid sphere (1808) → Thomson's plum pudding (1897) → Rutherford's nuclear model (1911) → Bohr's quantum model (1913). Each model addressed limitations of the previous.
2Thomson's Plum Pudding Model: Atom as positively charged pudding with electrons embedded like plums. Could not explain alpha scattering results.
3Rutherford's Alpha Scattering Experiment: Alpha particles from radioactive source directed at thin gold foil (~0.00004 cm). Most passed straight, some deflected at small angles, very few (~1 in 8000) bounced back at angles >90°.
4Rutherford's Nuclear Model: Atom has tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus (~10⁻¹⁴ m) containing nearly all mass. Electrons revolve around nucleus at large distance (~10⁻¹⁰ m). Most of atom is empty space.
5Nuclear Size: Nuclear radius R ≈ R₀A^(1/3) where R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm (10⁻¹⁵ m) and A is mass number. Nucleus is ~10⁴ times smaller than atom.
6Nuclear Composition: Nucleus contains protons (Z = atomic number) and neutrons (N = neutron number). Mass number A = Z + N. Nucleus charge = +Ze.
7Limitations of Rutherford Model: Could not explain atomic stability (accelerating electrons should radiate energy and spiral into nucleus) or line spectra.

Key Points

Point
Rutherford's experiment revealed tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.
Most alpha particles passed through gold foil — atom is mostly empty space.
Few bounced back — concentrated positive charge in nucleus.
Nuclear radius R = R₀A^(1/3), R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm.
Nucleus contains Z protons and N neutrons; A = Z + N.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing Thomson and Rutherford models: Thomson = positive pudding with electrons; Rutherford = tiny nucleus with electrons orbiting.
Thinking nucleus contains electrons: Nucleus contains only protons and neutrons (neutrons discovered later by Chadwick, 1932).
Believing all alpha particles bounce back: Only ~1/8000 scatter >90°; most pass through.
Confusing nuclear size with atomic size: Nucleus ~10⁻¹⁴ m, atom ~10⁻¹⁰ m — factor of 10⁴ difference.
Thinking Rutherford model explains spectra: It cannot explain line spectra or atomic stability.

Summary: Rutherford's alpha scattering experiment revealed a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus (~10⁻¹⁴ m) containing nearly all atomic mass. Most of the atom is empty space. Nuclear radius R = R₀A^(1/3). Rutherford's model could not explain stability or spectra — leading to Bohr's quantum model.

Discovery of Nucleus

#Concept
1Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden directed alpha particles at thin gold foil. Most passed almost straight through; a small fraction deflected sharply and very few returned. These observations could not be explained by positive charge spread uniformly through the atom.
2Rutherford inferred a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing nearly all atomic mass, surrounded by electrons; most atomic volume is empty space. Nuclear radius is about 10⁻¹⁵ m versus atomic radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m.
3For a head-on alpha particle, its kinetic energy converts to electrostatic potential energy at closest approach: Eα=(1/4πε₀)(2Ze²/rmin). Thus rmin=(1/4πε₀)(2Ze²/Eα), under the Coulomb-scattering model.
4Nuclear notation ᴬ_ZX gives proton number Z, nucleon number A and neutron number N=A−Z. Isotopes share Z but have different A; isobars share A but have different Z.
5Example: uranium-235 has Z=92, A=235 and N=143. Its nuclear charge is +92e.

Key Points

Point
Most particles passing through implies mostly empty atomic volume.
Rare large deflections imply concentrated positive charge.
Isotopes share proton number but differ in neutron number.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not claim the experiment established a modern planetary electron orbit.
Closest-approach estimates assume Coulomb interaction and head-on motion.

Summary: Rutherford scattering revealed a compact, positively charged nucleus and mostly empty atomic volume. Nuclear notation records proton, neutron and nucleon counts.

Nuclear Density; Mass Number; Atomic Number

#Concept
1Mass Number (A): Total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) in nucleus. A = Z + N. Dimensionless integer. Nuclei with same A but different Z are isobars.
2Atomic Number (Z): Number of protons in nucleus = number of electrons in neutral atom. Determines element identity and chemical properties. Nuclei with same Z but different N are isotopes.
3Neutron Number (N): Number of neutrons = A - Z. Nuclei with same N but different Z are isotones.
4Nuclear Density: Density ρ = mass/volume. Nuclear mass ≈ A × m_p (m_p ≈ 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg). Volume = (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)π(R₀A^(1/3))³ = (4/3)πR₀³A. Density ρ = A·m_p / ((4/3)πR₀³A) = 3m_p/(4πR₀³) — INDEPENDENT of A!
5Nuclear Density Value: ρ ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³ — about 10¹⁴ times denser than ordinary matter. Comparable to neutron star density.

Einstein's Mass-Energy Relation and Binding Energy

#Concept
1**Atomic mass unit (u):** $1\\\\,\ ext{u} = 1.660539\ imes 10^{-27}\\\\,\ ext{kg}$. Defined as exactly $1/12$ the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state. All nuclear masses are expressed in u for convenience.
2**Exact mass vs mass number:** The mass number $A$ is the integer count of nucleons. The exact mass in u differs slightly from $A$ due to mass defect. For example, $^{12}\ ext{C}$ has exact mass $12\\\\,\ ext{u}$ by definition, but $^{1}\ ext{H}$ has exact mass $1.007825\\\\,\ ext{u}$, not $1\\\\,\ ext{u}$.
3**Isotopes:** atoms of the same element ($Z$ same) with different mass numbers ($A$ different). They have identical chemical properties (same electron configuration) but different nuclear masses, stabilities, and decay modes. Example: $^{35}\ ext{Cl}$ ($75.78\\\\%$) and $^{37}\ ext{Cl}$ ($24.22\\\\%$) are chlorine isotopes.
4**Mass defect $\\\\Delta m$:** $\\\\Delta m = Zm_p + Nm_n - M_{\ ext{nucleus}}$, where $m_p \\\\approx 1.007276\\\\,\ ext{u}$ is the proton mass, $m_n \\\\approx 1.008665\\\\,\ ext{u}$ is the neutron mass, and $M_{\ ext{nucleus}}$ is the nuclear mass in u. The mass defect is always positive - the nucleus is lighter than its separated parts.
5**Packing fraction:** $f = (M - A)/A$, where $M$ is the exact atomic mass in u and $A$ is the mass number. $f$ measures the fractional deviation from $A$. Negative $f$ (mass less than mass number) indicates strong binding; positive $f$ indicates weaker binding. $^{56}\ ext{Fe}$ has the most negative $f$, making it the most stable nuclide.
6**Binding energy:** $BE = \\\\Delta m c^2$. The energy equivalent of the mass defect. In MeV, $BE\ ext{(MeV)} \\\\approx 931.494 \ imes \\\\Delta m\ ext{(u)}$. For $^{56}\ ext{Fe}$, $BE/A \\\\approx 8.8\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$, the peak of the binding-energy curve.
7**Binding energy per nucleon:** $BE/A$. Rises from $\\\\sim 1\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$ for light hydrogen to $\\\\sim 8.8\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$ near iron ($A \\\\approx 56$), then slowly declines to $\\\\sim 7.6\\\\,\ ext{MeV}$ for uranium. This curve explains why light nuclei release energy via fusion and heavy nuclei via fission.
8**Carbon-14 dating:** $^{14}\ ext{C}$ is a radioactive isotope with half-life $5730\\\\,\ ext{years}$. While alive, living organisms maintain $^{14}\ ext{C}/^{12}\ ext{C} \\\\approx 1.1\ imes 10^{-12}$. After death, $^{14}\ ext{C}$ decays via $\eta^-$; measuring the remaining $^{14}\ ext{C}$ gives the time since death (archaeological dating up to $\\\\sim 50\\\\,\ ext{kyr}$).
9**Isotopic abundance:** The natural abundance of each isotope (given as a percentage) determines the standard atomic weight listed in the periodic table. For chlorine, $\ar{A} = 0.7578 \ imes 34.96885 + 0.2422 \ imes 36.96590 \\\\approx 35.45\\\\,\ ext{u}$, matching the tabulated value.
10**Mass spectrometry:** A device that separates ions by their mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$). Ions are accelerated through electric fields and deflected by magnetic fields; lighter ions deflect more. The resulting spectrum shows peaks at different $m/z$ values corresponding to different isotopes, with peak heights proportional to abundance.
11**Stable vs. radioactive isotopes:** Only $\\\\sim 254$ isotopes are stable; over 3000 are known to be radioactive. Stable isotopes have balanced neutron-proton ratios; too many or too few neutrons leads to instability and radioactive decay.
12**Nuclide notation:** A nuclide is written as $^{A}_{Z}\ ext{X}$, where $A$ is the mass number (protons + neutrons), $Z$ is the atomic number (protons), and $\ ext{X}$ is the element symbol. Example: $^{238}_{92}\ ext{U}$ has 92 protons and $238 - 92 = 146$ neutrons.
13**Einstein's mass-energy relation (1905):** E = mc². Mass and energy are equivalent — a change in mass corresponds to a change in energy by ΔE = Δmc². The speed of light c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s, so c² ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s².
14**Rest energy:** The energy associated with an object's mass at rest is E_0 = m_0c². This is the minimum energy the mass can have. Even stationary mass has this enormous energy content.
15**Mass defect:** When nucleons (protons and neutrons) form a nucleus, the measured nuclear mass M_A is slightly less than the sum of the individual nucleon masses: (Zm_p + Nm_n). The difference Δm = (Zm_p + Nm_n) − M_A is called the mass defect. It is always positive.
16**Origin of mass defect:** The missing mass Δm converts to binding energy via E = Δmc². The nucleus is formed in an exothermic process, releasing energy. This energy holds the nucleus together — it is the binding energy.
17**Binding energy (BE):** BE = Δm × c². It is the energy required to completely disassemble a nucleus into its individual protons and neutrons at rest. Also equals the energy released when the nucleus forms from its constituent nucleons.
18**Binding energy per nucleon (BE/A):** BE divided by mass number A. This normalizes for nucleus size. The BE/A curve peaks at iron-56 (≈ 8.8 MeV per nucleon). For nuclei lighter than iron, fusion increases BE/A (releases energy). For nuclei heavier than iron, fission increases BE/A (releases energy).
19**Packing fraction:** f = (M - A)/A, where M is the actual atomic mass (in u) and A is the mass number. Introduced by Aston. Negative packing fraction means the nucleus is more stable than the average for that mass number. Positive means less stable. This led to the discovery of isotopes and mass defects.
20**Mass-energy equivalence in chemical reactions:** Chemical bonds have binding energies too, but they are much smaller (a few eV per bond) compared to nuclear binding (∼10⁶ eV). The mass defect in chemical bonds is negligible but measurable with precision instruments.
21**Energy units in nuclear physics:** 1 eV (electron volt) = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The binding energy of nucleons is typically 1-8 MeV per nucleon, while chemical bond energies are 1-10 eV per atom — nuclear energies are ∼10⁶ times larger.
22**Q-value of nuclear reactions:** Q = (mass of reactants - mass of products) × c². If Q > 0, the reaction is exothermic (releases energy). If Q < 0, it is endothermic (absorbs energy). Q equals the kinetic energy released or absorbed.
23**Stability and binding:** The most stable nuclides have the highest binding energy per nucleon. Iron-56, nickel-62, and iron-58 are the most tightly bound. Very light and very heavy nuclei are less stable.
24**Einstein's famous equation derivation:** From the special theory of relativity, the relativistic energy-momentum relation E² = (mc²)² + (pc)² simplifies to E = mc² when p = 0 (particle at rest). This holds for ALL particles, not just nuclear ones.

Nuclear Fission and Fusion

#Concept
1**Particle creation (pair production):** A high-energy photon (γ-ray) with energy hν ≥ 2mc² can create a particle-antiparticle pair. For electron-positron: γ → e⁺ + e⁻. The minimum photon energy is 2m_e c² = 1.022 MeV. Both rest mass energy (2mc²) and momentum must be conserved, requiring a nearby nucleus to absorb recoil.
2**Pair production threshold:** For a photon to create a particle of mass m at rest, the photon must have energy at least E_min = 2mc² (for pair production). In the center-of-mass frame, the threshold is exactly 2mc². At rest, the created particles have zero kinetic energy.
3**Annihilation:** When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate, converting their entire rest mass into energy. e⁺ + e⁻ → γ + γ (two photons to conserve momentum). Total energy released = 2m_e c² = 1.022 MeV.
4**Nuclear fission:** A heavy nucleus splits into two (or more) lighter nuclei plus neutrons, releasing large amounts of energy. Example: ²³⁵U + n → ⁹²Kr + ¹⁴¹Ba + 3n + 200 MeV. The neutrons released trigger a chain reaction.
5**Chain reaction and criticality:** In ²³⁵U fission, an average of 2-3 neutrons are released per fission. If, on average, exactly one neutron causes another fission, the reaction is critical. More than one → supercritical (exponential growth). Less than one → subcritical (reaction dies out).
6**Nuclear fusion:** Light nuclei combine to form heavier nuclei, releasing energy when the product has higher binding energy per nucleon. Example: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV. Fusion powers stars (proton-proton chain, CNO cycle) and hydrogen bombs.
7**Coulomb barrier in fusion:** Positively charged nuclei repel each other (Coulomb force). To overcome this and fuse, particles need high kinetic energy (high temperature) to approach within the nuclear force range (< 10⁻¹⁵ m). This requires temperatures of 10⁷-10⁸ K.
8**Deuterium-tritium (D-T) fusion:** The easiest fusion reaction to achieve on Earth. ²H + ³H → ⁴He (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV) + 17.6 MeV total. The neutron can be captured to generate heat for electricity. Requires ~100 million K.
9**Proton-proton chain (solar fusion):** The dominant fusion process in stars like the Sun. 4p → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν_e + 26.7 MeV. Involves proton to neutron conversion via weak interaction (beta-plus decay).
10**Fusion vs. fission energy per event:** A single U-235 fission releases ~200 MeV. A D-T fusion event releases ~17.6 MeV. However, fusion releases about 3-4 times more energy PER UNIT MASS because lighter nuclei have more nucleons.
11**Controlled vs. uncontrolled reactions:** Nuclear reactors use control rods (boron, cadmium) and moderators (water, graphite) to absorb excess neutrons and control the reaction. Nuclear weapons allow uncontrolled supercritical chain reactions producing massive energy release.
12**Binding energy perspective:** Fusion increases binding energy (releases energy) for nuclei lighter than iron (~8.8 MeV/nucleon). Fission increases binding energy for nuclei heavier than iron. Iron itself is the most stable — neither fission nor fusion releases energy.

Creation and Annihilation

#Concept
1Pair Production: A high-energy photon (gamma ray) converts into an electron-positron pair near a nucleus: γ + nucleus → e⁻ + e⁺ + nucleus. The nucleus recoils to conserve momentum. Threshold energy: E_γ ≥ 2m_ec² = 1.022 MeV.
2Pair Annihilation: An electron and positron collide and convert to photons: e⁻ + e⁺ → 2γ. Each photon has energy m_ec² = 0.511 MeV (if at rest), emitted in opposite directions (180° apart) to conserve momentum.
3Energy Conservation: In pair production, photon energy = 2m_ec² + kinetic energies of pair + recoil of nucleus. In annihilation, total energy (rest + kinetic) converts to photon energy.
4Momentum Conservation: A photon cannot produce an e⁻e⁺ pair in empty space — momentum cannot be conserved. A nearby nucleus (or electron) absorbs recoil momentum.
5Charge Conservation: Pair production creates net charge 0 (−e + +e = 0); annihilation starts with net charge 0. Charge is conserved.
6Applications: PET scans (positron emission tomography) use annihilation photons (511 keV each, back-to-back) for medical imaging. Positrons from radioactive tracers annihilate with electrons in tissue.
7Matter-Antimatter Asymmetry: Universe appears dominated by matter with very little antimatter — one of the great unsolved problems in physics (baryon asymmetry).

Thinking fusion releases more per reaction: Per reaction fission releases more (~200 vs 17.6 MeV), but per kg fusion releases more.
Believing all uranium can fuel reactors: Only ²³⁵U (0.7% of natural uranium) is fissile with thermal neutrons.
Thinking fusion is easy: Needs extreme confinement (magnetic or inertial) — no commercial plant yet.
Confusing critical mass with criticality: Critical mass is amount; criticality (k=1) is the condition.

Summary: Fission splits heavy nuclei (~200 MeV, neutron-triggered, chain reaction). Fusion joins light nuclei (~17.6 MeV/D-T, needs ~10⁸ K). Chain reaction requires critical mass. Reactors control fission with moderator and control rods. Fusion powers stars and offers clean energy but is technically challenging.
