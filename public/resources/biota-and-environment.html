Animal adaptation: Aquatic (primary and secondary), terre…

#Concept
1Animal adaptation: structural, physiological, and behavioural features that allow an animal to survive in a particular environment.
2Aquatic adaptation: primary aquatic (fishes, fully aquatic) and secondary aquatic (whales, seals — originally terrestrial).
3Aquatic features: streamlined (fusiform) body, fins, gills (countercurrent exchange), lateral line (fish), webbed feet, dense bones, salt glands.
4Terrestrial adaptation: cursorial (running — cheetah, horse), fossorial (burrowing — earthworm, mole), arboreal (tree-dwelling — monkey, sloth).
5Terrestrial features: waterproof skin (fur, scales, feathers), lungs, internal fertilisation, nitrogenous waste (urea/ammonia), strong limbs.
6Volant adaptation: flight; features: wings (feathers), hollow (pneumatic) bones, air sacs (double respiration), strong pectoral muscles, streamlined body, beak.
7Adaptations increase survival and reproduction in a given habitat; they are the result of natural selection.
8NEB focus: aquatic (primary + secondary), terrestrial (cursorial, fossorial, arboreal), and volant adaptations.
9Nepal examples: fish (Terai), rhododendron forest birds (volant), snow leopard (terrestrial cursorial), pangolin (fossorial).

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Primary aquatic animals were ALWAYS aquatic (fishes); secondary aquatic animals (whales, seals) evolved FROM terrestrial ancestors.
A fusiform (streamlined) body REDUCES water resistance; it is an adaptation for fast swimming.
Countercurrent exchange in fish gills allows ~80% O2 uptake efficiency (blood and water flow in opposite directions).
Hollow bones in birds reduce weight for flight; they are still strong (internal struts).
Arboreal animals (monkeys) have grasping hands/feet, forward-facing eyes (stereoscopic vision), and a prehensile tail (New World monkeys).
Fossorial animals (moles, earthworms) have reduced eyes, strong forelimbs for digging, and cylindrical bodies.

Animal Behaviour and Migration

#Concept
1Animal behavior: the actions and responses of animals to stimuli; includes innate (fixed) and learned behaviours.
2Reflex action: an automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, mediated by a reflex arc (receptor → sensory neuron → CNS → motor neuron → effector).
3Taxes: orientation responses (movement toward or away from a stimulus). Phototaxis (light), chemotaxis (chemical), thigmotaxis (touch), geotaxis (gravity).
4Taxis vs Kinesis: taxis is DIRECTIONAL movement toward/away from a stimulus; kinesis is NON-directional (random) movement in response to a stimulus.
5Dominance and leadership: social hierarchies in group-living animals (primates, wolves, fish); the dominant individual has priority access to food, mates, and territory.
6Fish migration: seasonal movement to feeding or breeding grounds; e.g., salmon (upstream spawning), mackerel (Nepal Terai rivers).
7Bird migration: long-distance seasonal movement (e.g., Himalayan birds migrate to lower altitudes in winter; storks migrate to the Terai).
8NEB focus: reflex action, taxes, dominance and leadership, fish and bird migration.
9Migration is a behavioural adaptation; it is seasonal and genetically programmed.

Environmental Pollution and Pesticides

#Concept
1Environmental pollution: the addition of substances or energy to the environment at a rate that exceeds its capacity to dilute, degrade, or absorb them.
2Air pollution: SO2 (coal), NOx (vehicles), CO (incomplete combustion), PM2.5/PM10 (particulates), ozone (ground-level), CFCs; sources: industry, vehicles, power plants, biomass burning.
3Effects of air pollution: respiratory diseases (asthma, bronchitis), acid rain, smog, global warming, ozone depletion.
4Kathmandu Valley: PM2.5 exceeds WHO limits ~10× in winter (stove smoke, vehicles, dust).
5Water pollution: domestic waste, industrial effluent, agricultural runoff (pesticides, fertilisers), oil spills, thermal pollution; effects: eutrophication, oxygen depletion, disease (cholera, typhoid).
6Soil pollution: heavy metals, pesticides, plastics, industrial waste; effects: reduced fertility, contamination of food, leaching to groundwater.
7Pesticides: chemicals to control pests; types: insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, rodenticides; effects: biomagnification, resistance, non-target species death, soil/water contamination.
8Control measures: reduce emissions, renewable energy, tree planting, waste treatment, organic farming, international agreements (Montreal, Paris).
9NEB focus: sources, effects, and control measures of air, water, and soil pollution; pesticides and their effects.
10Nepal: vulnerable to air (Kathmandu), water (river pollution), and soil (pesticide) pollution; deforestation and urbanisation are major contributors.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅

Ozone is beneficial in the STRATOSPHERE (blocks UV) but harmful at ground level (smog, air pollutant).

Pesticide resistance: pests evolve resistance over time; higher doses are needed.

Fish and bird migration

#Concept
1[Remember] Migration is the periodic, seasonal, two-way movement of animals between breeding and non-breeding habitats — distinct from one-way dispersal or permanent emigration.
2[Remember] Fish migration: anadromous fishes move from sea to fresh water to breed (e.g. Hilsa ilisha ascending Terai rivers in monsoon); catadromous fishes move from fresh water to sea to breed (e.g. eels, Anguilla).
3[Understand] Bird migration types: latitudinal or north–south (Siberian ducks wintering at Koshi Tappu and Chitwan), longitudinal, and altitudinal (pheasants and thrushes descending from hills to Terai in winter).
4[Understand] Causes and triggers: breeding urge, food scarcity, shortening photoperiod, falling temperature and hormonal change; navigation uses the sun compass, star patterns, Earth's magnetic field and geographical landmarks.
5[Apply] Contrast table — anadromous vs catadromous (direction, breeding ground, one example each); resident vs migratory vs partial-migrant birds with one Nepali example of each.
6[Analyze] Advantages (safe breeding grounds, abundant seasonal food) versus costs (enormous energy demand, predation, barriers such as dams and habitat loss); homing instinct and ringing/banding studies as evidence.
7[Evaluate/Create] NEB answer frame: define migration → classify fish and bird types → causes and navigation → Koshi Tappu/Chitwan and Terai-river examples → one advantage–cost line → labelled route sketch.

Key Points

Point
Migration definition and two-way nature
Anadromous vs catadromous with examples
Bird types, causes and navigation
Koshi Tappu/Chitwan examples plus route sketch

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Migration is two-way and seasonal; one-way dispersal or permanent emigration is NOT migration.
Anadromous = sea → river to breed; catadromous = river → sea to breed — do not swap the arrows.
Altitudinal migration (hills ↔ Terai) is still migration even though the distance is short.
Homing instinct and banding studies prove return journeys; do not claim birds 'get lost and stay'.

Pesticides and their effects

#Concept
1[Remember] Pesticides are chemicals that kill pests: insecticides (insects), herbicides (weeds), fungicides (fungi), rodenticides (rats) — e.g. DDT, BHC, malathion, 2,4-D.
2[Remember] Effects on pests: target kill and crop protection; effects beyond target — killing of pollinators and predators, pest resurgence and resistance after repeated use.
3[Understand] Human-health effects: acute poisoning (nausea, tremor) and chronic effects (organ damage, suspected carcinogenicity); Terai farm workers face the highest exposure risk.
4[Understand] Environmental effects: soil-microbe loss and fertility fall; water pollution and fish kills; biomagnification up food chains (classic case: DDT in birds); harm to bees and natural enemies.
5[Apply] Safe-use table — read label dose, wear protection, spray with wind direction, observe waiting period before harvest, never wash sprayers in ponds or rivers.
6[Analyze] Chemical control vs biological control vs IPM (Integrated Pest Management: resistant varieties + predators + need-based minimal spray); why blanket spraying fails in the long run.
7[Evaluate/Create] NEB answer frame: define with classes → target vs non-target effects → health + environment with biomagnification → safe-use and IPM conclusion with a Terai paddy example.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Insecticide kills insects, herbicide kills weeds, fungicide kills fungi — match the suffix to the target.
Biomagnification is concentration rising UP the food chain; bioaccumulation is build-up within one body.
Pest resurgence happens because pesticides also kill predators — more spray can mean more pests later.
IPM reduces but does not always eliminate chemicals; 'zero spray' is not the NEB definition.

Academic Extension: Evidence, Conditions & Open Questions

Topic bridge — Biota And Environment. Use this extension to connect the topic’s core material with deeper academic inquiry: state a claim, name the conditions under which it holds, and distinguish established knowledge from an open research or interpretive question.

Established academic fact

Ecosystems contain interacting organisms and non-living conditions. Energy flows through food webs and is dissipated as heat, while elements such as carbon and nitrogen cycle; biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

Population or ecosystem trends depend on place, season, sampling method and time scale. Distinguish a measured observation from a projection, and report uncertainty rather than treating one survey as a complete census.

Open research question — How Himalayan ecosystems respond to rapid change

Changes in snow and ice across the Hindu Kush Himalaya are documented, but timing and local consequences for water, habitats and particular species remain uncertain. Scenario-based projections are not guaranteed outcomes; monitoring and stating the scenario are essential.
