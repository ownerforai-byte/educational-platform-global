Energy Bands in Solids

#Concept
1**Band formation:** In isolated atoms, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When $N$ atoms aggregate into a solid, each atomic level splits into $N$ closely spaced levels, forming near-continuous energy bands. The broadening width is $\sim 1\text{--}10\,\text{eV}$, with individual levels separated by $\sim 10^{-22}\,\text{eV}$.
2**Valence band:** The highest energy band that is occupied (filled) at absolute zero. Formed from the valence-shell atomic orbitals. When completely filled with electrons, it cannot conduct electricity unless electrons are promoted to the conduction band.
3**Conduction band:** The next higher energy band, empty at absolute zero. Electrons here are delocalized across the crystal and are free to move under an applied electric field, contributing to electrical conduction.
4**Band gap $E_g$:** The energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band. It is the defining parameter: $E_g$ determines whether a material is a metal ($E_g = 0$, overlap), semiconductor ($E_g \sim 0.5\text{--}3\,\text{eV}$), or insulator ($E_g > 3\,\text{eV}$).
5**Forbidden gap:** The range of energies between the valence band top and conduction band bottom where no allowed electronic states exist. An electron must gain exactly $E_g$ energy to traverse this gap.
6**Direct vs indirect band gap:** In direct-gap materials (GaAs, InP), the minimum of the conduction band aligns in $k$-space with the maximum of the valence band. An electron can recombine with a hole without changing momentum, emitting a photon efficiently. In indirect-gap materials (Si, Ge), momentum offset means phonon involvement is required, making recombination less efficient for light emission.
7**Temperature effect on $E_g$:** In most semiconductors, $E_g$ decreases with temperature as $E_g(T) = E_g(0) - \alpha T^2/(T + \beta)$. For silicon: $E_g(T) = 1.17 - 4.73\times 10^{-4} T^2/(T + 636)$ in eV. This is why the resistivity of semiconductors decreases with temperature.
8**Bloch theorem:** Electron wavefunctions in a periodic crystal have the form $\psi_{nk}(\mathbf{r}) = e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}} u_{nk}(\mathbf{r})$, where $u_{nk}$ has the same periodicity as the crystal lattice. This explains why electrons in a solid behave like plane waves modulated by the periodic potential.
9**Effective mass:** Electrons near the band minimum behave as if they have a different mass $m^*$ due to the band curvature: $1/m^* = (1/\hbar^2)(d^2E/dk^2)$. $m^*$ can be lighter or heavier than the free electron mass, and differs for electrons vs holes.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusion: In metals, the conduction band is partially filled, OR the valence band overlaps the conduction band. Students often assume conduction band = empty, which is only true for insulators and semiconductors at absolute zero.
Confusion: A large band gap ($> 3\,\text{eV}$) means the material is an insulator only at room temperature. Diamond ($E_g = 5.5\,\text{eV}$) is an excellent insulator but can be doped like a semiconductor, making it useful for high-power electronics.
Confusion: The band gap is an energy difference, not a physical distance. $E_g = 1\,\text{eV}$ means electrons need $1\,\text{eV}$ of energy to jump from valence to conduction band — no spatial thickness is implied.
Confusion: The valence band is filled with electrons in insulators/semiconductors at 0 K, so no current flows. But a completely empty valence band would also not conduct — it is the partial occupancy (holes) that enables conduction.
Confusion: In p-type semiconductors, the valence band holes are NOT vacancies created by removing electrons from the valence band. They are treated as positively charged particles because electrons in a nearly-full band respond to electric fields as if they were holes.
Confusion: The effective mass $m^*$ is NOT a fixed constant — it depends on the band curvature at the Fermi level and direction in anisotropic crystals (e.g., silicon has different $m^*$ along different crystal directions).
Confusion: The forbidden gap has NO electronic states, but phonons (lattice vibrations) can have energies within this range. The term "forbidden" refers only to electronic states.

Energy Bands in Solids (Qualitative Ideas)

#Concept
1Atomic Energy Levels: An isolated atom has discrete energy levels (1s, 2s, 2p, etc.). Electrons occupy these levels according to Pauli exclusion principle.
2Formation of Energy Bands: When N atoms come together to form a solid, their wavefunctions overlap. Each atomic level splits into N closely spaced levels, forming an energy band. For macroscopic N (~10²³), levels form quasi-continuous bands.
3Valence Band: The highest energy band containing electrons at 0 K. It may be fully or partially filled. Electrons in this band are bound to atoms.
4Conduction Band: The next higher energy band, typically empty or partially filled at 0 K. Electrons here are free to move and conduct electricity.
5Band Gap (E_g): The energy gap between valence band and conduction band where no electron states exist. Size determines electrical classification.
6Energy vs Momentum (E-k diagram): In solids, electron energy depends on wavevector k. Bands form allowed ranges; gaps are forbidden ranges.
7Pauli Exclusion: Each quantum state can hold max 2 electrons (spin up/down). Band filling determines whether material conducts.

Key Points

Point
Isolated atoms have discrete levels; solids have energy bands.
N atomic levels split into N closely spaced levels forming a band.
Valence band: highest occupied band at 0 K.
Conduction band: band above valence band where electrons conduct.
Band gap E_g: forbidden energy region between valence and conduction bands.
Band structure determines whether solid is metal, insulator, or semiconductor.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Thinking bands are like atomic levels: Bands contain N closely spaced levels, not one level.
Believing valence electrons are the only ones in valence band: Core electrons also in bands but too tightly bound to conduct.
Confusing band gap with work function: Band gap is internal to material; work function is energy to remove electron from surface to vacuum.
Thinking all electrons in conduction band conduct: Only electrons near Fermi level participate significantly.
Believing bands exist in isolated atoms: Bands require interatomic overlap in solids.

Summary: In solids, discrete atomic energy levels broaden into energy bands. Valence band (highest occupied at 0 K), conduction band (above, where electrons conduct), and band gap E_g (forbidden region between them). Band structure determines metal/semiconductor/insulator classification.

Energy Bands in Solids

#Concept
1When many atoms form a solid, interactions split atomic energy levels into closely spaced allowed bands separated by forbidden energy gaps. Electrons cannot occupy energies in an ideal band gap.
2The valence band contains bonding electrons; the conduction band contains mobile carriers. The gap Eg=Ec−Ev and the band filling help explain conductivity.
3Metals have partially filled bands or overlapping bands. Insulators have a large gap and very few room-temperature carriers. Semiconductors have a smaller gap, so thermal or optical excitation can promote electrons into the conduction band.
4In an intrinsic semiconductor, promoting an electron leaves a hole in the valence band. Both the electron and the hole can carry current; heating can therefore increase carrier concentration.
5A photon can excite an electron across the gap if hν≥Eg. This underlies photoconductivity and semiconductor photodetectors.

Key Points

Point
Allowed bands and forbidden gaps arise in the periodic solid.
Carrier concentration and mobility jointly determine conductivity.
An electron promoted to conduction leaves a hole.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
A band gap is a forbidden energy range, not a physical gap in the material.
Band gap alone does not determine conductivity without carrier concentration and mobility.

Summary: Band theory explains conductivity through allowed energy bands, gaps and carrier availability. Thermal and optical excitation can create mobile electron–hole pairs.

Metals, Insulators and Semiconductors – Band Theory

#Concept
1**Metals – band overlap or partial filling:** In metals, the valence band is either partially filled (alkali metals like Na, K have one electron per atom in an s-band) OR it overlaps with the conduction band (alkaline earth metals like Mg). In both cases, the Fermi level $E_F$ lies within a band, so electrons have empty states immediately available at $E_F$. Even a small electric field accelerates electrons, giving high conductivity.
2**Insulators – wide band gap:** In insulators (diamond, SiO$_2$, NaCl), the valence band is completely filled and the conduction band is completely empty at 0 K. The band gap $E_g > 3\,\text{eV}$ (typically 5–10 eV). At room temperature, $k_B T \approx 0.026\,\text{eV} \ll E_g$, so thermal excitation across the gap is negligible. No carriers $\rightarrow$ no conduction.
3**Semiconductors – small band gap:** In semiconductors (Si, Ge, GaAs), the band gap is small ($E_g \approx 0.5\text{--}3\,\text{eV}$). At room temperature, a small but significant number of electrons are thermally excited from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving holes in the valence band. Both electrons (conduction band) and holes (valence band) contribute to conduction.
4**Temperature coefficient:** In metals, resistivity increases with temperature ($ ho \propto T$) because increased lattice vibrations (phonons) scatter electrons more. In semiconductors, resistivity decreases with temperature ($ ho \propto e^{E_g/2k_B T}$) because more electron-hole pairs are thermally generated. This opposite trend is a hallmark distinction.
5**Band gap values:** Silicon $E_g = 1.12\,\text{eV}$ (indirect). Germanium $E_g = 0.67\,\text{eV}$ (indirect). Gallium arsenide $E_g = 1.43\,\text{eV}$ (direct). Diamond $E_g = 5.5\,\text{eV}$ (insulator but wide-gap semiconductor when doped). InSb $E_g = 0.17\,\text{eV}$ (narrow-gap semiconductor).
6**Intrinsic carrier concentration:** For an intrinsic semiconductor, $n_i^2 = N_c N_v \exp(-E_g/k_B T)$, where $N_c$, $N_v$ are the effective density of states in the conduction and valence bands. At $300\,\text{K}$: $n_i(\text{Si}) \approx 1.5 \times 10^{16}\,\text{m}^{-3}$, $n_i(\text{Ge}) \approx 2.4 \times 10^{19}\,\text{m}^{-3}$, $n_i(\text{GaAs}) \approx 2.1 \times 10^{12}\,\text{m}^{-3}$.
7**Conduction mechanism:** In metals, only electrons conduct. In semiconductors, both electrons (negative charge, mobility $\mu_n$) and holes (positive charge, mobility $\mu_p$) conduct. Total conductivity $\sigma = n e \mu_n + p e \mu_p$, where $n, p$ are electron and hole concentrations.
8**Hole conduction:** A hole is the absence of an electron in a nearly-full valence band. When an electric field is applied, electrons move to fill the hole, making the hole appear to move opposite to the electron drift. Holes have positive effective charge $+e$ and positive effective mass $m_p^*$.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusion: Students often think insulators and semiconductors are fundamentally different materials — they are the SAME band structure, just with different $E_g$ values. Diamond ($5.5\,\text{eV}$) is an insulator, silicon ($1.12\,\text{eV}$) is a semiconductor; both have the diamond crystal structure.
Confusion: Metals do NOT have a band gap. The Fermi level lies inside a band. There is no "forbidden gap" separating valence from conduction band — they are the same band or overlapping bands.
Confusion: In metals, conductivity decreases with temperature because more phonons scatter electrons. In semiconductors, conductivity increases because more carriers are generated. Students mix up the direction of temperature dependence.
Confusion: The Fermi level in an intrinsic semiconductor lies near the MIDDLE of the band gap, not at the bottom of the conduction band. $E_F \approx E_g/2 + (k_B T/2) \ln(N_v/N_c)$. This is crucial for understanding doping effects.
Confusion: Holes are NOT real particles. They are quasiparticles representing collective motion of electrons in a nearly-full band. They have positive charge, but they are not positrons (antimatter).
Confusion: A partially filled valence band and a completely filled valence band with overlap to a conduction band are both metallic. The distinction is subtle — in the first case, there is one band partially filled; in the second, two bands overlap, so electrons can move freely into the higher band.
Confusion: Germanium has higher intrinsic carrier concentration than silicon at room temperature because its band gap is smaller ($0.67\,\text{eV}$ vs $1.12\,\text{eV}$), so thermal excitation is more probable. But Si is more widely used because it forms a stable native oxide (SiO$_2$).
Confusion: The band gap of a semiconductor is temperature dependent. $E_g$ decreases as temperature increases (lattice expansion, electron-phonon coupling). This means the temperature coefficient of resistance has contributions from both carrier generation and gap narrowing.

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

#Concept
1Band Theory Basis: Semiconductors have a small forbidden energy gap E_g between valence band (full at 0 K) and conduction band (empty at 0 K). For Si: E_g = 1.12 eV; Ge: E_g = 0.67 eV. Thermal energy kT ≈ 0.026 eV at 300 K allows some electrons to jump the gap.
2Intrinsic Semiconductor: Pure crystal with no impurities. At T > 0 K, thermal excitation creates electron-hole pairs: n_i = p_i = √(N_c N_v) exp(-E_g/2kT). Conductivity σ = n_i e (μ_e + μ_h). For Si at 300 K: n_i ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³.
3n-Type Semiconductor: Doped with pentavalent (group V) atoms (P, As, Sb) having 5 valence electrons. Four bond with Si; fifth is loosely bound (donor level E_d just below E_c). Ionization energy ~0.05 eV → nearly all donors ionized at room T. Majority carriers: electrons; minority: holes. n_n ≈ N_d ≫ p_n.
4p-Type Semiconductor: Doped with trivalent (group III) atoms (B, Al, Ga) having 3 valence electrons. Creates vacancy (acceptor level E_a just above E_v). Electrons from valence band fill acceptor levels, creating mobile holes. Majority carriers: holes; minority: electrons. p_p ≈ N_a ≫ n_p.
5Mass Action Law: In thermal equilibrium, np = n_i² regardless of doping type or level. Increasing majority carrier concentration suppresses minority carriers via recombination. Fundamental relation for device analysis.
6Fermi Level Position: Intrinsic: E_F lies near mid-gap. n-type: E_F shifts toward conduction band (closer to E_c). p-type: E_F shifts toward valence band (closer to E_v). Heavy doping can push E_F into bands (degenerate semiconductor).
7Conductivity Control: σ = e(nμ_e + pμ_h). Doping adjusts n or p over many orders of magnitude (10¹⁰ to 10¹⁹ cm⁻³). Mobility μ decreases at high doping due to impurity scattering. Optimal doping balances carrier density and mobility.
8Temperature Dependence: Low T: freeze-out region (carriers not ionized). Medium T: extrinsic region (all dopants ionized, n ≈ N_d or p ≈ N_a). High T: intrinsic region (thermally generated pairs dominate over dopants). Device operation typically in extrinsic region.
9Applications: p-n junctions (diodes, LEDs, solar cells), bipolar transistors (npn/pnp), MOSFETs (CMOS logic), integrated circuits. Modern electronics relies entirely on controlled doping of silicon.

Key Points

Point
Intrinsic: pure semiconductor, n = p = n_i, conductivity limited by E_g.
n-type: group V dopants donate electrons; n ≫ p; E_F near E_c.
p-type: group III dopants create holes; p ≫ n; E_F near E_v.
Mass action law np = n_i² holds always at equilibrium.
Doping controls conductivity over 9+ orders of magnitude.
Device function depends on spatial variation of doping profiles.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Thinking n-type means negative charge: Material is electrically neutral; 'n' refers to negative charge carriers (electrons), not net charge.
Believing holes are real particles: Holes are absence of electrons behaving as positive quasiparticles with effective mass.
Assuming doping adds free carriers without limit: Compensation, solubility limits, and defect formation constrain achievable doping.
Confusing intrinsic carrier density with doping level: n_i depends only on T and E_g; doping sets n or p independently.
Thinking higher doping always improves devices: Excessive doping causes tunneling, reduced lifetime, and contact degradation.

Summary: Semiconductors have electrical conductivity between conductors and insulators. Intrinsic semiconductors are pure (e.g., Si, Ge) with equal electron and hole concentrations generated thermally across the band gap. Extrinsic semiconductors are doped with impurities: n-type adds donor electrons (group V), p-type adds acceptor holes (group III). Doping dramatically increases conductivity and enables diode/transistor action.

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

#Concept
1**Intrinsic semiconductor:** A pure semiconductor crystal with no significant impurity atoms (ppm or less). Examples: pure silicon ($99.9999999\\\\%$), pure germanium. At any finite temperature, thermal excitation creates equal numbers of electrons and holes: $n = p = n_i$.
2**Extrinsic semiconductor:** A semiconductor deliberately doped with a small concentration of impurity atoms (typically $10^{15}\ ext{--}10^{18}\\\\,\ ext{cm}^{-3}$) to modify its electrical properties. Doping dramatically increases conductivity by orders of magnitude.
3**n-type doping:** Pentavalent donor atoms (Group V: P, As, Sb) are substituted for silicon atoms. Each donor has 5 valence electrons; 4 form covalent bonds with neighboring Si atoms, leaving 1 extra electron loosely bound. The donor ionization energy $E_d \\\\approx 0.01\ ext{--}0.05\\\\,\ ext{eV}$ (much smaller than $E_g$), so nearly all donors are ionized at room temperature. Electrons are majority carriers; holes are minority carriers.
4**p-type doping:** Trivalent acceptor atoms (Group III: B, Al, Ga) replace silicon atoms. Each acceptor has 3 valence electrons, creating a vacant bond (hole) in the lattice. The acceptor ionization energy $E_a \\\\approx 0.01\ ext{--}0.05\\\\,\ ext{eV}$. Holes are majority carriers; electrons are minority carriers.
5**Mass-action law:** In thermal equilibrium, $np = n_i^2$ holds for any semiconductor (intrinsic or extrinsic). For an n-type sample with donor concentration $N_d$, $n \\\\approx N_d$ and $p \\\\approx n_i^2/N_d$.
6**Carrier concentration:** At room temperature, all donors in n-type are ionized (freeze-out is negligible). For $N_d = 10^{16}\\\, ext{cm}^{-3}$, $n \\\\approx 10^{16}\\\, ext{cm}^{-3}$, which is $10^5$ times higher than intrinsic Si ($n_i \\\\approx 1.5\ imes 10^{10}\\\, ext{cm}^{-3}$). Conductivity increases by the same factor.
7**Compensation:** Adding both donors and acceptors partially cancels. If $N_d > N_a$, the net carrier concentration is $\\\\approx N_d - N_a$. This is used in device fabrication to set precise resistivities.
8**Charge neutrality:** In n-type: $n = N_d^+ + p$. In p-type: $p = N_a^- + n$. At room temperature, $N_d^+ \\\\approx N_d$ and $N_a^- \\\\approx N_a$, simplifying to $n \\\\approx N_d$ (n-type) or $p \\\\approx N_a$ (p-type).
9**Temperature regimes:** At low temperature (freeze-out), only a fraction of donors are ionized; $n \\\\propto \\\\exp(-E_d/2k_BT)$. At room temperature (extrinsic), all donors ionized; $n \\\\approx N_d$. At high temperature (intrinsic), thermal excitation across the gap dominates; $n \\\\approx n_i$ again.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusion: Doping increases the number of charge carriers but does NOT create new charge. The semiconductor remains electrically neutral overall: extra electrons from donors are balanced by positive donor ions.
Confusion: In n-type semiconductors, electrons are majority carriers and holes are minority carriers. The "n" stands for negative (electron) carriers - NOT for the type of dopant atom. N-type uses donor atoms, but "n" refers to electron conduction.
Confusion: A p-type semiconductor has holes as majority carriers but is NOT positively charged overall. It remains neutral because the immobile acceptor ions (negative) balance the mobile holes.
Confusion: Doping does NOT change the number of protons in the silicon lattice. $Z$ is still 14 for Si. Doping changes the NUMBER of free electrons/holes, not the elemental identity.
Confusion: In n-type, electrons are donated by the dopant (e.g., P), but the silicon atoms are still the majority of atoms. The dopant concentration is tiny ($10^{15}\ ext{--}10^{18}\\\\,\ ext{cm}^{-3}$) compared to Si atoms ($5\ imes 10^{22}\\\\,\ ext{cm}^{-3}$).
Confusion: The mobility of electrons ($\\\\mu_n$) is generally HIGHER than the mobility of holes ($\\\\mu_p$) in the same semiconductor because the conduction band has a lighter effective mass than the valence band (heavy-hole, light-hole bands).
Confusion: Extrinsic conductivity ($\\\\sigma = n e \\\\mu_n$) is not limited by the band gap because carriers are generated from donor/acceptor levels rather than from across the full gap.
Confusion: A compensated semiconductor (both donors and acceptors) does NOT become intrinsic. The net carrier concentration is $|N_d - N_a|$ if one dominates; only equal concentrations of donors and acceptors ($N_d = N_a$) make it intrinsic-like.
