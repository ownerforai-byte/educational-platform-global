Gas Laws: Boyle's, Charles', Avogadro's, Combined Gas Equation, Dalton's Law of Partial Pressure and Graham's Law of Diffusion

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning
2The gas laws are the experimentally established relations between the pressure (P), volume (V), absolute temperature (T) and amount (n) of a fixed mass of gas. They hold for an ideal gas, i.e. at low pressure and high temperature where molecular volume and intermolecular forces are negligible.
3### 2. Boyle's law (1662)
4At constant temperature the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure: V ∝ 1/P, so PV = constant (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂). Graphically, P versus V is a rectangular hyperbola and P versus 1/V is a straight line through the origin; each curve is called an isotherm. Molecular meaning: compressing the gas raises the number of collisions per unit area per unit time, which increases pressure.
5### 3. Charles' law (1787; published 1802)
6At constant pressure the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature: V ∝ T, so V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (isobar). A V–t(°C) plot is a straight line meeting the temperature axis at −273.15 °C, the origin of the absolute (Kelvin) scale. The same logic at constant **volume** gives the pressure law (Gay-Lussac): P ∝ T, P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ (isochore).
7### 4. Avogadro's law (1811)
8Equal volumes of all gases under the same conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules: V ∝ n at constant T and P. Consequences: molar volume of any gas at STP = 22.4 dm³ (22.4 L) and at RTP ≈ 24 dm³; 1 mol contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules; vapour density and molecular mass can be calculated from gas volumes.
9### 5. Combined gas equation and the ideal gas equation
10Combining Boyle, Charles and Avogadro for a fixed mass: $\frac{P_{1}V_{1}}{T_{1}}=\frac{P_{2}V_{2}}{T_{2}}$ and finally $PV=nRT$, where R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 2 cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. R is the work done by one mole of an ideal gas per kelvin of expansion at constant pressure (universal gas constant).
11### 6. Dalton's law of partial pressures (1801)
12The total pressure of a mixture of non-reacting gases is the sum of the partial pressures each gas would exert alone in the same volume: P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …; and P_i = x_i P_total where x_i is the mole fraction. Applications: gases collected over water (P_dry = P_total − aqueous tension), scuba and anaesthetic gas mixtures, and vapour-pressure corrections in the laboratory.
13### 7. Graham's law of diffusion (1833)
14The rate of diffusion (or effusion) of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its density or molar mass at the same T and P: $\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}}=\sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{M_{1}}}$. Applications: separation of isotopes (²³⁵UF₆/²³⁸UF₆ diffusion), leak detection, determination of molar mass, and explaining why a balloon filled with H₂ deflates faster than one with O₂.
15### 8. Applications
16• Calculating unknown P, V, T in experiments and industry (gas cylinders, LPG pressure, tyre pressure with temperature change, weather balloons, airbags).
17• Dalton's law is used for gases collected over water, in scuba-diving and anaesthesia gas mixtures, and in atmospheric humidity calculations; the combined gas equation underlies gas-flow and compression-ratio engineering.
18• Graham's law is used industrially to separate isotopes (²³⁵UF₆ from ²³⁸UF₆) and in the laboratory to determine molar masses and to detect leaks; Avogadro's law is the basis of molar-volume calculations and hence of all volumetric analysis of gases.
19### 9. Limitations
20• All the laws are **ideal-gas** laws: they are obeyed closely only at low pressure and high temperature; real gases deviate (and the deviation is strongest near the critical temperature, which is why gases cannot be liquefied merely by compression above T_c).
21• Each law needs a fixed mass of gas and holds P, or V, or T constant — applying two at once to a changing mass gives wrong answers.
22• Dalton's law applies only to non-reacting, non-associated ideal gases; it fails for reacting mixtures (NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl) and for gases that dissolve appreciably in the liquid present.
23• Graham's law is strictly valid for gases at equal temperature and pressure and for effusion through a small hole; it needs correction for high pressures, for heavier/polyatomic molecules and for diffusion through porous media.
24• Avogadro's law assumes equal molecular behaviour, though real molecules of different size and polarity behave differently; and the combined equation cannot represent a real gas — the van der Waals equation is required.
25**Fact.** Ideal gas equation PV = nRT; from kinetic theory P = ⅓ (Nm/m)⟨c²⟩, so PV = ⅓N m⟨c²⟩.
26**Fact.** r.m.s. speed c_rms = √(3RT/M); mean kinetic energy per mole = 3RT/2, per molecule = 3k_BT/2.
27**Fact.** Real gases deviate near condensation; Van der Waals: (P + a n²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Boyle's law means PV is always constant. ✅ Only for a *fixed mass* at *constant temperature*; PV changes when T or n changes.
❌ Doubling the Celsius temperature doubles the volume. ✅ Charles' law uses kelvin: 10 °C → 40 °C is a 293 → 313 K change, not a ×4 change.
❌ Dalton's law works for any gas mixture. ✅ It requires non-reacting gases; NH₃ and HCl react, so partial pressures are not additive.
❌ Graham's law compares diffusion rates directly with molar masses. ✅ Rates compare as the inverse square root of molar mass (or density).
❌ Gases obey PV = nRT exactly. ✅ Real gases obey it only at low P and high T; deviation is measured by the compressibility factor Z.

Gaseous State: Kinetic Theory of Gases and its Postulates

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning and purpose
2The kinetic theory of gases (Clausius, Maxwell, Boltzmann) explains the measurable behaviour of a gas — pressure, volume, temperature and diffusion — in terms of the motion of its molecules. It converts the empirical gas laws into a mechanical model: pressure arises from molecular collisions with the walls, and temperature measures the average molecular kinetic energy.
3### 2. Postulates (state them in order)
4• A gas consists of a very large number of extremely small molecules moving randomly and continuously in straight lines.
5• The actual volume of the molecules is negligible compared with the volume of the container — the gas is mostly empty space.
6• Molecules exert no force of attraction or repulsion on one another except during collision; intermolecular attraction is neglected.
7• Collisions between molecules, and between molecules and the walls, are perfectly elastic — no kinetic energy is lost.
8• Molecules are rigid, perfectly spherical and identical for a given gas; the duration of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
9• Pressure is produced by molecules striking the walls, and temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the molecules: KE = (3/2)kT per molecule = (3/2)RT per mole.
10### 3. Results derived from the postulates
11• Pressure equation: $PV=\frac{1}{3}mn\bar{c}^{2}$ for n molecules of mass m and mean square speed c̄².
12• With KE ∝ T this gives the ideal gas equation $PV=nRT$ and hence the laws of Boyle and Charles.
13• Speeds: $v_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}$, $\bar{c}=\sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}}$, $v_{mp}=\sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}}$, so v_mp < c̄ < v_rms.
14• Effusion and diffusion give Graham's law and Avogadro's law; a mixture gives Dalton's law of partial pressures.
15### 4. Meaning of the molecular speeds
16At 300 K the rms speed of O₂ is ≈483 m s⁻¹ and of H₂ ≈1930 m s⁻¹ — hundreds of metres per second, yet diffusion appears sluggish because molecules collide extremely often (mean free path ≈10⁻⁷ m at 1 atm) and follow a zig-zag path. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution shows a wide spread of speeds; as T rises the curve flattens and its peak shifts to higher speed.
17### 5. Applications
18• Explains gas pressure, the gas laws, Dalton's partial pressures, Graham's diffusion and effusion, and allows calculation of rms speeds, mean free path and collision frequency.
19• Used in the gaseous-diffusion enrichment of uranium (UF₆), vacuum technology and leak testing, and in atmospheric models — the escape of H₂ and He from Earth's atmosphere follows from their high molecular speeds.
20• Basis of statistical mechanics, gas thermometry, thermal conductivity of gases and the van der Waals corrections for real gases.
21### 6. Limitations of the kinetic theory
221. Molecules have finite volume, so at high pressure the free volume is smaller than the container volume and the theory's own assumption fails (real PV > nRT).
232. Intermolecular attractive forces exist; they make a real gas more compressible at moderate pressure and are responsible for liquefaction — neither is in the model.
243. Collisions are not perfectly elastic and molecules are not rigid spheres; polyatomic gases store energy in rotation and vibration, which the simple theory ignores (so it cannot give their specific heats correctly).
254. It presumes a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution and neglects quantum and relativistic effects (important for very light gases at low temperature).
265. It cannot describe the liquid state, critical phenomena or any structured/condensed behaviour, and it fails near the critical temperature where the ideal-gas picture collapses.
27**Fact.** Ideal gas equation PV = nRT; from kinetic theory P = ⅓ (Nm/m)⟨c²⟩, so PV = ⅓N m⟨c²⟩.
28**Fact.** r.m.s. speed c_rms = √(3RT/M); mean kinetic energy per mole = 3RT/2, per molecule = 3k_BT/2.
29**Fact.** Real gases deviate near condensation; Van der Waals: (P + a n²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Molecules keep moving at the rms speed. ✅ Speeds obey a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution; the rms value is only an average measure.
❌ Kinetic theory says molecules never interact. ✅ Interaction is neglected *except during collisions* — this idealisation is exactly what limits the model.
❌ Collisions destroy kinetic energy. ✅ Collisions are elastic; energy is redistributed, not lost.
❌ Pressure is a continuous push by molecules. ✅ Pressure is the average rate of momentum transfer during wall collisions.
❌ The theory is equally valid at 200 atm. ✅ Ideal-gas assumptions break down at high pressure and low temperature.

Deviation of Real Gases from Ideality

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of deviation
2A real gas obeys PV = nRT only approximately; the departure from that equation is called deviation from ideal behaviour. It arises because the two postulates of the kinetic theory are only idealisations — molecules do occupy volume, and intermolecular attractive forces do exist (otherwise no gas could ever be liquefied). Deviation is largest at **high pressure and low temperature**, and smallest for small, non-polar molecules such as H₂ and He.
3### 2. Compressibility factor Z
4$Z=\frac{PV}{nRT}$ (or Z = PV/RT for one mole) measures deviation: Z = 1 for an ideal gas, **Z < 1** when attraction dominates (moderate pressure, gas easier to compress than ideal), **Z > 1** when the finite molecular volume dominates (high pressure, gas harder to compress than ideal). Plotting Z against P shows that H₂ stays above 1 and rises steadily, while CO₂ dips below 1, crosses 1 and then rises — the classic experimental proof of deviation.
5### 3. Explanation of the two effects
6• **Attraction effect:** neighbouring molecules pull a molecule away from the wall, so it strikes with less momentum — the observed pressure is less than the ideal value (P_obs < P_ideal), which makes Z < 1.
7• **Volume effect:** the molecules themselves and their excluded volume reduce the free space, so the observed volume is greater than ideal (V_obs > V_ideal), making Z > 1.
8These two competing effects explain why Z may be less than 1 at moderate pressure and greater than 1 at high pressure, and why there is a temperature (Boyle temperature) at which Z ≈ 1 over a wide range of moderate pressures.
9### 4. van der Waals equation (corrections applied)
10$\left(P+\frac{an^{2}}{V^{2}}\right)(V-nb)=nRT$ for n moles, or $\left(P+\frac{a}{V^{2}}\right)(V-b)=RT$ for one mole. The constant **a** measures the strength of intermolecular attraction (pressure correction an²/V²) and **b** is the effective (excluded) molar volume due to finite molecular size — roughly four times the actual molecular volume. Larger a means easier liquefaction; larger b means a bulkier molecule.
11### 5. Critical constants and continuity of state
12Above the critical temperature Tc a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone; at the critical point the densities of liquid and vapour become equal. For a van der Waals gas: $V_{c}=3b$, $P_{c}=\frac{a}{27b^{2}}$, $T_{c}=\frac{8a}{27Rb}$ and the predicted critical compressibility factor $Z_{c}=\frac{P_{c}V_{c}}{RT_{c}}=\frac{3}{8}=0.375$. The reduced variables $P_{r}=P/P_{c}$, $V_{r}=V/V_{c}$, $T_{r}=T/T_{c}$ give the principle of corresponding states: all gases with the same reduced T and P have the same reduced volume.
13### 6. Other equations of state
14Dieterici's equation $P(V-b)=RT\,e^{-a/RTV}$ and the virial equation $PV=RT(1+B/V+C/V^{2}+\dots)$ improve the description; the virial coefficients are obtained from experiment. The van der Waals equation remains the standard NEB-level treatment because it explains liquefaction and the critical point qualitatively with only two constants.
15### 7. Applications
16• **Liquefaction and refrigeration:** below Tc, attractive forces (the 'a' term) allow liquefaction; the Joule–Thomson effect and Linde's method produce liquid N₂, O₂, He and LPG/CNG storage systems.
17• **Comparing gases:** a and b from tables rank gases by ease of liquefaction (SO₂ > CO₂ > N₂ > H₂), and Z charts guide compressor and pipeline design.
18• **Supercritical fluids:** above Tc and Pc, CO₂ becomes a supercritical solvent used for decaffeination and dry cleaning — an application of critical constants.
19• **Safety and storage:** knowing Tc explains why cylinders must be kept cool and why liquefied petroleum gas remains liquid under pressure.
20### 8. Numerical work
21Typical problems: compute Z from P, V, T; compare gases for near-ideal behaviour (smallest a and b, e.g. He); use the van der Waals equation to find P or V; and obtain Tc, Pc, Vc from a and b (or vice versa).
22### 9. Limitations of the van der Waals equation
23It is a qualitative improvement, not an exact theory: a and b are treated as constants although they vary with temperature; molecular shape, polarity and association (hydrogen bonding in water, ammonia) are ignored; the predicted Zc = 0.375 exceeds the measured values (0.23–0.31 for most gases); the equation does not reproduce isotherms precisely near the critical point and has no term for attractive forces acting over different ranges. Better results need more elaborate equations of state or computer simulation.
24**Fact.** Ideal gas equation PV = nRT; from kinetic theory P = ⅓ (Nm/m)⟨c²⟩, so PV = ⅓N m⟨c²⟩.
25**Fact.** r.m.s. speed c_rms = √(3RT/M); mean kinetic energy per mole = 3RT/2, per molecule = 3k_BT/2.
26**Fact.** Real gases deviate near condensation; Van der Waals: (P + a n²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.

Liquid State: Physical Properties of Liquids

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of the liquid state
2A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape: molecules are close together (short intermolecular distances) and slide over one another, so a liquid can flow, is practically incompressible and shows short-range order only. Intermolecular forces are strong enough to hold the molecules together but not strong enough to fix them in a lattice, as in a solid.
3### 2. Evaporation and condensation
4**Evaporation** is the escape of high-energy molecules from the *surface* of a liquid into the vapour phase at any temperature below the boiling point. It is endothermic (the liquid cools — earthen pots, sweating, petrichor), and its rate increases with temperature, surface area, wind/movement and dryness of the air, and decreases with humidity. **Condensation** is the reverse change of vapour to liquid, releasing the same latent heat; when the rates become equal a dynamic equilibrium is established and the vapour is said to be saturated (dew point for atmospheric condensation).
5### 3. Vapour pressure and boiling point
6Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by the vapour when it is in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature. It depends only on the nature of the liquid and the temperature — **not** on the amount of liquid or the size of the container. Volatile liquids have high vapour pressure (ether, acetone, alcohol); water has 23.8 mm Hg at 25 °C and 760 mm Hg at 100 °C. Vapour pressure rises steeply with temperature; the quantitative relation is the Clausius–Clapeyron equation.
7**Boiling** occurs when the vapour pressure of the liquid becomes equal to the external (atmospheric) pressure; the temperature at that point is the boiling point, and heat supplied thereafter goes into converting liquid to vapour (latent heat of vaporisation) so the temperature stays constant. Consequences: boiling point rises with external pressure (pressure cooker) and falls in the mountains/vacuum (water boils below 100 °C at high altitude; vacuum distillation for heat-sensitive liquids).
8### 4. Surface tension
9Molecules in the bulk are pulled equally in all directions, but a surface molecule experiences a net inward pull, so the surface behaves like a stretched membrane and tends to minimise its area. Surface tension γ is the force per unit length acting along the surface (γ = F/l), with units N m⁻¹ or J m⁻². It decreases as temperature rises (becoming zero at the critical temperature) and is lowered by soaps and detergents. Consequences: spherical drops, capillary rise in narrow tubes (used by plants and blotting paper), meniscus shapes (water concave, mercury convex), flotation of light insects and needles, and the functioning of detergents and wetting agents.
10### 5. Viscosity
11Viscosity is the internal friction (resistance to flow) between adjacent layers of a liquid moving with different speeds; the coefficient of viscosity η has units N s m⁻² (Pa s) or poise (P), with 1 P = 0.1 Pa s. Viscosity arises from intermolecular attraction and decreases rapidly as temperature rises (for gases it increases). Examples: honey and glycerol flow slowly (high η), water and ether flow easily; engine oils are graded by SAE numbers according to their viscosity, and lubricating oil must be chosen for the operating temperature.
12### 6. Other physical properties (brief)
13Liquids are practically incompressible (very high bulk modulus), diffuse slowly compared with gases, conduct heat but not electricity (except molten salts/metals), and expand slightly on heating. Their thermal conductivity makes them useful as coolants (water, oil) and their low compressibility is the basis of hydraulic brakes and hydraulic lifts.
14### 7. Applications
15• **Vapour pressure and boiling:** distillation and fractional distillation, steam distillation, vacuum distillation of heat-sensitive liquids (petroleum fractions, glycerine), autoclaves and pressure cookers, and the working of refrigerants.
16• **Evaporation:** cooling by evaporation (earthen pots, desert coolers, sweating), drying of paints and wet clothes, and evaporative humidifiers.
17• **Surface tension:** detergents and soaps (lowering γ improves wetting and emulsification), waterproofing agents, flotation of insects, capillary rise in xylem and blotting paper, and mercury barometers where the convex meniscus must be corrected.
18• **Viscosity:** selection of lubricating oils and greases for machines, engine-oil grades (SAE 30 etc.), flow of blood in vessels and the effect of temperature on it, pipeline transport of oil, and viscosity measurements to determine molar mass of polymers.
19### 8. Limitations
20There is no simple equation of state for liquids — the kinetic model that works for gases fails because liquids show short-range order and strong interactions; surface tension and viscosity are interpreted qualitatively (values depend on the measurement method and on impurities); vapour pressure is reduced by non-volatile solutes (Raoult's law), so impure samples give wrong boiling points, and the Clausius–Clapeyron treatment assumes that ΔH_vap is constant over the range (an approximation). Many real liquids are non-Newtonian (blood, paint, ketchup), so a single viscosity value is misleading, and near the critical temperature all these properties change drastically, which no simple description covers.

❌ / ✅
❌ Evaporation happens only at the boiling point. ✅ It occurs at every temperature from the liquid surface; boiling is evaporation throughout the bulk at the boiling point.
❌ Vapour pressure depends on how much liquid is present. ✅ It depends only on the liquid's nature and the temperature (and on the presence of solutes).
❌ Surface tension makes the surface a real skin. ✅ It is an inward pull on surface molecules that makes the surface behave like a stretched membrane.
❌ Glycerine is more viscous than water because it is denser. ✅ Viscosity comes from stronger intermolecular (H-bonding) attraction, not from density.
❌ A liquid boils when the vapour pressure exceeds the atmospheric pressure. ✅ Boiling starts when vapour pressure *equals* the external pressure (an overshoot just makes it boil faster).

Solid State: Types of Solids, Amorphous and Crystalline Solids

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of the solid state
2A solid has a definite shape and volume because its constituent particles are held in fixed mean positions by strong intermolecular (or interatomic/ionic) forces. The particles can only vibrate about their positions, so solids are rigid, practically incompressible and diffuse extremely slowly. Solids are classified by the *arrangement* of particles (crystalline vs amorphous) and by the *nature of the binding force* (ionic, covalent, molecular, metallic, hydrogen-bonded).
3### 2. Crystalline solids
4The particles are arranged in a regular, repeating three-dimensional pattern called a **crystal lattice**, which extends over a long range (long-range order). Properties: definite geometrical shape (crystal habit), sharp melting point, cleavage along definite planes, anisotropic physical properties (refractive index, thermal expansion, conductivity differ along different directions) and a characteristic X-ray diffraction pattern. Examples: NaCl, KCl, sugar, quartz, diamond, ice, alum, copper.
5### 3. Types of crystalline solids (by binding force)
6• **Ionic:** ions held by strong electrostatic forces (NaCl, CaF₂) — hard, brittle, high melting point, non-conducting as solids but conducting when molten or in solution, soluble in polar solvents.
7• **Covalent (network/atomic):** atoms joined by a continuous network of covalent bonds (diamond, graphite, SiC, quartz) — extremely hard, very high melting point, usually insulators or semiconductors (graphite conducts).
8• **Molecular:** molecules held by weak van der Waals forces or hydrogen bonds (iodine, dry ice, naphthalene, sugar, ice) — soft, low melting point, volatile, poor conductors.
9• **Metallic:** positive ion cores in a sea of delocalised electrons (Na, Cu, Fe) — lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat and electricity, melting points from very low (Hg, Ga) to very high (W).
10### 4. Amorphous solids
11Amorphous solids have only short-range order — the particles are randomly arranged over long distances, so there is no definite lattice. Properties: no definite geometrical shape, no sharp melting point (they soften gradually over a temperature range and have a glass-transition temperature Tg), isotropic properties, irregular cleavage and a broad diffuse X-ray pattern. They are described as pseudo-solids or **supercooled liquids** because their particles can flow very slowly. Examples: glass, rubber, plastics, amorphous sulfur and selenium, cellophane.
12### 5. Crystalline versus amorphous (comparison points)
13Melting point: sharp vs gradual; order: long-range vs short-range; shape: definite geometry vs irregular; cleavage: definite planes vs irregular; physical properties: anisotropic vs isotropic; X-ray diffraction: sharp lines vs broad halos; thermodynamic stability: stable vs metastable; examples: NaCl, diamond vs glass, rubber.
14### 6. Isotropy and anisotropy
15A substance is isotropic when a physical property has the same value in all directions (amorphous solids, liquids, gases) and anisotropic when the property depends on direction (single crystals). For example the refractive index of quartz differs along different crystallographic axes, and graphite conducts electricity along its layers but not across them.
16### 7. Applications
17• **Single crystals** are grown for technology — Czochralski-pulled silicon and germanium for semiconductors, quartz for oscillators and watches, sapphire for optical windows, and organic crystals for lasers and detectors.
18• Ionic and molecular crystals are the basis of the salt, sugar, fertiliser and pharmaceutical industries (recrystallisation is the standard purification method; crystal polymorphism controls drug dissolution).
19• Amorphous solids find wide use precisely because of their isotropic, easy-to-shape nature — window and container glass, optical fibres, plastics, rubber, amorphous silicon solar cells and metallic glasses.
20• Diamond, SiC and quartz illustrate structure-property relations: giant covalent lattices give extreme hardness and high melting points used in abrasives, cutting tools and refractories.
21### 8. Limitations
22The crystalline/amorphous division is not sharp: most materials are polycrystalline or semi-crystalline (plastics, silk, cotton, metals) with both ordered and disordered regions, so the perfect crystal is an idealisation. Anisotropy appears only in single crystals — a polycrystalline aggregate usually behaves isotropically in bulk. The classification by binding force is itself approximate, since many crystals show mixed bonding (graphite: covalent + van der Waals; ice: covalent + hydrogen bonding), and amorphous solids are **metastable**, so their properties drift with time (glass devitrification) and depend on thermal history rather than on composition alone.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Amorphous solids are true solids with a very high viscosity. ✅ They are regarded as pseudo-solids or supercooled liquids because they lack long-range order and flow over long times.
❌ All solids have sharp melting points. ✅ Amorphous solids soften over a range (glass-transition temperature); only crystalline solids melt sharply.
❌ Isotropy means the same as anisotropy. ✅ Isotropic = same property in all directions (amorphous); anisotropic = direction-dependent (single crystals).
❌ Graphite is a molecular solid. ✅ Graphite is a covalent network with strong covalent layers held by weak van der Waals forces — which explains its lubricity and conductivity.
❌ Metals melt at high temperature because they are covalently bonded. ✅ Metallic bonding strength varies widely; Hg melts at −39 °C while W melts at 3422 °C.

Ideal Gas and the Ideal Gas Equation: Universal Gas Constant and its Significance

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of an ideal gas
2An ideal (perfect) gas is a hypothetical gas that obeys the gas laws and PV = nRT exactly under all conditions. Its molecules are point masses with no volume and no intermolecular forces, and collisions are perfectly elastic. No real gas is ideal, but every gas approaches ideal behaviour at **low pressure and high temperature**.
3### 2. The ideal gas equation
4From Boyle (V ∝ 1/P), Charles (V ∝ T) and Avogadro (V ∝ n): $V\propto\frac{nT}{P}$, hence $PV=nRT$. Useful forms: $PV=\frac{m}{M}RT$ (mass m, molar mass M), $PM=dRT$ (density d) and $P=CRT$ for molar concentration C.
5### 3. Meaning and value of R (universal gas constant)
6For one mole of an ideal gas R = PV/T is the same for every gas — hence *universal*. Physically R is the work done when 1 mol of ideal gas expands at constant pressure per kelvin of temperature rise. Values: R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 8.314 × 10⁷ erg K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 2 cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Related constant: Boltzmann constant k = R/N_A = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (R is per mole, k is per molecule).
7### 4. Significance of R
8• Independent of the nature of the gas, it links P, V, T, n with energy — the bridge between the gas laws and thermodynamics (ΔH = ΔU + ΔnRT).
9• Mean kinetic energy of 1 mol of any gas = (3/2)RT; R also appears in the Arrhenius equation and in Cp − Cv = R.
10• It fixes the molar volume: from V = RT/P, 1 mol occupies 22.4 L at STP.
11### 5. Applications
12• Determining molar mass and density of gases and volatile liquids (Dumas and Victor Meyer methods) using PM = dRT.
13• Calculating gas volumes for stoichiometry, cylinder contents and gas storage, and calibrating flow meters and gas thermometers.
14• Basis of chemical-engineering calculations (compressor design, pipelines, airbags), of the kinetic theory and of thermodynamic work terms for gases.
15### 6. Limitations
16• PV = nRT is exact only for an ideal gas; real gases deviate measurably, and grossly at high pressure or near the critical temperature.
17• Both central assumptions are unattainable — molecules always have finite size and always attract one another (otherwise no gas could be liquefied).
18• It cannot describe condensation, critical phenomena or the liquid state, and fails for associating (H-bonded) or highly polar gases even at ordinary pressure.
19• R is constant only for an ideal gas; for a real gas PV/T varies with pressure, so the van der Waals equation or Z = PV/nRT corrections are needed.
20**Fact.** Ideal gas equation PV = nRT; from kinetic theory P = ⅓ (Nm/m)⟨c²⟩, so PV = ⅓N m⟨c²⟩.
21**Fact.** r.m.s. speed c_rms = √(3RT/M); mean kinetic energy per mole = 3RT/2, per molecule = 3k_BT/2.
22**Fact.** Real gases deviate near condensation; Van der Waals: (P + a n²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.

Liquid Crystals and their Applications

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning
2Liquid crystals are substances that are simultaneously partly ordered like a crystal and partly mobile like a liquid — a mesophase intermediate between the crystalline solid and the isotropic liquid. They flow and take the shape of the container, yet their rod- or disc-shaped molecules retain positional/orientational order, so their optical, electrical and mechanical properties are **anisotropic** (direction-dependent). F. Reinitzer first observed this behaviour in cholesteryl benzoate (1888).
3### 2. Characteristics
4• Molecules are elongated (rod-like) or flat and disc-like with flexible end chains; shape anisotropy produces the ordered mesophase.
5• Partial order gives birefringence (two refractive indices), anisotropic dielectric constant, viscosity, elasticity and magnetic susceptibility.
6• Order-disorder change occurs over a narrow, well-defined **temperature range** — below it a solid, above it an ordinary liquid.
7• **Thermotropic** LCs form on temperature change (displays); **lyotropic** LCs form in solution when concentration changes (soap, detergents, cell membranes).
8### 3. Classified mesophases
9• **Nematic:** molecules parallel, positions random — the display material.
10• **Smectic:** parallel molecules arranged in layers (soaps, some polymers).
11• **Cholesteric (chiral nematic):** layers twisted into a helix, strongly temperature-sensitive colours used in thermometers and mood strips.
12### 4. How an LCD works
13A thin nematic layer is sandwiched between transparent conducting electrodes and crossed polarisers. In the twisted-nematic cell the molecules untwist when a voltage is applied, changing the polarisation of the transmitted light, so each pixel switches from bright to dark. Millions of such cells form the pixels of calculators, watches, phones, monitors and televisions.
14### 5. Applications
15• **Displays:** LCD televisions, monitors, laptops, phones, digital watches, calculators and instrument panels — the largest use of liquid crystals.
16• **Temperature measurement and mapping:** thermochromic LCs change colour with temperature — fever strips, aquarium thermometers, mood strips, non-destructive thermal mapping of electronic circuits and hot-spot detection in medicine and industry.
17• **Sensors and optics:** pressure-sensitive LC films, optical switches and light valves, privacy glass, laser-beam steering and tunable lenses.
18• **Materials:** liquid-crystal polymers such as Kevlar give very strong, stiff fibres (protective clothing, aerospace composites); LCs are also used in chromatography, lubricants and detergents.
19### 6. Limitations
20• The useful mesophase exists only in a narrow temperature window, so devices must stay within their rated range — LCDs fail in extreme cold and darken in strong heat.
21• They are passive (non-emissive) and need a backlight, so brightness, contrast and viewing angle are limited compared with emissive displays.
22• Response is slower than emissive technologies and polarisers absorb much of the light, so efficiency is moderate.
23• They are sensitive to UV light, shock, contamination and moisture; some mesogens are chemically unstable or toxic and must be sealed carefully.
24• The rigid-rod model explains mesophases only qualitatively; quantitative transition temperatures need statistical theories (Maier–Saupe, Onsager).

Efflorescent, Deliquescent and Hygroscopic Solids; Crystallization, Crystal Growth and Water of Crystallization

#Concept
1### 1. Water of crystallisation — the concept behind the three classes
2Water of crystallisation is water chemically combined in a fixed stoichiometric ratio inside a crystal lattice (not merely adsorbed). Examples: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue vitriol), Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda), Na₂SO₄·10H₂O (Glauber's salt), MgCl₂·6H₂O, CaSO₄·2H₂O (gypsum), FeSO₄·7H₂O. It is usually driven off by heating, and the loss of mass gives the number of water molecules per formula unit (gravimetric determination); the anhydrous salt often differs in colour and crystal shape.
3### 2. Efflorescent solids
4Efflorescent substances lose their water of crystallisation (wholly or partly) spontaneously in dry air and crumble to a powder, because the vapour pressure of the hydrate exceeds the water-vapour pressure of the atmosphere. Examples: Na₂SO₄·10H₂O → Na₂SO₄ (white powder); Na₂CO₃·10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·H₂O; CuSO₄·5H₂O fades from blue towards pale blue/white; alum. Hot, dry weather speeds it up, so such salts must be stored in sealed containers.
5### 3. Deliquescent and hygroscopic solids
6**Deliquescent** solids absorb moisture from the air, dissolve in it and form a saturated solution (a puddle) — CaCl₂, MgCl₂, NaOH, KOH, FeCl₃·6H₂O, ZnCl₂. **Hygroscopic** substances absorb or adsorb moisture and become damp without dissolving — concentrated H₂SO₄, P₄O₁₀, CaO (quicklime), CuO, silica gel, glycerol. Hygroscopic materials are used as **drying agents/desiccants** (silica-gel sachets, conc. H₂SO₄ in desiccators, quicklime in packaging). Whether a salt deliquesces depends on humidity: the air must exceed its deliquescence relative humidity (NaCl ≈ 75% RH, CaCl₂ ≈ 30% RH), so a 'deliquescent' salt may simply effloresce or remain unchanged in dry air.
7### 4. Crystallization and crystal growth
8**Crystallisation** is the formation of a pure solid in crystalline form from a solution, melt or vapour: the solution is saturated and then supersaturated by cooling or slow evaporation, after which **nucleation** begins and **crystal growth** follows. Good crystals need slow, steady growth — slow cooling, a clean solution, a suitable solvent, undisturbed conditions and sometimes a seed crystal. Large single crystals are grown by slow evaporation (sugar, alum, KDP), by the Czochralski pulling technique from a melt (silicon and germanium for electronics), by zone refining, or from the vapour (sublimation and chemical vapour transport).
9### 5. Applications
10• Recrystallisation is the standard laboratory purification technique; soluble impurities stay behind in the mother liquor.
11• Industrial crystallisation produces salt, sugar, urea, fertilisers and pharmaceutical crystals, where crystal habit and size control tablet manufacture and food texture.
12• Single-crystal growth supplies semiconductor wafers, quartz oscillators, laser and detector crystals and gemstones; crystallisation is also used in water softening (scale removal) and freeze-drying.
13• Desiccants and drying agents (silica gel, CaCl₂, P₄O₁₀, H₂SO₄) protect instruments and goods from moisture and are essential for analytical weighing, while anti-caking agents stop efflorescent salts from caking.
14### 6. Limitations
15• The three terms describe behaviour, not a rigorous classification: the same substance can deliquesce in humid air and effloresce in dry air (Na₂SO₄ and Na₂CO₃ hydrates both do), because humidity, temperature and the hydrate's vapour pressure decide the outcome.
16• Efflorescence may stop at a lower hydrate rather than reach the anhydrous salt, so equations assuming complete dehydration can be wrong.
17• Deliquescence occurs only above the substance's critical relative humidity; below it the solid merely adsorbs a little water.
18• Determining water of crystallisation by heating fails if the substance decomposes or hydrolyses (MgCl₂·6H₂O loses HCl and water when heated strongly) or if the sample also picks up atmospheric moisture.
19• Crystallisation cannot remove impurities that co-crystallise as mixed (isomorphous) crystals, is slow, and always loses some product in the mother liquor, lowering the yield.

Introduction to Unit Crystal Lattice and Unit Cell

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning
2A **crystal lattice** is the regular three-dimensional arrangement of points (lattice points) in space at which the constituent particles of a crystal lie. A **unit cell** is the smallest repeating portion of that lattice which, when repeated in three dimensions, generates the whole crystal — it is the building block or 'brick' of the crystal. Lattice + the atoms/ions/molecules at its points = the crystal structure.
3### 2. Lattice parameters and crystal systems
4A unit cell is described by six parameters: the three edge lengths a, b, c and the three interaxial angles α, β, γ. Depending on their relative values, crystals fall into **7 crystal systems** (cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, hexagonal and rhombohedral) which give **14 Bravais lattices** when the four possible cell centreings are included.
5### 3. Types of unit cell
6• **Primitive (simple) cell:** particles only at the eight corners.
7• **Body-centred cell (BCC):** corners + one particle at the body centre.
8• **Face-centred cell (FCC):** corners + one particle at the centre of each of the six faces.
9• **End/base-centred cell:** corners + particles at the centres of two opposite faces.
10### 4. Share of each particle and atoms per cell
11Because a cell shares its particles with neighbours: a corner particle contributes 1/8, one on an edge 1/4, one on a face 1/2 and one at the body centre 1. Hence the effective number of atoms per unit cell, Z, is 1 for simple cubic, 2 for BCC and 4 for FCC.
12### 5. Geometry of the three cubic cells
13• Simple cubic: coordination number 6, a = 2r, packing efficiency 52.4% (polonium, rarely found).
14• Body-centred cubic: coordination number 8, √3a = 4r, packing efficiency 68% (Na, K, Fe, Cr, W).
15• Face-centred cubic (cubic close-packed): coordination number 12, √2a = 4r, packing efficiency 74% (Cu, Ag, Au, Al, Pb) — the densest possible packing of spheres.
16Density of a crystal follows from the cell contents: $\rho=\frac{ZM}{a^{3}N_{A}}$ where M is the molar mass and a the edge length.
17### 6. Meaning and applications of the unit cell
18The unit cell turns the microscopic structure into measurable quantities: from the edge length (measured by X-ray diffraction) and the cell contents, the **density** and the **Avogadro constant** can be determined; the cell also explains why FCC metals (Cu, Al) are soft and ductile while BCC metals (Fe, W) are stronger, why some crystals cleave along definite planes, and how doping works — substitutional or interstitial foreign atoms in the silicon lattice create n-type and p-type semiconductors.
19Other applications: identification and purity testing of minerals, alloys and pharmaceutical polymorphs by X-ray diffraction; design of alloys and ceramics; gemstone identification; and understanding of ionic conductivity and diffusion in solids.
20### 7. Limitations
21The ideal lattice model is an idealisation: real crystals contain point defects (Schottky and Frenkel defects, vacancies, interstitials) and extended defects (dislocations, grain boundaries) that control important properties such as ionic conductivity, mechanical strength and colour, but are absent from the perfect-cell picture. The hard-sphere model treats atoms as rigid touching spheres, whereas electron density is diffuse and atoms are not hard spheres, so packing-efficiency values are approximate. The model describes only periodic structures — quasicrystals and modulated structures have no single repeating cell, and in solid solutions or alloys the cell parameters vary continuously with composition rather than having a fixed value for a 'unit cell'. Finally, the unit cell gives no direct information about bonding or electronic band structure.

Boyle's Law in Detail — Isotherms, Numericals and Limitations

#Concept
1### Focus of this tab
2The first gas law in full: statement, graphs, molecular explanation, numericals and where it breaks — the pattern to reproduce for Charles' and Avogadro's laws.
3### Statement (1662, Robert Boyle)
4At constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure: V ∝ 1/P, i.e. PV = constant, or P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Conditions: fixed mass, constant T, ideal behaviour (low P).
5### Graphical forms (all three are examinable)
6• P versus V: rectangular hyperbola (each temperature gives one hyperbola — an isotherm; higher T curves lie farther from the origin).
7• P versus 1/V: straight line through the origin, slope = nRT.
8• PV versus P: horizontal straight line for an ideal gas; real gases show a dip (attraction) then a rise (volume effect) — the first visible sign of non-ideality.
9### Molecular explanation
10Halving the volume doubles the number density of molecules, so wall collisions per unit area per second double, doubling the pressure — the inverse relation is pure collision statistics at fixed average molecular speed (fixed T).
11### Numericals
12• V₂ = P₁V₁/P₂: 500 cm³ at 700 mm Hg → at 1400 mm Hg: 250 cm³.
13• Density application: since m fixed, d ∝ P at constant T — a compressed gas is proportionally denser.
14• Bubble problem: an air bubble rising from depth doubles its volume as pressure halves (plus water-column corrections with 10 m water ≈ 1 atm).
15### Limitations
16• Ideal-gas law: fails at high pressure (PV rises above nRT) and near liquefaction; gases with strong attraction (CO₂, SO₂, NH₃) deviate most; H₂ and He deviate least.
17• Requires fixed mass and constant temperature — a temperature change during compression invalidates it.
18• Cannot be extrapolated to the liquid state; below the critical temperature compression produces condensation, not further Boyle-law shrinkage.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ PV = constant for any gas under any conditions. ✅ Only for fixed mass at constant temperature and ideal behaviour.
❌ Doubling pressure halves the temperature. ✅ Boyle's law holds T constant; the P–T relation is Gay-Lussac's law.
❌ The P–V graph is a straight line. ✅ It is a hyperbola; the straight line appears for P versus 1/V.

The Gaseous State — Characteristics and Measurable Properties

#Concept
1### Focus of this tab
2What defines a gas and the four measurable variables (P, V, T, n) whose relationships the gas laws and kinetic theory explain — the orientation before the postulates.
3### Defining characteristics of the gaseous state
4• No fixed shape or volume — a gas fills its container completely.
5• Very low density compared with liquids/solids (air ≈ 1.3 kg m⁻³ vs water 1000 kg m⁻³); highly compressible; diffuse rapidly.
6• Molecules are far apart (intermolecular distances ≈ 10 molecular diameters at STP) with negligible attraction — hence expansion and compressibility.
7• Exerts pressure uniformly in all directions (momentum transfer on wall collisions).
8• Mixes completely and homogeneously (diffusion, effusion through pinholes).
9### The four state variables
10• **Pressure (P):** force per unit area; units Pa (N m⁻²), atm (1 atm = 101325 Pa = 760 mm Hg), bar (1 bar = 10⁵ Pa); measured with barometers/manometers.
11• **Volume (V):** space occupied; 1 m³ = 1000 L = 10⁶ cm³ (mL).
12• **Temperature (T):** kelvin only in gas laws; T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15.
13• **Amount (n):** moles; links to mass via molar mass and to particles via N_A.
14### What the gas laws connect
15Boyle (P–V at fixed T), Charles (V–T at fixed P), Gay-Lussac (P–T at fixed V), Avogadro (V–n at fixed P, T), combined equation and PV = nRT. Each law fixes two variables and studies the other two — the 'constant-holding' logic of all gas-law experiments.
16### Why gases need a molecular model
17The measured regularities (PV = nRT for all gases at low P) are too universal to be accidental: they point to a common microscopic structure. The kinetic theory supplies it — postulates → PV = ⅓mnc̄² → PV = nRT — and its idealisations (point molecules, no attraction) mark exactly where real gases deviate.
18The postulate → consequence mapping: each idealisation of the kinetic theory, what it explains, and which real-gas failure it causes. A different cut through the material from the main postulates note.
19### Postulate → consequence → failure table
20• 'Molecules are point masses (negligible volume)' → explains compressibility and large empty space → fails at high pressure (free volume < container volume, real PV > nRT).
21• 'No intermolecular attraction except during collision' → explains indefinite expansion and Dalton's partial pressures → fails near liquefaction (attraction lowers observed pressure, real Z < 1) and is the reason gases condense at all.
22• 'Collisions perfectly elastic' → kinetic energy redistributed, not lost; gas temperature stays constant in a rigid container → fails slightly (real collisions excite vibrations; polyatomic gases need internal degrees of freedom for their specific heats).
23• 'Random straight-line motion, huge numbers' → statistics (Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution) explains steady macroscopic pressure from microscopic chaos → fails for very low densities where molecule counts fluctuate.
24• 'KE ∝ T' → defines absolute temperature microscopically: KE = (3/2)kT per molecule, (3/2)RT per mole → explains why T must be kelvin and why all gases share the same R.
25### The central derivation chain
26$PV=\frac{1}{3}mn\bar{c}^{2}$ (pressure from momentum transfer) + KE ∝ T (postulate) → $PV=nRT$ → Boyle's, Charles' and Avogadro's laws fall out immediately. One set of postulates explains *all* the gas laws at once — the model's greatest virtue.
27### What the model explains beyond the laws
28Graham's diffusion/effusion (r ∝ 1/√M), Dalton's partial pressures (independent molecules), Graham's law of effusion, viscosity (momentum transfer between layers) and Brownian-motion style randomness of molecular speeds.
29### Where the idealisations bite (recap for exams)
30High P: molecular volume matters. Low T / near Tc: attraction matters. Polyatomic gases: rotation/vibration. Very light gases at low T: quantum effects. Liquid state: attraction and volume together — outside the model entirely.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Gases have negligible mass. ✅ They have small but definite mass — 22.4 L of air weighs about 29 g at STP.
❌ Temperature in gas laws may be taken in °C. ✅ Absolute (kelvin) temperature only; 0 °C is 273.15 K.
❌ A gas exerts pressure only downward. ✅ Pressure is isotropic — equal in every direction.
❌ 'Negligible molecular volume' is a minor technicality. ✅ It is one of the two postulates whose failure defines all real-gas deviation (the other is no attraction).
❌ Kinetic theory explains why gases liquefy. ✅ It cannot — liquefaction comes from the attractive forces it assumes away; that requires the van der Waals model.
❌ Elastic collisions mean molecules never exchange energy. ✅ They exchange freely but the *total* KE is conserved.

Academic Extension: Evidence, Conditions & Open Questions

Established academic fact

A phase diagram summarizes which phase is stable at combinations of temperature and pressure. The triple point is where three phases coexist at equilibrium; the critical point marks the end of the liquid–vapour boundary, beyond which liquid and gas are not distinct phases.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

Simple gas laws assume a dilute gas and approximately ideal behaviour. Real gases deviate most strongly at high pressure and near condensation, where molecular size and intermolecular attractions matter; phase boundaries depend on the substance and measured conditions.

Open research question — Circular materials and selective recycling

Researchers are working to recover and reuse active battery materials at scale. Real waste streams vary in chemistry and condition, so sorting, safe disassembly, purity, energy use and economic viability all constrain the process.
