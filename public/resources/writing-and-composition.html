Essay Writing — argumentative, descriptive, narrative, expository

#Concept
1**What it is:** an essay argues, describes, narrates or explains a topic in organised paragraphs. The examiner marks STANCE + STRUCTURE + evidence, not length.
2**Universal skeleton:** Introduction (hook + thesis) → Body paragraphs (ONE reason each: topic sentence → explanation → example) → optional counter-argument (acknowledge, then rebut) → Conclusion (restate thesis + consequence).
3**Four types:** *Argumentative* (clear side, reasons, admit the other side), *Descriptive* (5 senses, spatial order, show-don't-tell), *Narrative* (one incident: setup → conflict → climax → reflection), *Expository* (explain causes/process in numbered factors, no opinion).
4**Tense logic:** essays live in PRESENT for general truths; past only inside narrative examples; predictions use will/would.
5**Full guide:** Full model answers, starting-line banks and exam tips: [/knowledge/writing](/knowledge/writing).

Paragraph Writing

#Concept
1**What it is:** ONE controlling idea in 4–6 sentences — the engine behind every essay, letter and report.
2**Structure:** (1) *Topic sentence* — main idea + your angle, specific not generic. (2) *Supporting sentences* — each adds ONE fact/example/reason, logically ordered. (3) *Concluding sentence* — restates or lands a consequence; no new ideas here.
3**Unity test:** if a sentence could belong to another paragraph, delete it. **Coherence test:** the reader follows without re-reading — via connectors and repeated key words.
4**Exam discipline:** 90–120 words; start with the idea, not with 'The'; end with consequence.

Formal Letter Writing — editor, application, complaint, inquiry

#Concept
1**What it is:** a letter to someone you do NOT know (editor, manager, office). Half the marks are the fixed BLOCK LAYOUT; the other half is polite business tone.
2**Block order:** Sender's address → Date → Recipient's designation + address → Subject line → Salutation ('Dear Sir/Madam') → Body (3 short paragraphs: who you are + purpose / details / polite request) → Complimentary close → Signature + full name in capitals.
3**The pairing rule that trips everyone:** 'Dear Sir/Madam' → end **Yours faithfully**; 'Dear Mr/Ms Sharma' (real name) → end **Yours sincerely**.
4**Tone grammar:** no contractions (*do not*, *I am*); polite modals (*would*, *could*, *may*); passive voice for formality ('the matter is being investigated').
5**Variants:** *Editor* (public issue + call for action), *Job application* (post + skills + 'Enclosure: CV'), *Complaint* (what, when, defect + remedy + deadline), *Inquiry/Order* (price/availability/terms).
6**Full guide:** Full model answers, starting-line banks and exam tips: [/knowledge/writing](/knowledge/writing).

Informal Letter Writing

#Concept
1**What it is:** a letter to someone you know well — friend, family. Same skeleton, different temperature: warm, natural, personal.
2**Block order:** Sender's address → Date → Salutation with first name ('Dear Rahul,') → Warm opening (ask about them / react to their letter) → Main body (news, invitation, reply) → Warm closing → 'Your loving friend,' + first name only.
3**Tone grammar:** contractions welcome (it's, don't, I'm); question tags create warmth ('You remember that day, don't you?'); tenses mix naturally — past for news, present for feelings, future for plans.
4**Exam check:** answer anything their last letter asked — examiners verify you responded; keep within ~130 words.

Email Writing

#Concept
1**Format.** A formal email has the recipient's address, a clear subject line, a salutation, a concise body and a closing with the sender's name and designation.
2**Subject line.** The subject states the purpose in a few precise words so the reader knows the request before opening the message.
3**Tone.** Formal emails use complete sentences and polite requests; informal emails to friends may use contractions and a casual tone.
4**Body.** Open with the purpose, develop one or two short paragraphs of essential detail, and end with the action you want — a reply, a meeting or confirmation.
5**Netiquette.** Keep the message brief, avoid slang and all-capital text, use CC and BCC appropriately, and recheck names and addresses before sending.

#Concept

2- **Narrative**: Tells a story with a sequence of events
3- **Descriptive**: Paints a picture with words
4- **Expository**: Explains or informs objectively
5- **Argumentative**: Presents a claim with evidence

11### 3. Argumentative Essay Tips

Key Points

Point
Types: narrative tells a story with a sequence of events; descriptive paints a picture with words; expository explains objectively; argumentative presents a claim with evidence; persuasive convinces the reader.
Introduction = hook + thesis statement.
Body = topic sentence + evidence + analysis, with one main idea per paragraph.
Conclusion = restate thesis + summary + final thought.
Descriptive technique: use sensory details and show rather than tell.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Argumentative = personal opinion. ✅ Must be backed by evidence and logic.
❌ Descriptive = listing facts. ✅ Should engage all five senses.

Summary: This chapter sets out the five types of essay - narrative, descriptive, expository, argumentative and persuasive - and the three-part structure common to all of them: an introduction with a hook and a thesis statement, body paragraphs built on a topic sentence with evidence and analysis, and a conclusion that restates the thesis and adds a final thought. It also gives type-specific technique, such as using credible evidence and answering counterarguments in argumentative writing, and appealing to the five senses in descriptive writing. For the entrance exam, expect an essay of 5-7 paragraphs and roughly 250-300 words on a familiar topic.

Report Writing and Summarisation

#Concept
1**What it is:** factual, third-person, past-tense reporting of an event (sports day, blood donation camp) — not a story, not an opinion.
2**Structure:** Headline (short, factual) → Byline ('By a Staff Reporter') + dateline ('Kathmandu, 12 May:') → Lead paragraph answering WHO did WHAT, WHERE, WHEN, WHY in 2–3 sentences → Body in chronological order → a short quotation from the chief guest → closing outcome.
3**Tense/voice grammar:** past simple for every event; passive voice is standard for ceremony facts ('trophies were distributed'); reported speech for quotes; third person only — never 'I'.
4**Summarisation partner skill:** compress any passage into its core idea in your own words within the word limit — keep the author's viewpoint, drop examples and repetition.

Article Writing

#Concept
1**Structure.** An article opens with a catchy heading and byline, develops the topic in paragraphs, and closes with a strong concluding remark.
2**Introduction.** The opening paragraph should hook the reader and state the topic's relevance — a striking fact, question or short anecdote works well.
3**Body paragraphs.** Each paragraph develops one aspect (cause, effect, example, statistic) with a clear topic sentence and supporting evidence.
4**Style.** Write for the intended audience, keep sentences varied, use linking words (moreover, however, in conclusion), and stay within the word limit.
5**Conclusion.** The final paragraph should restate the central message or suggest a solution, leaving the reader with a clear takeaway.

Story Writing

#Concept
1**What it is:** a COMPLETE narrative arc judged on plot — situation → problem → turning point → resolution → (often) a quiet moral.
2**Structure:** Opening (if the prompt gives a first line, USE it verbatim and continue) → Complication → Rising action → Climax (the decisive moment — slow down here) → Resolution + realisation/moral.
3**Tense machinery:** past simple drives the plot; past continuous paints background ('I was dawdling home'); past perfect marks earlier events ('which she had lost'); direct speech at the climax adds life.
4**Keep ONE tense zone and ONE narrator** — drifting between present and past, or I/he, breaks the story.

Speech Writing

#Concept
1**What it is:** writing meant to be SPOKEN — short sentences, rhythm, direct address. The examiner checks 'speakability'.
2**Structure:** Salutation in order of seniority ('Respected Chairperson, judges, teachers, and my dear friends —') → Self-intro (if required) → Hook (question/fact/quotation) → 2–3 clear points ('First… Second… Finally…') → Persuasive devices (triads, repetition, rhetorical questions) → Strong closing appeal + 'Thank you.'
3**Grammar for the ear:** imperatives command action ('Let us pledge…'); rhetorical questions make the audience answer mentally; triads create rhythm.
4**Test:** read your draft aloud — where you gasp, rewrite. Repeat your core phrase 2–3 times.

Dialogue Writing

#Concept
1**Purpose.** Dialogue writing presents a conversation between two or more speakers that reveals character, advances the story or conveys information naturally.
2**Format.** Each speech starts with the speaker's name or label followed by a colon; speeches alternate, and stage directions appear in brackets if needed.
3**Natural speech.** Lines should sound spoken — short sentences, everyday vocabulary, contractions and questions — rather than formal written prose.
4**Punctuation.** Direct speech is enclosed in quotation marks, and a new paragraph begins whenever the speaker changes.
5**Consistency.** Keep each speaker's tone, vocabulary and personality consistent throughout the exchange.

Review Writing — book and film

#Concept
1**What it is:** a balanced critical evaluation — what the work is, what it does well, what falls short, and whether it is worth the reader's time.
2**Structure:** Title + basic details (title, author/director, year, genre) → Brief SPOILER-FREE summary (2–3 sentences) → Strengths (one concrete example/quote) → Weaknesses (fair, with why it matters) → Recommendation + verdict/rating.
3**Review grammar:** present simple dominates ('the story follows…'); evaluative adjectives (compelling, uneven, luminous); concessive fairness ('although the plot is thin, the philosophy is profound'); soft hedging ('readers may find…').
4**Never spoil the ending** — restraint reads as mature judgement.

Summary and Note-making

#Concept
1**Summary:** read for gist → underline key ideas → draft (author's main idea first, then 2–4 key supports) → cut modifiers/examples to fit the word limit → verify meaning is unchanged and nothing is copied.
2**Summary grammar:** four paraphrase moves — synonym swap (threat→danger), word-class change (pollute→pollution), voice change (active↔passive), sentence merge; report verbs frame it ('the passage argues…'). Never add your own opinion.
3**Note-making:** TITLE in caps → numbered sub-headings (1, 2, 3 — noun phrases, one per paragraph) → compact key-phrase points (a, b, c) → abbreviations (&, →, e.g.) with an 'Abbreviations used' note.
4**Note grammar:** nominalise verbs for headings ('agricultural expansion'); drop articles/auxiliaries in points; every point under 7 words.

Comprehension and Unseen Passage

#Concept
1**What it is:** extracting and restating passage information — the trap is copying whole sentences; the skill is locate-then-restate.
2**Method:** skim for gist → scan for the question's keywords (the answer lives nearby) → answer in complete sentences with synonyms → match the question type (WH → answer the WH; 'Why' → 'Because…'; true/false → quote evidence) → verify against the text.
3**Grammar of answers:** convert passage nouns into clauses ('its existence' → 'that it exists'); use report frames ('the passage states that…'); keep the passage's tense zone; quote marks ONLY when demanded.
4**Length discipline:** 1-mark answers = one sentence; 2-mark answers = answer + reason/evidence.
5**Full guide:** Full model answers, starting-line banks and exam tips: [/knowledge/writing](/knowledge/writing).
6**The four-step comprehension method:** (1) SKIM — read the passage once quickly (60 seconds) to get the gist. Don't stop for unknown words. (2) SCAN — read the questions FIRST, then scan the passage for keywords related to each question. (3) LOCATE — find the exact sentence(s) that contain the answer. Underline them. (4) RESTATE — write the answer in YOUR OWN WORDS using complete sentences. Never copy the passage verbatim.
7**Why copying is the #1 comprehension error:** Examiners explicitly penalise copied sentences. If the passage says 'The river was polluted with industrial waste,' and you write 'The river was polluted with industrial waste,' you get ZERO marks for that answer. You must PARAPHRASE: 'Industrial waste had contaminated the river.' This proves you UNDERSTOOD the meaning, not just your ability to copy.
8**Paraphrasing techniques for exam success:** (1) *Synonym swap*: 'happy' → 'joyful', 'big' → 'large', 'sad' → 'sorrowful'. (2) *Word class change*: 'The government decided' → 'The government's decision was…' (3) *Voice change*: 'They built the bridge' → 'The bridge was built by them.' (4) *Clause reduction*: 'Although it was raining heavily, they went out' → 'Despite the heavy rain, they went out.' (5) *Active to passive*: 'The teacher gave the students homework' → 'The students were given homework by the teacher.'
9**Question type mastery — WH questions:** (1) *What* — answer with a noun or clause: 'What caused the flood?' → 'Heavy rainfall caused the flood.' (2) *Why* — answer with a reason: 'Why did she refuse?' → 'She refused because she had lost faith in the system.' (3) *How* — answer with a method or manner: 'How did they solve the problem?' → 'They solved it by combining traditional knowledge with modern techniques.' (4) *When/Where/Who* — direct factual answers. ALWAYS use complete sentences unless told otherwise.
10**Question type mastery — true/false/not given:** (1) *True* — the passage explicitly confirms the statement. (2) *False* — the passage explicitly contradicts the statement. (3) *Not Given* — the passage doesn't mention the information at all. 'Not Given' is different from 'False' — it means the information is simply absent, not wrong. This distinction is heavily tested.
11**Question type mastery — short answer vs. long answer:** (1) *Short answer* (1 mark): one sentence, direct to the point. (2) *Long answer* (2-3 marks): answer + supporting evidence/reason from the passage. For 2-mark questions, always include BOTH the answer AND the evidence. Format: 'X happened because Y [evidence from passage].' Don't just state the answer — justify it.
12**Vocabulary-in-context questions:** When asked 'What does the word X mean in the passage?', do NOT write a dictionary definition. Write what the word means IN THAT CONTEXT. 'In the passage, "reluctant" means unwilling or hesitating because the character had no choice but to agree despite her reservations.' Always anchor your answer in the passage's context.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Copying vs. paraphrasing: Copying = writing the exact words from the passage (0 marks). Paraphrasing = expressing the SAME MEANING in YOUR OWN WORDS (full marks). Example: Passage says 'The children played joyfully in the garden.' Copy: 'The children played joyfully in the garden' (wrong). Paraphrase: 'The kids had fun playing outdoors in the garden' (correct). Always paraphrase unless the question says 'quote from the passage.'
Inference vs. opinion: An inference is a LOGICAL conclusion drawn from evidence in the passage. An opinion is YOUR PERSONAL belief that may or may not be supported by the passage. Inference: 'The character was likely anxious because she kept checking her watch.' Opinion: 'I think the character was anxious because I get nervous too.' Inferences use passage evidence; opinions use personal experience. Examiners want inferences.
True vs. Not Given: True = the passage CONFIRMS the statement. False = the passage CONTRADICTS the statement. Not Given = the passage NEITHER confirms nor contradicts — the information is simply absent. Example: Passage says 'Rahul went to school.' Statement: 'Rahul walked to school.' This is NOT GIVEN — we know he went, but not HOW. Don't assume; stick to what's in the passage.
Short answer vs. long answer format: A short answer (1 mark) is one complete sentence that directly answers the question. A long answer (2-3 marks) is answer + evidence/reason. Format for long answers: 'The answer is X because Y [with reference to the passage].' For short answers, skip the 'because' — just state the answer directly. Over-answering short questions wastes time; under-answering long questions loses marks.

Grammar for Writing — sentences, clauses, punctuation

#Concept
1**Sentence types as tools:** simple (one clause — POWER), compound (two main clauses via and/but/so or a semicolon — equal weight), complex (main + subordinate clause via because/although/when/if — shows the relation between ideas). Mix them: examiners reward rhythm.
2**Clauses vs phrases:** a phrase has no subject-verb pair and cannot stand alone; a dependent clause has one but leans on the main clause ('Because it rained.' alone is a fragment). Relative clauses (who/which/that) add noun detail — commas only for extra information.
3**Punctuation logic:** full stop ends a thought; comma separates list items, follows openers, precedes and/but/so between full sentences; semicolon joins balanced sentences; colon introduces; apostrophe = possession or contraction (its vs it's is the classic trap). Comma splice = two full sentences joined by only a comma — fix it.
4**Tense + agreement discipline:** pick a tense zone per paragraph (essays: present; reports: past); subjects agree with verbs ('Each of the students WAS present'); a/an by SOUND ('an hour', 'a university').

Academic Extension: Evidence, Conditions & Open Questions

Topic bridge — Writing And Composition. Use this extension to connect the topic’s core material with deeper academic inquiry: state a claim, name the conditions under which it holds, and distinguish established knowledge from an open research or interpretive question.

Established academic fact

Strong writing and reading use evidence purposefully: a claim should be traceable to textual or factual support, and an explanation should show how that evidence answers the task. Audience and purpose shape structure and register.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

For reading, distinguish what a passage states from inference; for writing, follow the requested genre, audience and word limits. Do not infer an author’s intention or treat a single interpretation as certain without supporting evidence.

Open research question — How evidence supports more than one reading

Literary interpretation is evidence-based rather than a hunt for one hidden answer. A defensible open reading offers at least two text-supported interpretations, cites exact details, and explains what complicates each; authorial intention should not be asserted without evidence.

Inquiry prompt

In “Writing And Composition”, which two interpretations can the exact wording support, and what detail complicates each? Record the line or passage; leave the issue open if the text does not decide it.
