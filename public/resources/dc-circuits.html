Electric Current and Drift Velocity

#Concept
1**Electric current.** Current is the rate of flow of charge, I = q/t; its SI unit is the ampere (1 A = 1 C/s) and conventional current is taken from positive to negative.
2**Drift velocity.** Free electrons gain a tiny average velocity v_d along the field between collisions: v_d = eEτ/m = eVτ/(ml); it is of the order of mm/s even in strong currents.
3**Relation I = nAev_d.** In time t electrons move v_d·t, so the charge crossing area A is q = nAev_d·t, giving I = nAev_d, where n is the number of free electrons per unit volume.
4**Current density and mobility.** J = I/A = nev_d is the current density; mobility μ = v_d/E, and conductivity σ = neμ connects drift to the microscopic picture.
5**Macroscopic vs microscopic.** Ohm's law in microscopic form is J = σE; the same field that drives the drift also sustains the current the ammeter reads.

Electric Currents, Drift Velocity and Current

#Concept
1**Current $I$:** rate of flow of charge. $I = Q/t$. Units: ampere (A).
2**Conventional current:** flows from positive to negative. Electron flow is opposite, from negative to positive.
3**Drift velocity $v_d$:** the average velocity of free electrons in a conductor under an electric field. It is very slow, about $10^{-4}\,\text{m/s}$.
4**Relation:** $I = nAev_d$, where $n$ is electron density, $A$ is cross-sectional area, $e$ is electron charge, and $v_d$ is drift velocity.
5 Why signal is fast despite slow drift:** when you switch on a circuit, the electric field propagates at nearly the speed of light, setting all electrons into drift motion simultaneously. The electrons themselves drift slowly, but the signal is instantaneous.
6**Derivation of $I = nAev_d$:** in time $dt$, all electrons within distance $v_d\,dt$ of a cross-section pass through it. Number of electrons $= nA\,v_d\,dt$. Charge $dQ = nAev_d\,dt$. Hence $I = dQ/dt = nAev_d$.
7**Typical drift velocity:** for copper ($n \approx 8.5 \times 10^{28}$ electrons/m$^3$) with $I = 10$ A and $A = 1\,\text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}$ m$^2$: $v_d = I/(nAe) = 10/(8.5 \times 10^{28} \times 10^{-6} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 7.4 \times 10^{-4}$ m/s.
8**No field, no drift:** without an electric field, electrons move randomly at thermal speeds ($\sim 10^5$ m/s) but with zero net velocity, so no current flows.
9**Current density $\vec{J}$:** current per unit area. $J = I/A = nev_d$. Vector form: $\vec{J} = nq\vec{v}_d$.
10**Relaxation time $\tau$:** average time between successive collisions of an electron with the lattice ions. $v_d = eE\tau/m$, where $m$ is electron mass.
11**Microscopic vs macroscopic:** $I = nAev_d$ is the microscopic view. Ohm's law $V = IR$ is the macroscopic view. They are connected through resistivity $\rho = m/(ne^2\tau)$.
12**Sign convention:** current is a scalar quantity. The direction of positive current flow is defined as the direction of motion of positive charge, even though in metals the actual charge carriers (electrons) move opposite.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Drift velocity is very slow ($10^{-4}$ m/s) but the signal (electric field) travels at nearly the speed of light. These are two entirely different things — electrons don't race from switch to bulb; the field establishes itself instantly.
The magnitude of current $I$ is the same everywhere in a series circuit. Current is not consumed — electrons drift at the same average speed everywhere, just as water flows at the same rate through a pipe of uniform cross-section.
A larger cross-sectional area means more charge carriers can flow simultaneously, so for the same current, the drift velocity decreases: $v_d = I/(nAe)$. A thick wire carries current with slower drift velocity than a thin wire of the same material.
The thermal speed of electrons ($\sim 10^5$ m/s) is about $10^9$ times the drift velocity ($\sim 10^{-4}$ m/s). The thermal motion is random and contributes no net current; only the drift component does.
Current is a scalar, not a vector. It has magnitude but the 'direction' is just a convention for analysis. The actual charge carriers (electrons in metals) move opposite to the defined current direction.

Ohm's Law, Resistance, Resistivity and Conductivity

#Concept
1**Ohmic conductor:** obeys Ohm's law. The $I$-$V$ graph is a straight line through the origin. Resistance is constant.
2**Non-ohmic conductor:** does not obey Ohm's law. The $I$-$V$ graph is curved. Resistance changes with voltage or current.
3**Examples of non-ohmic:** diode (conducts easily in one direction only), filament lamp (resistance increases with temperature as current increases), thermistor (resistance decreases with temperature).
4**Why filament lamp is non-ohmic:** as current increases, the filament heats up, increasing its resistivity. The $I$-$V$ curve bends because $R$ is not constant.
5**Dynamic resistance:** $R_d = dV/dI$ at a point on the $I$-$V$ curve. For non-ohmic devices, this is the useful measure of resistance at a specific operating point.
6**Forward bias diode:** current flows easily once the voltage exceeds the threshold ($\\\\approx 0.7$ V for silicon, $\\\\approx 0.3$ V for germanium). Below threshold, very little current flows.
7**Reverse bias diode:** almost no current flows (except a tiny leakage current). At the breakdown voltage, current increases sharply and the diode may be damaged.
8**Thermistor:** a semiconductor device whose resistance decreases sharply with temperature (NTC type). Used as temperature sensors and inrush current limiters.
9**Zener diode:** operates in reverse breakdown at a precise voltage. It maintains a nearly constant voltage across it, used as a voltage regulator.
10**Filament lamp $I$-$V$ curve:** starts linearly at low voltage (cool filament, low resistance). At higher voltages, the curve bends toward the voltage axis as the filament heats and resistance increases.
11**Temperature coefficient of filament:** tungsten has $\\\\alpha \\\\approx 0.0045$ $^{\\\\circ}$C$^{-1}$. A 100 W, 230 V bulb has cold resistance of about $50\\\\,\\\\Omega$ but hot resistance of $529\\\\,\\\\Omega$.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Ohm's law $V = IR$ is a definition of resistance, not a law that all materials obey. Only ohmic conductors have a linear $I$-$V$ relationship. For non-ohmic devices, $R = V/I$ is still defined but varies.
A filament lamp becomes a better conductor (lower resistance) when cold. This is why bulbs often burn out at the moment they are switched on - the initial current surge is 10 times the steady-state current.
The dynamic resistance $dV/dI$ is different from the static resistance $V/I$. For a non-ohmic device, the static resistance is $V/I$ while the dynamic resistance is the slope of the $I$-$V$ curve at that point.
A diode is not simply a 'one-way valve' for current. In reverse bias, a tiny leakage current (nA to $\\\mu$A range) still flows. In forward bias, the current is exponentially related to voltage, not linearly.
Thermistors are not simply temperature-dependent resistors. Their resistance changes exponentially with temperature: $R = R_0 e^{\eta(1/T - 1/T_0)}$, which makes them highly sensitive to small temperature changes.

Ohm's Law and Resistance

#Concept
1**Ohm's law.** At constant physical conditions (temperature, state) the potential difference across a conductor is directly proportional to the current: V ∝ I, or V = IR.
2**Resistance and resistivity.** R = ρL/A: resistance grows with length L and falls with area A; resistivity ρ (unit Ω·m) is a property of the material itself.
3**Temperature dependence.** For metals R_t = R_0(1 + αt) with α > 0; semiconductors have negative temperature coefficient as carrier density rises with heat.
4**Conductance and conductivity.** Conductance G = 1/R (siemens) and conductivity σ = 1/ρ; better conductors have large σ and small ρ.
5**Limitations.** Ohm's law does not hold for diodes, electrolytes, superconductors or conductors at changing temperature — there V–I is not a straight line through the origin.

Current-Voltage Relations: Ohmic and Non-Ohmic Resistance

#Concept
1Ohm's Law: For an ohmic conductor at constant physical conditions (especially temperature), the current $I$ flowing through it is directly proportional to the potential difference $V$ across its ends: $V = IR$, where $R$ is the resistance. The graph of $V$ vs $I$ is a straight line through the origin.
2Resistance Definition: Resistance $R$ of a conductor is defined as the ratio of potential difference across it to the current through it: $R = V/I$. SI unit: ohm ($\Omega = ext{V/A} = ext{V}^2/ ext{W}$). Dimensions: $[M L^2 T^{-3} I^{-2}]$.
3Resistivity: The resistance of a uniform conductor depends on its geometry and material: $R = \rho L/A$, where $\rho$ is resistivity (material property), $L$ is length, and $A$ is cross-sectional area. SI unit of $\rho$: $\Omega\cdot ext{m}$. Conductivity $\sigma = 1/\rho$.
4Ohmic Conductors: Conductors that obey Ohm's law ($V$-$I$ graph is linear) are called ohmic. Examples: metals at constant temperature, electrolytes, carbon resistors. Their resistance is independent of $V$ and $I$ (within limits).
5Non-Ohmic Conductors: Conductors that do NOT obey Ohm's law ($V$-$I$ graph is non-linear). Examples: diode, transistor, thermistor, filament lamp, vacuum tube, electrolyte at high voltage. Their resistance varies with $V$ or $I$ (dynamic resistance $r_d = dV/dI$).
6Temperature Dependence: For metals, resistance increases with temperature: $R_T = R_0(1 + \alpha\Delta T)$, where $\alpha$ is temperature coefficient of resistance (positive for metals). For semiconductors, $\alpha$ is negative (resistance decreases with temperature).
7Filament Lamp: A filament lamp is non-ohmic because its temperature rises as current increases, changing its resistance. Cold resistance is lower than hot resistance.
8Diode: A p-n junction diode conducts easily in forward bias (small resistance) and blocks in reverse bias (large resistance). Its $V$-$I$ characteristic is highly non-linear.
9Superconductors: Below a critical temperature $T_c$, certain materials exhibit zero electrical resistance ($R = 0$) and expel magnetic fields (Meissner effect). Examples: mercury ($T_c \approx 4.2\text{ K}$), niobium-titanium alloys.

Key Points

Point
Ohm's law: $V = IR$ for ohmic conductors at constant temperature.
Resistance $R = V/I$; SI unit ohm ($\Omega$).
Resistance depends on geometry and material: $R = \rho L/A$.
Ohmic conductors have linear V-I graphs; non-ohmic have non-linear graphs.
Examples of non-ohmic: diode, filament lamp, thermistor, transistor.
Temperature coefficient $\alpha$ is positive for metals, negative for semiconductors.
Dynamic resistance $r_d = dV/dI$ differs from static resistance $V/I$ for non-ohmic devices.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing ohmic with linear: Ohm's law requires linearity AND constant temperature; a heated metal is not ohmic even if its V-I graph looks linear at low currents.
Believing $R = V/I$ is always constant: For non-ohmic devices, $V/I$ changes with operating point; only $dV/dI$ (dynamic resistance) is meaningful locally.
Thinking resistivity changes with dimensions: $\rho$ is an intrinsic material property; only $R$ changes with $L$ and $A$.
Assuming all metals obey Ohm's law perfectly: At very low temperatures or high fields, even metals deviate.
Confusing resistance with resistivity: Resistance depends on geometry; resistivity does not.

Summary: Ohm's law ($V = IR$) defines resistance for ohmic conductors with linear V-I characteristics at constant temperature. Resistance depends on geometry and material via $R = \rho L/A$. Non-ohmic conductors (diodes, filament lamps, thermistors) have non-linear V-I graphs where dynamic resistance $dV/dI$ differs from static resistance $V/I$. Temperature coefficient of resistance is positive for metals and negative for semiconductors.

Potential Divider

#Concept
1**Potential Divider:** This topic covers the fundamental concepts of potential divider in the physics curriculum.
2Understanding potential divider is essential for solving problems and building higher-level mathematical skills.
3Key definitions and theorems related to potential divider should be memorized and practiced regularly.
4**Definition:** two or more resistors in series across a voltage source, used to obtain a fraction of the source voltage.
5**Output voltage:** $V_{out} = V_{in} \ rac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$, where $R_2$ is the resistor across which the output is taken.
6**Why useful:** allows you to set any voltage between 0 and $V_{in}$ by adjusting the ratio of resistances.
7**Loading effect:** if a load resistor $R_L$ is connected across $R_2$, the output voltage changes because $R_2$ is now in parallel with $R_L$, reducing the effective resistance.
8**Potentiometer:** a continuous potential divider using a uniform resistance wire. The output voltage is proportional to the length of wire between the wiper and one end.
9**Voltage divider rule:** for $n$ resistors in series, the voltage across resistor $R_i$ is $V_i = V_{total} \\\\cdot R_i / R_{total}$.
10**Unloaded divider:** when no current is drawn from the output, the output voltage is exactly $V_{in} \\\\cdot R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$.
11**Loaded divider analysis:** with load $R_L$ across $R_2$, the effective resistance of the lower branch becomes $R_2' = R_2 R_L/(R_2 + R_L)$. The new output is $V_{out} = V_{in} \\\\cdot R_2'/(R_1 + R_2')$.
12**Design rule:** to minimize loading effects, choose $R_1, R_2 \\\\ll R_L$ so the divider draws much more current than the load.
13**Potentiometer as voltage source:** a potentiometer can provide any fraction of the input voltage from 0 to $V_{in}$. The three terminals are the two ends of the resistive track and the sliding wiper.
14**Current through the divider:** even with no load, the divider draws current $I = V_{in}/(R_1 + R_2)$, which causes power dissipation $P = V_{in}^2/(R_1 + R_2)$.

Resistances in Series and Parallel

#Concept
1**Series:** same current through all resistors. $R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + \ldots$. Voltage divides: $V_i = I R_i$.
2**Parallel:** same voltage across all resistors. $1/R_{eq} = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + \ldots$. Current divides: $I_i = V/R_i$.
3**Why series increases resistance:** the current must pass through each resistor sequentially, so each adds its opposition to the total.
4**Why parallel decreases resistance:** multiple paths allow more current for the same voltage, equivalent to increasing the cross-sectional area of a single conductor.
5**Special case — two parallel resistors:** $R_{eq} = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$. This is always less than the smaller resistor.
6**Series voltage divider:** in a series chain, each resistor gets a fraction of the total voltage proportional to its resistance: $V_i = V_{total} \cdot R_i / R_{total}$.
7**Parallel current divider:** in a parallel group, each resistor gets a fraction of the total current inversely proportional to its resistance: $I_i = I_{total} \cdot R_{eq}/R_i$.
8**Power in series:** $P = I^2 R$. Larger resistors dissipate more power in series because they carry the same current but have larger voltage drop.
9**Power in parallel:** $P = V^2/R$. Smaller resistors dissipate more power in parallel because they draw more current at the same voltage.
10**Identical resistors in parallel:** $n$ identical resistors of value $R$ in parallel give $R_{eq} = R/n$. This is useful for sharing current among multiple resistors.
11**Kirchhoff's laws:** series resistors satisfy KVL (sum of voltages equals source). Parallel resistors satisfy KCL (sum of currents equals total).
12**Mixed networks:** reduce step by step — find series or parallel groups, replace with equivalents, and repeat until one resistor remains.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Resistors in series add (like capacitors in parallel). Resistors in parallel combine inversely (like capacitors in series). The rules for resistors and capacitors are always opposite.
The equivalent resistance of a parallel combination is always less than the smallest individual resistor. Do not think of it as simply 'dividing' — the math is $R_{eq} = (R_1 R_2)/(R_1 + R_2)$.
In series, the same current flows through all resistors, but the voltage drop across each is different (proportional to its resistance). In parallel, the same voltage is across each, but current differs.
When a resistor is added in parallel, the equivalent resistance decreases. When added in series, it increases. This is because parallel paths provide more conduction channels.
Two resistors of equal value $R$ in parallel give $R/2$. Two resistors of equal value in series give $2R$. These are the most common benchmark results in circuit analysis.

EMF and Internal Resistance

#Concept
1**EMF ($\\\\mathcal{E}$):** the open-circuit voltage of a source. It is the work done per unit charge in moving charge around the complete circuit.
2**Internal resistance $r$:** the resistance inside the cell due to electrolyte and electrodes. It causes the terminal voltage to drop when current flows.
3**Terminal voltage:** $V = \\\\mathcal{E} - Ir$. When no current flows ($I=0$), $V = \\\\mathcal{E}$. Under load, $V < \\\\mathcal{E}$.
4**Cells in series:** $\\\\mathcal{E}_{total} = \\\\mathcal{E}_1 + \\\\mathcal{E}_2 + \\\\ldots$, $r_{total} = r_1 + r_2 + \\\\ldots$. Series adds both EMF and internal resistance.
5**Cells in parallel:** $\\\\mathcal{E}_{total} = \\\\mathcal{E}$ (if all equal), $r_{total} = r/n$ for $n$ identical cells. Parallel reduces internal resistance, allowing higher current delivery.
6**Maximum power transfer:** the power delivered to an external load $R$ is maximum when $R = r$. The maximum power is $P_{max} = \\\\mathcal{E}^2/(4r)$.
7**Current in a simple circuit:** $I = \\\\mathcal{E}/(R + r)$, where $R$ is the external resistance and $r$ is the internal resistance.
8**Efficiency:** the efficiency of power transfer is $\\\\eta = R/(R + r)$. Maximum efficiency approaches 100% when $R \\\\gg r$, but maximum power requires $R = r$.
9**Short circuit current:** when $R = 0$, $I_{sc} = \\\\mathcal{E}/r$. This can be very large and dangerous, causing heating and damage.
10**Cells with different EMFs:** when cells with different EMFs are connected in parallel, circulating currents flow between them. This can damage cells and should be avoided.
11**Real cell behavior:** as a cell discharges, its EMF slowly decreases and its internal resistance increases. An old battery reads lower than its rated voltage even when not loaded.
12**Emf.** The emf E of a cell is the work done by the cell in moving unit charge once around the whole circuit; it is measured with a voltmeter across the open terminals and is not a force.
13**Terminal potential difference.** With current flowing, internal resistance drops Ir inside the cell, so the terminal voltage is V = E − I·r while discharging (V = E + I·r while charging).
14**Internal resistance.** r depends on the electrolyte (r = ρl/a), electrode area and separation; a fresh cell has small r which grows with use.
15**V–I characteristic.** A plot of V against I is a straight line whose slope gives r and whose intercept on the V axis gives E — the standard graph question.
16**Practical limits.** Short-circuit current I_s = E/r; the external load receives maximum power when its resistance equals r, which is also why terminal voltage falls under heavy load.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
EMF is not a force - it is a voltage (energy per unit charge). The name is historical. EMF is measured in volts, not newtons.
Terminal voltage $V = \\\\mathcal{E} - Ir$ is less than EMF when current flows out of the cell. But when charging the cell, current flows into the positive terminal, so $V = \\\\mathcal{E} + Ir$ - the terminal voltage is greater than EMF.
In series, both EMF and internal resistance add. In parallel, the equivalent EMF is the same as one cell, but the equivalent internal resistance is reduced, allowing more current.
Maximum power transfer ($R = r$) occurs at 50% efficiency. For efficient power delivery, you want $R \\\\gg r$ (high efficiency, low power). For maximum power, you sacrifice efficiency.
A fresh battery has a high open-circuit voltage (close to its EMF) but drops significantly under load if its internal resistance has increased with age. That is why old batteries seem weak.
Carefully distinguish between similar-looking concepts in emf and internal resistance.
Pay attention to the conditions under which formulas and theorems apply.
Practice with multiple examples to solidify your understanding.

Electromotive Force of a Source, Internal Resistance

#Concept
1EMF Definition: Electromotive force (EMF, $\mathcal{E}$) is the energy supplied by a source per unit charge to move charge through the complete circuit. It is NOT a force but a potential difference measured in volts. $\mathcal{E} = W/q$ where $W$ is work done by the source and $q$ is charge moved.
2Terminal Potential Difference: When current $I$ flows, the terminal voltage $V$ across the source terminals is $V = \mathcal{E} - Ir$, where $r$ is internal resistance. The voltage drop $Ir$ inside the source is called lost volts.
3Internal Resistance: Every real source (cell, battery, generator) has internal resistance $r$ due to electrolyte resistance, electrode resistance, and chemical factors. It causes the terminal voltage to drop below EMF when current is drawn.
4Open Circuit: When $I = 0$ (open circuit), $V = \mathcal{E}$ — the terminal voltage equals the EMF since there is no internal voltage drop.
5Short Circuit: When terminals are shorted ($R = 0$), $I = \mathcal{E}/r$ — maximum current flows, limited only by internal resistance. This can damage the source.
6Cells in Series: For $n$ identical cells each of EMF $\mathcal{E}$ and internal resistance $r$ in series: total EMF $= n\mathcal{E}$, total internal resistance $= nr$, equivalent $V = n\mathcal{E} - Inr$.
7Cells in Parallel: For $n$ identical cells each of EMF $\mathcal{E}$ and internal resistance $r$ in parallel: equivalent EMF $= \mathcal{E}$, equivalent internal resistance $= r/n$ (current capacity increases).
8Mixed Grouping: Cells in series-parallel combinations balance voltage and current requirements for specific applications.
9Determination of EMF and Internal Resistance: Measure terminal voltage $V$ for different currents $I$. Plot $V$ vs $I$: y-intercept $= \mathcal{E}$, slope $= -r$. Alternatively use a potentiometer for accurate EMF measurement.
10Power Delivered: Power delivered to external load $R$ is $P = I^2R = (\mathcal{E}/(R+r))^2 R$. Maximum power transfer occurs when $R = r$, with $P_\text{max} = \mathcal{E}^2/4r$.

Key Points

Point
EMF is energy per unit charge supplied by the source; measured in volts.
Terminal voltage $V = \mathcal{E} - Ir$; internal drop $Ir$ is called lost volts.
Open circuit: $V = \mathcal{E}$; short circuit: $I = \mathcal{E}/r$.
Cells in series add EMF and internal resistance; parallel cells reduce effective internal resistance.
Maximum power transfer when external resistance equals internal resistance ($R = r$).

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing EMF with terminal voltage: EMF is open-circuit voltage; terminal voltage drops under load due to $Ir$.
Thinking EMF is a force: EMF is energy per unit charge (volts), not a mechanical force.
Believing parallel cells add EMF: Parallel identical cells have same EMF as one cell; only current capacity increases.
Assuming max power = max efficiency: At $R = r$, power is max but efficiency is only 50%.
Ignoring internal resistance: Real sources always have internal resistance affecting terminal voltage.

Summary: EMF is the energy supplied per unit charge by a source; terminal voltage $V = \mathcal{E} - Ir$ drops due to internal resistance. Cells in series add EMF and internal resistance; parallel cells keep EMF same but divide internal resistance. Maximum power transfer occurs when load resistance equals internal resistance.

EMF, Internal Resistance and Cells in Combination

#Concept
1**EMF ($\mathcal{E}$):** the open-circuit voltage of a source. It is the work done per unit charge in moving charge around the complete circuit.
2**Internal resistance $r$:** the resistance inside the cell due to electrolyte and electrodes. It causes the terminal voltage to drop when current flows.
3**Terminal voltage:** $V = \mathcal{E} - Ir$. When no current flows ($I=0$), $V = \mathcal{E}$. Under load, $V < \mathcal{E}$.
4**Cells in series:** $\mathcal{E}_{total} = \mathcal{E}_1 + \mathcal{E}_2 + \ldots$, $r_{total} = r_1 + r_2 + \ldots$. Series adds both EMF and internal resistance.
5**Cells in parallel:** $\mathcal{E}_{total} = \mathcal{E}$ (if all equal), $r_{total} = r/n$ for $n$ identical cells. Parallel reduces internal resistance, allowing higher current delivery.
6**Maximum power transfer:** the power delivered to an external load $R$ is maximum when $R = r$. The maximum power is $P_{max} = \mathcal{E}^2/(4r)$.
7**Current in a simple circuit:** $I = \mathcal{E}/(R + r)$, where $R$ is the external resistance and $r$ is the internal resistance.
8**Efficiency:** the efficiency of power transfer is $\eta = R/(R + r)$. Maximum efficiency approaches 100% when $R \gg r$, but maximum power requires $R = r$.
9**Short circuit current:** when $R = 0$, $I_{sc} = \\\\mathcal{E}/r$. This can be very large and dangerous, causing heating and damage.
10**Cells with different EMFs:** when cells with different EMFs are connected in parallel, circulating currents flow between them. This can damage cells and should be avoided.
11**Real cell behavior:** as a cell discharges, its EMF slowly decreases and its internal resistance increases. An old battery reads lower than its rated voltage even when not loaded.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
EMF is not a force - it is a voltage (energy per unit charge). The name is historical. EMF is measured in volts, not newtons.
Terminal voltage $V = \\\\mathcal{E} - Ir$ is less than EMF when current flows out of the cell. But when charging the cell, current flows into the positive terminal, so $V = \\\\mathcal{E} + Ir$ - the terminal voltage is greater than EMF.
In series, both EMF and internal resistance add. In parallel, the equivalent EMF is the same as one cell, but the equivalent internal resistance is reduced, allowing more current.
Maximum power transfer ($R = r$) occurs at 50% efficiency. For efficient power delivery, you want $R \\\\gg r$ (high efficiency, low power). For maximum power, you sacrifice efficiency.
A fresh battery has a high open-circuit voltage (close to its EMF) but drops significantly under load if its internal resistance has increased with age. That is why old batteries seem weak.

Work and Power in Circuits

#Concept
1**Electrical work.** Moving charge q through potential V costs W = qV = VIt; in a pure resistance this equals I²Rt = V²t/R — the energy converted.
2**Electric power.** P = VI = I²R = V²/R, measured in watts; 1 horsepower ≈ 746 W and commercial rating is often in kW.
3**Energy unit.** 1 kWh (one 'unit' on a meter bill) = 3.6 × 10⁶ J — the energy a 1000 W appliance uses in an hour.
4**Joule's heating.** A current I through resistance R heats it by H = I²Rt (Joule's law); this is the working principle of heaters, bulbs and fuses.
5**Maximum power transfer.** A source of emf E and internal r delivers maximum power to an external R when R = r; the efficiency of energy transfer is then only 50%.

Work and Power in Electrical Circuits

#Concept
1**Electrical work:** $W = VIt = I^2Rt = V^2t/R$. Work done by a current is the energy transferred.
2**Electrical power:** $P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R$. Power is the rate of energy transfer.
3**Joule heating:** $P = I^2R$ is the power dissipated as heat in a resistor. This is also called $I^2R$ loss.
4**Kilowatt-hour:** unit of electrical energy. $1\\\\,\ ext{kWh} = 3.6 \ imes 10^6\\\\,\ ext{J}$. This is what electricity meters measure.
5**Efficiency:** useful power output divided by total power input. Transmission lines use high voltage to reduce $I^2R$ losses because $P_{loss} \\\\propto I^2$.
6**Power in different elements:** in a resistor, all electrical energy is converted to heat: $P = I^2R$. In a motor, electrical energy converts to mechanical work plus heat: $P_{input} = VI = I^2R + P_{mech}$. In a battery being charged, electrical energy is stored as chemical energy: $P_{input} = \\\\mathcal{E}I + I^2r$.
7**Energy conservation in circuits:** the total electrical energy supplied equals the sum of energy dissipated as heat and energy converted to other forms: $V_{source} \\\\cdot I \\\\cdot t = I^2Rt + \ ext{other forms}$.
8**Power rating of resistors:** resistors are rated by the maximum power they can dissipate without damage. Common ratings are 0.25 W, 0.5 W, and 1 W. Exceeding this causes overheating and failure.
9**Domestic electrical cost:** the cost of running an appliance is $\ ext{Cost} = P(\ ext{kW}) \ imes t(\ ext{h}) \ imes \ ext{rate (per kWh)}$. A 100 W bulb running for 5 hours uses 0.5 kWh.
10**Power factor:** for AC circuits, $P = VI\\\\cos\\\\phi$, where $\\\\cos\\\\phi$ is the power factor. For DC circuits, $\\\\cos\\\\phi = 1$, so $P = VI$ always.
11**Heat energy in calories:** $H = 0.24 I^2 R t$ calories (mechanical equivalent of heat). In SI units, $H = I^2 R t$ joules.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Power $P = I^2R$ and $P = V^2/R$ are both correct but apply in different contexts. Use $I^2R$ when current is known (series circuits). Use $V^2/R$ when voltage is known (parallel circuits). Using the wrong one gives a wrong answer because $V$ and $I$ are not independent.
Electrical energy is measured in joules (J) or kilowatt-hours (kWh), NOT in watts (W). Watt is a unit of power (energy per time). $1\\\\,\ ext{kWh} = 3.6 \ imes 10^6$ J.
A 100 W bulb connected to 230 V draws less current than a 60 W bulb (because $I = P/V$, so the 100 W bulb has $I = 0.43$ A vs $I = 0.26$ A for the 60 W bulb). The 100 W bulb actually has LOWER resistance.
In a series circuit, the higher-wattage bulb glows dimmer because it has lower resistance, and in series the same current flows through both: $P = I^2R$, so the higher resistance (60 W bulb) gets more power.
Power dissipated in internal resistance ($I^2r$) is wasted energy. This is why battery-powered devices get warm - the internal resistance converts some chemical energy to heat instead of useful work.

Established academic fact

Coulomb’s law models the force between point charges; superposition lets the vector contributions from several charges be added. In circuit problems, charge and energy conservation support Kirchhoff’s rules.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

Ohm’s law V = IR is a useful relation for ohmic materials over conditions where resistance is approximately constant. It is not universal: temperature changes and components such as diodes can produce non-ohmic behaviour.
