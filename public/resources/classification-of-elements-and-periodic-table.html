Modern Periodic Law and Modern Periodic Table

#Concept
1### 1. Historical Evolution of Periodic Classification
2• **Dobereiner's Law of Triads (1829):** Grouped elements into triads where atomic mass of middle element was roughly arithmetic mean of first and third (e.g., Li 7, Na 23, K 39; Ca 40, Sr 88, Ba 137; Cl 35.5, Br 80, I 127). Failed because only a few triads could be identified.
3• **Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865):** When elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic mass, every eighth element shows similar physical and chemical properties like musical notes (Sa, Re, Ga, Ma...). Failed after Calcium (valid only up to Ca, Z=20) and could not accommodate noble gases discovered later.
4• **Lothar Meyer's Curve (1869):** Plotted atomic volume against atomic weight. Alkali metals occupied sharp peaks; alkaline earth metals occupied descending curves; halogens occupied ascending slopes; transition metals occupied valleys.
5• **Mendeleev's Periodic Law (1869):** 'The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses.' Mendeleev left gaps for undiscovered elements (Eka-Boron = Scandium, Eka-Aluminium = Gallium, Eka-Silicon = Germanium). Anomalies: Inverted pairs (Ar 39.9 before K 39.1; Co 58.9 before Ni 58.7; Te 127.6 before I 126.9), no fixed position for Hydrogen, isotopes could not be accommodated.
6### 2. Moseley's Experiment and Modern Periodic Law (1913)
7• English physicist Henry Moseley bombarded various metal anticathodes with high-speed cathode rays (electrons) and studied characteristic X-ray emission spectra.
8• **Moseley's Law:** The square root of the frequency (√ν) of the characteristic X-ray spectral line is directly proportional to the atomic number (Z) of the target element:
9 $$\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)$$
10 where ν = frequency of K_α X-ray line, Z = atomic number (nuclear charge), a and b are constants characteristic of the spectral line series (screening constant b ≈ 1 for K-series).
11• **Fundamental Conclusion:** Plot of √ν vs Z yields a straight line, whereas plot of √ν vs atomic mass (A) does not. This conclusively proved that **atomic number (Z)**, representing the net positive nuclear charge (number of protons), is the fundamental property governing the chemical identity of elements, NOT atomic mass.
12• **Statement of Modern Periodic Law:** 'The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.'
13### 3. Resolution of Mendeleev's Anomalies by Modern Periodic Table
14• **Anomalous Pairs Resolved:** Argon (Z=18) naturally precedes Potassium (Z=19); Cobalt (Z=27) naturally precedes Nickel (Z=28); Tellurium (Z=52) naturally precedes Iodine (Z=53) purely by increasing atomic numbers, regardless of atomic weights.
15• **Position of Isotopes:** Since isotopes share identical atomic numbers (same Z, different mass number A), they occupy one single position in the periodic table without conflict.
16• **Scientific Division into Blocks:** Reflects filling of electron shells according to quantum mechanics (Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion, Hund's rule).
17### 4. Structural Layout of the Long Form (Bohr's Form) Periodic Table
18• **Periods (7 Horizontal Rows):** Corresponds to principal quantum number n of outermost valence shell.
19 - Period 1 (n=1): 2 elements (1s²)
20 - Period 2 (n=2): 8 elements (2s² 2p⁶)
21 - Period 3 (n=3): 8 elements (3s² 3p⁶)
22 - Period 4 (n=4): 18 elements (4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁶)
23 - Period 5 (n=5): 18 elements (5s² 4d¹⁰ 5p⁶)
24 - Period 6 (n=6): 32 elements (6s² 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6p⁶, including Lanthanides ₅₈Ce–₇₁Lu)
25 - Period 7 (n=7): 32 elements (7s² 5f¹⁴ 6d¹⁰ 7p⁶, including Actinides ₉₀Th–₁₀₃Lr)
26• **Groups (18 Vertical Columns):** Elements having identical outer electronic configurations and exhibiting similar chemical properties.
27• **IUPAC Nomenclature for Elements with Z > 100:** Uses numerical roots for digits: 0 = nil, 1 = un, 2 = bi, 3 = tri, 4 = quad, 5 = pent, 6 = hex, 7 = sept, 8 = oct, 9 = enn. Suffix '-ium'. Example: 104 = Unnilquadium (Unq), 118 = Ununoctium (Uuo / Oganesson).

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Modern Periodic Law is based on atomic mass like Mendeleev's law. ✅ Modern Periodic Law is strictly based on atomic number (Z), discovered by Moseley via X-ray spectroscopy.
❌ Isotopes need separate positions in the modern periodic table. ✅ Isotopes possess identical atomic numbers, so they naturally share the exact same slot in the modern table.
❌ Cobalt was placed before Nickel by Mendeleev as an error. ✅ Mendeleev placed Co before Ni based on chemical properties despite Co being heavier (58.93 vs 58.69); modern table confirms Co (Z=27) correctly precedes Ni (Z=28).
❌ Moseley's graph plotted frequency ν against atomic weight. ✅ Moseley plotted the square root of frequency (√ν) against atomic number (Z), obtaining a perfect straight line: √ν = a(Z - b).
❌ Period number corresponds to total number of electrons. ✅ Period number corresponds directly to the principal quantum number (n) of the outermost valence electron shell.

Classification into Groups, Periods and Blocks (s, p, d, f Blocks & Metal Classifications)

#Concept
1### 1. Structural Architecture of the Modern Periodic Table (Bohr's Long Form)
2• Based on Moseley's Modern Periodic Law: The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers (nuclear charge, Z).
3• Consists of 7 horizontal rows called **Periods** and 18 vertical columns called **Groups** (IUPAC 1 to 18).
4• **Period 1:** 2 elements (H, He) — shortest period (fills 1s subshell).
5• **Periods 2 & 3:** 8 elements each (Li–Ne, Na–Ar) — short periods (fill 2s, 2p and 3s, 3p subshells).
6• **Periods 4 & 5:** 18 elements each (K–Kr, Rb–Xe) — long periods (fill 4s, 3d, 4p and 5s, 4d, 5p subshells).
7• **Period 6:** 32 elements (Cs–Rn, including 14 Lanthanides from ₅₈Ce to ₇₁Lu) — very long period (fills 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p).
8• **Period 7:** 32 elements (Fr–Og, including 14 Actinides from ₉₀Th to ₁₀₃Lr) — complete radioactive period (fills 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p).
9### 2. s-Block Elements (Groups 1 & 2)
10• **Definition:** Elements in which the differentiating (last) electron enters the outermost s-orbital.
11• **General Valence Electronic Configuration:** ns¹⁻² (Group 1 Alkali metals: ns¹; Group 2 Alkaline earth metals: ns²; where n = 2 to 7).
12• **Group 1 (Alkali Metals):** ₃Li, ₁₁Na, ₁₉K, ₃₇Rb, ₅₅Cs, ₈₇Fr. Soft, silvery-white metals, highly electropositive, lowest ionization enthalpies in their periods.
13• **Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals):** ₄Be, ₁₂Mg, ₂₀Ca, ₃₈Sr, ₅₆Ba, ₈₈Ra. Harder than alkali metals, +2 fixed oxidation state, basic oxides (except amphoteric BeO).
14• **Key Physical Characteristics:** Low melting and boiling points due to weak metallic bonding (only 1 or 2 valence electrons in bonding sea). Densities are very low (Li, Na, K float on or displace water).
15• **Flame Test:** Readily excited s-electrons emit signature colors: Li (Crimson), Na (Golden Yellow), K (Lilac/Violet), Rb (Red-Violet), Cs (Sky Blue), Ca (Brick Red), Sr (Crimson), Ba (Apple Green). Note: Be and Mg do NOT give flame coloration because their electrons are held tightly by high effective nuclear charge.
16• **Chemical Properties:** Extremely strong reducing agents (standard reduction potentials: Li is most negative at -3.04 V due to exceptionally high hydration enthalpy: ΔH_hyd of Li⁺). React vigorously with water to liberate H₂: 2M + 2H₂O → 2M⁺ + 2OH⁻ + H₂↑.
17• **Ammoniated Electrons:** Dissolve in anhydrous liquid ammonia to yield deep blue, electrically conductive, paramagnetic solutions: M + (x+y)NH₃ → [M(NH₃)_x]⁺ + [e(NH₃)_y]⁻.
18• **Diagonal Relationship:** Li resembles Mg, and Be resembles Al due to similar ionic charge/radius ratio (polarizing power) and nearly identical electronegativities.
19### 3. p-Block Elements (Groups 13 to 18)
20• **Definition:** Elements in which the differentiating electron enters any of the three degenerate outermost p-orbitals.
21• **General Valence Electronic Configuration:** ns² np¹⁻⁶ (He: 1s² is placed in Group 18 due to its filled shell noble gas behavior).
22• **Families:** Group 13 (Boron family: ns² np¹), Group 14 (Carbon family: ns² np²), Group 15 (Pnictogens: ns² np³), Group 16 (Chalcogens: ns² np⁴), Group 17 (Halogens: ns² np⁵), Group 18 (Noble/Inert Gases: ns² np⁶).
23• **Uniqueness:** Only block containing all three categories of matter: Metals (e.g., Al, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi), Metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, Po), and Non-metals (C, N, O, F, Ne, P, S, Cl, Ar, Se, Br, Kr, I, Xe, Rn).
24• **Inert Pair Effect:** Reluctance of the valence s-electrons to participate in chemical bonding in heavier elements of post-transition p-block elements due to ineffective shielding of intervening d and f electrons. Consequence: Lower oxidation states become progressively more stable down the group: Tl⁺ > Tl³⁺, Pb²⁺ > Pb⁴⁺, Bi³⁺ > Bi⁵⁺. Hence, PbO₂ and PbCl₄ act as strong oxidizing agents.
25• **Catenation & Multiple Bonding:** Carbon shows the highest catenation power due to strong C–C single bond strength (348 kJ/mol). Second-row p-elements readily form stable pπ–pπ multiple bonds (C=C, C≡C, N≡N, C=O), whereas heavier analogues form weaker dπ–pπ or dπ–dπ bonds.
26• **Anomalous Behavior of 2nd Period Elements (B, C, N, O, F):** Small atomic radii, high electronegativity, absence of vacant d-orbitals in valence shell (maximum covalency strictly limited to 4, cannot form hypervalent species like SF₆ or PCl₅).
27### 4. d-Block Elements (Transition Metals, Groups 3 to 12)
28• **Definition:** Elements in which the differentiating electron enters the penultimate (n-1)d subshell.
29• **General Valence Electronic Configuration:** (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻² (where n = 4, 5, 6, 7).
30• **Four Transition Series:** 3d-series (₂₁Sc to ₃₀Zn), 4d-series (₃₉Y to ₄₈Cd), 5d-series (₅₇La, ₇₂Hf to ₈₀Hg), 6d-series (incomplete/actinoid-linked).
31• **True vs Non-Typical Transition Elements:** A transition element is defined as an element having a partially filled d-orbital in its elemental ground state or in any of its common oxidation states. Zinc (₃₀Zn), Cadmium (₄₈Cd), and Mercury (₈₀Hg) have completely filled d-orbitals in elemental ground state (d¹⁰ s²) and in their +2 oxidation state (d¹⁰). Hence, they are non-typical or pseudo-transition elements.
32• **Variable Oxidation States:** Due to minimal energy difference between (n-1)d and ns electrons, both sets participate in bonding. Manganese (Mn) exhibits maximum oxidation states in 3d series from +2 to +7 (e.g., KMnO₄). Osmium (Os) and Ruthenium (Ru) reach +8 (OsO₄).
33• **Color of Transition Metal Ions:** Due to d-d electronic transitions. Degenerate d-orbitals split in presence of ligand fields into lower t₂g and upper eg levels. Absorption of visible light promotes electrons: Cu²⁺ (3d⁹, blue), Fe³⁺ (3d⁵, yellow/brown), Cr³⁺ (3d³, green), Ni²⁺ (3d⁸, green). Ions with d⁰ (Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺) or d¹⁰ (Zn²⁺, Cu⁺, Ag⁺) are colorless.
34• **Magnetic Properties:** Due to unpaired electrons. Magnetic moment calculated via spin-only formula: μ = √(n(n + 2)) Bohr Magnetons (BM), where n = number of unpaired electrons. Max in 3d is Fe³⁺ / Mn²⁺ (n = 5, μ = 5.92 BM).
35• **Catalytic Properties:** High surface area and ability to adopt multiple oxidation states to form unstable reaction intermediates (e.g., Finely divided Fe in Haber process, V₂O₅ in Contact process for H₂SO₄, Ni in alkene hydrogenation, Pt in Ostwald process).
36• **Interstitial Compounds & Alloys:** Small non-metal atoms (H, C, N, B) lodge into interstitial voids of metallic lattices (e.g., Steel, TiC, Fe₃H), imparting extreme hardness, high melting points, and chemical inertness.
37### 5. f-Block Elements (Inner Transition Elements)
38• **Definition:** Elements in which the differentiating electron enters the anti-penultimate (n-2)f subshell.
39• **General Valence Electronic Configuration:** (n-2)f¹⁻¹⁴ (n-1)d⁰⁻¹ ns² (where n = 6 for 4f Lanthanides, n = 7 for 5f Actinides).
40• **Lanthanide Series (4f):** 14 elements from Cerium (₅₈Ce) to Lutetium (₇₁Lu). Preceded by Lanthanum (₅₇La).
41• **Actinide Series (5f):** 14 elements from Thorium (₉₀Th) to Lawrencium (₁₀₃Lr). Preceded by Actinium (₈₉Ac).
42• **Oxidation States:** +3 is the universal, predominant, and most thermodynamically stable oxidation state for all Lanthanides. Some show +2 or +4 when achieving f⁰, f⁷, or f¹⁴ stable subshells (e.g., Ce⁴⁺ is 4f⁰, Eu²⁺ is 4f⁷, Yb²⁺ is 4f¹⁴). Actinides show wider range (+3, +4, +5, +6, +7) because 5f, 6d, and 7s have comparable energy levels.
43• **Lanthanide Contraction (Crucial NEB Exam Concept):** Steady and progressive decrease in atomic and ionic radii (Ln³⁺) from La to Lu. Cause: Highly diffuse shape and poor screening/shielding power of 4f electrons, allowing nuclear charge (Z) to pull the outer 6s shell inward.
44• **Consequences of Lanthanide Contraction:**
45 1. Identical radii of 4d and 5d pairs: Zr (160 pm) ≈ Hf (159 pm), Nb (146 pm) ≈ Ta (146 pm), Mo ≈ W. They occur together in nature and are extremely difficult to separate chemically.
46 2. Decrease in basic strength of hydroxides: As size shrinks from La³⁺ to Lu³⁺, covalent character of Ln–OH bond increases (Fajans' Rule). Thus, La(OH)₃ is the most basic, and Lu(OH)₃ is the least basic (most amphoteric).
47 3. Gradual increase in ionization energy, electronegativity, and electrode potential across the series.
48• **Actinides Character:** All are radioactive. Beyond Uranium (Z > 92) are synthetic man-made transuranic elements.
49### 6. Special High-Yield Classes of Elements in NEB Syllabus
50• **Coinage Metals (Group 11 / IB):** Copper (₂₉Cu), Silver (₄₇Ag), Gold (₇₉Au).
51 - Possess anomalous ground state configuration: (n-1)d¹⁰ ns¹ (extra stability of fully filled d-subshell).
52 - Chemically inert, high positive reduction potentials (Cu: +0.34 V, Ag: +0.80 V, Au: +1.50 V). Resist oxidation by atmospheric O₂ and non-oxidizing acids (HCl, dilute H₂SO₄).
53 - Dissolve in oxidizing acids: Cu + 4HNO₃ (conc) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O.
54 - Gold dissolves only in Aqua Regia (3 parts conc. HCl : 1 part conc. HNO₃): Au + HNO₃ + 4HCl → H[AuCl₄] + NO↑ + 2H₂O.
55 - Unmatched electrical and thermal conductivity: Ag > Cu > Au.
56 - Form stable complexes: [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ (Tollens' reagent for aldehyde detection), [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ (Schweizer's reagent for dissolving cellulose).
57• **Volatile Metals (Group 12 / IIB):** Zinc (₃₀Zn), Cadmium (₄₈Cd), Mercury (₈₀Hg).
58 - Electronic configuration: (n-1)d¹⁰ ns². Completely filled d-orbitals in both atomic and ionic (+2) states.
59 - Melting & Boiling points: Unusually low because paired electrons cannot participate in metallic lattice bonding. Hg is the only liquid metal at standard room temperature (MP: -38.8°C; BP: 356.7°C). Relativistic contraction pulls 6s electrons close to the nucleus, rendering them inert.
60 - Distinct from typical transition metals: Show only +2 oxidation state (never variable valency, except Hg which forms mercurous dimer [Hg–Hg]²⁺). Their compounds (ZnSO₄, CdCl₂, HgCl₂) are diamagnetic and white/colorless (no d-d transitions).
61 - Amalgams: Mercury forms liquid or solid alloys called amalgams with nearly all metals (e.g., Sodium amalgam Na-Hg, Zinc amalgam Zn-Hg used in Clemmensen reduction). Exceptions: Iron (Fe), Platinum (Pt), and Tungsten (W) do not form amalgams and are used as storage containers.
62### 7. Comprehensive Classification: Metals vs Non-Metals vs Metalloids
63• **Metals (~78% of elements):** Electropositive elements with low ionization enthalpies (1–3 valence electrons). Form giant metallic crystal lattices with delocalized electron gas. Form basic oxides (e.g., Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH). Good conductors, malleable, ductile, high sonority.
64• **Non-Metals (18 elements total):** Electronegative elements with high ionization enthalpies and electron affinities (4–7 valence electrons, plus noble gases). High electronegativity. Form covalent or molecular lattices. Form acidic oxides (SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄) or neutral oxides (CO, NO, N₂O). Electrical insulators (except graphite).
65• **Metalloids (Semimetals — B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, Po):** Borderline elements situated along the diagonal dividing line between metals and non-metals in the p-block.
66 - Possess intermediate electrical conductivity that increases with rising temperature (semiconductors, negative temperature coefficient of resistance).
67 - Form amphoteric or weakly acidic oxides (e.g., As₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AsCl₃ + 3H₂O; As₂O₃ + 6NaOH → 2Na₃AsO₃ + 3H₂O).
68 - Doping: Silicon and Germanium are doped with Group 13 elements (B, Ga) to create p-type semiconductors, or Group 15 elements (P, As) to create n-type semiconductors, forming p-n junctions for solar cells and microprocessors.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ All transition elements are d-block, and all d-block elements are transition elements. ✅ All transition elements belong to d-block, but Zn, Cd, and Hg are d-block elements that are NOT transition elements because their d-subshell is completely filled (d¹⁰) in ground state and common +2 oxidation state.
❌ Hydrogen is an alkali metal because it sits in Group 1. ✅ Hydrogen has an 1s¹ configuration but is a non-metal with high ionization enthalpy (1312 kJ/mol), forming covalent bonds and H⁻ hydride ions.
❌ Helium belongs to p-block because it sits in Group 18. ✅ Helium has the valence configuration 1s² (s-block), but is placed in Group 18 above p-block noble gases due to its completely filled valence shell and inert noble chemical properties.
❌ Fluorine has the highest electron gain enthalpy (electron affinity) in the periodic table. ✅ Chlorine (Cl) has higher negative electron gain enthalpy than Fluorine (-349 kJ/mol vs -328 kJ/mol) because Fluorine's 2p subshell is very small and compact, causing strong inter-electronic repulsions that hinder incoming electrons.
❌ Ionization enthalpy always increases monotonically across a period from left to right. ✅ Exceptions exist: Beryllium (1s² 2s²) has higher IE₁ than Boron (1s² 2s² 2p¹) because 2s electrons penetrate closer to the nucleus and are fully paired; Nitrogen (2s² 2p³) has higher IE₁ than Oxygen (2s² 2p⁴) due to the extra stability of half-filled p-orbitals.
❌ Lanthanum (₅₇La) and Actinium (₈₉Ac) are f-block elements. ✅ La and Ac are d-block elements (outer configuration 5d¹ 6s² and 6d¹ 7s²). The f-block begins with Cerium (₅₈Ce) and Thorium (₉₀Th).
❌ Lanthanide contraction means the atoms expand down the group. ✅ Contraction means atomic/ionic radii decrease steadily across the 4f series from Ce to Lu due to poor shielding by diffuse 4f orbitals.
❌ Copper, Silver, and Gold are non-transition metals because their ground state is d¹⁰. ✅ They are transition metals because their common oxidation states have partially filled d-orbitals: Cu²⁺ is 3d⁹, Au³⁺ is 5d⁸.
❌ Graphite is an insulator like other non-metals. ✅ Graphite conducts electricity along its hexagonal sheets due to free delocalized π-electrons resulting from sp² hybridization.
❌ All oxides of metals are basic. ✅ Most are basic, but metal oxides with high oxidation states are acidic (e.g., CrO₃ with Cr⁶⁺, Mn₂O₇ with Mn⁷⁺), and some are amphoteric (Al₂O₃, ZnO, PbO, SnO).
❌ Coinage metals include Zinc, Cadmium, and Mercury. ✅ Coinage metals are strictly Group 11 (Cu, Ag, Au). Group 12 (Zn, Cd, Hg) are Volatile Metals.
❌ Mercury dissolves all metals to form amalgams. ✅ Mercury does NOT amalgamate with Iron (Fe), Platinum (Pt), or Tungsten (W); therefore, mercury is safely shipped and stored in iron flasks.

IUPAC Classification of Elements

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning — what IUPAC is and what it decides
2IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) is the world authority that standardises chemical nomenclature, atomic weights and the format of the periodic table. Its classification of elements covers group numbering 1–18, the s/p/d/f block division, the metal–nonmetal–metalloid grouping, collective group names, and the naming of newly discovered elements.
3### 2. IUPAC group numbering and collective names
4• 18 groups numbered left to right and 7 periods; the old European (IA, IIA, … IB) and American (IA, IB, … VIIIA) systems were replaced by 1–18 to remove ambiguity.
5• Collective names: Group 1 alkali metals (Li–Fr, H excluded), Group 2 alkaline earth metals (Be–Ra), Group 15 pnictogens (N–Bi), Group 16 chalcogens (O–Po), Group 17 halogens (F–At), Group 18 noble gases (He–Rn).
6• Blocks: s-block (Groups 1–2 plus He), p-block (Groups 13–18 except He), d-block (Groups 3–12, transition metals), f-block (lanthanides 58–71 and actinides 90–103, inner transition metals).
7### 3. IUPAC naming of new elements (Z > 100)
8Discoverers propose a name and a two-letter symbol after independent verification. Until approval, a systematic name is built from digit roots — 0 nil, 1 un, 2 bi, 3 tri, 4 quad, 5 pent, 6 hex, 7 sept, 8 oct, 9 enn — joined in order with the suffix '-ium' (temporary three-letter symbols). Examples: 104 unnilquadium (Unq) → Rutherfordium (Rf); 113 ununtrium → Nihonium (Nh); 114 ununquadium → Flerovium (Fl); 115 → Moscovium (Mc); 116 ununhexium → Livermorium (Lv); 117 ununseptium → Tennessine (Ts); 118 ununoctium (Uuo) → Oganesson (Og).
9### 4. Classification by physical and chemical character
10• **Metals:** the majority (s-, d-, f-blocks and lower p-block) — lustre, malleability, conductivity, basic oxides.
11• **Non-metals:** upper right of the p-block (C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Se, Br, I, noble gases) — acidic oxides, poor conductors.
12• **Metalloids (semi-metals):** B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te (sometimes Po) — intermediate properties; the basis of semiconductors.
13• **Representative (typical) elements:** s- and p-blocks; **transition elements:** d-block (variable oxidation states, coloured ions, catalysts); **inner transition:** f-block.
14### 5. Applications
15• One agreed numbering and block scheme makes international communication and literature searching unambiguous.
16• Predicting chemistry from group position: Group 1 metals are strong reducing agents, Group 17 halogens strong oxidising agents, Group 18 inert; oxide character changes from basic to acidic across a period (Na₂O, MgO basic → Al₂O₃ amphoteric → SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₃ acidic).
17• Standard atomic weights (from terrestrial isotopic abundance) and systematic naming keep trade, industry and metrology consistent — for example in specifying semiconductor-grade silicon or alloy composition.
18• The naming and verification procedure for superheavy elements is a direct product of this classification work.
19### 6. Limitations
20IUPAC classification is a labelling and standardisation scheme, not an explanation — it does not say *why* properties repeat (that needs Z_eff, quantum numbers and electronic configuration). Some boundaries are arbitrary: the metal–non-metal change is gradual and metalloid membership differs between sources; H sits in Group 1 yet resembles the halogens; He is a Group 18 noble gas with the s-block configuration 1s². The composition of Group 3 for periods 6 and 7 (La/Ac versus Lu/Lr) has been debated for decades, and the '-ium' systematic names are stopgaps, so older books still print them. The chemistry of elements with Z > 103 is largely predicted, not measured.
21**Fact.** v = V₀ sin ωt; i = I₀ sin(ωt + φ); RMS V_rms = V₀/√2.
22**Fact.** Impedance: pure R → Z = R; L → Z = ωL; C → Z = 1/(ωC); LCR resonance at ω₀ = 1/√(LC).
23**Fact.** Average power P = V_rms I_rms cosφ; in pure L or C, average power over a cycle = 0.

Nuclear charge and effective nuclear charge

#Concept
1**Nuclear charge.** The total positive charge of the nucleus is Ze, where Z is the atomic number and e the elementary charge; it is the nucleus's pull on the electron cloud.
2**Shielding effect.** Inner-shell electrons repel outer electrons and cancel part of the nuclear pull; this screening is greatest in multi-electron atoms.
3**Effective nuclear charge.** Zeff = Z − σ, where σ is the shielding constant; outer electrons feel this reduced attraction rather than the full nuclear charge.
4**Trends across a period.** Electrons enter the same shell while Z rises, so shielding changes little and Zeff increases — atomic radius falls and ionisation enthalpy rises.
5**Trends down a group.** Each new shell adds shielding that nearly cancels the added nuclear charge, so radius grows and ionisation enthalpy falls down the group.

Periodic Trend and Periodicity: Radii, Ionization Energy, Electron Affinity, Electronegativity, Metallic Character

#Concept
1### 1. Meaning of periodicity
2Periodicity is the recurrence of similar physical and chemical properties at regular intervals when elements are arranged in order of atomic number. Its cause is the periodic repetition of the *valence-shell electronic configuration*: trends across a period follow from increasing Z_eff, trends down a group from increasing n and increasing shielding.
3### 2. Atomic radii
4Radius is measured as covalent radius (half the bond length in a homonuclear molecule), metallic radius (half the distance between neighbouring metal atoms) or van der Waals radius (from non-bonded contacts — the largest). **Across a period the radius decreases** (Li 152 → F 64 pm) because nuclear charge rises faster than shielding; **down a group it increases** (Li → Cs) because a new shell is added. Period 4 shows a normal contraction after the d-block, and the 5d series follows the 4d series closely — the lanthanide contraction (poor 4f shielding).
5### 3. Ionic radii
6• A cation is smaller than its parent atom (Na 186 pm → Na⁺ 95 pm); an anion is larger (Cl 99 pm → Cl⁻ 181 pm) because added electrons increase repulsion while the nuclear charge is unchanged.
7• Down a group the ionic radius increases for the same charge (Li⁺ < Na⁺ < K⁺ < Rb⁺ < Cs⁺); across a period ions of the same charge get smaller.
8• **Isoelectronic series** (same number of electrons): radius decreases as nuclear charge increases, N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺; for one element Fe³⁺ < Fe²⁺.
9### 4. Ionisation energy (IE)
10The minimum energy needed to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom; measured in kJ mol⁻¹ or eV per atom (H: 1312 kJ mol⁻¹ = 13.6 eV). **It increases across a period** (Li 520 → Ne 2081 kJ mol⁻¹) because Z_eff rises and size falls, and **decreases down a group** as size increases. Anomalies: Be > B and N > O (2s² stability; 2p pairing repulsion in oxygen), repeated as Mg > Al and P > S. Successive IEs always increase (IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃), and a large jump marks the end of a shell — Na IE₁ = 496, IE₂ = 4562 kJ mol⁻¹.
11### 5. Electron affinity (EA)
12The energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron, X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g); EA is often reported as the energy *released*, so state the sign convention in answers. It generally becomes more exothermic **across a period** and less so **down a group** as size and shielding increase. Chlorine has a higher EA than fluorine (349 vs 328 kJ mol⁻¹) because F's very small 2p shell causes extra electron–electron repulsion. Noble gases have ~zero or positive EA, and Be and N have very small values because of filled/half-filled stability.
13### 6. Electronegativity (EN)
14The tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract the shared electron pair. Pauling's scale takes F = 4.0 (highest), O = 3.5, N = 3.0, H = 2.1, Cs ≈ 0.7; the Mulliken scale uses (IE + EA)/2. **EN increases across a period and decreases down a group**; noble gases are assigned little or no value. Bond polarity follows from ΔEN: > 1.7 usually ionic, < 0.5 essentially non-polar covalent (HF, ΔEN = 1.9, is polar covalent).
15### 7. Metallic and non-metallic character
16Metallic character **decreases across a period** (Na → Cl) and **increases down a group** (Li → Cs): metals lose electrons easily (low IE), non-metals gain them (high IE, high EA/EN). Consequences: oxide character moves from basic → amphoteric → acidic across a period, and the diagonal pairs (Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si) show metallic character reappearing diagonally.
17### 8. Applications
18• Reactivity predictions: alkali metals become more reactive down the group (low IE); halogen oxidising power falls from F to I (high EA/EN mainly for F and Cl).
19• Explains the acidic/basic character of oxides, the amphoteric behaviour of Al₂O₃ and the change from ionic NaCl to covalent AlCl₃ across period 3.
20• Used in materials selection (semiconductors Si, Ge), in predicting bond polarity and dipole moments, and in understanding ion sizes for crystal packing and complex formation.
21### 9. Limitations of the trends
22All the trends are *general*, not exact: the Be/B, N/O, Mg/Al and P/S breaks come from penetration and pairing, and the d- and f-blocks show many irregularities (Cr, Cu, lanthanide contraction, poor 4f shielding, relativistic effects in heavy elements). Radii are not directly measurable and covalent, metallic and van der Waals values differ by 0.5–1 Å, so only one consistent type may be compared. Electron affinity depends on the stated convention, and electronegativity values differ between scales (Pauling, Mulliken, Allred–Rochow), with noble-gas values ill-defined. Above all, trends are qualitative — quantitative prediction needs quantum calculations or Z_eff with accurate shielding.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ Atomic radius increases across a period. ✅ It decreases because Z_eff rises faster than shielding.
❌ An anion is smaller than its parent atom. ✅ It is larger — added electrons increase repulsion while nuclear charge stays the same.
❌ IE increases smoothly across a period. ✅ Be > B and N > O (also Mg > Al, P > S) are standard breaks from 2s² stability and 2p pairing.
❌ Fluorine has the highest electron affinity. ✅ Chlorine does (349 vs 328 kJ mol⁻¹) because of extra repulsion in F's tiny 2p shell.
❌ Electronegativity is directly measurable. ✅ It is a derived, scale-dependent parameter (Pauling, Mulliken, Allred–Rochow).

Established academic fact

Atomic number defines an element; isotopes differ in neutron number. Periodic properties show useful trends, but subshell energies and electron pairing explain exceptions, so a trend is a guide rather than a law without exceptions.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

Lewis structures and the octet rule are electron-accounting models, especially useful for many second-period main-group compounds. Odd-electron, electron-deficient and hypervalent species show why the octet rule is not universal.

Open research question — Predicting the chemistry of superheavy elements

The shortest-lived superheavy atoms are produced one at a time, making chemical measurements exceptionally difficult. Relativistic effects may alter expected periodic trends; experiments and calculations continue to test how far familiar patterns extend.
