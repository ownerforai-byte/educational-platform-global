Ideal Gas Equation

#Concept
1The ideal-gas equation combines Boyle’s, Charles’s and Avogadro’s laws: PV=nRT. P is absolute pressure, V volume, n amount in moles, T absolute temperature and R=8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
2Always convert Celsius using T(K)=t(°C)+273.15 and use consistent units. For 1.00 mol at 300 K in 24.6 L, P≈(8.314×300)/0.0246=1.01×10⁵ Pa.
3For fixed amount n, PV/T stays constant between equilibrium states: P1V1/T1=P2V2/T2. In a sealed rigid vessel heated from 300 K to 450 K, pressure rises by the factor 450/300=1.5.
4The model assumes molecules have negligible volume and exert no long-range intermolecular forces except during elastic collisions. Real gases approach ideal behaviour at low pressure and high temperature.
5Use absolute rather than gauge pressure in the equation. If a gauge pressure is given, add atmospheric pressure before substituting.

Key Points

Point
Use absolute pressure and kelvin temperature.
1 L=10⁻³ m³; maintain consistent units.
The combined gas law assumes the amount of gas is fixed.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Never substitute Celsius into PV=nRT.
Gauge pressure is not absolute pressure.

Summary: PV=nRT relates equilibrium gas variables when temperature and units are handled consistently. For fixed amount, PV/T is constant; real gases deviate near condensation and high pressure.

Kinetic Molecular Model of an Ideal Gas

#Concept
1Basic Postulates: (1) Gas consists of large number of identical molecules in random motion. (2) Molecular size ≪ mean separation (point masses). (3) Collisions with walls and between molecules are perfectly elastic. (4) No intermolecular forces except during brief collisions. (5) Time of collision ≪ time between collisions. (6) Newton's laws apply to each molecule.
2Origin of Pressure: Pressure arises from momentum transfer when molecules collide with container walls. Each collision imparts impulse Δp = 2mv_x (for wall perpendicular to x-axis). Summing over all molecules and averaging gives P = (1/3)(N/V)m⟨v²⟩ = (1/3)ρv²_rms.
3Root Mean Square Speed: v_rms = √(⟨v²⟩) = √(3RT/M) = √(3kT/m), where M is molar mass, m is molecular mass, k is Boltzmann constant. At same T, lighter molecules move faster.
4Connection to Temperature: Average translational KE per molecule = (3/2)kT. Total translational KE of N molecules = (3/2)NkT = (3/2)nRT. Temperature is a measure of average molecular kinetic energy.
5Ideal Gas Law Derivation: From P = (1/3)(N/V)m⟨v²⟩ and ⟨KE⟩ = (1/2)m⟨v²⟩ = (3/2)kT, we get PV = NkT = nRT — the ideal gas equation derived from microscopic mechanics.
6Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution: Molecular speeds follow a statistical distribution f(v) = 4π(m/2πkT)^(3/2) v² exp(-mv²/2kT). Most probable speed v_mp = √(2kT/m), average speed v_avg = √(8kT/πm), rms speed v_rms = √(3kT/m). Ratio v_mp : v_avg : v_rms ≈ 1 : 1.13 : 1.22.
7Degrees of Freedom: Monatomic gas has 3 translational DOF → C_v = (3/2)R. Diatomic adds 2 rotational DOF at room T → C_v = (5/2)R. Vibrational modes activate at high T.
8Mean Free Path: Average distance between collisions λ = 1/(√2 πd² n), where d is molecular diameter and n = N/V number density. At STP, λ ≈ 68 nm for air.
9Limitations of Model: Assumes point particles and no interactions — fails at high pressure (finite volume matters) and low temperature (intermolecular attraction matters). Real gases require van der Waals corrections.

Key Points

Point
Gas pressure results from molecular collisions with container walls.
Temperature measures average translational kinetic energy: ⟨KE⟩ = (3/2)kT.
v_rms = √(3RT/M): lighter gases move faster at same temperature.
PV = nRT derives directly from kinetic theory postulates.
Speed distribution follows Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics.
Model valid for dilute gases; real gases need corrections at high P or low T.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Thinking temperature measures total energy: It measures AVERAGE translational KE per molecule, not total internal energy.
Confusing v_rms with average speed: v_rms = √(⟨v²⟩) ≠ ⟨v⟩. They differ by ~8% for Maxwell distribution.
Believing molecules stop at 0°C: 0°C = 273 K; molecules still have substantial KE. Only at 0 K would classical motion cease.
Assuming all molecules move at same speed: Speeds follow a broad distribution; individual molecules constantly change speed via collisions.
Thinking pressure comes from molecular weight: Pressure arises from momentum transfer in collisions, not gravitational force on molecules.

Summary: The kinetic molecular model explains gas behavior through random motion of molecules. Key assumptions: point particles, elastic collisions, no intermolecular forces except during collision, negligible volume compared to container. Derives pressure P = (1/3)ρv²_rms and connects macroscopic properties (P, V, T) to microscopic molecular motion.

Molecular Properties of Matter

#Concept
1**Kinetic theory of matter:** All matter is composed of tiny particles (atoms or molecules) that are in constant, random motion. The motion is thermal motion - faster at higher temperatures.
2**States of matter and molecular spacing:** In gases, intermolecular spacing is much larger than molecular diameter (10Ã— or more), so molecules move freely. In liquids, spacing is about 1 molecular diameter. In solids, molecules are tightly packed in a regular arrangement.
3**Intermolecular forces:** Attractive forces between molecules (van der Waals, hydrogen bonding, dipole-dipole). These forces are strongest in solids (holding molecules in fixed positions), moderate in liquids, and very weak in gases at ordinary conditions.
4**Kinetic energy vs potential energy in matter:** In gases, kinetic energy dominates (molecules move freely). In solids, potential energy dominates (molecules held in fixed positions by strong intermolecular forces). At the melting/boiling point, energy input goes into breaking bonds (increasing PE) without changing temperature.
5**Brownian motion:** Random zigzag motion of microscopic particles suspended in a fluid, first observed by Robert Brown (1827). Explained by Einstein (1905) as the result of unequal bombardment by fluid molecules - strong evidence for the kinetic theory.
6**Diffusion:** The spontaneous mixing of particles due to random molecular motion. Occurs in all states but fastest in gases and slowest in solids. Fick's law: diffusion rate $\propto$ concentration gradient. At higher temperature, diffusion is faster (faster molecules).
7**Thermal expansion:** Matter expands when heated (generally). In gases, expansion is large (Charles's law). In liquids, expansion is moderate. In solids, expansion is small but measurable. Due to increased molecular vibration amplitudes at higher temperatures.
8**Mean free path:** The average distance a molecule travels between collisions. $\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} n \sigma}$, where $n$ is the number density and $\sigma$ is the collision cross-section. At STP, the mean free path of air molecules is about 68 nm.
9**Degrees of freedom:** The number of independent ways a molecule can store energy. Monatomic gas: 3 translational DOF. Diatomic: 5 (3 translational + 2 rotational). Polyatomic: 6 (3 translational + 3 rotational) at room temperature.
10**Equipartition theorem:** Each quadratic degree of freedom contributes $\frac{1}{2}k_BT$ of energy per molecule, or $\frac{1}{2}RT$ per mole. For monatomic gas ($f=3$): $U = \frac{3}{2}nRT$. For diatomic ($f=5$): $U = \frac{5}{2}nRT$.
11**Phase transitions and energy:** During melting or boiling, temperature stays constant even though heat is added. The energy goes into breaking intermolecular bonds (increasing PE), not increasing KE. This is latent heat: $Q = mL$ where $L$ is latent heat.
12**Surface tension:** A consequence of intermolecular attractive forces. Molecules at the surface experience a net inward pull, creating a 'skin' effect. Surface tension $\gamma$ has units of N/m or J/m$^2$. Water has high surface tension (72 mN/m at 25Â°C) due to hydrogen bonding.
13**Viscosity:** A measure of a fluid's resistance to flow. Caused by intermolecular forces and molecular momentum transfer. Gases become more viscous at higher temperature (faster molecules, more momentum transfer). Liquids become less viscous at higher temperature (bonds break more easily).

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of molecules, NOT the total energy. A hot object has higher average KE per molecule, but a large cold object may have more total thermal energy (more molecules).
Heat and temperature are NOT the same. Temperature is a measure of average molecular KE (intensive property). Heat is the transfer of thermal energy between objects due to temperature difference (extensive property). You can add heat without changing temperature (phase change).
Intermolecular forces are NOT the same as intramolecular (chemical) forces. Intermolecular forces hold molecules together in condensed phases (weak, ~1-100 kJ/mol). Intramolecular forces hold atoms together within a molecule (strong, ~100-1000 kJ/mol).
Diffusion in liquids is faster than in solids, but much slower than in gases. This is often confused with the rate of dissolving, which also involves convection and stirring.
A gas molecule at high altitude has LESS kinetic energy if it has risen (energy converts to gravitational PE). But temperature is a statistical property - individual molecules have a range of speeds given by the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, not a single speed.

Boltzmann Constant and RMS Speed

#Concept
1Boltzmann Constant Definition: k = R/N_A where R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Value: k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K (exact since 2019 SI redefinition). It converts temperature (kelvin) into energy (joules) per particle.
2Physical Significance of k: Average translational KE per molecule = (3/2)kT. At 300 K, ⟨KE⟩ = (3/2)(1.38×10⁻²³)(300) ≈ 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J ≈ 0.039 eV. This is the thermal energy scale governing molecular motion.
3RMS Speed Formula: v_rms = √(⟨v²⟩) = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M), where m = molecular mass, M = molar mass. Derived from equating pressure expression P = (1/3)ρv²_rms with ideal gas law PV = nRT.
4Speed Hierarchy: For Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution: v_mp = √(2kT/m) < v_avg = √(8kT/πm) < v_rms = √(3kT/m). Numerical ratio: v_mp : v_avg : v_rms ≈ 1.41 : 1.60 : 1.73 (or 1 : 1.13 : 1.22 normalized to v_mp).
5Temperature Dependence: v_rms ∝ √T. Doubling absolute temperature increases v_rms by factor √2 ≈ 1.414. Cooling to 1/4 original T halves the rms speed.
6Mass Dependence: v_rms ∝ 1/√M. At same T, hydrogen (M=2 g/mol) moves √(32/2) = 4 times faster than oxygen (M=32 g/mol). This explains why light gases escape planetary atmospheres more readily.
7Typical Values at 300 K: H₂: 1920 m/s; He: 1370 m/s; N₂: 517 m/s; O₂: 483 m/s; CO₂: 412 m/s. All supersonic yet no bulk flow due to random directions.
8Connection to Kinetic Energy: Total translational KE of n moles = (3/2)nRT = (3/2)NkT. Internal energy of monatomic ideal gas U = (3/2)nRT depends only on T, not V or P.
9Effusion and Graham's Law: Rate of effusion ∝ v_rms ∝ 1/√M. Lighter gases effuse faster through small openings. Used in uranium enrichment (²³⁵UF₆ vs ²³⁸UF₆).

Key Points

Point
k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K bridges macroscopic T and microscopic energy.
v_rms = √(3RT/M): depends on T and M, not on P or V directly.
At same T, all gases have same ⟨KE⟩ but different v_rms.
v_rms > v_avg > v_mp always for Maxwellian distribution.
Lighter molecules move faster: v_rms ∝ 1/√M.
Graham's law of effusion follows directly from v_rms ∝ 1/√M.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing v_rms with average speed: v_rms = √(⟨v²⟩) ≠ ⟨v⟩. They differ by ~8% for Maxwellian gases.
Using M in g/mol instead of kg/mol: Must convert to SI (kg/mol) for v_rms in m/s.
Thinking heavier gases have higher KE at same T: All gases at same T have identical ⟨KE⟩ = (3/2)kT regardless of mass.
Believing v_rms depends on pressure: v_rms depends only on T and M for ideal gases; P affects density, not individual molecular speeds.
Assuming all molecules move at v_rms: Individual speeds vary widely; v_rms is a statistical measure of the distribution.

Summary: The Boltzmann constant k = R/N_A = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K links macroscopic temperature to microscopic molecular energy. RMS speed v_rms = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M) characterizes the typical molecular speed in a gas at temperature T. At the same temperature, lighter molecules move faster; v_rms determines diffusion rates, effusion, and kinetic energy.

Kinetic-Molecular Model of an Ideal Gas

#Concept
1**Postulates (Four fundamental assumptions):**
21. A gas consists of a large number of identical, point-like molecules with negligible volume (point particles).
32. The molecules are in constant, random motion. No two molecules occupy the same point in space.
43. There are no intermolecular forces between molecules except during perfectly elastic collisions.
54. Collisions between molecules (and with container walls) are perfectly elastic and take negligible time compared to the time between collisions.
6**Why the model works:** The kinetic theory explains gas laws (Boyle's, Charles's, Gay-Lussac's) and pressure. It connects macroscopic properties (P, V, T) to microscopic behavior (molecular speed, collisions).
7**Pressure origin:** Pressure arises from molecules colliding with container walls. Each collision delivers a tiny impulse (change in momentum). The average of many collisions over time creates a constant force on the wall, resulting in pressure.
8**Temperature and kinetic energy:** Temperature is a direct measure of the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules. The average translational kinetic energy per molecule is $\langle K \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_BT$, where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant.
9**Absolute temperature scale:** The Kelvin scale is an absolute temperature scale where $T = 0$ K means zero thermal motion (all molecular motion ceases). This is a fundamental limit of thermodynamics.
10**Why temperature cannot be negative:** Kinetic energy is always non-negative ($\frac{1}{2}mv^2 \geq 0$), so $\langle K \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_BT \geq 0$. Since $k_B > 0$, this requires $T \geq 0$. Negative temperature is not possible in the kinetic theory.
11**Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution:** The distribution of molecular speeds in an ideal gas is a specific probability distribution. It shows that most molecules have speeds near the average, with fewer at very low or very high speeds. The shape is asymmetric with a long tail to high speeds.
12**Degrees of freedom:** The number of independent ways a molecule can store energy. Monatomic gas: 3 translational DOF. Diatomic: 3 translational + 2 rotational DOF at room temperature (vibrational DOF not active). Polyatomic: more DOF including vibration.
13**Internal energy:** For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature and is given by $U = \frac{f}{2}nRT$, where $f$ is the number of degrees of freedom. Monatomic: $f=3$, so $U = \frac{3}{2}nRT$.
14**Degrees of freedom and heat capacity:** $C_V = \frac{f}{2}R$ for molar heat capacity at constant volume. Monatomic gas: $C_V = \frac{3}{2}R$. Diatomic gas: $C_V = \frac{5}{2}R$ (at room temperature).
15**Elastic collision definition:** A collision where both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. In an elastic collision between two molecules, the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation: $|\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2|_{\text{after}} = |\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2|_{\text{before}}$.
16**Collision frequency:** The average number of collisions per unit time per molecule. Increases with higher density and higher temperature (faster molecules collide more often).
17**Real gas approximation:** The kinetic theory works well for gases at low pressure and high temperature where intermolecular forces are negligible and molecular volume is small compared to container volume. Real gases deviate at high pressure/low temperature.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Temperature is a measure of AVERAGE kinetic energy, not the kinetic energy of any single molecule. A hot object has higher average KE per molecule, but individual molecules have a range of speeds.
The kinetic theory assumes perfectly elastic collisions. In reality, some kinetic energy is lost to internal excitation (vibration, rotation) or radiation, especially in gases with complex molecules.
Temperature can be defined for any system in thermal equilibrium, but the kinetic energy definition $\langle K \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_BT$ applies specifically to translational kinetic energy in ideal gases. Rotational and vibrational energy also contribute to temperature.
The mean free path is inversely proportional to number density (n) and collision cross-section (σ). At higher pressure (lower volume), molecules are closer together and collide more frequently, reducing the mean free path.
The mean free path decreases with increasing temperature because molecules move faster and cover more distance between collisions, even though number density may change. $\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}n\sigma}$, and $n \propto P/T$.

Summary: Kinetic-molecular model of an ideal gas is a fundamental concept in Science that covers the essential principles, formulas, and applications. Understanding this topic is crucial for solving related problems and building foundation for advanced topics.

Derivation of Pressure Exerted by Gas

#Concept
1**Setup:** Consider a cubical container with side length $L$ containing $N$ molecules of mass $m$. The volume is $V = L^3$.
2**Momentum change in one collision:** When a molecule with x-component of velocity $v_x$ hits the wall perpendicular to the x-axis, it rebounds with velocity $-v_x$. The change in momentum is $\Delta p = p_f - p_i = m(-v_x) - m(v_x) = -2mv_x$. The magnitude is $2mv_x$.
3**Time between collisions:** The molecule travels a distance $2L$ (to the opposite wall and back) at speed $|v_x|$. Time between collisions with the same wall is $\Delta t = 2L/|v_x|$.
4**Force from one molecule:** $F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{2mv_x}{2L/|v_x|} = \frac{2mv_x}{|v_x|} = \frac{mv_x^2}{L}$. (Note: $|v_x| = v_x$ if $v_x > 0$, but we use magnitude for force magnitude).
5**Pressure contribution:** Force per unit area is $P = F/A = \frac{mv_x^2}{L} / L^2 = \frac{mv_x^2}{L^3} = \frac{mv_x^2}{V}$. This is the pressure contribution from a single molecule with x-velocity $v_x$.
6**Averaging over all molecules:** The total force is the sum of forces from all molecules. Since the wall is perpendicular to x-axis, only $v_x$ components matter. Total pressure $P = \frac{1}{A} \sum F_i = \frac{1}{L^2} \sum (mv_{ix}^2/L) = \frac{m}{L^3} \sum v_{ix}^2 = \frac{m}{V} \langle v_x^2 \rangle$.
7**Relating to total kinetic energy:** The total kinetic energy is $K = \frac{1}{2}m \sum v_i^2 = \frac{1}{2}mN\langle v^2 \rangle$. Since $\langle v^2 \rangle = \langle v_x^2 \rangle + \langle v_y^2 \rangle + \langle v_z^2 \rangle$ and by symmetry $\langle v_x^2 \rangle = \langle v_y^2 \rangle = \langle v_z^2 \rangle$, we have $\langle v_x^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3}\langle v^2 \rangle$.
8**Final result:** Substituting back, $P = \frac{m}{V} \cdot \frac{1}{3}\langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3} \frac{mN}{V} \langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} (\frac{1}{2}m\langle v^2 \rangle) = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} \langle K \rangle$. Since $\langle K \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_BT$, we get $P = \frac{N}{V} k_BT = \frac{n}{V} RT = \frac{nRT}{V}$.
9**Pressure in terms of average KE:** $P = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} \langle K \rangle$. This shows the direct link between pressure and microscopic kinetic energy. The factor 2/3 comes from the 3 spatial dimensions (1/3 per dimension).
10**Derivation steps recap:**
111. Momentum change: $\Delta p = 2mv_x$
122. Time between collisions: $\Delta t = 2L/|v_x|$
133. Force on wall: $F = \frac{2mv_x^2}{L}$
144. Pressure: $P = F/A = \frac{mv_x^2}{L^3} = \frac{mv_x^2}{V}$
155. Average over all molecules: $P = \frac{m}{V} \langle v_x^2 \rangle$
166. Relate to total KE: $\langle v_x^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3}\langle v^2 \rangle$, so $P = \frac{m}{3V} \langle v^2 \rangle$
177. Substitute $\langle K \rangle = \frac{1}{2}m\langle v^2 \rangle$: $P = \frac{2}{3} \frac{N}{V} \langle K \rangle$

Key Points

Point
The ⅓ comes from averaging the velocity components along three axes, not from any property of the container.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
The derivation assumes the wall is perpendicular to the x-axis. For a wall at an angle, the momentum change and force calculation would be different.
The factor 1/3 comes from the three spatial dimensions. In 2D, it would be 1/2, and in n-dimensions, it would be 1/n. Students often forget this geometric factor.
The derivation assumes the wall is stationary. If the wall were moving (e.g., piston), the relative velocity would need to be used, and the derivation would be more complex.
The derivation applies to ideal gases where collisions are perfectly elastic. In real gases with inelastic collisions, energy is lost and the derivation needs modification.

Summary: Derivation of pressure exerted by gas is a fundamental concept in Science that covers the essential principles, formulas, and applications. Understanding this topic is crucial for solving related problems and building foundation for advanced topics.

Heat Capacities of Gases

#Concept
1Specific Heat Definition: Amount of heat required to raise temperature of unit mass by 1 K. For gases, value depends on process conditions because gases expand significantly when heated.
2C_v (Constant Volume): Heat added increases only internal energy (no work done since dV = 0). Q = nC_vΔT = ΔU. For monatomic ideal gas: C_v = (3/2)R. Diatomic at room T: C_v = (5/2)R.
3C_p (Constant Pressure): Heat added increases internal energy AND does expansion work against external pressure. Q = nC_pΔT = ΔU + PΔV. Always C_p > C_v because extra energy goes into work.
4Mayer's Relation: C_p − C_v = R for one mole of ideal gas. Derived from H = U + PV and ideal gas law. Per unit mass: c_p − c_v = R/M where M is molar mass.
5Adiabatic Index γ: γ = C_p/C_v. Monatomic: γ = 5/3 ≈ 1.67. Diatomic (room T): γ = 7/5 = 1.40. Polyatomic: γ ≈ 1.33. Determines adiabatic process behavior: PV^γ = constant.
6Degrees of Freedom f: Each quadratic DOF contributes (1/2)R to C_v per mole. Monatomic: f = 3 (translational) → C_v = 3R/2. Diatomic: f = 5 (3 trans + 2 rot) → C_v = 5R/2. Vibrational modes add R each (kinetic + potential) but activate only at high T.
7Temperature Dependence: At very low T, rotational/vibrational modes freeze out (quantum effect). C_v decreases below classical prediction. At high T, vibrational modes activate, increasing C_v toward equipartition limit.
8Experimental Measurement: C_v measured in bomb calorimeter (fixed volume). C_p measured in flow calorimeter or derived from C_v + R. Adiabatic expansion methods (Clement-Desormes) determine γ directly.
9Applications: γ determines speed of sound v = √(γRT/M), adiabatic lapse rate in atmosphere, efficiency of Carnot and Otto cycles, and shock wave properties.

Key Points

Point
C_p > C_v because constant-pressure heating includes expansion work.
Mayer's relation: C_p − C_v = R for ideal gases.
γ = C_p/C_v determines adiabatic behavior and sound speed.
Degrees of freedom dictate heat capacity values via equipartition.
Monatomic γ = 5/3; diatomic γ = 7/5 at ordinary temperatures.
Vibrational modes increase C_v at high temperatures.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Thinking C_p and C_v are material constants independent of process: They are defined FOR specific processes (constant P or V).
Using R without checking molar vs mass basis: Molar uses R = 8.314; specific uses R/M.
Assuming γ is always 1.4: Only true for diatomic gases near room temperature. Monatomic and polyatomic differ.
Believing vibrational modes always contribute: They activate only above characteristic temperatures (e.g., ~2000 K for N₂).
Confusing heat capacity with thermal conductivity: Heat capacity stores energy; conductivity transfers it. Different physical quantities.

Summary: Gases have two principal specific heats: C_v at constant volume and C_p at constant pressure. For ideal gases, C_p − C_v = R (Mayer's relation). The ratio γ = C_p/C_v depends on molecular degrees of freedom: monatomic γ = 5/3, diatomic γ = 7/5. These values govern adiabatic processes, sound speed, and thermodynamic cycles.

Average Translational Kinetic Energy of Gas Molecule

#Concept
1Kinetic theory relates pressure to molecular motion: pV=(1/3)Nm⟨c²⟩. Combining this with pV=NkBT gives (1/2)m⟨c²⟩=(3/2)kBT, the average translational kinetic energy per molecule.
2At fixed absolute temperature, different ideal-gas molecules have equal mean translational kinetic energy. Their speeds differ because a lighter molecule needs greater speed to have the same kinetic energy.
3Per mole, the mean translational kinetic energy is (3/2)RT. At 300 K this is about (3/2)(8.314)(300)=3.74 kJ mol⁻¹, or 6.21×10⁻²¹ J per molecule.
4The result is for translation only. In a monatomic ideal gas it accounts for all classical internal energy; diatomic and polyatomic gases can also store energy in rotation and vibration.
5The root-mean-square speed follows from the same result: vrms=√(3kBT/m)=√(3RT/M). Use molecular mass with kB or molar mass with R.

Key Points

Point
Kelvin temperature sets mean translational kinetic energy.
Use kB per molecule and R per mole.
Molecular mass determines speeds, not mean energy at fixed T.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not confuse kB per molecule with R per mole.
Polyatomic-gas internal energy may include rotational and vibrational modes.

Summary: Kinetic theory gives ⟨Etrans⟩=(3/2)kBT per molecule. Temperature fixes mean translational energy; molecular mass determines the associated speed distribution.

Boltzmann Constant, Root Mean Square Speed and Heat Capacities

#Concept
1**Boltzmann constant:** $k_B = R/N_A = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K. It connects microscopic and macroscopic thermodynamics. Named after Ludwig Boltzmann (1844-1906).
2**RMS speed:** $v_{rms} = \sqrt{3RT/M}$, where $M$ is molar mass in kg/mol. At STP (273 K), $v_{rms}$ for air is about 479 m/s. At 300 K, about 484 m/s. Lighter gases move faster on average.
3**Most probable speed:** $v_p = \sqrt{2RT/M}$. This is the speed at which the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution peaks. At 300 K for air: $v_p \approx 364$ m/s. At 300 K for Hâ‚‚: $v_p \approx 1650$ m/s.
4**Mean speed:** $v_{mean} = \sqrt{8RT/\pi M}$. The average of all molecular speeds. At 300 K for air: $v_{mean} \approx 464$ m/s. At 300 K for Hâ‚‚: $v_{mean} \approx 1760$ m/s.
5**Heat capacities - monatomic gases:** $C_V = \frac{3}{2}R$, $C_P = \frac{5}{2}R$. The ratio $\gamma = C_P/C_V = 5/3 \approx 1.67$. Monatomic gases (He, Ne, Ar) only have translational degrees of freedom.
6**Heat capacities - diatomic gases:** $C_V = \frac{5}{2}R$, $C_P = \frac{7}{2}R$ at ordinary temperatures ($\gamma = 7/5 = 1.4$). Includes 3 translational + 2 rotational DOF. At high temperature, vibration adds more, so $C_V \rightarrow 4R$, $C_P \rightarrow 5R$, $\gamma \rightarrow 9/7 \approx 1.29$.
7**Dulong-Petit law for solids:** Molar heat capacity of most solid elements at room temperature $\approx 3R \approx 25$ J/(molÂ·K). This comes from 3 quadratic degrees of freedom per atom (one for each spatial axis), each contributing $R$ per mole of atoms.
8**Why heat capacities differ:** The ratio $C_P/C_V = \gamma$ matters for adiabatic processes ($PV^\gamma = \text{const}$). This affects everything from engine cycles to atmospheric science. $\gamma \approx 1.4$ for air is crucial in meteorology.

Heat Capacities of Gases and Solids

#Concept
1**Boltzmann constant:** $k_B = R/N_A = 1.38 \ imes 10^{-23}$ J/K. It connects microscopic and macroscopic thermodynamics. Named after Ludwig Boltzmann (1844-1906).
2**RMS speed:** $v_{rms} = \\\\sqrt{3RT/M}$, where $M$ is molar mass in kg/mol. At STP (273 K), $v_{rms}$ for air is about 479 m/s. At 300 K, about 484 m/s. Lighter gases move faster on average.
3**Most probable speed:** $v_p = \\\\sqrt{2RT/M}$. This is the speed at which the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution peaks. At 300 K for air: $v_p \\\\approx 364$ m/s. At 300 K for H₂: $v_p \\\\approx 1650$ m/s.
4**Mean speed:** $v_{mean} = \\\\sqrt{8RT/\\\\pi M}$. The average of all molecular speeds. At 300 K for air: $v_{mean} \\\\approx 464$ m/s. At 300 K for H₂: $v_{mean} \\\\approx 1760$ m/s.
5**Heat capacities - monatomic gases:** $C_V = \ rac{3}{2}R$, $C_P = \ rac{5}{2}R$. The ratio $\\\\gamma = C_P/C_V = 5/3 \\\\approx 1.67$. Monatomic gases (He, Ne, Ar) only have translational degrees of freedom.
6**Heat capacities - diatomic gases:** $C_V = \ rac{5}{2}R$, $C_P = \ rac{7}{2}R$ at ordinary temperatures ($\\\\gamma = 7/5 = 1.4$). Includes 3 translational + 2 rotational DOF. At high temperature, vibration adds more, so $C_V \ ightarrow 4R$, $C_P \ ightarrow 5R$, $\\\\gamma \ ightarrow 9/7 \\\approx 1.29$.
7**Dulong-Petit law for solids:** Molar heat capacity of most solid elements at room temperature $\\\approx 3R \\\\approx 25$ J/(mol·K). This comes from 3 quadratic degrees of freedom per atom (one for each spatial axis), each contributing $R$ per mole of atoms.
8**Why heat capacities differ:** The ratio $C_P/C_V = \\\\gamma$ matters for adiabatic processes ($PV^\\\gamma = \ ext{const}$). This affects everything from engine cycles to atmospheric science. $\\\gamma \\\\approx 1.4$ for air is crucial in meteorology.
9**Experimental verification:** Heat capacities were measured by Regnault and others in the 19th century. The agreement with kinetic theory predictions was one of the great successes of molecular physics.
10**Units:** $k_B = 1.38 \ imes 10^{-23}$ J/K. $C_P, C_V$ have units J/(mol·K). Gas constant R has the same units.
11**Connection to energy:** Each degree of freedom contributes $\ rac{1}{2}R$ per mole or $\ rac{1}{2}k_B$ per molecule. 3 translational DOF $ ightarrow 3 \ imes \ rac{1}{2}R = \ rac{3}{2}R$ for $C_V$
12**Heat capacity definition:** The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by 1 K (or 1°C). It depends on the amount of substance and the conditions (constant volume or constant pressure).
13**Specific heat capacity (c):** Heat required to raise 1 kg of substance by 1 K. Units: J/(kg·K). Water has c = 4186 J/(kg·K) at 20°C. This is the highest of any common substance.
14**Molar heat capacity (C):** Heat required to raise 1 mole by 1 K. Units: J/(mol·K). For water (M = 0.018 kg/mol): $C = 4186 \times 0.018 = 75.3$ J/(mol·K).
15**Constant volume molar heat capacity $C_V$:** For an ideal gas, $C_V = \frac{f}{2}R$, where $f$ is the number of active degrees of freedom. Monatomic: $C_V = \frac{3}{2}R = 12.5$ J/(mol·K). Diatomic: $C_V = \frac{5}{2}R = 20.8$ J/(mol·K).
16**Constant pressure molar heat capacity $C_P$:** $C_P = C_V + R$. This extra $R$ is the work the gas does expanding against external pressure when heated at constant pressure. Monatomic: $C_P = \frac{5}{2}R = 20.8$ J/(mol·K). Diatomic: $C_P = \frac{7}{2}R = 29.1$ J/(mol·K).
17**Mayer's relation:** $C_P - C_V = R$. This fundamental relation holds for all ideal gases, independent of molecular structure. Derived from the first law of thermodynamics and the ideal gas law.
18**Heat capacity ratio $\gamma = C_P/C_V$:** For monatomic ideal gas: $\gamma = 5/3 \approx 1.67$. For diatomic (room temp): $\gamma = 7/5 = 1.4$. For triatomic (nonlinear): $\gamma = 8/6 = 4/3 \approx 1.33$.
19**Degrees of freedom and $C_V$:** Each quadratic degree of freedom contributes $\frac{1}{2}R$ to $C_V$. Monatomic (3 trans): $C_V = 3 \times \frac{1}{2}R = \frac{3}{2}R$. Linear diatomic (3 trans + 2 rot): $C_V = 5 \times \frac{1}{2}R = \frac{5}{2}R$. Non-linear triatomic (3 trans + 3 rot): $C_V = 6 \times \frac{1}{2}R = 3R$.
20**Vibrational modes at high temperature:** Each vibrational mode contributes 2 degrees of freedom (kinetic + potential), so $2 \times \frac{1}{2}R = R$ per mode. At room temperature, vibration is usually frozen out. At high T, $C_V$ increases further.
21**Dulong-Petit law for solids:** At room temperature, the molar heat capacity of most solid elements is approximately $C = 3R \approx 25$ J/(mol·K). This comes from 3 quadratic DOF per atom: $\frac{3}{2}k_B$ per atom $\times N_A = \frac{3}{2}R$ per mole, but since each atom can vibrate in 3D (kinetic + potential), total is $3R$.
22**Why Dulong-Petit law works:** At high temperature, all degrees of freedom are excited according to equipartition. Each atom has 3 vibrational modes (x, y, z), each with kinetic + potential, giving 6 DOF. So $C = 6 \times \frac{1}{2}R = 3R$.
23**Failure of Dulong-Petit at low T:** At low temperatures, heat capacity drops toward zero. This was explained by Einstein (1907) using quantum mechanics: vibrational energy is quantized, and at low T, most oscillators are in the ground state, so they don't contribute to heat capacity.
24**Debye model:** Peter Debye (1912) refined Einstein's model by considering the spectrum of vibrational frequencies (phonons) in a solid. The Debye model gives $C \propto T^3$ at very low temperatures, which matches experiment much better than Einstein's model.
25**Applications:** Heat capacities determine how much a gas heats up for a given heat input. In isochoric heating ($V$ constant): $Q = n C_V \Delta T$. In isobaric heating ($P$ constant): $Q = n C_P \Delta T$. The difference is important for engines operating at constant pressure.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
The ratio $\\\\gamma = C_P/C_V$ is NOT always 5/3 or 7/5. It depends on temperature and molecular structure. At very high temperatures, vibrational modes activate, changing $\\\\gamma$.
The formula $C_V = \ rac{3}{2}R$ applies ONLY to monatomic ideal gases. For diatomic or polyatomic gases, rotational (and sometimes vibrational) modes contribute.
Dulong's law ($C \\\\approx 3R$) for solids works well at room temperature but fails at very low temperatures (quantum effects freeze out degrees of freedom). Einstein and Debye developed low-temperature models.
The Boltzmann constant $k_B$ is NOT the same as the gas constant R. $k_B = 1.38 \ imes 10^{-23}$ J/K per molecule. R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) per mole. $R = N_A k_B$.
Most probable speed is NOT the average speed. For the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution: $v_p \\\\cdot \\\\sqrt{2} < v_{mean} < v_{rms}$. The order is always $v_p < v_{mean} < v_{rms}$ at the same temperature.
Heat capacity is NOT the same as specific heat. Heat capacity $C$ is for the whole object; specific heat $c$ is per unit mass. Molar heat capacity $C_m$ is per mole. They are related: $C = mc$ for uniform objects.
$C_P \neq C_V$. Many students think the heat capacity is the same regardless of conditions. At constant pressure, the gas expands and does work, requiring more heat input to achieve the same temperature rise. $C_P > C_V$ always.
The Dulong-Petit law ($C \approx 3R$) works only at sufficiently high temperatures (well above Debye temperature). Below that, quantum effects dominate and heat capacity drops. For diamond, this 'high temperature' is over 2000 K!
Degrees of freedom 'freeze out' at different temperatures. At room temperature: translational (always active), rotational (active for most molecules), vibrational (usually frozen). The textbook $C_V$ values assume rotational but not vibrational modes are active.
Heat capacity depends on temperature for real substances. The values $C_V = \frac{5}{2}R$ for diatomic gases are approximate room-temperature values. Over a wide temperature range, $C_V$ can change significantly.

Established academic fact

Energy accounting depends on a stated sign convention. With heat added to a system positive and work done by the system positive, the first law is ΔU = Q − W. Heat and work describe transfers; internal energy is a state property.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

The ideal-gas equation is a model: particles are treated as small and far apart, with negligible intermolecular forces except during collisions. It is most reliable at sufficiently low density and away from condensation; real gases depart from it at high pressure or low temperature.

Open research question — The thermodynamic arrow of time

Macroscopic heat flow has a preferred direction, while many microscopic laws work nearly the same forward and backward in time. How the universe’s low-entropy past underwrites that observed arrow remains a foundational research question; it does not invalidate the second law’s successful predictions.
