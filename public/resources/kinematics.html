Instantaneous Velocity and Acceleration

#Concept
1**Type 2 — alternate treatment:** this version complements the original note with the limit-definition approach, calculus forms and graphical slope reasoning for instantaneous velocity and acceleration.
2**Average quantities first:** average velocity $v_{avg} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ uses the whole interval; average acceleration $a_{avg} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ likewise. They smooth out everything that happened in between — a car doing 100 km in 2 h has $v_{avg} = 50$ km/h regardless of traffic.
3**The limit idea:** instantaneous velocity is the average velocity as $\Delta t \to 0$: $v = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt}$. It answers 'how fast RIGHT NOW' — exactly what a speedometer reads.
4**Instantaneous acceleration:** $a = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity — the 'slope of the slope'.
5**Graphical meaning:** on an $x$–$t$ graph, velocity = slope of the tangent at the instant (not the chord); on a $v$–$t$ graph, acceleration = slope of the tangent, and the AREA under the curve between two times gives the displacement.
6**Sign reasoning:** $v > 0$ → moving forward; $v < 0$ → moving backward; $a > 0$ with $v > 0$ → speeding up; $a > 0$ with $v < 0$ → slowing down (a negative velocity becoming more negative). 'Slowing down' means $v$ and $a$ have OPPOSITE signs — the classic exam trap.
7**Worked calculus example:** $x(t) = 5t^2 - 3t$. Then $v = dx/dt = 10t - 3$ and $a = dv/dt = 10\,\text{m/s}^2$ (constant). At $t = 2$ s: $v = 17$ m/s; the particle is momentarily at rest when $t = 0.3$ s.
8**Uniform vs non-uniform acceleration:** if $a$ is constant the SUVAT equations apply; if $a$ varies with time, position or velocity, only the calculus definitions (or graphs) work. This is why the derivative definition is the more general and more fundamental one.
9**NEB exam focus:** define instantaneous velocity/acceleration as limits; differentiate a given $x(t)$; find when a particle is at rest or reverses; convert between $x$–$t$, $v$–$t$ and $a$–$t$ graphs (slopes and areas).

Key Points

Point
Instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent of the $x$–$t$ graph; instantaneous acceleration is the slope of the $v$–$t$ graph.
Displacement is the area under the $v$–$t$ graph; change in velocity is the area under the $a$–$t$ graph.
$v = 0$ does not imply $a = 0$ — turning points have zero velocity but non-zero acceleration.
Speeding up ⟺ $v$ and $a$ share the same sign; slowing down ⟺ opposite signs.
Derivatives go down the chain $x \to v \to a$; integrals go up $a \to v \to x$, using initial conditions.
The limit definition is valid for ANY motion — SUVAT equations are special cases of constant acceleration only.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ 'Instantaneous velocity equals average velocity.' ✅ Only when motion is uniform. In general $v_{inst} = dx/dt$ while $v_{avg} = \Delta x/\Delta t$; they coincide only if the $x$–$t$ graph is a straight line.
❌ 'Negative acceleration always means slowing down.' ✅ Slowing means $v$ and $a$ have OPPOSITE signs. A ball thrown upward (up = positive) has $v > 0$ with $a = -g$ — it slows going up and speeds up coming down, with the SAME downward acceleration throughout.
❌ 'The area under an $x$–$t$ graph gives velocity.' ✅ The SLOPE of $x$–$t$ gives velocity; the AREA under a $v$–$t$ graph gives displacement. Mixing these up is the classic graph-question error.
❌ 'At the turning point, acceleration is zero.' ✅ At a reversal, $v = 0$ but $a$ is NOT zero (e.g. a ball at the top of its flight has $v = 0$, $a = g$ downward). Zero velocity ≠ zero acceleration.
❌ 'Speed can be negative.' ✅ Speed is $|v|$, always non-negative; velocity carries the sign (direction).
❌ 'If $v = 0$ at an instant, the particle has stopped permanently.' ✅ $v = 0$ can be a momentary pause (top of a throw) — check the acceleration to know what happens next.

Summary: Instantaneous velocity and acceleration are defined as limits: $v = dx/dt$ and $a = dv/dt = d^2x/dt^2$. Graphically, $v$ is the tangent slope of the $x$–$t$ curve and $a$ the tangent slope of the $v$–$t$ curve, while areas under $v$–$t$ and $a$–$t$ curves give displacement and velocity change. These definitions work for all motion — uniform or not — making them more fundamental than the constant-acceleration equations. Key exam skills: differentiating given $x(t)$, locating rest/reversal instants, and reading slopes and areas correctly with attention to signs. Relative Velocity

#Concept
1**Definition of relative velocity:** The velocity of an object B as measured from the reference frame of another moving object A. It is the velocity of B relative to A.
2**Same direction (one-dimensional):** If car A moves at velocity v_A and car B at v_B in the same direction, the velocity of B relative to A is v_{B/A} = v_B - v_A. If v_B > v_A, B moves ahead relative to A; if v_B < v_A, B falls behind.
3**Opposite directions (one-dimensional):** If A and B move toward each other with speeds v_A and v_B, the relative speed (magnitude of relative velocity) is v_{rel} = v_A + v_B. The direction of v_{B/A} is toward A if B is moving toward A.
4**Vector form (two/three dimensions):** \vec{v}_{B/A} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A. This is the velocity of B in the ground frame minus the velocity of A in the ground frame. In components: (v_{B/A})_x = v_{Bx} - v_{Ax}, etc.
5**Relative velocity in 2D — Rain example:** If rain falls vertically downward at speed v_r and a person runs horizontally at speed v_p, the rain appears to come at the person at an angle heta where tan heta = v_p / v_r. The apparent speed is \sqrt{v_r^2 + v_p^2}.
6**River crossing (boat relative to water vs. ground):** Let \vec{v}_{bw} be the boat's velocity relative to the water (its heading and speed), and \vec{v}_{wg} be the water's velocity relative to ground (current). Then the boat's ground velocity is \vec{v}_{bg} = \vec{v}_{bw} + \vec{v}_{wg}. To reach a point directly across, the boat must angle upstream.
7**Relative velocity of approach/separation:** For two objects approaching, the rate at which their separation decreases is v_{rel} = v_A + v_B (if opposite directions) or |v_B - v_A| (if same direction). For collision problems, relative velocity is crucial.
8**Frame of reference transformation:** To transform from frame A to frame B, subtract the velocity of frame A: \vec{v}' = \vec{v} - \vec{v}_A. The laws of physics (Newton's laws) are the same in all inertial frames (Galilean relativity).
9**Galilean velocity addition:** In classical mechanics, velocities add vectorially: \vec{v}_{B/C} = \vec{v}_{B/A} + \vec{v}_{A/C}. This chain rule allows finding velocity of B relative to C via an intermediate frame A.
10**Relative motion on an incline:** If a block slides down an incline at speed v relative to the incline, and the incline itself moves (e.g., on a truck), the block's ground velocity is the vector sum of its velocity relative to the incline and the incline's velocity relative to ground.
11**Closing speed vs. relative velocity:** Closing speed is the rate at which the distance between two objects decreases. For objects moving directly toward each other, closing speed = |v_A| + |v_B|. This is the magnitude of the relative velocity only if they are on a collision course.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Relative velocity is NOT the same as the difference in speeds. For two cars moving at 60 km/h and 40 km/h in the SAME direction, the relative speed is 20 km/h, NOT 100 km/h. For opposite directions, the relative speed is the SUM (100 km/h).
When subtracting vectors, the ORDER MATTERS: \vec{v}_{B/A} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A. This is the velocity of B AS SEEN BY A. \vec{v}_{A/B} = \vec{v}_A - \vec{v}_B = -\vec{v}_{B/A}. They are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction.
A common mistake in river problems: students add the boat's speed to the current speed as scalars. You must add them as VECTORS: the boat's velocity relative to water PLUS water's velocity relative to ground. The angle matters!
In the rain problem, the rain does NOT change its actual velocity — it still falls vertically. Only the APPARENT velocity (relative to the moving person) is angled. The person tilts their umbrella forward to block the apparent rain.
Galilean relativity breaks down at speeds close to the speed of light (c). Einstein's special relativity replaces it with the Lorentz velocity addition formula: v = (v1 + v2)/(1 + v1v2/c^2). For everyday speeds (<< c), this reduces to Galilean addition.

Equations of Motion — Graphical Treatment

#Concept
1**Type 2 — alternate treatment:** this version derives all three equations of motion graphically (from the velocity–time graph) — the derivation style NEB asks for 'using a graphical method'.
2**Setup:** a body starts with initial velocity $u$ and accelerates uniformly at $a$; after time $t$ its velocity is $v$. On a $v$–$t$ graph this is a straight line from $(0, u)$ to $(t, v)$ — the straightness is exactly the meaning of uniform acceleration.
3**First equation (from the slope):** slope of the line = acceleration $a = \frac{v - u}{t}$. Rearranging: $v = u + at$ — 'final velocity = initial velocity + rate of change of velocity × time'.
4**Second equation (from the area):** displacement = area under the $v$–$t$ line = area of the trapezoid = area of rectangle $ut$ + area of triangle $\frac{1}{2} \times t \times (v - u)$. Since $v - u = at$: $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$.
5**Second equation, alternate area view:** average velocity $\times$ time: $s = \left(\frac{u + v}{2}\right)t$ — the trapezoid's mean-height form. Both views give the same result and both are accepted in exams.
6**Third equation (eliminating $t$):** from $t = \frac{v-u}{a}$ substitute into $s = \left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)t$: $s = \frac{(u+v)(v-u)}{2a} = \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2a}$, giving $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ — the time-free equation, ideal when $t$ is neither given nor asked.
7**Reading the other graphs:** on an $x$–$t$ graph, constant $a$ appears as a parabola (curvature fixed); the tangent slope at any point is $v$. On an $a$–$t$ graph, constant $a$ is a horizontal line and the area under it ($a \times t$) gives the change in velocity $v - u$.
8**Displacement in the $n$-th second:** $s_n = u + \tfrac{a}{2}(2n - 1)$ — obtained from areas: distance covered between $t = n-1$ and $t = n$. For free fall from rest this gives the odd-numbers rule 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 (Galileo).
9**NEB exam focus:** derive $v = u + at$ and $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ with a labelled $v$–$t$ graph; derive $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$; state which equation to choose from what is given/asked; apply to braking, free fall and projected bodies.

Key Points

Point
Slope of $v$–$t$ = acceleration; area under $v$–$t$ = displacement.
The three equations hold ONLY for constant acceleration.
$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ is the equation of choice when time is unknown.
$s = \left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)t$ is the graphical 'average velocity × time' form.
Sign convention decides deceleration: keep one positive direction throughout the problem.
Distance in the $n$-th second ≠ distance in $n$ seconds — the classic NEB distinction.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ 'The area under the $v$–$t$ graph gives acceleration.' ✅ Area gives DISPLACEMENT; the SLOPE gives acceleration. Swap-proof: slope of $v$–$t$ = $a$ (m/s²), area of $v$–$t$ = $s$ (m).
❌ Using $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ for the distance in the $n$-th second directly with $t = n$. ✅ That gives the total distance in $n$ seconds. The $n$-th SECOND distance is $s_n = u + \tfrac{a}{2}(2n-1)$, i.e. $s(n) - s(n-1)$.
❌ Applying $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ with sign errors during braking. ✅ Take one positive direction and keep it: braking at 20 m/s with $a = -5$ m/s² gives $0 = 400 + 2(-5)s \Rightarrow s = 40$ m — the minus sign is essential.
❌ 'The equations work if acceleration changes gradually.' ✅ All three equations REQUIRE constant acceleration; if $a$ varies, use graphs/areas or calculus.
❌ Treating $u$ and $v$ as interchangeable. ✅ $u$ is the velocity at $t = 0$; $v$ is at time $t$. A body dropped from rest has $u = 0$; a body thrown up has $u = +20$ m/s while $a = -10$ m/s².
❌ Confusing distance with displacement when the body reverses. ✅ The equations give displacement (vector). For total path length through a reversal, split the motion at the $v = 0$ instant and add the two parts.

Summary: All three equations of motion follow from the straight-line $v$–$t$ graph of uniform acceleration: the slope gives $v = u + at$; the area (rectangle + triangle, or the trapezoid mean-height $\frac{u+v}{2}t$) gives $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$; eliminating $t$ yields $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$. The $a$–$t$ graph (area = change in velocity) and the $x$–$t$ parabola complete the graphical picture. These derivations are standard NEB questions, and the equations themselves — with careful sign conventions — solve braking, free-fall and multi-stage motion numericals.

Motion of a Freely Falling Body

#Concept
1**Type 2 — alternate treatment:** this version works free fall entirely through the equations of motion with sign conventions and the up-and-down symmetry argument — the pattern NEB numericals follow.
2**Definition:** a freely falling body moves under gravity ALONE — no air resistance, no push after release. All bodies fall with the same acceleration $g \approx 9.8$ m/s² (take 10 m/s² in numericals) independent of mass — Galileo's famous result.
3**Sign convention method:** choose upward positive. Then for a body projected up at $u$: $v = u - gt$, $h = ut - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, $v^2 = u^2 - 2gh$. The SAME equations with $u = 0$ describe a dropped body; with the throw downward, $u$ enters negative.
4**Up–down symmetry:** a body thrown up with speed $u$ returns to the launch point with the same speed (downward); time up = time down $= u/g$; total flight $T = 2u/g$. Air resistance would break this symmetry — its absence is the point of 'free' fall.
5**Key instants for a body thrown up:** at the top, $v = 0$ but $a = g$ downward (never zero); height reached $h_{max} = u^2/2g$; velocities at equal heights going up and down are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
6**Dropped from a height $h$:** $v = gt$, $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. Time to fall: $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$. Falling 1:3:5:7 distances each second (Galileo's odd-number rule).
7**Bodies with initial downward or upward motion from a height** (e.g. a ball thrown down at 5 m/s): $v^2 = u^2 + 2gh$ with BOTH terms positive when down is the direction of travel — the third equation handles every case once signs are set.
8**Relative fall problems:** a ball dropped from a descending/ascending lift, or two balls released with a time gap — write each body's position $y_i(t)$ with the same origin, then use their difference. E.g. separation between a dropped ball and one thrown down 1 s later grows as $\Delta y = \tfrac{1}{2}g + (u)t$.
9**NEB exam focus:** state that $g$ is mass-independent (with the Galileo/feather-coin argument); compute maximum height, time of flight, velocity on impact; apply up–down symmetry; solve dropped/thrown-from-lift problems with one consistent sign convention.

Key Points

Point
Free fall acceleration $g$ is independent of mass, size and material.
At every instant of the flight, $a = g$ downward — including the top ($v = 0$, $a \ne 0$).
Up–down symmetry: equal speeds at equal heights, equal times up and down.
Use ONE sign convention throughout a problem; put $g$'s sign in consistently.
'Dropped' = $u = 0$; 'thrown' = $u \ne 0$ — the equations differ by the $ut$ term.
Air resistance is neglected in free fall; it is what produces terminal velocity in reality.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ 'Heavier bodies fall faster.' ✅ In free fall ALL bodies have the same acceleration $g$; observed differences come from air resistance — demonstrated by the Apollo 15 hammer–feather drop on the Moon.
❌ 'At the top of the flight, acceleration is zero because the body stops.' ✅ $v = 0$ but $a = g$ downward at ALL points of the flight. If $a$ were zero at the top, the body would stay there!
❌ Using $g = -10$ m/s² AND taking $h$ positive upward in $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$. ✅ Put the sign in ONCE: either $g$ negative with up positive, or down positive with $g$ positive — never both conventions mixed.
❌ 'A body thrown up takes longer to rise than to fall.' ✅ Without air resistance the times are EXACTLY equal ($u/g$ each way); with air resistance the fall takes LONGER, not the rise.
❌ 'Velocity on returning to the thrower is less than the launch speed.' ✅ Equal in magnitude (symmetry). Only air resistance or bounce changes it.
❌ Treating 'dropped' and 'thrown down' identically. ✅ Dropped means $u = 0$; thrown down means $u \ne 0$ downward — the equations differ by that initial term.

Summary: Free fall is uniform acceleration $g$ with no air resistance: all bodies fall alike. With one consistent sign convention, the three equations of motion give every free-fall result — dropped bodies ($h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, $v = \sqrt{2gh}$), bodies thrown up ($h_{max} = u^2/2g$, $T = 2u/g$), and mixed cases. The up–down symmetry (equal times and speeds) is the key structural insight, and $v = 0$ with $a = g$ at the top is the classic conceptual test point. #Concept
1**Type 2 — alternate treatment:** this version works free fall entirely through the equations of motion with sign conventions and the up-and-down symmetry argument — the pattern NEB numericals follow.
2**Definition:** a freely falling body moves under gravity ALONE — no air resistance, no push after release. All bodies fall with the same acceleration $g \approx 9.8$ m/s² (take 10 m/s² in numericals) independent of mass — Galileo's famous result.
3**Sign convention method:** choose upward positive. Then for a body projected up at $u$: $v = u - gt$, $h = ut - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, $v^2 = u^2 - 2gh$. The SAME equations with $u = 0$ describe a dropped body; with the throw downward, $u$ enters negative.
4**Up–down symmetry:** a body thrown up with speed $u$ returns to the launch point with the same speed (downward); time up = time down $= u/g$; total flight $T = 2u/g$. Air resistance would break this symmetry — its absence is the point of 'free' fall.
5**Key instants for a body thrown up:** at the top, $v = 0$ but $a = g$ downward (never zero); height reached $h_{max} = u^2/2g$; velocities at equal heights going up and down are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
6**Dropped from a height $h$:** $v = gt$, $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. Time to fall: $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$. Falling 1:3:5:7 distances each second (Galileo's odd-number rule).
7**Bodies with initial downward or upward motion from a height** (e.g. a ball thrown down at 5 m/s): $v^2 = u^2 + 2gh$ with BOTH terms positive when down is the direction of travel — the third equation handles every case once signs are set.
8**Relative fall problems:** a ball dropped from a descending/ascending lift, or two balls released with a time gap — write each body's position $y_i(t)$ with the same origin, then use their difference. E.g. separation between a dropped ball and one thrown down 1 s later grows as $\Delta y = \tfrac{1}{2}g + (u)t$.
9**NEB exam focus:** state that $g$ is mass-independent (with the Galileo/feather-coin argument); compute maximum height, time of flight, velocity on impact; apply up–down symmetry; solve dropped/thrown-from-lift problems with one consistent sign convention.

Key Points

Point
Free fall acceleration $g$ is independent of mass, size and material.
At every instant of the flight, $a = g$ downward — including the top ($v = 0$, $a \ne 0$).
Up–down symmetry: equal speeds at equal heights, equal times up and down.
Use ONE sign convention throughout a problem; put $g$'s sign in consistently.
'Dropped' = $u = 0$; 'thrown' = $u \ne 0$ — the equations differ by the $ut$ term.
Air resistance is neglected in free fall; it is what produces terminal velocity in reality.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ 'Heavier bodies fall faster.' ✅ In free fall ALL bodies have the same acceleration $g$; observed differences come from air resistance — demonstrated by the Apollo 15 hammer–feather drop on the Moon.
❌ 'At the top of the flight, acceleration is zero because the body stops.' ✅ $v = 0$ but $a = g$ downward at ALL points of the flight. If $a$ were zero at the top, the body would stay there!
❌ Using $g = -10$ m/s² AND taking $h$ positive upward in $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$. ✅ Put the sign in ONCE: either $g$ negative with up positive, or down positive with $g$ positive — never both conventions mixed.
❌ 'A body thrown up takes longer to rise than to fall.' ✅ Without air resistance the times are EXACTLY equal ($u/g$ each way); with air resistance the fall takes LONGER, not the rise.
❌ 'Velocity on returning to the thrower is less than the launch speed.' ✅ Equal in magnitude (symmetry). Only air resistance or bounce changes it.
❌ Treating 'dropped' and 'thrown down' identically. ✅ Dropped means $u = 0$; thrown down means $u \ne 0$ downward — the equations differ by that initial term.

Summary: Free fall is uniform acceleration $g$ with no air resistance: all bodies fall alike. With one consistent sign convention, the three equations of motion give every free-fall result — dropped bodies ($h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, $v = \sqrt{2gh}$), bodies thrown up ($h_{max} = u^2/2g$, $T = 2u/g$), and mixed cases. The up–down symmetry (equal times and speeds) is the key structural insight, and $v = 0$ with $a = g$ at the top is the classic conceptual test point.

Projectile Motion and Its Applications

#Concept
1**Type 2 — alternate treatment:** this version builds projectile motion from the independence of the two components and derives all standard results step by step — the derivation chain NEB expects.
2**The governing idea:** horizontal and vertical motions are INDEPENDENT. Gravity acts only vertically, so the horizontal velocity stays constant ($v_x = u\cos\theta$) while the vertical motion is free fall with initial speed $u\sin\theta$. The path is the combination — a parabola.
3**Component equations (up positive, launch at angle $\theta$ with speed $u$):** horizontal: $x = (u\cos\theta)t$, $v_x = u\cos\theta$; vertical: $y = (u\sin\theta)t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, $v_y = u\sin\theta - gt$.
4**Time of flight:** set $y = 0$ on landing: $T = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}$ — twice the time to the top ($u\sin\theta/g$).
5**Maximum height:** at the top $v_y = 0$: $H = \dfrac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}$ — vertical throw with initial speed $u\sin\theta$.
6**Horizontal range:** $R = v_x T = \dfrac{u^2\sin 2\theta}{g}$. Since $\sin 2\theta$ peaks at $2\theta = 90°$, **maximum range at $\theta = 45°$**, and complementary angles ($30°$ & $60°$, $15°$ & $75°$) give the SAME range.
7**Velocity at any instant:** $v = \sqrt{(u\cos\theta)^2 + (u\sin\theta - gt)^2}$ at angle $\phi = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{u\sin\theta - gt}{u\cos\theta}\right)$ below/above horizontal. On landing, magnitude equals $u$ (symmetry) with direction $-\theta$.
8**Trajectory equation:** eliminate $t$: $y = x\tan\theta - \dfrac{g x^2}{2u^2\cos^2\theta}$ — a parabola. Also useful: $R = 4H\cot\theta$ connecting range and height.
9**Applications to quote:** cannon/artillery ranges, projectile games (javelin, football), water-fountain arcs, bombing-release problems (bomb keeps the plane's horizontal velocity), and projectile on an inclined plane (extension: $R = \frac{2u^2\cos\theta\sin(\theta-\alpha)}{g\cos^2\alpha}$ for incline angle $\alpha$ — beyond syllabus but good insight).
10**NEB exam focus:** state independence of motions; derive $T$, $H$, $R$; prove max range at 45°; show complementary angles share range; compute velocity (magnitude and direction) at a given time or height; draw the labelled trajectory.

Key Points

Point
Horizontal and vertical motions are independent; they share only TIME.
$v_x$ is constant; $v_y$ changes by $g$ every second; the path is a parabola.
At the top: $v_y = 0$, speed $= u\cos\theta$ (minimum), $a = g$ downward.
Landing speed equals launch speed; landing angle equals −launch angle (symmetry).
$R$ is maximum at 45°; complementary angles give equal ranges but different heights.
$R \propto u^2$ and $H \propto u^2$ — doubling launch speed quadruples both.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
❌ 'A projectile's horizontal velocity decreases during flight.' ✅ $v_x = u\cos\theta$ is CONSTANT (no horizontal force in the ideal model); only $v_y$ changes.
❌ 'At the top of the trajectory the velocity is zero.' ✅ Only $v_y = 0$; the horizontal component $u\cos\theta$ persists — speed at the top is $u\cos\theta$, the MINIMUM of the flight.
❌ 'The path is part of a circle / straight line decaying.' ✅ The trajectory is a PARABOLA — the graph of $y = x\tan\theta - gx^2/(2u^2\cos^2\theta)$.
❌ Using $\sin\theta$ instead of $\sin 2\theta$ in the range. ✅ $R = u^2\sin 2\theta/g$; and $\sin 2\theta$ is maximal at $\theta = 45°$, not $90°$.
❌ 'Complementary angles give different ranges.' ✅ $\sin 2(90°-\theta) = \sin(180°-2\theta) = \sin 2\theta$ — $30°$ and $60°$ land at the SAME spot (the 60° shot flies higher).
❌ Doubling $u$ doubles the range. ✅ $R \propto u^2$ — doubling launch speed QUADRUPLES range (and quadruples max height too).

Summary: Projectile motion is the superposition of constant horizontal velocity and vertical free fall, giving a parabolic path. The three headline results — $T = 2u\sin\theta/g$, $H = u^2\sin^2\theta/2g$, $R = u^2\sin2\theta/g$ — all follow from the component equations, with maximum range at 45° and equal ranges at complementary angles. Speed at the top is $u\cos\theta$ (never zero), landing speed equals launch speed, and horizontal-launch problems are handled by letting the vertical fall set the time. These results assume no air resistance and constant $g$.
