Work Done by a Constant Force and a Variable Force

#Concept
1Work by Constant Force: W = F·d·cosθ = Fd cosθ where F is force magnitude, d is displacement, θ is angle between force and displacement vectors. SI unit: joule (J = N·m). Scalar quantity — can be positive, negative, or zero.
2Work by Variable Force: When force varies with position, W = ∫F(x)·dx from x₁ to x₂. Graphically, W = area under F-x curve between limits. For F = kx (spring): W = ½kx².
3Positive Work: Force component in direction of displacement (0° ≤ θ < 90°). Example: gravity on falling object, applied force pushing object forward.
4Negative Work: Force component opposite to displacement (90° < θ ≤ 180°). Example: friction on sliding object, gravity on object thrown upward.
5Zero Work: θ = 90° (force perpendicular to displacement) or d = 0 or F = 0. Example: centripetal force in circular motion, carrying object while walking horizontally.
6Work-Energy Theorem: Net work on object equals change in kinetic energy: W_net = ΔK = ½mv_f² - ½mv_i². Valid for constant and variable forces, conservative and non-conservative.
7Units: 1 J = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m²/s². Erg (CGS): 1 erg = 10⁻⁷ J. Electronvolt: 1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J.

Key Points

Point
W = Fd cosθ for constant force — scalar, can be +/-/zero.
Variable force: W = area under F-x curve = ∫F dx.
Spring: W = ½kx².
Work-energy theorem: W_net = ΔK (always valid).
Zero work when force ⟂ displacement (θ = 90°).

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing force component parallel vs perpendicular to displacement: Only parallel component does work.
Thinking holding a weight stationary does work: d = 0 → W = 0 mechanically.
Using W = Fd without cosθ when force not parallel to displacement.
Believing friction always does negative work: Yes, kinetic friction always opposes relative motion → negative work.
Confusing work done by net force vs individual forces: W_net = ΔK; individual works sum to W_net.

Summary: Work by constant force W = Fd cosθ; by variable force W = ∫F dx = area under F-x graph. Work-energy theorem W_net = ΔK always holds. Spring work W = ½kx². Work is scalar, zero when force ⟂ displacement.

Work Done by a Constant Force and a Variable Force

#Concept
1**Definition of work (constant force):** $W = F s \\\\cos\ heta$, where $F$ is the magnitude of the constant force, $s$ is the displacement, and $\ heta$ is the angle between the force vector and displacement vector. Work is a scalar quantity - it has magnitude but no direction.
2**Sign of work:** Work is POSITIVE when $0^\\\\circ \\\\le \ heta < 90^\\\\circ$ (force component along displacement). Work is ZERO when $\ heta = 90^\\\\circ$ (force perpendicular to displacement, e.g., centripetal force, normal force on horizontal surface). Work is NEGATIVE when $90^\\\\circ < \ heta \\\\le 180^\\\\circ$ (force opposes displacement, e.g., kinetic friction, gravity when lifting).
3**Work by multiple forces:** Total work is the sum of works by individual forces: $W_{\ ext{net}} = \\\\sum W_i = \\\\sum F_i s \\\\cos\ heta_i$. This is equivalent to the work done by the net force: $W_{\ ext{net}} = F_{\ ext{net}} s \\\\cos\ heta_{\ ext{net}}$.
4**Variable force definition:** When force varies with position, work is the integral of force over the displacement: $W = \\\\int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x) \\\\, dx$ (for 1D motion). Graphically, this is the area under the $F$ vs $x$ curve between the initial and final positions.
5**Work by a spring force (Hooke's law):** For an ideal spring, $F = -kx$. Work done BY the spring when moving from $x_i$ to $x_f$: $W_{\ ext{spring}} = \\\\int_{x_i}^{x_f} (-kx) dx = \ rac{1}{2}k x_i^2 - \ rac{1}{2}k x_f^2$. Work done ON the spring (by external agent) is the negative of this.
6**Work by gravity (near Earth):** Gravity is a constant force $F_g = mg$ downward. If an object moves vertically by $\\\\Delta y$ (up positive), $W_g = -mg \\\\Delta y$. If it moves horizontally, $W_g = 0$. For a path with vertical drop $h$, $W_g = +mgh$ (gravity does positive work).
7**Work by a constant force in 2D/3D:** $W = \\\\vec{F} \\\\cdot \\\\vec{s} = F_x s_x + F_y s_y + F_z s_z$. Only the component of force parallel to displacement contributes. This dot product form is frame-independent.
8**Work-energy connection (preview):** The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: $W_{\ ext{net}} = \\\\Delta K = \ rac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \ rac{1}{2}mv_i^2$. This is the work-energy theorem, derived from Newton's second law.
9**Path dependence for variable forces:** For a variable force, work depends on the path taken. For conservative forces (gravity, spring), work is path-independent and equals $-\\\\Delta U$. For non-conservative forces (friction), work depends on the actual path length.
10**Graphical interpretation:** The area under the $F$-$x$ curve (with sign) gives the work. Positive area = positive work; negative area = negative work. For a spring, the $F$-$x$ graph is a straight line through origin with slope $k$, and the area is a triangle $\ rac{1}{2}kx^2$.
11**Work by a varying force in 2D:** $W = \\\\int_C \\\\vec{F} \\\\cdot d\\\\vec{r} = \\\\int_C (F_x dx + F_y dy)$. This is a line integral. For a force $\\\\vec{F}(x,y)$, the work along path C depends on the specific curve C unless $\\\\vec{F}$ is conservative ($\ abla \ imes \\\\vec{F} = 0$).
12**Constant force vs variable force summary:** Constant force: $W = F s \\\\cos\ heta$ (simple product). Variable force: $W = \\\\int F(x) dx$ (area under curve). Both are special cases of the general definition $W = \\\\int \\\\vec{F} \\\\cdot d\\\\vec{r}$.
13**Example - work by gravity on an incline:** Block slides down incline of angle $\ heta$, length $L$. Vertical drop $h = L \\\\sin\ heta$. Work by gravity: $W_g = mgh = mgL \\\\sin\ heta$. This equals force component parallel to incline ($mg\\\\sin\ heta$) times distance $L$.
14**Example - work by external agent stretching a spring:** Slowly stretch spring from 0 to $x$ (quasi-static, acceleration $\\\\approx 0$). Applied force $F_{\ ext{app}} = +kx$ (matches spring force). Work done: $W = \\\\int_0^x kx dx = \ rac{1}{2}kx^2$. This energy is STORED as elastic potential energy in the spring.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Work is NOT force times distance in general - it is force component in the direction of displacement times distance. $W = F s$ only when force and displacement are parallel ($ heta = 0$). Many students forget the $\\\cos\ heta$ factor.
Work done BY a force vs work done ON an object: $W_{\ ext{by}} = -W_{\ ext{on}}$. The work-energy theorem uses $W_{\ ext{net}}$ (work done BY all forces ON the object). Work done BY the object on something else is the negative.
Holding a heavy object stationary: You exert a force, but displacement is zero, so $W = 0$! You get tired because your muscles do internal biochemical work (energy conversion), not mechanical work on the object. This is a classic misconception.
Centripetal force does NO work: $F_c$ is always perpendicular to velocity (displacement), so $\\\\cos 90^\\\\circ = 0$. It changes direction, not speed. A planet in circular orbit around the Sun has zero work done by gravity.
For a variable force, you CANNOT just multiply 'average force' by distance unless you know the average is the integral average. The correct method is always integration or graphical area. A common error: averaging endpoints $\ rac{F_i + F_f}{2}$ only works for linear forces.

Power

#Concept
1Power is the rate of energy transfer: average P=ΔW/Δt and instantaneous P=dW/dt. Its SI unit is watt, 1 W=1 J s⁻¹.
2For translation, dW=F⃗·dr⃗ gives P=F⃗·v⃗=Fv cosθ. Only the force component parallel to velocity transfers energy; a perpendicular centripetal force does no work in uniform circular motion.
3Worked example: a 200 kg load rises steadily at 0.50 m s⁻¹. The lifting force is mg=1960 N for g=9.8 m s⁻², so useful power is Fv=980 W. In 10 s the load rises 5 m and gains 9800 J, confirming P=W/t=980 W.
4At constant speed, net power is zero because engine power balances resistive forces. For rotation, P=τ⃗·ω⃗; efficiency η=Pout/Pin distinguishes useful output from input.
5A 50 N force at 60° to a crate moving at 3.0 m s⁻¹ transfers energy at P=50×3×cos60°=75 W, not 150 W.

Key Points

Point
Use the force component parallel to velocity.
At constant speed net power is zero though a motor may deliver positive power.
State whether input or output power is requested.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not use P=Fv unless force and velocity are parallel or include cosθ.
Motor output is not the net power on a constant-speed vehicle.

Summary: Power measures energy-transfer rate. For translation it is the force–velocity dot product; practical machines distinguish useful output from losses.

Work-Energy Theorem — Kinetic and Potential Energy

#Concept
1**Work-Energy Theorem (statement):** The net work done by all forces acting on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy: $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = K_f - K_i = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2$. This is a scalar equation — direction is handled through the dot product in work.
2**Derivation from Newton's Second Law:** $F = ma = m \frac{dv}{dt}$. Multiply both sides by $v = dx/dt$: $F v = m v \frac{dv}{dt} = m \frac{d}{dt}(\frac{1}{2}v^2)$. Integrate over time: $\int F v dt = \int F dx = W = \Delta(\frac{1}{2}mv^2) = \Delta K$. This shows the theorem is mathematically equivalent to $F=ma$.
3**Kinetic energy definition:** $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. Always positive (since $v^2 \ge 0$). Scalar quantity — depends on speed, not velocity direction. A 1 kg mass at 10 m/s has $K = 50$ J. Doubling speed quadruples KE (v² dependence).
4**Potential energy — general concept:** Potential energy $U$ is energy stored due to the position or configuration of a system in a conservative force field. It is defined ONLY for conservative forces. The change in potential energy is the negative of the work done by the conservative force: $\Delta U = -W_{\text{conservative}}$.
5**Gravitational potential energy (near Earth):** $U_g = mgh$, where $h$ is height above an arbitrarily chosen reference level (usually ground or lowest point in problem). $\Delta U_g = mg\Delta h$. Work by gravity: $W_g = -\Delta U_g = mg(h_i - h_f)$. Only HEIGHT difference matters, not the path taken.
6**Elastic potential energy (spring):** For a spring obeying Hooke's law $F = -kx$, $U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ where $x$ is displacement from natural length. Work by spring: $W_s = -\Delta U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx_i^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx_f^2$. $U_s$ is always $\ge 0$ (minimum at $x=0$).
7**Mechanical energy:** $E = K + U = K + U_g + U_s + \dots$. When ONLY conservative forces do work, $W_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{conservative}} = -\Delta U$. Then $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = -\Delta U$, so $\Delta K + \Delta U = 0$, meaning $E = \text{constant}$. This is conservation of mechanical energy.
8**Non-conservative forces and the general work-energy relation:** When non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance, applied forces) are present: $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta E = \Delta K + \Delta U$. This is the most general form. $W_{\text{nc}}$ is the work done by ALL non-conservative forces. If $W_{\text{nc}} = 0$, mechanical energy is conserved.
9**Kinetic energy in different frames:** KE is frame-dependent. In lab frame: $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. In CM frame: $K_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{2}\mu v_{\text{rel}}^2$ where $\mu$ is reduced mass. The total KE = $K_{\text{CM}}$ (motion of CM) + $K_{\text{rel}}$ (internal motion). Only internal KE can be converted to heat in inelastic collisions.
10**Potential energy reference point is arbitrary:** $U = mgh$ uses $h$ measured from any reference. Changing reference adds a constant to all $U$ values, which cancels in $\Delta U$. Always choose the reference to simplify the problem (e.g., $U=0$ at ground level, or at equilibrium of spring).
11**Energy approach vs force approach:** Force approach: $F=ma$, solve for acceleration, integrate for velocity/position. Energy approach: $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta E$, solve directly for speed or height. Energy is often simpler when forces vary or path is curved, but it gives speeds, not trajectories or times.
12**Work done by gravity in a closed loop:** Zero. $W_g = -\Delta U_g$, and for a closed loop $\Delta U_g = 0$ (you return to the same height). This is why gravity is conservative. Work done by friction in a closed loop: NEGATIVE (energy lost to heat). Friction is non-conservative.
13**Power and the work-energy theorem:** $P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{dK}{dt} = Fv$ (when $F$ and $v$ are parallel). The rate of change of kinetic energy equals the power delivered by the net force. For a particle moving in a circle at constant speed, $\Delta K = 0$, so net work = 0, and centripetal force does zero work ($F \perp v$).

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
The work-energy theorem uses NET work (sum of work by ALL forces). A common error is using work by just one force. If you push a block at constant speed on a rough floor, your work is positive, friction's work is negative, NET work = 0, $\Delta K = 0$ — the theorem holds perfectly.
Potential energy is NOT defined for non-conservative forces. You CANNOT write $U_{\text{friction}} = \mu mg x$. Friction dissipates energy as heat; there is no 'friction potential' to store and recover energy. This is a fundamental distinction.
Mechanical energy conservation ($K+U=\text{constant}$) ONLY applies when $W_{\text{nc}}=0$. If there is ANY friction, air resistance, or external pushing/pulling, mechanical energy is NOT conserved. You MUST use $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta E$ instead.
The reference point for potential energy does NOT affect the physics. $\Delta U$ is what matters. If you set $U=0$ at the table vs floor, the KE at the bottom is the same. Students often waste time 'finding the right' reference — any reference works if used consistently.
Kinetic energy can NEVER be negative ($v^2 \ge 0$). Potential energy CAN be negative (e.g., $U = -GMm/r$ for universal gravity). Total mechanical energy can be negative (bound systems like planets in orbit). Only ENERGY DIFFERENCES are physically meaningful.

Summary: Work-energy theorem; kinetic and potential energy is a fundamental concept in Science that covers the essential principles, formulas, and applications. Understanding this topic is crucial for solving related problems and building foundation for advanced topics.

Conservation of Energy

#Concept
1**Law of conservation of energy:** Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another or transferred between systems. The total energy of an isolated system remains constant over time.
2**Mechanical energy conservation:** When only conservative forces (gravity, spring force, electrostatic force) act on a system, mechanical energy E = K + U is conserved: ΔK + ΔU = 0, or K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f.
3**Potential energy types:** Gravitational PE near Earth: U_g = mgh (h = height above reference). Elastic PE of a spring: U_s = ½kx² (x = compression/extension from natural length). For a universal gravitational field: U = -GMm/r.
4**Non-conservative forces and energy loss:** Friction, air resistance, and viscous drag are non-conservative. They convert mechanical energy into thermal energy (heat). Work done by non-conservative forces: W_nc = ΔE_mech = E_final - E_initial.
5**Total energy conservation including heat:** Even with friction, total energy is conserved if you include thermal energy: ΔK + ΔU + ΔE_thermal = 0. The first law of thermodynamics: ΔU_internal = Q - W, where Q is heat added, W is work done BY the system.
6**Pendulum energy conservation:** For a simple pendulum (mass m, length L, angle θ): At the bottom, K = ½mv², U = 0. At the extremes, v = 0, K = 0, U = mgL(1-cosθ). With no air resistance: ½mv² + mgL(1-cosθ) = constant.
7**Spring-mass system:** For a mass m on a spring with constant k: At equilibrium, K = ½mv², U = 0. At max extension x = A, v = 0, K = 0, U = ½kA². Energy oscillates: ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kA² = constant.
8**Roller coaster physics:** At the top of a hill, the car has maximum PE (mgh) and minimum KE. At the bottom, maximum KE and minimum PE. Total energy constant (neglecting friction). Minimum speed at top of vertical loop: v ≥ √(gr) to stay on track.
9**Energy in collisions:** In elastic collisions, both momentum and KE are conserved. In inelastic collisions, momentum is conserved but KE is not (some converts to heat/sound). In perfectly inelastic collisions, objects stick together and KE loss is maximum.
10**Conservation of energy in nuclear reactions:** Mass-energy equivalence E = mc² shows mass is another form of energy. In nuclear fission/fusion, the total mass of products is less than reactants — the mass defect Δm appears as kinetic energy of products: ΔE = Δmc².
11**Power and energy relationship:** Power is the rate of energy transfer: P = dE/dt. If power is constant, E = Pt. If power varies, E = ∫P dt. Energy delivered is the area under the P-t curve.
12**Real-world energy transformations:** Chemical energy (fuel) → thermal (burning) → mechanical (engine) → electrical (generator) → electromagnetic (light) → thermal (absorber). At each step, some energy becomes heat (second law).
13**Reference point for potential energy:** Gravitational PE is defined relative to a chosen zero level. The choice is arbitrary — only differences in PE matter. Usually we set U = 0 at the lowest point in the problem or at ground level.

Key Points

Point
Mechanical energy (K + U) is conserved ONLY when all forces are conservative.
Friction and air resistance are non-conservative — they convert mechanical energy to heat.
The work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔK) holds for ALL forces, conservative or not.
Total energy (including thermal) is always conserved — this is the first law of thermodynamics.
Choose the reference level for PE at the lowest point to simplify calculations.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Energy is NOT created or destroyed — it only transforms. When a pendulum stops swinging due to friction, the energy doesn't 'disappear'; it becomes thermal energy in the air and the pivot. The FIRST law of thermodynamics says energy is conserved in all processes.
Conservation of mechanical energy (K + U = constant) ONLY holds when ALL forces are conservative. If there is ANY friction, air resistance, or non-conservative force, mechanical energy is NOT conserved. You must include W_nc or track thermal energy.
Potential energy is NOT absolute — it is defined relative to an arbitrary zero point. A ball on a table has U = mgh relative to the floor, but U = mg(h+H) relative to the basement. Only PE differences matter physically.
In a collision, momentum is ALWAYS conserved (if no external forces). Kinetic energy is ONLY conserved in perfectly ELASTIC collisions. Many students think KE is always conserved in collisions — it is NOT. Real collisions are usually somewhat inelastic.
The work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔK) and energy conservation (ΔK + ΔU = -W_nc) are DIFFERENT. The first is about NET work changing kinetic energy. The second is about total mechanical energy. With conservative forces, W_net = -ΔU, so W_net + ΔU = ΔK, which is the work-energy theorem.

Summary: Energy cannot be created or destroyed — only transformed. When only conservative forces act, mechanical energy (K + U) is conserved. Non-conservative forces (friction) convert mechanical energy to heat, but total energy is always conserved. The work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔK) is the key tool for solving problems.

Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

#Concept
1**Conservative force definition:** A force is conservative if the work done by it depends ONLY on the initial and final positions, not on the path taken. Equivalently, the work done around any closed loop is zero: ∮F·dr = 0.
2**Path independence:** For a conservative force, W = U_i - U_f = -(U_f - U_i) = -ΔU. The work is the negative of the change in potential energy. This means we can define a potential energy function U(r) such that F = -∇U (force is the negative gradient of potential).
3**Examples of conservative forces:** Gravity (near Earth: F = -mg, U = mgh), spring force (F = -kx, U = ½kx²), electrostatic force (F = kQq/r², U = kQq/r), uniform electric field (F = qE, U = qEy).
4**Non-conservative force definition:** A force is non-conservative if the work done depends on the path taken. The work around a closed loop is NOT zero: ∮F·dr ≠ 0. Energy is dissipated as heat or sound.
5**Examples of non-conservative forces:** Kinetic friction (F_f = μN, work = -μN × distance traveled — depends on path length!), air resistance/viscous drag (proportional to v or v², opposes motion), rolling friction, human muscle force (metabolic energy not recoverable).
6**Potential energy association:** Conservative forces ALLOW the definition of potential energy because the work done against them is stored and recoverable. Non-conservative forces do NOT have an associated potential energy — the energy is lost from the mechanical system.
7**Closed loop test:** Take any closed path. For gravity: lift a mass up height h (W = -mgh), lower it down (W = +mgh). Net work = 0. For friction: slide a block around a closed square (perimeter = 4L). Each side does work = -μmgL. Net work = -4μmgL ≠ 0.
8**Mixed force systems:** When both conservative and non-conservative forces act, use: W_nc = ΔE_mech = (K_f + U_f) - (K_i + U_i). This is the most general energy equation for mechanical systems. If W_nc = 0, mechanical energy is conserved.
9**Energy dissipated by friction:** For a block sliding distance d with kinetic friction μ_k, the thermal energy generated is Q = μ_k N d. This comes from the work done BY friction: W_f = -μ_k N d. The mechanical energy lost equals the heat generated.
10**Conservative force and curl:** In 3D, a force F is conservative iff its curl is zero: ∇ × F = 0 (everywhere in a simply connected region). This is a mathematical test — if the force field has no circulation, it is conservative.
11**Electrostatic force is conservative:** The work done by an electrostatic field depends only on endpoints. This allows defining electric potential V = U/q and voltage differences. The electric field is the negative gradient: E = -∇V.
12**Why it matters for problem solving:** If all forces are conservative, use K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f (simple). If non-conservative forces are present, use W_nc = ΔE_mech. Always identify which forces are conservative and which are not.

Key Points

Point
Conservative forces (gravity, spring, electrostatic) allow potential energy definition.
Non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance) dissipate energy as heat.
The closed-loop test: if net work around a closed path is zero, the force is conservative.
When both types act, use W_nc = ΔE_mech (general energy equation).
Friction always does negative work — it removes mechanical energy from the system.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Friction does NOT have a potential energy. Many students try to write U_friction = μmgx or something similar. This is WRONG — friction is non-conservative, and the energy it 'removes' from the mechanical system becomes heat, not stored potential energy.
A force being 'conservative' does NOT mean the total energy of the system is conserved. It means mechanical energy can be conserved IF that force is the ONLY force doing work. If you have conservative forces PLUS non-conservative forces, mechanical energy is not conserved even though some forces are conservative.
Normal force and tension are NOT conservative forces — they are constraint forces that do zero work (they are perpendicular to displacement). They don't appear in the energy equation because W = 0, not because they have potential energy.
The term 'non-conservative' does NOT mean 'energy is destroyed.' It means mechanical energy is converted to other forms (heat, sound) that are NOT tracked by potential energy. Total energy is ALWAYS conserved (first law).
Air resistance (drag) is non-conservative. The work done by drag depends on the path (speed profile). You CANNOT define a 'drag potential energy.' The energy lost to drag becomes heat in the air.

Summary: Conservative forces (gravity, spring, electrostatic) do path-independent work and allow potential energy definition. Non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance) dissipate energy as heat. The general energy equation W_nc = ΔE_mech handles both types — if W_nc = 0, mechanical energy is conserved.

Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

#Concept
1**Elastic collision:** A collision in which both total momentum AND total kinetic energy are conserved. The bodies rebound without permanent deformation, no sound/heat is produced. The relative velocity of approach equals the relative velocity of separation: v₂ - v₁ = u₁ - u₂.
2**Inelastic collision:** A collision in which momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is NOT conserved. Some kinetic energy is converted to internal energy (heat, sound, deformation). The coefficient of restitution e < 1.
3**Perfectly inelastic collision:** The extreme case where the colliding bodies stick together after collision and move with a common velocity. Momentum is conserved; KE loss is maximum (but not 100% because momentum must be conserved, so some KE remains as center-of-mass motion).
4**One-dimensional elastic collision formulas:** For masses m₁, m₂ with initial velocities u₁, u₂: v₁ = (m₁-m₂)u₁/(m₁+m₂) + 2m₂u₂/(m₁+m₂), v₂ = (m₂-m₁)u₂/(m₁+m₂) + 2m₁u₁/(m₁+m₂). These come from conservation of momentum + conservation of KE.
5**Special case — equal masses (m₁ = m₂) in elastic collision:** The masses exchange velocities! v₁ = u₂, v₂ = u₁. If m₂ was initially at rest (u₂ = 0), then v₁ = 0 (first mass stops) and v₂ = u₁ (second mass moves off with the first mass's original speed).
6**Special case — very massive m₁, stationary m₂ (m₁ ≫ m₂, u₂ = 0):** m₁ barely slows: v₁ ≈ u₁. m₂ shoots forward at v₂ ≈ 2u₁ (twice the initial speed). This is how a golf club hits a ball, or a bat hits a baseball.
7**Coefficient of restitution (e):** e = (relative speed of separation) / (relative speed of approach) = (v₂ - v₁)/(u₁ - u₂). For perfectly elastic: e = 1. For perfectly inelastic: e = 0. For inelastic: 0 < e < 1. e depends on the materials (e.g., tennis ball on concrete: e ≈ 0.75; steel on steel: e ≈ 0.9; putty: e ≈ 0).
8**Energy loss in inelastic collisions:** ΔK = K_f - K_i = -½ μ v_rel² (1 - e²), where μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) is the reduced mass and v_rel = u₁ - u₂ is the relative velocity. The energy lost goes to heat, sound, and permanent deformation.
9**Two-dimensional collisions:** Momentum is conserved as a vector: p_x and p_y separately. For elastic collisions, KE is also conserved. The scattering angles depend on the impact parameter. Center-of-mass frame analysis often simplifies 2D collisions.
10**Center-of-mass frame:** In the CM frame, total momentum is zero. In an elastic collision, the speeds of particles don't change, only their directions change. The scattering is isotropic for identical particles in the CM frame.
11**Real-world applications:** Car crash safety (crumple zones increase collision time, reduce force — KE is dissipated controllably). Billiards (nearly elastic). Newton's cradle (demonstrates momentum and energy transfer through elastic collisions). Sports (baseball, tennis, golf — coefficient of restitution of ball/racket/club matters).
12**Explosion — reverse collision:** An object at rest splits into fragments with kinetic energy. Momentum is conserved (total momentum before = 0, after = 0). KE after > KE before — the energy comes from internal chemical/nuclear energy stored in the object. This is a 'reverse' inelastic collision.

Key Points

Point
Elastic collision: both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved.
Inelastic collision: momentum conserved, kinetic energy NOT conserved (some → heat/sound).
Perfectly inelastic: bodies stick together, KE loss is maximum.
Coefficient of restitution e = 1 (elastic), e = 0 (perfectly inelastic), 0 < e < 1 (inelastic).
Equal-mass elastic collision (one at rest): the masses exchange velocities.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
In a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects DO NOT lose ALL their kinetic energy. They lose the MAXIMUM possible KE consistent with momentum conservation. The center-of-mass kinetic energy (½Mv_cm²) MUST remain because momentum is conserved. Only the KE relative to the center of mass can be lost.
The coefficient of restitution e is NOT the ratio of final to initial KE. e = (v₂ - v₁)/(u₁ - u₂) is the ratio of relative speeds. The fraction of KE retained is e² (for equal masses) or a more complex formula for unequal masses. Many students confuse e with KE ratio.
Momentum is ALWAYS conserved in an isolated system (no external forces). Kinetic energy is ONLY conserved in perfectly elastic collisions. Students often think KE is always conserved in collisions — it is NOT. Real collisions are usually somewhat inelastic (0 < e < 1).
For a ball bouncing on the floor: The floor/earth is so massive it doesn't move. The ball's speed before and after impact relates by e = v_after/v_before. The ball loses (1-e²) of its KE each bounce. The floor provides the impulse to change momentum; the earth's recoil is negligible.
A collision where kinetic energy is 'gained' (superelastic) is possible if internal energy is released (explosion, chemical reaction, spring release). These are NOT standard collisions and require tracking internal energy changes.

Summary: In an elastic collision, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. In an inelastic collision, momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not (some converts to heat/sound). In a perfectly inelastic collision, the bodies stick together and KE loss is maximum. The coefficient of restitution e measures the elasticity of the collision.
