Dimensions

#Concept
1Dimensions express a quantity using base quantities. In mechanics, mass M, length L and time T give velocity [v]=LT⁻¹, acceleration [a]=LT⁻² and force [F]=MLT⁻².
2Find dimensions from definitions: work W=Fs gives [W]=ML²T⁻²; power P=W/t gives [P]=ML²T⁻³. Pressure is force per area, [p]=ML⁻¹T⁻².
3Dimensional homogeneity requires every term added in an equation to have the same dimensions. In s=ut+(1/2)at², both ut and at² have dimension L.
4Worked check: in v²=u²+2as, v² and u² are L²T⁻²; as is (LT⁻²)L=L²T⁻². The equation is dimensionally possible, though this test cannot prove it is correct.
5Dimensional analysis can check units and infer powers in a relation, but cannot determine dimensionless constants such as 1/2 or functions involving dimensionless variables.

Key Points

Point
Derive dimensions from defining relations.
Only like dimensions may be added.
Dimensional correctness is necessary, not sufficient.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Dimensions differ from units: length has dimension L and SI unit metre.
A dimensionally correct equation may still be physically wrong.

Summary: Dimensional analysis checks the powers of base units in equations and catches inconsistent terms, but cannot supply all constants or prove a physical law.

Errors

#Concept
1Random errors vary unpredictably between readings; repeated measurements and averaging reduce their influence. Systematic errors shift results consistently because of calibration, zero offset or method and are not removed by simple repetition.
2Accuracy is closeness to an accepted value; precision is agreement among repeated readings. A set can be precise but inaccurate if a systematic offset is present.
3If a true value is known, absolute error is |xmeasured−xtrue|; relative error is absolute error divided by the reference value; percentage error is 100 times relative error.
4Example: readings 10.2, 10.3 and 10.1 cm average to 10.2 cm. Compared with accepted 10.0 cm, absolute error is 0.2 cm and percentage error is 2%.
5Check an instrument’s zero before use, repeat readings where possible, report units and uncertainty, and avoid unsupported digits beyond the instrument resolution.

Key Points

Point
Repeats estimate scatter and reduce random uncertainty.
Calibration and zero checks address systematic error.
Report uncertainty with units and sensible significant figures.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
A small spread does not prove accuracy.
Do not report more digits than the instrument supports.

Summary: Measurement uncertainty has random and systematic causes. Repetition improves random estimates; calibration and method checks are needed for systematic offsets.

L,S,A,Q and N

#Concept
1This legacy heading groups basic measurement practice. Record a measured quantity as a numerical value and unit; choose direct measurement when possible and a geometric or displacement method when it is not.
2Measure length with a suitable scale or vernier instrument, area from dimensions for regular shapes, and volume from geometry or liquid displacement for irregular solid objects that do not absorb or dissolve in water.
3For a rectangular plate A=lb; for a cylinder V=πr²h. A graduated cylinder gives an irregular object’s volume from the rise in water level, provided the object is fully submerged without trapped air.
4Worked example: a 2.0 cm by 3.0 cm by 4.0 cm block has V=24 cm³. If its mass is 60 g, density ρ=m/V=2.5 g cm⁻³=2500 kg m⁻³.
5Record instrument resolution, repeat readings when useful, keep units consistent and round the final derived quantity to a justified precision. The title acronym is retained from the source rather than expanded speculatively.

Key Points

Point
A measurement requires magnitude and unit.
Select a method matched to shape and material.
Track units through each calculation.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
The source acronym is ambiguous; do not infer an unsupported expansion.
Do not mix cm³ with m³ or grams with kilograms in one calculation.

Summary: Reliable measurement requires an appropriate method, unit and justified precision. Derived area, volume and density values inherit uncertainty from measured dimensions and mass.

M,P Errors

#Concept
1Repeated readings have a mean value and a spread. A common school estimate of mean absolute error is the average of |xi−x̄|; probable error is sometimes defined as 0.6745 times the mean absolute error for normally distributed random errors.
2For sums and differences, a conservative maximum-error estimate adds absolute uncertainties: z=x±y gives Δz≈Δx+Δy. For products or quotients, relative uncertainties add: Δz/|z|≈Δx/|x|+Δy/|y|.
3For a power law z=xⁿ, relative uncertainty is approximately |n|Δx/|x|. Thus a 1% radius error gives about 2% area error for A=πr².
4Worked example: L=10.0±0.1 cm and W=5.0±0.1 cm. For A=LW, fractional uncertainty≈0.1/10+0.1/5=0.03, or 3%; A=50 cm² with estimated uncertainty 1.5 cm².
5These are first-order rules for small uncertainties. State the method and avoid suggesting an exact result when measurements are approximate.

Key Points

Point
Keep absolute and relative errors distinct.
A squared quantity doubles percentage uncertainty to first order.
Round the value consistently with its uncertainty.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Probable-error terminology varies by textbook; state its statistical convention.
First-order rules assume small uncertainties and do not replace a full statistical model.

Summary: Error propagation depends on how measured quantities combine: add absolute errors for sums and relative errors for products and powers.

Physics and Measurement

#Concept
1A physical quantity is a measurable property expressed as magnitude and unit, such as 2.5 m or 4.0 s. A number without a unit is incomplete unless the quantity is dimensionless.
2SI base quantities include length (m), mass (kg), time (s), current (A), temperature (K), amount (mol) and luminous intensity (cd). Derived units combine these; 1 N=1 kg m s⁻².
3Scalars have magnitude only (mass, time, energy); vectors have magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, force). Their addition rules differ.
4Example: 3 m east followed by 4 m north gives path length 7 m but displacement magnitude √(3²+4²)=5 m. Distance and displacement are different quantities.
5Weight is gravitational force measured in newtons; mass is measured in kilograms. Near Earth W=mg, but mass does not change when the gravitational field changes.

Key Points

Point
A measurement needs a unit.
Scalars and vectors obey different addition rules.
Mass is not weight; distance is not displacement.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
A negative vector component indicates direction, not negative magnitude.
Do not report weight in kilograms or mass in newtons.

Summary: Physics uses quantities, units and correct mathematical structure. Distinguishing scalar from vector quantities prevents errors in displacement, velocity and force calculations.

Error Propagation & Significant Figures Rules

#Concept
1Significant figures communicate measurement precision. In 0.00450 m there are three significant figures: leading zeros locate the decimal; the final zero after the decimal is significant.
2For multiplication or division, round the final result to the fewest significant figures among measured inputs. For addition or subtraction, round to the least precise decimal place.
3Example: 2.34×1.2=2.808 rounds to 2.8 (two significant figures). But 12.11+0.3=12.41 rounds to 12.4 (one decimal place).
4Keep guard digits in intermediate steps and round once at the end. Exact counts and defined conversion factors do not limit significant figures.
5For powers, propagate measurement uncertainty before rounding: if A=πr², a 1% uncertainty in r gives approximately 2% uncertainty in A.

Key Points

Point
Leading zeros are placeholders, not significant.
Round at the end of multistep calculations.
Use decimal-place rules for sums and significant-figure rules for products.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not apply the fewest-significant-figures rule to addition.
Exact counted values do not impose measurement precision.

Summary: Significant figures and uncertainty keep reported precision tied to measurements. Apply the operation-specific rule and avoid premature rounding.

Vector

#Concept
1A vector has magnitude and direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration and force are vectors; distance, speed, mass and energy are scalars.
2Choose axes and resolve A into Ax=A cosθ, Ay=A sinθ when θ is measured from +x. Components are signed according to their direction and are added separately.
3Worked example: a 10 N force at 30° above +x has Fx=8.66 N and Fy=5.00 N. Recovering its magnitude gives √(8.66²+5.00²)≈10 N.
4Add vectors by components: (3,4)+(−1,2)=(2,6), with magnitude √40≈6.32. The dot product ABcosθ is scalar; the cross product has magnitude ABsinθ and a perpendicular direction.
5Check a result against the diagram: the resultant should lie between the component directions, and its magnitude should not exceed the sum of component magnitudes.

Key Points

Point
Set axes and angle reference before resolving.
Components carry signs; magnitude is nonnegative.
Check resultant direction and magnitude geometrically.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
The angle in component formulas must be measured from the stated axis.
A negative component indicates direction, not negative length.

Summary: Vector calculations become systematic through components: resolve, add componentwise, then recover magnitude and direction. Scalars do not use this procedure.
