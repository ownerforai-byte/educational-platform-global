Dispersive Power

#Concept
1**Dispersion:** splitting of white light into its constituent colours because the refractive index of a material depends on wavelength. This occurs because the speed of light in a medium varies with wavelength — violet light (shorter wavelength) slows down more than red light (longer wavelength), so it bends more.
2**Pure spectrum:** a spectrum in which colours do not overlap. Achieved with a narrow slit and a single prism so that each wavelength emerges at a distinct angle. The narrow slit ensures each colour comes from a single point source, eliminating colour blending.
3**Dispersive power $\omega$:** $\omega = \frac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1}$, where $n_v$, $n_r$, $n_y$ are refractive indices for violet, red, and yellow (sodium D line). Dispersive power is a dimensionless quantity — it is the ratio of angular dispersion to the mean deviation.
4**Crown vs flint glass:** crown glass has $\omega \approx 0.016$, flint glass has $\omega \approx 0.031$. Flint glass disperses about twice as much, which is why it is used in spectroscope prisms where a wide angular spread is desired.
5**Angular dispersion:** $\delta_v - \delta_r = (n_v - n_r)A$ for a small-angle prism, or equivalently $(n_v - n_r)A = \omega(n_y - 1)A$. The spread of colours is proportional to both the refractive-index difference and the prism angle $A$.
6**Deviation:** for a small-angle prism, the mean deviation is $\delta_y = (n_y - 1)A$. The prism bends all wavelengths, but different amounts — this differential bending is what creates the spectrum.
7**Why dispersion occurs:** the refractive index follows Cauchy's relation $n(\lambda) = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} + \frac{C}{\lambda^4}$, so shorter wavelengths have higher $n$ and bend more. This wavelength dependence is a property of the electronic response of the material.
8**Unit analysis:** dispersive power is dimensionless (ratio of two quantities with the same units). Angular dispersion has units of radians. The prism angle $A$ is in radians or degrees.
9**Rainbow formation:** a natural example of dispersion — sunlight undergoes refraction, internal reflection, and a second refraction inside water droplets, separating into the seven colours of the spectrum.

Key Points

Point
Dispersive power is dimensionless and material-only: it compares how much a material separates colours relative to how much it bends them.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Dispersive power is NOT the same as angular dispersion — dispersive power is dimensionless (a ratio), while angular dispersion has units of angle and depends on the prism angle.
A larger refractive index does not necessarily mean a larger dispersive power — flint glass has both a higher $n$ and a higher $\omega$ than crown glass, but the two properties are independent.
Dispersion is NOT caused by the prism absorbing different colours — all colours pass through; they simply bend by different amounts because $n$ depends on wavelength.
The colours in a spectrum do not have sharp boundaries — the seven colours (VIBGYOR) are a convention. The spectrum is a continuous gradient of wavelengths.
For a large-angle prism, the small-angle approximation $\delta = (n-1)A$ is no longer valid — one must use Snell's law at each surface separately.

Dispersive Power

#Concept
1White light contains a range of wavelengths. In ordinary glass, refractive index is greater for violet than red, so violet bends more through a prism: this wavelength-dependent separation is dispersion.
2For a thin prism of apex angle A and small deviations, a colour with refractive index n has deviation δ=(n−1)A. Therefore the red–violet angular spread is Δδ=(nv−nr)A.
3Dispersive power compares colour spread with mean yellow deviation: ω=(δv−δr)/δy=(nv−nr)/(ny−1). It is dimensionless and describes relative dispersion of the material, not the prism angle.
4Worked example: nv=1.532, nr=1.514 and ny=1.523 give ω=0.018/0.523≈0.0344. A higher ω means more red–violet separation for the same mean deviation.
5The thin-prism ratio follows by dividing Δδ=(nv−nr)A by δy=(ny−1)A; A cancels. In a different surrounding medium, use refractive indices relative to that medium.

Key Points

Point
For normal dispersion in glass, violet deviates more than red.
Dispersive power is a relative spread; total deviation also depends on A.
The thin-prism formula assumes small angles.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not confuse dispersive power with total deviation; total deviation depends on apex angle.
Use small-angle thin-prism relations only within their approximation.

Summary: A prism separates colours because refractive index varies with wavelength. For a thin prism, ω=(nv−nr)/(ny−1) measures relative colour spread.

Pure Spectrum and Dispersive Power

#Concept
1A prism forms a spectrum because refractive index varies with wavelength. In normal dispersion, violet has a larger index and deviation than red. A pure spectrum has narrow, well-separated colour images with little overlap.
2For a thin prism, angular dispersion is Δδ=(nv−nr)A and yellow mean deviation is δy=(ny−1)A. Their ratio is ω=Δδ/δy=(nv−nr)/(ny−1).
3Worked example: if δy=3° and ω=0.06, the red–violet spread is Δδ=0.18°. A material with twice the dispersive power gives twice the spread at the same mean deviation.
4To obtain a purer spectrum, use a narrow slit, collimate the incident beam and focus the dispersed rays with a lens. A wide slit broadens each colour image and causes neighbouring bands to overlap.
5A spectrometer uses a collimator slit and telescope to form and inspect a sharp spectrum. Resolution improves when the source image is narrow and the optical alignment is correct.

Key Points

Point
A pure spectrum minimises overlap of adjacent colour images.
Dispersive power is relative spread, not absolute deviation.
Narrow slits improve resolution at the cost of brightness.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not equate a broad rainbow with a pure spectrum; slit width affects purity.
The thin-prism ratio assumes small deviations.

Summary: A pure spectrum requires narrow, collimated light and well-separated colour images. Thin-prism dispersive power is ω=(nv−nr)/(ny−1).

Chromatic and Spherical Aberration

#Concept
1Chromatic aberration is colour fringing caused by a lens having different focal lengths for different wavelengths. In ordinary glass, blue/violet light is refracted more strongly than red, so their images do not share one focus.
2Longitudinal chromatic aberration is the axial separation Δf=|fv−fr| of two colour foci. Transverse chromatic aberration is a wavelength-dependent lateral image displacement, often visible at the edge of a wide field.
3Spherical aberration has a different cause: marginal and paraxial rays of the same colour focus at different points because the surface is spherical. It can be reduced with an aperture stop or aspheric surfaces.
4Worked comparison: if red focuses at 50.0 cm and blue at 49.0 cm, longitudinal chromatic aberration is 1.0 cm. This is axial focus separation, not a 1 cm sideways image shift.
5An achromatic doublet pairs converging crown glass with diverging flint glass so two selected colours focus together. Stopping down reduces marginal-ray error but does not remove material dispersion.

Key Points

Point
Chromatic error depends on wavelength; spherical error depends on ray height.
Achromats correct two selected colours; aperture stops reject marginal rays.
Aperture reduction also reduces image brightness.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Stopping down does not make glass non-dispersive.
Primary achromatism reduces selected colour separation but may leave secondary spectrum.

Summary: Chromatic aberration comes from wavelength-dependent focal length; spherical aberration comes from paraxial–marginal focus separation. Their corrections differ.

Achromatism and Its Applications

#Concept
1An optical pair is achromatic for two chosen colours when their wavelength-dependent deviations cancel and both colours share a final focus. The lenses remain dispersive individually; their effects compensate.
2For two thin lenses in contact with signed focal lengths f1,f2 and dispersive powers ω1,ω2, primary achromatism requires ω1/f1+ω2/f2=0. A crown converging lens is commonly paired with a more dispersive flint diverging lens.
3Worked design: f1=+20 cm, ω1=0.02 and ω2=0.04 require 0.02/20+0.04/f2=0, giving f2=−40 cm. The negative sign identifies a diverging flint element.
4The net power is 1/F=1/f1+1/f2, so the doublet can remain converging while cancelling colour spread. Camera, telescope and microscope objectives use achromatic groups to reduce coloured fringes.
5Primary achromatism corrects two selected wavelengths; other colours may still form a secondary spectrum. More elaborate apochromatic systems correct additional wavelengths.

Key Points

Point
Opposite-signed powers let dispersion cancel while net power remains nonzero.
Keep focal-length signs in the achromatism equation.
Two-colour correction can leave secondary spectrum.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not omit the negative focal length of the diverging lens.
The simple condition assumes thin lenses in contact.

Summary: An achromatic doublet balances wavelength dispersion with signed lens powers: ω1/f1+ω2/f2=0. It reduces but does not eliminate every colour error.

Academic Extension: Evidence, Conditions & Open Questions

Topic bridge — Dispersion. Use this extension to connect the topic’s core material with deeper academic inquiry: state a claim, name the conditions under which it holds, and distinguish established knowledge from an open research or interpretive question.

Established academic fact

Ray optics predicts reflection, image formation and refraction well when apertures and objects are much larger than the wavelength. The wave model explains interference and diffraction that ray diagrams cannot.

Conditions, assumptions & limits

A thin-lens or mirror equation assumes ideal geometry and a paraxial approximation (rays close to the principal axis and making small angles). Real aberrations and finite apertures limit the ideal image.

Open research question — What is dark matter made of?

Astronomical observations provide strong evidence for unseen mass through gravitational effects, but its particle identity has not been established. Researchers test candidate explanations by looking for distinct signals; an open question about the cosmos does not make established classroom models false.
