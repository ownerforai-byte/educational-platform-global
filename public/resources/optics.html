Optics — Introduction

#Concept
1Nature of Light: Light is an electromagnetic wave that exhibits both wave and particle (photon) behavior. Speed of light in vacuum c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s.
2Ray Optics (Geometrical Optics): Light travels in straight lines in a homogeneous medium. Valid when object sizes and apertures are much larger than the wavelength λ. Deals with reflection, refraction, and image formation by mirrors and lenses.
3Wave Optics (Physical Optics): Explains interference, diffraction, and polarization — phenomena that cannot be explained by ray optics alone. Required when apertures or obstacles are comparable to λ.
4Fermat's Principle of Least Time: Light travels between two points along the path that takes the least time. This principle derives both the law of reflection and Snell's law of refraction.
5Reflection: Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection (i = r). The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane.
6Refraction: When light passes from one medium to another, it bends. Snell's law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where n is the refractive index of the medium.
7Refractive Index: n = c/v, where v is the speed of light in the medium. Absolute refractive index is relative to vacuum. Relative refractive index ⁿ₂n₁ = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁.

Key Points

Point
Light exhibits both wave and particle (photon) dual nature.
Ray optics is valid when object sizes ≫ wavelength λ.
Wave optics explains interference, diffraction, and polarization.
Fermat's principle of least time derives reflection and refraction laws.
Refractive index n = c/v measures how much light slows in a medium.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing reflection with refraction: Reflection = light bounces back; Refraction = light bends entering a new medium.
Thinking speed changes during reflection: Speed remains the same in the same medium during reflection.
Believing frequency changes during refraction: Frequency stays constant; only wavelength and speed change.
Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal: All optical angles are measured from the normal.
Confusing refractive index with optical density: Higher n means optically denser, not necessarily physically denser.

Summary: Geometrical (ray) optics and wave optics: light obeys reflection and refraction (Snell's law) derived from Fermat's principle; interference, diffraction and polarization demand the wave picture. Ray optics is valid while object sizes and apertures are much greater than the wavelength.

Mirrors and Lenses

#Concept
1Plane Mirror: Forms a virtual, erect, same-sized image behind the mirror. Image distance equals object distance. Lateral inversion occurs (left-right reversed).
2Spherical Mirrors: Concave (converging) and convex (diverging) mirrors. Key terms: pole (P), center of curvature (C), radius of curvature (R), focus (F), focal length (f = R/2), principal axis.
3Mirror Formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u, where u = object distance, v = image distance, f = focal length. Sign convention: distances measured from pole; positive in direction of incident light.
4Magnification: m = -v/u = h_i/h_o, where h_i is image height and h_o is object height. Negative m means inverted image.
5Convex Lens: Converging lens. Thicker at center. Forms real inverted images (except when object within focal length). Lens formula: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u.
6Concave Lens: Diverging lens. Thinner at center. Always forms virtual, erect, diminished images.
7Power of Lens: P = 1/f (f in meters), unit: diopter (D). Convex lens: P > 0. Concave lens: P < 0. Lenses in contact: P = P₁ + P₂ + ...
8Spherical Aberration: Marginal rays focus at different points than paraxial rays. Minimized by using small apertures or parabolic mirrors.

Key Points

Point
Plane mirror: virtual, erect, same-size image, image distance = object distance.
Concave mirror: converging, can form real or virtual images depending on object position.
Convex mirror: diverging, always forms virtual, erect, diminished image.
Mirror formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u with sign convention.
Lens formula: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u with sign convention.
Power P = 1/f in diopters; convex positive, concave negative.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Confusing mirror formula (1/f = 1/v + 1/u) with lens formula (1/f = 1/v - 1/u): The sign before 1/u differs.
Thinking convex mirrors always form real images: Convex mirrors always form virtual, erect, diminished images.
Believing magnification m = v/u without the negative sign: m = -v/u, the negative sign indicates inversion.
Confusing real and virtual images: Real images can be projected on a screen; virtual images cannot.
Using wrong sign convention: Always measure from pole/optical center and follow the Cartesian convention consistently.

Summary: Mirrors and lenses form images by reflection and refraction. Plane mirrors form virtual images; spherical mirrors (concave/convex) and lenses use the mirror/lens formula with sign conventions to determine image position, size, and nature.

N1

#Concept
1Geometrical optics represents light by rays travelling in straight lines through a uniform medium. At reflection, incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane, with angle of incidence equal to angle of reflection.
2At refraction, Snell’s law n1 sin i=n2 sin r uses angles measured from the normal. Frequency stays constant across a stationary boundary while speed and wavelength change.
3Worked example: a ray enters glass with n=1.50 from air at i=30°. sin r=sin30°/1.50=1/3, giving r≈19.5°; the ray bends toward the normal.
4When light goes from higher to lower index, it bends away from the normal. If incidence exceeds the critical angle, total internal reflection occurs, with sin c=n2/n1.
5Ray optics works when apertures and obstacles are much larger than wavelength; diffraction and interference require a wave description when dimensions approach wavelength.

Key Points

Point
Draw the normal before measuring optical angles.
Higher-index entry bends toward the normal.
TIR requires high-to-low index and i>c.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
At i=c the refracted ray travels along the boundary; TIR occurs for i>c.
Frequency does not change at a stationary boundary.

Summary: Reflection, refraction and total internal reflection predict many optical paths. Angles are measured from the normal, and the ray model fails at wavelength-scale apertures.

N2

#Concept
1A plane mirror forms an image where backward extensions of reflected rays meet. The image is virtual, upright, equal in size and as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
2For a spherical mirror, pole P and centre of curvature C define the axis; for paraxial rays the principal focus F is halfway to C, so f=R/2.
3A concave mirror can form real or virtual images according to object position. A convex mirror gives an upright, diminished virtual image for a real object, providing a wide field of view.
4Worked example: a concave mirror has f=10 cm and object distance u=30 cm in a real-positive convention. 1/v=1/10−1/30=1/15, so v=15 cm; m=−v/u=−0.5.
5Principal ray construction uses a ray parallel to the axis reflected through F and a ray through C reflected back along itself. Their intersection gives image position.

Key Points

Point
Object position determines concave-mirror image type.
A convex mirror trades image size for field of view.
The spherical-mirror focus is halfway to C for paraxial rays.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
The displayed mirror equation uses a real-positive convention.
f=R/2 assumes a spherical mirror and paraxial rays.

Summary: Spherical mirrors form images by reflection. Use the paraxial relation f=R/2 and a consistent mirror equation to determine image position and type.

N3

#Concept
1A converging thin lens brings parallel paraxial rays to a real focus; a diverging lens makes them spread as if from a virtual focus. The optical centre and principal foci lie on the principal axis.
2With Cartesian convention and light travelling left to right, the thin-lens equation is 1/f=1/v−1/u. A real object has u<0; a real image has v>0.
3Worked example: f=+15 cm and u=−30 cm give 1/v=1/15−1/30=1/30, so v=+30 cm. Magnification m=v/u=−1: real, inverted and same size.
4A diverging lens has f<0 and forms a virtual, upright, diminished image of a real object. Ray diagrams check signs and whether an image can be caught on a screen.
5Lens power is P=1/f when f is in metres. A short focal length means high magnitude of power, but image size still depends on object placement.

Key Points

Point
Use one sign convention throughout.
Real rays meet; virtual rays only appear to meet.
A virtual image cannot be directly projected onto a screen.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not use the mirror equation in a lens problem.
The simple lens equation assumes a thin lens and paraxial rays.

Summary: Thin-lens equations and ray diagrams connect focal length to image position and magnification. Signed distances encode real/virtual and upright/inverted outcomes.

Mirrors and Lenses

#Concept
1Mirrors form images by reflection; lenses form images by refraction. Principal rays for a spherical mirror include a parallel ray reflected through F and a ray through C reflected back; for a thin lens, a parallel ray passes through (or appears from) F.
2Use the proper signed equations: mirror 1/f=1/u+1/v and thin lens 1/f=1/v−1/u under the stated real-positive mirror and Cartesian lens conventions. Magnification conventions also differ: mirror m=−v/u; lens m=v/u.
3Worked mirror example: concave f=15 cm and object distance u=30 cm give 1/v=1/15−1/30=1/30, so v=30 cm and m=−1: real, inverted and same size.
4Worked lens example: f=+10 cm and real object u=−30 cm give 1/v=1/10−1/30=1/15, so v=+15 cm and m=−0.5: real, inverted and half-size.
5Ray diagrams provide an independent check on equation signs, image orientation, size and whether the image can be caught on a screen.

Key Points

Point
Equations and ray sketches should agree.
Mirrors and lenses use different signed image equations.
Image size depends on object distance as well as focal length.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not copy a mirror formula into a lens problem unchanged.
Simple equations assume paraxial rays and thin optical elements.

Summary: Mirrors and lenses share ray-construction logic but use different signed image equations. Apply the correct convention, then classify image position, orientation and size.

OPTICS N1

#Concept
1Geometrical optics treats light as rays and predicts reflection and refraction without tracking wave phase. It is useful when optical elements are much larger than visible wavelengths.
2At a reflecting boundary i=r; at a refracting boundary n1 sin i=n2 sin r. A higher-index medium has lower light speed, v=c/n.
3When light travels from higher to lower refractive index, refraction bends away from the normal. Above the critical angle c, total internal reflection occurs: sin c=n2/n1.
4Example: glass n1=1.50 to air n2=1.00 gives c=sin⁻¹(1/1.50)≈41.8°. At incidence 50° there is TIR; at 30° the ray refracts into air.
5When an opening approaches the wavelength, diffraction spreads the wave and a ray-only model is insufficient; interference and polarisation also require wave optics.

Key Points

Point
TIR requires high-to-low refractive index and i>c.
Angles are from the normal.
Wave effects matter at wavelength-sized openings.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
A low-to-high index ray cannot undergo total internal reflection.
At exactly the critical angle the refracted ray grazes the boundary.

Summary: Ray optics combines reflection, refraction and total internal reflection to explain common paths; wave optics is needed at wavelength scales.

Reflection at Curved Surface

#Concept
1A spherical reflecting surface acts as a spherical mirror. For paraxial rays, a concave mirror converges parallel rays to a real focus; a convex mirror makes them diverge from a virtual focus.
2The centre of curvature C is distance R from pole P; paraxial focus F is halfway between, so f=R/2. Wide-angle marginal rays do not meet this approximation exactly.
3Using Cartesian distances positive to the right, a concave mirror has f=−15 cm. For u=−30 cm, 1/v=1/f−1/u=−1/15+1/30=−1/30, so v=−30 cm and m=−v/u=−1.
4Construct an image with a ray parallel to the principal axis (reflected through F for a concave mirror) and a ray through C (retraces itself). Their intersection locates a real image; backward extensions locate a virtual image.
5Spherical aberration occurs when marginal rays focus differently from paraxial rays. A smaller aperture reduces it by excluding marginal rays.

Key Points

Point
Keep mirror signs consistent with the stated convention.
R is measured from pole to centre of curvature.
Aperture reduction suppresses marginal-ray aberration.

Common Confusions

❌ / ✅
Do not substitute the thin-lens equation for the mirror equation.
f=R/2 assumes a spherical reflecting surface and small angles.

Summary: Curved-mirror image formation follows reflection geometry. The paraxial focus lies halfway to the centre of curvature, and signed equations agree with ray constructions.
