Unit 4: Classification of Elements & Periodic Trends

Strictly authored according to the official Curriculum Development Centre (CDC) and NEB syllabus.

1. Moseley's Law 2. s, p, d, f Blocks 3. Coinage Metals 4. Volatile Metals 5. Lanthanide Contraction 6. Trend Anomalies

1. Moseley's Experiment & Modern Periodic Law

In 1913, Henry Moseley bombarded various target metals with high-speed cathode rays (electrons) and analyzed the emitted characteristic X-rays. He discovered that the square root of the frequency (√ν) of the emitted X-ray is directly proportional to the atomic number (Z), not the atomic weight:

√ν = a(Z - b) Where ν = frequency of X-ray, Z = atomic number, a & b are constants.

Modern Periodic Law Statement:

"The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers."

NEB Question: "Why did Moseley use atomic number instead of atomic mass?"
Answer: Because atomic number equals the number of nuclear protons, determining the actual nuclear charge and electronic structure, resolving anomalies like Ar (39.9) preceding K (39.1), and Co (58.9) preceding Ni (58.7).

2. Division into s, p, d, and f Blocks

Elements are categorized into four distinct blocks based on the subshell in which the differentiating (last) electron enters:

  • s-Block (Groups 1 & 2): General valence configuration ns¹⁻². Highly reactive, electropositive metals, low ionization energy, characteristic flame colors (except Be and Mg).
  • p-Block (Groups 13–18): Configuration ns² np¹⁻⁶. Encompasses all three physical states (solids, Br₂ liquid, gases) and contains non-metals, metalloids, and post-transition metals. Exhibits the Inert Pair Effect down groups 13-15.
  • d-Block (Groups 3–12): Transition elements with configuration (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻². Characterized by variable oxidation states, colored complex ions, paramagnetic character, and catalytic activity.
  • f-Block (Inner Transition): Configuration (n-2)f¹⁻¹⁴ (n-1)d⁰⁻¹ ns². Arranged in two 15-element series: 4f Lanthanides (₅₇La–₇₁Lu) and 5f Actinides (₈₉Ac–₁₀₃Lr). Subject to steady Lanthanide Contraction.

3. Coinage Metals (Group 11 / IB: Cu, Ag, Au)

Group 11 elements—Copper (Cu), Silver (Ag), and Gold (Au)—are historically known as the Coinage Metals due to their traditional use in minting durable currency.

Cu (Z=29): [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹
Ag (Z=47): [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s¹
Au (Z=79): [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s¹

Why are Coinage Metals classified as Transition Elements despite having d¹⁰ ground states?

According to IUPAC, transition elements must have incompletely filled d-orbitals in either ground state or any of their common oxidation states. Copper forms Cu²⁺ (3d⁹), Gold forms Au³⁺ (5d⁸), both possessing partially filled d-subshells; hence they are true transition metals.

4. Volatile Metals (Group 12 / IIB: Zn, Cd, Hg)

Zinc (Zn), Cadmium (Cd), and Mercury (Hg) have completely filled (n-1)d¹⁰ ns² configurations:

Zn (Z=30): [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² (Zn²⁺: 3d¹⁰)
Cd (Z=48): [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s² (Cd²⁺: 4d¹⁰)
Hg (Z=80): [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s² (Hg²⁺: 5d¹⁰)

Because their d-orbitals remain completely full in both atomic and all common oxidation states, there are no unpaired d-electrons to participate in metallic bonding. Metallic bonds are consequently very weak, resulting in low melting/boiling points (hence volatile metals). Mercury is liquid at standard room temperature.

NEB Question: "Why are Zn²⁺ salts colorless and diamagnetic while Cu²⁺ salts are colored and paramagnetic?"
Answer: Zn²⁺ has a completely filled 3d¹⁰ configuration (all paired electrons; no vacant d-orbital for d-d transition → colorless and diamagnetic). Cu²⁺ has a 3d⁹ configuration with 1 unpaired electron → undergoes visible light absorption via d-d electronic transition → colored (blue) and paramagnetic.

5. Lanthanide Contraction

As we move from ₅₈Ce to ₇₁Lu across the 4f series, each successive electron enters the inner 4f subshell while nuclear charge increases by +1.

"The steady, cumulative decrease in atomic and ionic radii of lanthanide elements with increasing atomic number is called Lanthanide Contraction."

Cause:

The 4f orbitals have a very diffuse, elongated shape and possess an extremely poor shielding effect. Consequently, the increasing effective nuclear charge pulls outer valence shells closer, producing a gradual contraction.

Consequences for NEB Examinations:

  • Similarity of 4d and 5d elements: The atomic radii of Zr (160 pm) and Hf (159 pm), Nb and Ta, Mo and W are almost identical. Chemical separation of Zr and Hf is extremely challenging.
  • Basicity of Hydroxides: Decreases from La(OH)₃ to Lu(OH)₃. As cation size shrinks, covalent character increases (Fajans' rule); hence La(OH)₃ is the most basic, and Lu(OH)₃ is the least basic.

6. High-Yield Periodic Trend Anomalies

Ionization Enthalpy: N > O and Be > B

Nitrogen ([He] 2s² 2p³) has an extra-stable half-filled 2p subshell requiring higher energy to remove an electron than Oxygen ([He] 2s² 2p⁴). Beryllium ([He] 2s²) has a fully filled 2s subshell with higher penetration than Boron ([He] 2s² 2p¹).

Electron Affinity: Chlorine > Fluorine

Although Fluorine is more electronegative, its 2p orbital is extraordinarily compact. Adding an electron creates severe inter-electronic repulsion, making electron gain enthalpy of Chlorine (-349 kJ/mol) more exothermic than Fluorine (-328 kJ/mol).

Density Anomaly: Potassium < Sodium

Potassium (0.86 g/cm³) is less dense than Sodium (0.97 g/cm³) because of an unusually large increase in atomic volume from Na to K caused by the presence of vacant 3d orbitals.
