2.1.1 INTRODUCTION 
The living species are the product of millions  of years of organic evolution on earth. During 
this period many species appeared and disappeared. We are familiar  with only a few species 
which inhabit or visit our surroundings. Even a short look at the small number of species around 
us at once shows diversity of life. A rose appears different from a lotus; a dog appears different 
from a cat. This simple instance is just a glimpse of the vast variety that exists in entire living 
world. The varieties of living things found on the earth are known as biodiversity or biological 
diversity. Biodiversity includes all the plants, animals, microorganisms and fungi. The study of 
varieties  of plants from all sources is termed as floral diversity.  It includes  number of plants 
present,  variation  within and between species  at the genetic  level and role and function  that 
plays in an ecosystem.  
 
2.1.2 THREE DOMAINS OF LIFE 
‘Domains’ are the top level classification  that categorizes the life in the most general way. The 
three  domain  of  life  is  a  biological  classification  discovered  by  Carl  Woese  (1990).  This 
classification  system is also known as “Three Domains Classification”  because it divides the 
life forms  into three domains  on the basis of differences  of rRNA sequence. The domains of 
life are Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya.

2.1.3 BINOMIAL NOMENCLATURE 
There are millions  of plants and animals in the world. The plants and animals in our area are 
known by their  local  names. The same  plants  and animals  are known by different  common 
names in different languages, even within a country. This creates the confusion on recognizing 
the organisms. So, the standard method of naming the organisms is required. Carolous Linnaeus 
develops a system of scientific or technical names for animals and plants. The system is known 
as binomial nomenclature. In this system, each organism is given a name of two words. The 
first  word indicates  the genus or generic epithet.  The second word refers  to the species or 
specific  epithet.  For example:  the scientific  name of rice is written  as Oryza sativa.  In this 
binomial  name, Oryza represents  the  genus  and sativa indicate  a  particular  species.  These 
binomial nomenclatures ensure that a name has not been used for any other organism.  
 
The binomial nomenclature (scientific  name) are established on basis of certain principles and 
criteria  developed  by  number  of  scientists  belonging  to  International  Code  for  Botanical 
Nomenclature (ICBN), International  Code for Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN), International 
Code  for  Bacterial  Nomenclature  (ICBaN)  and  International  Code of  Viral  Nomenclature 
(ICVN).  
 
Some important rules of binomial nomenclature are as follows:  
1. The  Scientific  name  should  be  derived from  Latin  or  Greek  languages  or  should be 
Latinized.  
2. The scientific name consists of two names, in which the first name denotes the generic 
name while second name denotes the specific name. 
3. The  generic  name should always begin with capital letter while specific  name should 
begin from small letter.  
4. The scientific name should be written in italics  if printed or computerized or should be 
separately underlined if handwritten.  
5. The generic  name is always single word and can be abbreviated by using only its first 
letter  followed by  dot if  the generic  name  is  mentioned  earlier  (like, E.  coli)  while 
specific name may be single or compound and never be used in abbreviated form. (like, 
Hibiscus rosa-sinesis) 
6. The  name  of author is  written either in  full  or in  abbreviated form after the name  of 
species. E.g. Mangifera  indica L. Here ‘L’ indicates the name of author i.e. Linnaeus.

1. Complexity of cell structure: Prokaryotic or eukaryotic

5. Phylogenetic relationship:  evolutionary history of an organism

2.2.1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION 
Fungus (latin;  fungour- to flourish)  are non-green, nucleated, non-vascular thallophytes. They 
are eukaryotic  organisms and include yeasts, moulds and mushrooms. The branch of Botany 
that deals with fungi is called mycology. The scientist  who is concerned with fungi is called 
mycologist.  
 
They grow  in  diverse  habitats  i.e.  found  in  almost  every  available  habitat  on earth  where 
organic material is present. They occur in air, water, soil and on animals and plants. They prefer 
to grow in warm and humid places. Fungi are multicellular  except yeast. Except yeast, the body 
of fungus is  made up of long,  slender  thread like  structures  called  hyphae. The network of 
hyphae is known as mycelium. Some hyphae are continuous tubes filled  with multinucleated 
cytoplasm called as coenocytic hyphae while other have septa or cross walls in their hyphae. 
The cell wall is of fungi is made up of chitin.  
 
Fungi are heterotrophic  and absorb soluble organic matter  from dead substrates  and hence are 
called  saprophytes.  Some  are parasites  (e.g. Fusarium). They can also live  as symbionts-in 
association with algae as lichens and with roots of higher plants as mycorrhiza. Reproduction 
in fungi takes place by vegetative, asexual and sexual method.

Systematic Position 
Kingdom : Mycota 
Division : Zygomycota 
Class  : Phycomycetes 
Order  : Mucorales

Occurrence 
It is  commonly  known as black  mould or bread  mould. They are  saprophytic  and occur on 
bread, jam, jelly, cheese, pickle, leather, cattle dung, food stuffs and soil rich in humus.  
 
 
Morphology 
The vegetative bodies of Mucor consist of long, cylindrical, branched, coenocytic hyphae. The 
group of hyphae is known as mycelium. Mycelium consists of three types of hyphae: 
a. Submerged/sub-terranean hyphae: They are highly branched, smaller and penetrate the 
substratum  spreading  inside  the  body  of  substratum.  They  help  in  absorption  of 
nutrients and fixation.  
b. Prostrate  hyphae: They are highly branched and spread in all direction  on the surface 
of substratum  that helps to fix to the substratum. 
c. Aerial  hyphae: They are aerial  hyphae growing  vertically  from  the prostate  hyphae. 
They bear  sporangia  at  its  tip  so  it  is  also  known as  sporangiophore.  They  help  in 
asexual reproduction.  


 
 
 
Figure 2.4.5 Morphology of vegetative body of Mucor 
 
Reproduction 
Mucor reproduce by vegetative, asexual and sexual method. 
 
1. Vegetative reproduction:  
It  takes  place  by fragmentation.  During unfavourable  condition  the hyphae  are fragmented 
mechanically or by decay. On return of favourable condition, these fragments develop into new 
mycelium.  
 
2. Asexual Reproduction: 
Asexual  reproduction  takes  place  by  non-motile  structure  known  as  sporangiospores, 
chlamydospores and oidia,  
 
a. Sporangiospores or spores:  
The reproduction by sporangiophore  takes place during favourable condition. The spores are 
produced inside the sporangia which develop at the apices of special erect aerial hyphae called 
sporangiophores. The sporangium consists of two distinct region-the outer fertile region called 
sporangial  proper  and  inner  dome  shaped  sterile  region  called  columella.  The  nuclei  of 
sporangial  proper  divide  mitotically  for  several  times  and form  number  of  multinucleated 
sporangiospore.  
 


 
 
 
Figure 2.4.6 Reproduction by sporangiospore in Mucor 
  
After maturation, the columella swells and the sporangial wall burst so the sporangiospores are 
liberated out. On reaching a suitable substratum, they germinate to give rise to new mycelium.  
 
b. Chlamydospores:  
They  are  formed  during  unfavourable  condition.  The  protoplasm  of 
hyphae collects at certain places, rounds off accumulates  a lot of food 
materials  and develops  a thick  wall to form  chlamydospores.  During 
favourable  condition,  these  chlamydospores  germinate to  form  new 
mycelium.  
 
 
c. Oidia:  
Hyphae of certain Mucor when growing in a liquid medium 
 rich in sugar, become septate. Then septate cells round  
 off to form reproductive structures called oidia. They take 
 part  in fermentation  and multiply  by budding like  yeast. When taken to 
solid medium, oidia produce new mycelia.  
 
 
 
3. Sexual Reproduction: 
Sexual reproduction in Mucor takes place by conjugation (i.e. gametangial copulation) during 
unfavourable conditions. It takes place between the mycelia of two different mating types (i.e. 
heterothallic).  
During sexual reproduction, two opposite stains of hyphae i.e. +ve strain and –ve strain come 
close together and develop short club-shaped protuberances called progametangia. They come 
in contact at the tip and septum appears in each progametangium  that divides  it into a basal 
suspensor  and  a  terminal  gametangium.  After  sometime,  the  contact  walls  of  the  two 
gametangia dissolve and two gametes fuse together to form zygote having number of diploid

2.2.4 YEAST 
Systematic Position 
Kingdom : Mycota 
Division : Ascomycota 
Class  : Saccharomycetes 
Order  : Saccharomycetales 
Family  : Saccharomycetaceae 
Genus  : Saccharomyces 
 
Occurrence 
They are saprophytic fungi and are cosmopolitan  in distribution.  They grow on rotting fruits, 
cane juice, nectaries of flowers, sweets, decaying organic matter and milk products. Some yeast 
grows as parasites on plants and animals (e.g. Candida albicans).  
 
 
Morphology 
Yeast is non-mycelial unicellular fungi having oval or spherical cell. Sometimes, the yeast may 
form  branched  or  unbranched  temporary  chain  of  up  to  100  cells  and  this  is  known  as 
pseudomycelium. Cell wall of each cell is thin, delicate and made of chitin. Cell membrane lies 
inner  to  cell  wall.  The  cytoplasm  is  containing  the  cell  organelles  like  mitochondria, 
endoplasmic  reticulum,  Golgi  bodies,  ribosome,  and reserve  food  materials  in the  form  of 
glycogen or fats. A large central  vacuole surrounded  by the membrane,  tonoplast  is present. 
Nucleus is attached at one end of a large central vacuole. Such type of vacuole is called nuclear 
vacuole.  


 
 
 
Figure 2.4.10 Structure of Yeast cell 
 
Reproduction 
Yeast reproduces by vegetative and sexual method.  
1. Vegetative method:  
Vegetative method in yeast takes place during favourable condition. It takes place by budding 
and fission method. 
 
a. By budding:  
It is the common method of reproduction in yeast, takes place during favourable condition i.e. 
in sugar solution.  
 
Figure 2.4.11 Budding in Yeast 
During this process, a small  bud like out growth appears at one end of the parent cell which 
gradually  enlarges  in size.  The nucleus enlarges  and divides  amitotically  into  two daughter 
nuclei. One nucleus along with other cytoplasmic  contents  passes into the bud and the other 
remains  in  the  parent  cell.  The vacuole  almost  disappears  at  this  stage.  Gradually  the  bud 
becomes almost of the same size as the parent cell. Wall formation occurs between the bud and 
mother cell. Then the bud behaves as a new individual. During rapid growth, the daughter cell 
also  gives  bud  while  still  attached  to  the  mother  cell,  this  form  false  mycelium  known as 
pseudomycelium.  
 
b. By fission:  
During this process, a somatic cell of yeast enlarges in size and its nucleus is divided into two 
daughter nuclei. Then a partition  wall is formed  at the middle of the cell dividing  the parent 
protoplasm into two halves each having one nucleus. These halves then separate into two cells 
and lead an independent life.  


 
 
 
Figure 2.4.12 Fission in yeast 
 
2. Sexual Reproduction: 
It takes place  during unfavorable  condition. The sex organs are not formed  in yeast and the 
sexual fusion occurs between two vegetative  cells of opposite mating  type, which behave as 
gametangia. It is known as conjugation. Depending upon the prominence of haploid or diploid 
phase, the sexual life cycle of yeast is of following types:  
a. Haplobiontic life cycle: 
This type of sexual life  cycle occurs in Schizosaccharomyces octosporus. In this type of life 
cycle, the haploid phase is dominant than diploid phase.  
 
Figure 2.4.13 Haplobiontic  life cycle of yeast 
Two haploid cells of opposite  strain come together and behave as gametangia.  They develop 
an outgrowth toward each other and come in contact with each other. The contact wall dissolves 
and forms conjugation  tube. The nuclei of two gametangia  in the tube fuse to form a diploid 
zygote. The zygote grows and acts as an ascus. The diploid nucleus divides meiotically  to form 
four haploid nuclei which further divide mitotically to form eight nuclei. Each nucleus develops 
into  an ascospore  within  the  ascus. After  maturity,  the  wall  of  the  ascus  ruptures  and  the 
ascospores are liberated and act as haploid, uninucleate independent vegetative cell of yeast.  
 
b. Diplobiontic life cycle: 
This type of sexual life cycle occurs in Saccharomyces ludwigii. In this type of life cycle, the 
diploid phase is dominant than haploid phase.   
 During unfavourable condition, the diploid somatic cell directly behaves as ascus. The 
nucleus undergoes meiotic cell division to form four haploid somatic nuclei of two mating type 


 
 
that  develop  into  four  ascospores.  The  ascospores  of  the  opposite  matting  types  undergo 
conjugation  inside the ascus. The zygote cell formed  within the ascus develops a germ tube. 
The germ  tubes  break the  ascus  wall and form  a ‘sprout  mycelium’.  The sprout mycelium 
produces several diploid somatic cells.

c. Haplodiplobiontic life cycle:  
This type of sexual life  cycle occurs in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. In this type of life cycle, 
both haploid and diploid phases are equally dominant in the life cycle.  
 During this type of life  cycle, two haploid somatic cells unite to form diploid zygote. 
Zygote also behaves as diploid somatic cell and multiplies  by budding to produce number of 
diploid  somatic  cells.  Each of the  diploid  somatic  cell  behaves as  ascus,  nucleus  of which 
divides  meiotically  to  produce  four  haploid  nuclei.  Each  of  these  nuclei  organizes  into 
ascospores. Ascospores, on liberation  from  ascus  multiply  by budding and produce haploid 
somatic cells.

2.2.6 ECONOMIC IMPORTANCE OF FUNGI 
Fungi have both useful and harmful effects in our daily life.  
 
Useful Effects: 
1. Medicinal value: 
Different types of fungi are used in the production of numbers of drugs. Penicillin, claviformin 
and other antibiotic are extracted from Penicillium species. Vitamin B-complex, vitamin A and 
vitamin  B-12  are  obtained  from Saccharomyces  cerevisiae and Eremothemium  ashbyii 
respectively. Different types of steroids are also synthesized from fungi.  
 
2. Food value:  
Fungi are used as food by humans from a long time ago. The fruiting bodies of mushroom are 
used as food due to their high protein content and have good amount of vitamins, minerals and 
very less amount of fat.  
 
  


 
 
3. Industrial value: 
Fungi are used in the production of different types of commercially  important products such as 
alcoholic  products  (using  yeast  like, Saccharomyces,  Rhizopus,  Penicillium etc),  bakery 
products (using yeast), Cheese (using Penicillium species), enzymes (like, amylase, cellulose, 
invertase  etc.) and other organic acids (like;  citric acid, fumaric acid, lactic acid, oxalic acid, 
etc).  
 
4. Soil fertility: 
Decomposition of litter and wood in the forest takes place by the different types of fungi. Fungi 
like Fusarium, Chaetomium, Aspergillus, etc can decompose the structural  polymers such as 
cellulose, hemicellulose,  lipid, protein, starch etc. By decomposing the organic matters, fungi 
help to increase minerals  and other substances in the soil, hence the fertility  of soil increases.  
 
5. Plant Nutrition: 
The mycorrhizal association of fungi and root of higher plants help in absorption of water and 
minerals. 
 
6. Production of plant hormone: 
Some soil funguses are used to produce plant hormone like Gibberellin.  
 
7. Biological control: 
Some  fungi  are used  to  control  plant  diseases  in  sustainable  disease  management  systems. 
Beauveria bassiana is a naturally occurring fungus in soil has been use for control of soil borne 
insects like beetle etc.  
 
Harmful Effects: 
Fungi are directly or indirectly harmful to the human beings in various ways. They may cause 
diseases of plants, human beings, animals;  spoilage of food, etc. 
 
1. Plant diseases: 
Fungi cause several diseases to varieties  of economic value plants. Such as, club root disease 
in  cabbage,  wart  disease  in  potato,  damping  off  disease  in  various  seedlings,  red  rots  in 
sugarcane etc.  
 
2. Rotting of fruit and food: 
Poor storage of crops and fruits leads to the growth of fungi causing high economic losses like 
Penicillium species  causes  green  rot  on  fruit, Aspergillus species  cause  black  rot  in  fruit, 
Aspergillus flavus cause green rot in grains,  etc. Fungi spoil the vegetables,  dairy  products, 
meat etc.  
 
3. Human and animal diseases: 
Certain fungi are parasite on human and animals causing infection of skin, hair or nails. Aquatic 
fungi live as parasite on egg and gills of fishes causing severe damage to them.  
 
  


 
 
4. Damage of clothes: 
Fungi can grow on wet clothes and shoes hence damage them. Clothes made from natural fibers 
such as cotton, wool, silk are more susceptible than those made from synthetic fibres.  
 
5. Damage of paper and wood: 
Many fungi destroy  the paper of books and newspapers in moist  conditions.  The species  of 
Polyporus cause the damage and decay the timber wood.  
 
6. Mushroom poisoning: 
Poisonous  mushroom  causes  the  different  types  of  health  hazards  in  human  beings  like 
diarrhea, vomiting, gastric disturbance and even death of person.

2.3.1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION

2.4.4 SPIROGYRA 
Systematic Position 
Kingdom : Plantae 
Division : Algae 
Class  : Chlorophyceae 
Order  : Conjugales 
Family  : Zygnemaceae 
Genus  : Spirogyra 
 
Occurrence 
It is one of the common green algae found as free floating masses in the still water of reservoirs, 
tanks, pools, lakes, ditches, and slow running streams  and rivers.  Due to silky long filament 
and slippery nature, it is also known as pond scum or water silk.  
       
Fig: 2.4.4  (a) Spirogyra in habitat (b) Spirogyra filament under microscope   
(c) Spirogyra under high resolution 
 
Plant body 
The plant  body is green, cylindrical  thallus.  It  is thread  like long unbranched  filament.  The 
filament  is  slimy  due  to  presence  of  mucilage  sheath.  Each filament  consists hundreds of 
similar  cells  placed  end  to  end  in  a  single  row. The  young  filaments  are  attached  to  the 
substratum while the adult plants are free floating.  
 
Cell structure 
The cells of the filament  are cylindrical in form and consist of two parts, the cell wall and the 
protoplast.  The cell wall surrounds  the protoplast and is protective  in function. It consists  of 
two layers;  inner  layer  is  made up of  cellulose  and forms  a complete  envelope  around  the 
protoplast  while the outer layer is made of pectin substances. The protoplast is differentiated 
into plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, one or more chloroplast and a central vacuole. The 


 
 
plasma  membrane  surrounds the  cytoplasm.  The  cytoplasm  is  granular  fluid  mass  and 
peripheral due to presence of large central vacuole. The single large nucleus remains suspended 
in the center of the vacuole by a number of cytoplasmic  strands. The cytoplasm  of Spirogyra 
consists  of spirally  arranged ribbon  shape chloroplast.  The number  of chloroplast  varies  in 
number from one to sixteen in different species. In each chloroplast, nodules like protein bodies 
are present. These protein bodies store starch, and is known as pyrenoids.  
 
Fig: 2.4.5 Cell structure of Spirogyra 
Reproduction 
Spirogyra reproduces by vegetative, asexual and sexual method. 
1. Vegetative method:  
Vegetative  reproduction  takes  place  by fragmentation  under  favourable  condition. It  is  the 
common  method  of  reproduction. The  dissociation  of  filament  takes  place  by  mechanical 
injury, death of intercalary cells, etc. Each fragment later independently forms long filament of 
Spirogyra. 
                                        
Fig: 2.4.6 Fragmentation in Spirogyra 
 
2. Asexual method: 


 
 
Asexual  reproduction  is  less  common  in Spirogyra. It  takes  place  by  the  formation  of 
aplanosproes, akinetes and azygospores. Under unfavourable condition, the protoplast of each 
vegetative cell shrinks and develops a wall around it to form aplanospores. Each aplanospore 
germinates  to form  a new filament.  Akinetes  are  thick  walled  cells  of  filament  formed  by 
formation of additional wall under unfavourable condition. On return of favourable conditions, 
each akinete germinates into a new filament. Sometimes the gametes are failed to fuse in some 
species. These gametes  round off and secrete  a thick walled  which surrounds them  to form 
azygospores  or  parthenospores.  Each  parthenospore  gives  new  filament  during  favourable 
condition.  
                                         
Fig: 2.4.7 Asexual reproduction in Spirogyra (A) Akinete (B) Aplanospore  
(C) Azygospore 
 
3. Sexual reproduction 
The Spirogyra shows conjugation  type of sexual reproduction.  Conjugation is an isogamous 
type. It involves the fusion of entire contents of the two morphologically similar unspecialized 
vegetative cells, known as gametangia. Sexual reproduction takes place towards the end of the 
growing season. It is of two types:  
a. Scalariform conjugation (Ladder type):  
It is the most common method and takes place at night between two different filaments  (i.e. in 
heterothallic species of Spirogyra). In this process, two filaments of different strains come close 
and lie  side  by  side. Both  filaments  get  surrounded  by common  sheath  of mucilage.  Now, 
special  conjugating  cells  of  these  two  filaments  develop  small  outgrowths  known  as 
protuberances or papillae in opposite direction. The papillae elongate and come in contact with 
each other. At the point of contact, the tips of these outgrowths dissolve by an enzyme, cytase 
and form hollow tubular structure called conjugation tube. At this stage it resembles as ladder. 
Hence,  the  conjugation  is  called  scalariform  (Gr.  Scala=ladder;  forma=shape).  After  the 
formation of conjugation tube, the protoplasm of one cell functions as male gametangium and 
other cell as female  gametangium.  The protoplasts  of gametangium  shrink  to form  gametes. 
The protoplasmic  content of male gametangia  moves towards the female  gametangia  though 
conjugation  tube and fuse to form zygote. The zygote secrete wall around it, which is called 
zygospore. The cells of another filament remain empty.   


 
 
           
Fig: 2.4.8 Different stages of Scalariform conjugation in Spirogyra 
 
b. Lateral conjugation  
It is of rare type and involves the fusion of gametes from two adjacent cells of the same filament 
in monocious or homothallic  species. It is of two types:  
i. Indirect lateral conjugation: 
Indirect lateral  conjugation is the common method of lateral conjugation. In this conjugation, 
two adjacent cells  of same  filament  function as  male  and female  gametangium.  The lateral 
outgrowth  of the  two adjacent  cells  forms  papillae.  The papillae  grow in size  and come  in 
contact  with each  other. The common  wall between  them dissolves by enzyme and hollow 
tubular outgrowth is formed known as lateral tube. The protoplasmic  content of one cell which 
functions as male gametangium behaves as male gamete and moves through the lateral tube to 
female gametangium which behaves as female gamete. The protoplasmic  content of both male 
and female gametangia is fused to form diploid zygospore.  
 
                   Fig: 2.4.9 Different stages of indirect lateral  conjugation in Spirogyra 
 
ii. Direct lateral conjugation 
It is primitive  type of conjugation occurring in few species. In this conjugation, the lower cell

2.5.1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION 
Bryophytes   (Greek,   bryon-moss,   phyton-plant)   are 
essentially  small  plants  from  a  few millimeters  to  a few 
centimeters.  Bryophytes are photosynthetic,  non-vascular, 
true  embryophytes and  were  among  the  first  plants  that 
lived  in  the  water  and settled  in  terrestrial  environment. 
They  inhabit  almost  all  ecosystems  on  the  earth.  The 
geographical ranges are wider than those of vascular plants 
due  to  the  long  distance  dispersal  of  small  spores,  huge 
survival  rate  and  settling  specific  microhabitats  over  the 
huge areas.  
The bryophyte includes liverworts, hornworts and mosses. 
Bryophytes occupy  a  position  between  algae  and 
pteridophyta. They  are  regarded  as  amphibians  of  plant 
kingdom.  (because  they are  water  dependent  for 
fertilization  while  their  vegetative  structure  is  adapted  to  a  terrestrial  life). The branch  of 
biology that deals  with bryophytes  is called bryology. A German botanist  Johann Hedwig is 
known as father of bryology.  
2.5.2 CHARACTERISTICS FEATURES OF BRYOPHYTES 
Some of the basic features of bryophytes are given below: 
1. Bryophytes usually  grow on damp, moist and shady places. They form a green carpet 
on damp soil, rocks, walls and barks of trees.

2.5.3 CLASSIFICATION 
The division bryophyta has been divided into three classes: 
1. Hepaticopsida (Liverworts) 
The name hepaticopsida comes from the word ‘hepatic’ which means liver. So, ‘Hepaticopsida’ 
literally  means ‘class of liver like plants. The main characteristic  features are given below:  
i. The plant body is gametophyte which is dorsoventral and may be thalloid or foliose 
(i.e. differentiated  into leaves and stem like structures).  Leaf, if present, are without 
mid-rib.  
ii. The ventral surface of thallus is provided with scales and rhizoids. Rhizoids are 
unicellular and unbranched. 
iii. Sex organs are borne on dorsal side of the thallus embedded in tissues.  
iv. The sporophyte may be simple, or differentiated  into foot and capsule, or into a foot, 
seta and capsule.  
v. The sporophyte is completely  dependent on gametophyte for its nutrition. 
vi. The columella is absent in the capsule. 
vii. Spores germinate to produce gametophytic  plant body. A distinct protonema stage is 
absent.  
Examples: Marchantia, Riccia, Porella, etc. 
 
2. Anthocerotopsida (hornworts) 
The name  anthoceroptotsida comes  from  the  Greek  word ‘Antho  and  ceros’  which means 
flower  and  horn  respectively. So,  ‘Anthocerotopsida’  literally  means  ‘class  of  horn  like 


 
 
structure  that  develop  as  part  of  sporophyte  stage  of  the  plant’s  life  cycle. The  main 
characteristic  features are given below:  
i. The plant body is gametophyte which is dorsoventral flattened and thalloid.  
ii. Rhizoids are unicellular  and unbranched with smooth wall. Ventral scales are absent.  
iii. Sex organs are present dorsally embedded in the thallus.  
iv. The sporophyte is elongated, cylindrical and horn like structure. It is divided into 
foot, seta and capsule.  
v. The sporophyte is parasite on gametophyte.  
vi. The columella is present within the capsule. 
vii. Mucilagenous cavities are present inside the thallus, which are invaded by nitrogen 
fixing cyanobacteria.   
viii. Spores germinate to produce protonema stage and then to gametophytic  plant body.  
Examples: Anthoceros, Notothylas, Megaceros, etc.  
 
3. Bryopsida (Mosses) 
The name bryopsida comes from the Greek word ‘bryon’ which means moss. The main 
characteristics  features are given below:  
i. The plant body is gametophyte and is differentiated  into spirally arranged leaves, axis 
and rhizoids.  
ii. Rhizoids are multicellular,  branched and have oblique septa.  
iii. Sex organs are formed at apical portion of stem.  
iv. The sporophyte is differentiated  into foot, seta and capsule.  
v. Sporophyte is parasite on gametophyte.  
vi. The columella is present in the capsule and capsule contains ring of teeth like 
structure known as periostome. 
vii. Spores germinate to produce protonema stage and then to gametophytic plant body.  
Examples: Polytrichum, Funaria, Sphagnum, etc.

Vegetative reproduction takes place by various methods. Some methods are described below:

2.5.5 ECONOMIC IMPORTANCE OF BRYOPHYTA 
 
The bryophytes are used since from stone age when pre-historic  man had made uses of feather 
moss  for  extinguishing  fire,  nappies  etc.  Nowadays,  these  plants  have  attracted 
environmentalists,  botanists, industrialists,  horticulturists  and pharmacists only because of their 
useful  characteristics.  The  economic  uses  of  bryophytes  are  described  in  following  two 
headings:  
 
Figure 2.5.6 V.S. of Sporophyte 


 
 
1. Ecological Importance 
a. Bog succession:  
The mosses  play  an important  role  in  bog succession  from  water  to  climax  forest.  The 
bryophytes especially Sphagnum plants along with other hydrophytes form a dense surface 
covering  over the water  below. This covering  gives the  appearance of the  soil  from  the 
surface. These areas are known as quacking bogs. Later on these bogs are converted into 
swamps. Ultimately these swamps are replaced by the forest growth.  
b. Formation of soil:  
Mosses along with lichens are supposed to be pioneers over the bare rocky surface. They 
grow on rocks and add organic matter to the substratum  after their death that makes the rocks 
suitable for the growth of higher plants.  
c. Food and shelter:  
Numerous invertebrates eat bryophytes, lay their eggs on them or shelter in them. They form 
a vital part of the construction material  of the nest of some birds.  
d. Soil conservation:  
Mosses grow in dense mats over the soil surface. They bind the soil particles and prevent soil 
erosion by running water.  
e. Pollution:  
The bryophytes are very sensitive to air pollution. Both alive and air dried mosses can absorb 
metals. The accumulation of heavy metal in mosses enables them to be used as atmospheric 
and aquatic pollution indicators.    
 
2. Medicinal uses:  
The bryophytes contain numerous potentially  useful compounds so they help to cure 
ringworms, ash of moss is mixed with honey and fat to treat cuts, burn and wounds. 
Liverworts are used for liver disease. Certain bryophytes are also used to obtain a number of 
antibiotic substances. Sphagnum is used in antiseptic absorbent bandage.  
 
3. Packing material:  
Most of the mosses are used as packing material after being dried. They make a fairy good 
packing material in the case of glass ware and other fragile goods.  
 
4. A source of fuel:  
The hardened and compact dark coloured rich in carbon formed by compression of dead 
remains of moss (Sphagnum species) accumulated year after year is called peat. These peats 
are dried, cut into pieces and used as fuel.  
 
5. In experimental Botany:  
The liverworts and mosses have played an important role as research tools in the various field 
of Botany such as genetics, experimental morphology and physiology. 
 
6. Ornamental value:  
Bryophytes have been used for green house crops, potted ornamental plants and in garden 
soil.  
 
7. Industrial Uses:  
Mosses are used to make carpets; peats are used to obtain alcohol, ammonium sulphate, 
paraffin, brown dye, tanning materials,  etc.

Occurrence 
Dryopteris are found in moist and shady places. They are terrestrial  plant, widely distributed

Plant body 
The plant body is  perennial,  evergreen, vascular  sporophytic  and is differentiated  into root, 
stem and leaves.

The roots are adventitious that bear root hair and root cap. They arise from the basal portion of 
stem. The main function  of root  is anchorage and absorption  of minerals  from the soil. The 
stem  is  perennial,  short,  underground and modified  into rhizome.  It  is covered  with brown 
coloured  scales  called  ramenta.  The leaves  are petiolate  and pinnately  compound that arise 
from  the  upper  surface  of  rhizome.  The leaf  is known as frond.  Each  leaf  is  composed  of 
numerous  leaflets  or pinnae. The pinnae are again divided into  small  pinnules. The leaf  has 
long petiole which ends in a long rachis that bears pinnae. Mature leaves are of two types; the 
fertile  leaf  bearing sori called  sporophyll  and sterile  leaf  without sori called  tropophyll. The 
young leaf is rolled up with the tip in the centre known as circinate leaf.  
Reproduction 
 Reproduction in fern takes place by vegetative, asexual and sexual method.  
1. Vegetative method: 
It takes place by fragmentation. The older part of the stem dies and decays upto dichotomy and 
branches get separated. Then, each branch develops into new plant. 
2. Asexual method: 
It is the process of reproduction  by the formation  of spores. The plant body of Dryopteris is 
sporophyte. The spores are produced in sporangia which are found in sorus (pl. sori). The sori 
are found on ventral surface of sporophyll. Each sorus is covered by kidney shaped protective 
covering called indusium that contains numerous sporangia. Each sporangium has oval capsule 
and multicellular  stalk. The capsule wall consists  of two distinct  region; thick walled region 
known as annulus and thin walled region known as stomium.  The capsule consists  of 12-16 
diploid spore mother cells.  


 
 
 
  Figure 2.5.3  Internal Structure of Sorus 
 
The spore mother  cells  undergo meiotic cell  division  to form  48-64 haploid  spores. All  the 
spores are of similar type hence, the plant is homosporous.  
2. Sexual Reproduction: 
After maturation of spores, the capsule wall breaks at stomium and spores are dispersed. After 
falling on a suitable soil the spores germinate to gametophytic stage known as prothallus.  
 
 
The prothallus is haploid, dorsiventrally  flattened, multicellular,  green, heart shaped structure. 
It is monoecious i.e. male and female reproductive organs are present on same thallus. At the 
anterior  portion  it  consists  of  apical  notch.  Ventral  surface  bear  rhizoids  which  help  in 
anchorage and absorption of water and nutrients from the soil. It is independent and the mode 
of nutrition is autotrophic. The female sex organs are developed near the apical notch while the 
male sex organs are developed at posterior rhizoidal region.  
An antheridium  is  a  multicellular  oval  or  rounded  structure  without  stalk.  It  consists  of 
androcytes  which  metamorphose  into  multiflagellate  antherozoids  or  spermatozoids.  The 
antheridial  wall breaks when it absorbs water, then the antherozoids  are liberated and are set 
free to swim in water.  
An archegonium  is  flask shaped;  consist  of large basal  venter and narrow neck. The venter 
consists of large egg cell and venter canal cell. The neck consists of binucleate neck canal cell.

Figure 2.5.3  Structure of archegonium 
 
After maturation  of archegonium, venter canal cell and neck canal cell disintegrate  to form a 
passage. The passage is filled with mucilage and malic  acid. The malic  acid of archegonium 
attracts the swimming antherozoids liberated from antheridia. Some of them enter the passage 
of archegonium, but only one antherozoid fertilize  with egg to form diploid zygote (Oospore).  
Although there are several archegonia in the prothallus  only one is fertilized.  The oospore is 
the beginning  of sporophytic  generation  of fern  plant. The zygote divides  and redivides  by 
mitotic cell division to form embryo, which later develops into sporophyte. Initially, the young 
sporophyte  is  fully  dependent  on prothallus.  Once the  roots  are  developed,  the  sporophyte 
becomes fully independent and then the prothallus disintegrates.

Pteridophhtes are economically very important.  Some are mentioned below:

Many  ferns  are  grown  as  ornamental  plants  in  gardens  for  their  large,  showy  and

Pteridophytes serve as a good source of animal food. The young leaf tips of ferns (like,-

3. Soil Binding: 
Pteridophytes prevent  soil  erosion  by  binding  the  soil  even  along  hill  slopes.  Many 
pteridphytes species are used in soil conservation. 
 
4. Medicines: 
Some  pteridophytes  are  of  medicinal  value.  The  spores  of Lycopodium have  been

6. Forensic Investigation: 
Moss  spores are  used in forensic investigations  as finger print powder. It is also  used 
as flash powder for photography. 
 
7.  Shelter for organisms:  
Pteridophyte  provides  habitat  for  a  variety  of  small   organisms,   such  millipods, 
arthropods, larvae, bacteria and cyanobacteria. It also gives shelter to fish and frog eggs.  
 
8. Nitrogen fixation: 
Azolla (a water fern) has a symbiotic association  with nitrogen fixing cyanobacterium. 
It is inoculated to paddy fields to function as biofertilizer.

2.7.1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION 
Gymnosperms (Greek: Gymnos-naked; sperma-seed)  are naked seed plants because seeds are 
not enclosed in fruit. They are more ancient than the angiosperms in evolutionary terms. They 
were dominant  in Jurassic and cretaceous period  of Mesozoic era. Most of the gymnosperm 
have now become extinct.   
 
The members are distributed  throughout the temperate  and tropical  region of the world. They 
are widely distributed  in the cold climates where snow, rather than rain, is the source of water. 
Gymnosperms are mostly  perennial, xerophytic  and evergreen plants. They are woody trees, 
shrubs and rarely climbers. Aquatic and herbaceous forms are totally absent. Theophrastus first 
used the word gymnosperm.

2.7.3 STRUCTURE AND REPRODUCTION IN PINUS 
Structure:  
Pinus are  large,  perennial  and  evergreen  tall  tree.  It  is  worldwide  in  distribution  mostly 
confined to northern hemisphere. They are mostly present in the temperate  regions. The plant 
body is sporophyte. The plant bears longer branches towards the base and progressively shorter 
branches towards the upper region, giving a pyramid like appearance. The plant body is divided 
into root, stem and leaves. It has tap root system  with lateral  roots. The lateral roots are well 
developed and spread over a large area. The root hairs  are replaced by mycorrhizal  roots  in 
older lateral  root. In mycorrhizal root, there is a symbiotic  association with fungi. The root 
helps in absorption and fixation. The stem is cylindrical, erect and remains covered with bark. 
The main trunk is unbranched. It has branches confined to the apical region. These branches 
form a characteristics  canopy i.e. pyramid  like appearance. The branches are dimorphic  (i.e. 
two types of branches) - Branches of unlimited growth or long shoots which contain apical bud 
and grow indefinitely;  whereas Branches of limited  growth or dwarf shoots which are devoid 
of any apical  bud, thus are limited  in their  growth. There are two types  of leaves, the scale 
leaves  and  the  green  foliage  leaves  (modified as needles).  The  scale  leaves  are  small, 
membranous and brownish in colour and are protective in function. They present in both long 
and dwarf shoot. The foliage leaves are green and needle like present in dwarf shoot only. The 
dwarf shoot with cluster of foliage leaves are known as ‘spurs’. The number of needles in each 
spur varies from species to species. The foliage leaves helps in photosynthesis.  
 
Reproduction:  
 
Pinus are monocious (i.e. male and female reproductive organ developed in same individual). 
The male and female reproductive structures,  known as cones or strobili  develop on different 
branches of the same tree.  


 
 
 
Male cone

The male cones are much smaller. They are produced in clusters near the tip of the long shoots. 
Each male  cone is oval in shape and ranges  from 1.5 cm to 2.5 cm in length. It  consists  of 
spirally arranged specialized  leaves known as microsporophylls  or stamens around the central 
cone axis. Each microsporophyll  bears two sacs like microsporangia (pollen sac) on the ventral 
side.  The  microsporangia  produce  a  large  number  of  microspores  (pollen  grains).  Each 
microspore or pollen grain is a rounded and yellow-coloured, light, uninucleate structure  with 
two outer covering an inner intine and an outer exine. It has ballon like wings. The wings help 
in the dispersal of spores by wind.  
        
Female cone 
The female cones develop laterally  in the axil of scale leaves. They are formed in clusters  in 
place of long shoots. They are produced on the lower branches from those of the male cones. 
There may be one to four cones on each long shoot. Each cone consists of central axis bearing 
spirally  arranged scales. The scales are of two types; bract scales and ovuliferous  scales. The 
bract scales are thin membranous and are directly attached to the central axis. The ovuliferous 
scales are present on the ventral surface of each bract scale. Each ovuliferous scale bears two 
ovules (megasporangia),  situated  side by side on upper side. The ovule (megasporangium) is 
inverted (i.e. anatropous) has a mass of nucellar tissue surrounded by a single integument. The 
micropylar end of the ovule is directed towards the central axis. The diploid megaspore mother 
cell  present  in  the  nucellus  of  ovule  undergoes  meiosis  and  forms  a  linear  tetrad  of  four 
megaspores.  Upper  three  megaspores  degenerate  but  the  lowermost  megaspore  becomes 
functional.  The functional  megaspore (embryo  sac) increases  in size and occupies the major 
part of the nucellus. This functional megasprore develop into female gametophyte.

Pollination and Fertilization 
Microspore  is  the first  cell  of  male  gametophyte.  It  germinates  inside  microsporangium.  It 
divides and forms 4-celled stage (2-prothalial  cell, 1-antheridial  cell and 1-tube cell). These 4-
celled  stage  pollen  grains  released  and pollination  takes  place.  The pollination  in Pinus is 
anemophilous  (i.e.  takes  place  by  wind).  At  the  time  of  pollination  the  ovule  secrete 
mucilaginous  fluid from the micropyle and pollen grains entangled on it. These pollen grains, 
then, draws into the pollen chamber and remain inactive for few months. Then, tube cell project 
to form pollen tube, which elongate towards the archegonium. The antheridial cell divides to 
form stalk  cell and body cell. The body cell again divides to form  two motile  unequal male 
nuclei.  
 
The functional  megaspore is the first cell of the female gametophyte. Two to four cells of the 
female gametophyte at the micropylar end function as archegonial initials and form two to four 
flask shaped archegonia. Each archegonium consists of a large venter, a short neck and a very 
large egg or oosphere. The neck is without neck canal cells.  
 
The fertilization  takes place after about one year of pollination. The pollen tube containing two 
male nuclei elongates and penetrates the archegonium through micropyle and reaches up to the 
egg.  One of  the  male  nuclei  fuses  with  the  egg  forming  diploid  zygote  (oospore)  and  the 
remaining  one get degenerated. The zygote is the first  cell of sporophyte. The fertilized  egg 
(Zygote) undergoes mitotic  division and develops into embryo. After fertilization,  integument 
and megasporangium  are  converted  into  seed coat  and seed respectively.  A mature  embryo 
consists  of  radicle  towards the  micropylar  end  and  plumule  towards  the  inner  side.  It is 
surrounded by cotyledons. Some part of the ovuliferous scale fuses with the developing seed 
to form wing, which helps in dispersal  in air. Seeds are generally  dispersed  by wind. Under 
favourable conditions, the seed germinates into new sporophyte.

2.8.1 INTRODUCTION TO ANGIOSPERM 
The term angiosperms  (Greek; angios-enclosed, sperma-seed) means ‘enclosed seed’ because 
the ovules or seeds are enclosed within a hollow ovary. They are the most common among land 
plants and form the largest group of plant kingdom. These plants are found almost everywhere 
in  all  possible  type  of habitat  and climate  and may  be annual,  biennial  or  perennial  herbs, 
shrubs,  trees  and  climbers.  Angiosperms show  great  diversity  both  in  vegetative and  in 
reproductive structures. They show variation in life span, size, form, habit, etc.

1. THE ROOT 
The  root  is  generally  non-green,  underground  and 
cylindrical part of the plant developed from the radicle 
of  the  embryo.  It  is  negatively  phototropic  without 
nodes and internodes. The whole extent of the roots of 
a plant is called the root system. The development  of 
the  root  system  differs  fundamentally  in  vascular 
plants and may be classified in two groups: 
 
 
 
a. Tap root system:  
The radicle elongates to form primary  or main 
root  which  grows inside  the soil.  It produces 
number  of  lateral  roots  called  as  secondary 
roots,  which  further  bear  tertiary  roots  and 
rootlets.  The  primary  root  and  its  branches 
constitute the tap root system. Tap root system is 
generally  found in dicot plants.  
 
b. Fibrous root system:  
In  monocot  plants,  primary   root  arises from 
radicle,  does  not  develop  further  and  soon  is 
replaced by many  thin roots developed from the 
base of stem.  It is known as fibrous root system. 
In some plants, such as grass, sugarcane,  banyan 
tree,  roots  develop  from  part  of  plant  other 
than  the  radicle  and  are  called  adventitious 
roots.  
 
Morphologically  roots  have  four  distinct 
regions – Root  cap,  meristematic  zone,  elongation 
region and maturation  region. Root cap is terminal 
cap like structure. It protects tender apex of root. Just 
behind the root cap a region of small and thin walled 
cells  are found known as meristematic  zone. They 
divide  repeatedly  and  increase  cell  number.  The 
newly formed cells lose the power of cell division but 
undergo rapid elongation and enlargement which are 
responsible  for  the  increase  in  length  of  root. This

2. THE STEM 
The stem is the axis of aerial part of the plant. It shows 
positive  phototropic  and  negative  geotropic  growth. 
Stem arises from plumule of embryo in a germinating 
seed.    It  has nodes and internodes.  Branches,  leaf, 
flower bud, fruits and bracts are developed from nodes. 
The portion between two nodes is known as internode. 
The stem  is  generally  green  during  young stage  and 
later  often  become  woody  and  dark  brown.  The 
growing apex of the stem remains covered by a number 
of tiny leaves which arch over it. Buds are the stem’s 
primary  growing  points  which  may  be  terminal  or 
axillary. Terminal  buds are located  at  the  tip  of the 
stem  which help in increment  of length  of plant. The 
axillary bud is formed at the junction  between a leaf 
and the stem. It produces the lateral branches. There are number of minute pores on stems that 
allow for gas exchange called as lenticels. The stem is covered by outer protective layer known 
as bark.  
 
Functions of stem: 
i. The main stem is usually thick, aerial  and stout and keeps the plant in erect position.  
ii. The  stem conducts water and minerals  from the root to the leaf. It also transports  the 
prepared food from the leaf to the different parts of plant body.  
iii. Underground modified stem helps to store the food. 
iv. In fleshy stem of cactus help to store the water. 
v. The young green stem in certain plants are modified which helps in photosynthesis. 
vi. Thorns, the modified form of stem help to protect the plant from grazing animals. 
vii. Many aerial,  sub-aerial  and underground stem help in vegetative propagation.

3. THE LEAF 
The leaf is a lateral generally  flattened structure  borne on the stem. The leaves develop from 
the nodes and have axillary buds in its axil. It is normally green in colour and its main function 
is photosynthesis. All the leaves of a plant are known as phyllome. Leaves originate from shoot 
apical meristem  and are arranged in acropetal order.  
 
Parts of leaf 
 
 
 
A typical leaf consists of three main parts: leaf base, petiole and lamina. The leaves are attached 
to stem  by leaf  base and may bear lateral  small  leaf  like structures  called  stipules.  The leaf 
having stipule is called stipulate and leaf without stipule is called exstipulate. The petiole is the 
stalk of leaf that holds the blade to light. It connects the lamina with branch of stem. The leaf 
with petiole  is called  petiolate  leaf  while the  leaf  without petiole  is called  sessile  leaf. The 
lamina or the leaf blade is the green flattened part of the leaf with network of veins. The lamina 
is the most important  part of the leaf since this is the site  of food manufacture  for the whole 
plant. The vein that runs from petiole to the apex is called as mid rib or main vein. The mid rib 
produces thinner lateral veins which further give rise to veinlets.  
 
The arrangement of veins and veinlets in the lamina of leaf is called as venation. When 
the veinlets  form  a network, the venation is  called reticulate  venation (e.g. leaves of dicot 
plant).  When the veins  run parallel  to each other  within  a lamina,  the venation  is  called  as 
parallel venation (e.g. leaves of monocot plant).

Parts of flower 
A typical flower consists of four different types of whorls present on thalamus.  
1. Calyx:  The unit of calyx is  called sepal.  It is  the outermost whorl, generally green  in 
colour  (sepaloid)  or  sometime  colourful  (petaloid) that  protects the  flower  in  bud 
condition. The sepals may be free (i.e. polysepalous)  or fused (i.e. gamosepalous).  
 
2. Corolla : The unit of corolla is called petal. It is variously coloured whorl of flower. Its 
bright coloured petals attract the insects which help in pollination. The free petals are 
termed as polypetalous and fused petals are termed as gamopetalous.  
 
Calyx and corolla  are accessory  organs. In some flowers the calyx and corolla  are not 
distinct and constitute single  whorl known as perianth. The unit of perianth is termed

other members  of the same  whorls is  known as aestivation.  Following  are the major 
types of aestivation:  
a. Valvate – sepals and petals in a whorl just touch one another at the  margin  without 
overlapping. E.g. Mustard, Custard apple etc.  
b. Twisted – margin of adjacent sepals or petals overlap regularly. E.g. Hibiscus, Lady 
finger etc.  
c. Imbricate – sepals  and petals overlap each other but not in any regular  manner. E.g. 
Gulmohar, etc.  
d. Vexillary – Occurs in  pentamerous flower. In this the largest one overlaps  the two 
adjacent which in  turn overlap  two smallest  anterior.  It is  also  known as  descending 
imbricate. E.g. pea, bean etc.

Basal  placentation:  a  single  ovule  is  borne  at  the  base  of  unilocular  ovary.  E.g. 
sunflower, marigold etc.

6. THE FRUIT 
Fruit is one of the characteristics  of flowering plant. After fertilization, the ovule forms  seed 
and ovary developed into fruit. So, fruit is defined as “Matured or ripened ovary, developed 
after fertilization.” Sometimes,  a fruit  is formed without  fertilization  of ovary; this  type of 
fruit is called as parthenocarpic fruit which is seedless (e.g. seedless banana, seedless grapes 
etc). When only  the ovary of the  flower develops  into  the fruit,  it  is  called true fruit (e.g. 
mango, lichhi etc). In some cases the portions other than ovary, i.e. thalamus or calyx may also 
take part in the development of the fruit; these types of fruit are called false fruit (e.g. apple, 
pear etc.) 
 
Parts of fruit  
Generally,  the  fruit  consists  of two parts  i.e.  pericarp  (develop  from  ovary wall)  and seeds 
(develop from ovules). The pericarp may be dry or fleshy. When pericarp is thick and fleshy, it 
is differentiated  into epicarp, mesocarp and endocarp. Epicarp is the outer part that forms the 
skin of fruit. Mesocarp is edible and fleshy part present inner to epicarp. Endocarp is innermost 
hard region that encloses the seeds.

2.8.3 CONCEPT OF TAXONOMY 
Taxonomy is the science of naming, describing and classifying organisms including all plants, 
animals  and microorganisms,  based on morphological,  behavioural, genetic  and biochemical 
variations. In short, taxonomy is defined as ‘the branch of biology that deals with identification, 
nomenclature and classification  of organisms.  
Identification  is the process of finding the correct name and place of an organism in a system 
of classification. Nomenclature is the system of giving proper names or scientific  names to all 
the organisms so that these can be easily distinguished  from one another. Classification  is the 


 
 
system  of  grouping  the  organisms  according  to  their  resemblances  or  differences  in 
characteristics.  Carolus Linnaeus is considered as the father of taxonomy. He is the one who 
developed a procedure to name and organize species.  
 
Systematics  is  the  science  that  concerns  with  identification,  nomenclature,  description  and 
classification  of organisms based on unique properties of every species and groups of species 
at every level of classification  from species to kingdom. It helps to understand the relationship 
among diverse species.  
 
2.8.4 TAXONOMIC HIERARCHY 
A taxon (plural: taxa) is a group of organisms that are classified as a unit. It means a category 
or  taxonomic  group  of  any  rank  like  species,  genus,  family  etc.  Taxonomic  hierarchy  or 
hierarchial  system  was  proposed  by  Linnaeus.  It  includes  seven  categories  arranged  in 
descending order from Kingdom to species. The arrangement of taxonomic categories in their 
proper order  on the basis  of their  taxonomic  ranks is called  taxonomic  hierarchy. There are 
seven obligate  taxonomic categories  in taxonomic  hierarchy like kingdom, phylum/division, 
class, order, families,  genus and species. These taxonomic  categories are further  classified  as 
major  categories  (like- Kingdom,  phylum/division,  class,  order  and  families)  and  minor 
categories (like-genus and species).  
 
Taxonomic Categories 
a. Species: It is  the basic  unit  of  taxonomy.  It consist  of  closely  resemble  individuals 
which are genetically distinct, interbreed freely and produce fertile offspring.  
b. Genus: It is the group of species which have common characters. 
c. Family: It is the group of one or more genera having common characters. 
d. Order: It is the group of family with common characters.  
e. Class: It is the group of order with common characters.  
f. Phylum/Division: It is the group of class  with common characters.  The term division 
is used for plant and the term phylum is used for animals.  
g. Kingdom: Kingdom is  the highest  category in taxonomy hierarchy.  It is  the group  of 
phylum/division  having common characters.

2.8.5 CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM 
Classification  is  defined  as  the  system  of  grouping  the  organisms  according  to  their 
resemblance  or differences  in characteristics.  Organisms  have been classified  from different 
points of view at different times. The various types of classification  are described below:  
a. Artificial system: 
It is based on one or a few easily observed characters (like habit, habitat, size, etc.) rather than 
on natural  or evolutionary  relationships.  It is  easier  and quicker  method  of classification  of 
organisms in the field. For example, the grouping of plants into herbs, shrubs and trees. There 
are many drawbacks in this system such as lack of detail information, heterogeneous grouping 
of organisms, separation of closely related organisms etc. Example: Linnaeus Classification.  
 
b. Natural system: 
It is based on large number of characters and utilizes  all the information  available at the time. 
This system of classification  gives us a true idea about natural relationship existing among the 
organisms.  This  system  of  classification  avoids  the  heterogeneous  grouping  of  organisms. 
Example: Bentham and Hooker’s classification  system.  
 
c. Phylogenetic system: 
It is based on detailed characters of organisms as well as proper phylogenetic and evolutionary 
characters. It enables to determine the ancestors of any taxon. However, it seems a bit difficult 
to  classify  perfectly  on  the  basis  of  evolutionary  tendencies due  to  imperfection  of  fossil 
records.  So,  at  present  the  organisms  are  classified  partly  according  to  natural  and  partly 
according to phylogenetic basis. E.g. Engler and Prantl’s system of classification.
