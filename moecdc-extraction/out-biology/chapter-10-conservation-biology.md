10.1.1 CONCEPT OF BIODIVERSITY 
Biodiversity,  or  biological  diversity,  encompasses  the wide array  of life  on Earth from  tiny 
microbes  to large  whales. Out of an estimated 8.7 million  eukaryotic species, only 1.4 million 
have  been  identified, leaving  86%  of land  and  91%  of  marine  species undiscovered.  These 
diverse species  are  adapted to specific  environments,  ranging  from mountain  peaks to ocean 
depths and polar ice caps to rainforests. Biodiversity includes all life forms—plants, animals, 
fungi,  and microorganisms - along  with  the communities  they  create  and  the  habitats  they 
inhabit. In essence, biodiversity, a blending word of ‘biology’ and ‘diversity’, encapsulates the 
rich variety of life and ecosystems found on our planet which also refers to the variety among 
living  organisms  in  terrestrial,  marine,  and aquatic  ecosystems,  comprising  diversity  within 
species, between species, and of ecosystems. It holds significance in both human-managed and 
natural ecosystems, providing essential goods and services that sustain human society. 
In other words, biodiversity is the umbrella  term which includes the number and frequency of 
ecosystem,  species  and genes  in  a  given  assemblage  or  the totality of ‘genes,  species  and 
ecosystems’ of a region.

LEVELS OF BIODIVERSITY 
There are mainly three levels  of biodiversity: 
 
Figure 10.1.1 Different levels of biodiversity 
 
a. Species Diversity:  Species diversity is defined as the variety in the number and richness  of 
the species  of a  region  or the count of different species  coexisting  within a community.  The 
greater  the variety  of species,  the healthier  the ecosystem,  forming  crucial  links  in  the food 
chain and web. 
 
b. Ecological Diversity (community  and ecosystem  diversity): It is the most extensive level 
of biodiversity. It encompasses  the number and variety of interactions in ecosystems  within a 
specific  area.  Earth  showcases  ecosystem  diversity  with  diverse  environments  like  forests, 
oceans, deserts, wetlands, and grasslands.  
 
c. Genetic Diversity: All the life forms, spanning from microbes to plants and humans, possess 
genes carrying distinct traits and characteristics. Genetic diversity is the diversity in the number 
and types of genes  as well  as  chromosomes  present  in different species  and the variation  of 
genes and alleles  in the same  species.  A larger  population  for reproduction offers more  gene 
combinations in the offspring. 
 
Importance of biodiversity 
Biodiversity is crucial for following purposes: 
i. Preserving the variety of life forms. 
ii. Responsibly utilizing  ecosystems, species,  and genetic resources. 
iii. Ensuring fair distribution of benefits from genetic resource utilization. 
iv. Providing  recreational,   cultural,   and  spiritual   nourishment   for  personal   and  social 
wellness. 
v. Maintaining  and  functioning  the  ecosystems  which  offers  essential  services  such  as 
oxygen supply, clean air and water, pollination, pest control, and wastewater treatment. 
 
  


 
 
BIODIVERSITY IN NEPAL 
Nepal  boasts  rich  biodiversity,  housing  valuable  flora  and  fauna.  Our  country’s  unique 
assemblage  of diverse flora and fauna is because of varied topography and climatic  condition 
within a  short distance.  The  country  features six  different biomes,  35 forest types,  and 118 
ecosystems  classified  based  on  their  altitude,  variations  in  climate  and  vegetation.  This 
diversity includes  numerous  endemic species,  making  Nepal a heaven for unique  and varied 
life forms. 
 
LOSS OF BIODIVERSITY 
The  permanent  loss  of  different kinds  of  plants,  animals,  and  other  living  things  is  called 
extinction of biodiversity. This happens when species disappear from the Earth forever. If these 
species  disappear,  it can  disrupt the balance  of nature and harm  ecosystems.  The  world has 
faced species  extinction  at an  alarming  rate mainly  because  of human  interference.  Nepal is 
also facing a significant decline in biodiversity, contending with various threats. That's why it's 
important for everyone  to work together to protect Nepal's biodiversity  and the animals  that 
call it home.  
 
Major causes of decline of biodiversity are: 
i. habitat encroachment 
ii. poaching 
iii. unsustainable resource use 
iv. introduction of alien or invasive species 
v. human-wildlife conflicts 
vi. filling up wetlands 
vii. climate change 
viii. overgrazing 
ix. invasive species 
x. natural disasters 
xi. pollution due to large-scale infrastructure projects.

10.1.2 BIODIVERSITY CONSERVATION 
Biodiversity conservation involves safeguarding and scientifically managing diverse life forms 
to sustain their levels and provide lasting benefits for current and future generations. It protects 
plant,  animal,  microbial,  and genetic resources  crucial  for food production, agriculture,  and 
ecosystem functions like soil fertilization, nutrient recycling,  pest control, erosion  prevention, 
and crop and tree pollination.  Conservation practices aim  to manage nature and resources  for 
present and future generations, ensuring the sustained well-being of humanity. 
 
There are two basic strategies of biodiversity conservation: 
a. In-Situ Conservation (within  natural habitat): This  method protects organisms  in  their 
natural habitats like national parks and other protected areas. For instance, in Chitwan National 
Park, we have centers for Elephant Breeding, Gharial Conservation, and Vulture Conservation. 


 
 
b. Ex-Situ Conservation  (outside  natural  habitat): This  type  of  conservation  strategy 
includes  protection  of animals  outside their  natural  habitats  such  as  botanical  or  zoological 
gardens, cryopreservation, seed gene banks etc. 
 
10.1.3 NATIONAL PARKS IN NEPAL 
A National Park  is an area  dedicated to safeguarding and nurturing  wildlife and their  natural 
habitats  for  scientific,  educational,  and recreational  purposes.  In Nepal,  these  parks  aim  to 
conserve  the  environment,  including  plants,  animals,  and scenic  landscapes,  ensuring  their 
proper  management  and utilization.  National  parks  hold  scientific,  aesthetic,  and ecological 
significance,  strictly  prohibiting  activities  like  wildlife  poaching,  tree  cutting,  grazing,  and 
settlement. These  protected zones  contribute to eco-tourism  development and are recognized 
by the IUCN. Nepal boasts 12 national parks, covering approximately 23.39% of the country's 
land, totaling about 10,288 sq. km.

10.1.4 WILDLIFE RESERVE OF NEPAL 
Wildlife reserves are dedicated areas for conserving and managing animals  and their habitats. 
They  focus on  protecting rare,  endangered, or  specific  animals  by carefully  managing  their 
living  spaces  to increase  their  numbers.  Similar  to national  parks,  activities  like  poaching, 
hunting, tree cutting, grazing domestic animals,  and constructing houses are strictly forbidden 
in  wildlife  reserves.  Entry  for  people  and  facilities  that  could  impact  wildlife  negatively, 
including  tourism-related  development,  is  also  restricted.  Nepal  has  one  Wildlife  Reserve, 
namely  Koshi Tappu,  covering  a total area of 175 sq. km.  This  reserve  serves  as a sanctuary 
for the protection of wildlife. 
 
1. KOSHI TAPPU WILDLIFE RESERVE 
Area  : 176 sq. km.  
Established : 1976 
Location : Saptari and Sunsari (Eastern Nepal) 
Features: 
i. Koshi Tappu Wildlife Reserve experiences  three seasons. 
ii. It is primarily  composed of tall grasslands. 
iii.  the  Predominantly  tall  khar-pater   grassland  with  khair-sissoo   scrub   forest  and 
deciduous mixed riverine forest. 
iv. The reserve is a valued habitat for a variety of wildlife, primarily  wild buffalo (around 
159) and 20 other animal  species  like  Hog deer ,Wild boar , Spotted deer, Blue bull, 
Rock Python, etc. 
v. The  Koshi  Barrage  is  a  vital  resting  place  for several  migratory  birds,  including  87 
winter and trans-Himalayan migratory species. 
vi. Koshi river  is home for 80 species of fish, endangered Gharial crocodile and Gangetic 
dolphin.

10.1.5 HUNTING RESERVE 
An area where, in the past, people were legally permitted to hunt wild animals for food or sport. 
Currently, Nepal  strictly prohibits  hunting through conservation laws.  However, there is  one

Blue Sheep and Himalayan  Thaar  is  permitted. The  Dhorpatan Hunting Reserve caters to the 
hunting  needs  of both  Nepalese  and foreign  hunters  specifically  targeting  Himalayan  Blue 


 
 
Sheep and Himalayan  Tahr. Dhorpatan Hunting Reserve stands as the sole  hunting reserve  in 
Nepal. 
 
DHORPATAN HUNTING RESERVE 
Area  : 1325 sq. km.  
Established : 1983 (gazetted in 1987) 
Location : Rukum, Myagdi and Baglung 
Features: 
i. Dhorpatan Hunting Reserve is the only hunting reserve in Nepal. 
ii. Its height ranges from 3000 m to over 7000 m. 
iii. Meadows above 4000 m, called patans, are vital for animals  like  blue sheep and other 
plant-eating animals. 
iv. The reserve is split into six parts for managing hunting. 
v. Grazing  of livestock  starts in  February  and continues  till  October,  with over  80,000 
animals  entering the reserve yearly. 
vi. Its common plant species include fir, pine, birch, rhododendron, hemlock, oak, juniper 
and spruce. 
vii. Above 50% of the area of the reserve at altitude is pastureland. 
viii. This area is a top spot for blue sheep, which are highly sought after for hunting. 
ix. It is a home for animals  like  blue sheep (counted to be 852 in 2007), leopards, gorals, 
serows, Himalayan tahrs, black bears, deer, wild pigs, monkeys, langurs, and hares; 137 
types of birds primarily  including pheasants and partridges. 
x. Endangered  species  in  the  reserve  include  musk  deer,  wolves,  red  pandas,  cheer 
pheasants, and danphes. 
xi. The  authority  responsible  for issuing  hunting  licenses  is  the Department of National 
Parks and Wildlife Conservation.

10.1.7 BIODIVERSITY HOTSPOTS  
Biodiversity hotspots are areas characterized by both abundant biological diversity, with a rich 
variety  of  plants  and  animals,  and  significant  threats  to  their  survival.  Essentially,  these 


 
 
biogeographic  regions  exhibit  high  productivity  but  are  constantly  at  risk  of  destruction, 
necessitating  protective  measures.  Forest  habitats  serve  as  prime  examples  of  biodiversity 
hotspots, facing ongoing threats like illegal  logging, pollution, and deforestation. The concept 
of hotspots was introduced by Norman Myers in 1988. 
 
Do You Know? 
Norman  Myers,  the  world’s  leading  environmental  analysts, 
introduced the concept of hotspots in 1988, identifying regions 
with high  biodiversity  to prioritize  conservation  efforts due to 
their ecological  significance. 
 
Nepal's  Himalayan  region  has  been  identified as  one  of  the  world's  ten  most  endangered 
biodiversity hotspots. The leading four biodiversity hotspots include Upper Amazonia/Guyana 
Shield, the Congo Basin, New Guinea/Melanesian Islands, and the Himalaya - Nepal. 
 
10.1.8 WETLAND  
Wetlands, like  marshes  and swamps, are areas  with shallow  water or moisture-saturated soil. 
They  include marshes,  estuaries,  mudflats, ponds, swamps, deltas, shallow seas,  bogs, lakes, 
and floodplains. These lands are fertile for agriculture and teeming with diverse wildlife. They 
play a crucial role in high-mountain and lowland ecosystems. Wetlands offer various benefits, 
serving as habitats for fish, wildlife, including endangered species. 
 
In Nepal, there are 242 wetlands occupying 743,500 hectares (about 5% of the country's area) 
and  are  considered  important  productive  ecosystems  of  the  world.  Some  of  them  are: 
Phoksundo  Wetland,  Koshi  Tappu  Wildlife  Reserve,  Jagadishpur  Reservoir,  Gosaikunda 
Wetland, Gokyo Wetland Series,  Ghodaghodi Lake  Area, Beeshazar  and Associated Lakes, 
Maipokharai,  etc. 
 
Nepal is committed to the conservation  of these sites of immense  importance and has signed 
the  Ramsar  Convention  in  1988.  Ten  wetlands  are  listed  as  Ramsar  sites  recognizing  the 
international importance. They are tabulated below.

Benefits of Wetlands 
Besides  recreational  and  educational  benefits,  studying wetlands  helps  us  understand their 
importance in sustaining life and ecosystems. Following are a few benefits of wetland: 
i.Wetlands foster rich and diverse ecosystems, providing habitats for various plant and animal 
species. 
ii.They act as natural buffers, absorbing and storing excess water during heavy rainfall,  helping 
prevent floods. 
iii.Wetlands filter and purify water by trapping pollutants and sediments, contributing to improved 
water quality. 
iv.Wetlands offer opportunities for recreational activities such as bird watching, fishing, and eco-
tourism. 
v.They play a role in climate regulation by storing carbon and helping to mitigate the impacts of 
climate change. 
vi.Wetlands  are  often  fertile  areas  suitable  for  agriculture,  supporting  crops  and  sustaining 
livelihoods. 
vii.Many  threatened and  endangered species  rely  on  wetlands for  their  survival,  making  them 
crucial  for biodiversity conservation. 
viii.Studying wetlands enhances our understanding of ecosystems, biodiversity, and environmental 
science. 
ix.Vegetation in wetlands helps stabilize soil, preventing erosion and maintaining the integrity of 
surrounding landscapes. 
x.Wetlands hold cultural value for many communities,  often being sites of historical importance 
and traditional practices. 
 
10.1.9 RAMSAR SITES 
The  term  "simsar",  in  Nepali,  refers  to  wetland indicating  an  area  with a  continuous  water 
source, including rice  fields, waterlogged zones, and ponds. Currently, Nepal has ten Ramsar 
sites,  covering  5.5%  of the country, recognized  for their  international importance  under  the 
Ramsar  Convention.  The  Ramsar   Convention  (intergovernmental   environmental  treaty), 


 
 
established in 1971 by UNESCO and named after the city of Ramsar  in Iran, aims  to protect 
wetlands globally.  It is  the first  treaty  for  conserving  natural  resources,  focusing  on  halting 
wetland loss and managing remaining  ones wisely through international collaboration,  policy-
making, capacity building, and technology transfer.

CONSERVATION AND MANAGEMENT OF WILDLIFE 
Conservation involves wisely using the Earth's resources for today and preserving them for the 
future. Wildlife conservation  focuses on safeguarding species  at risk of extinction. Protecting 
global  wildlife is  a crucial  environmental  task. The  UN Conference on Biodiversity in  1992 
revealed that out of around 30 million  species,  only 1.5 million  are known. Natural extinction 
occurs,  but current rates  indicate potential loss  of a quarter  of Earth's species  in the next 30 
years. Conservation efforts are vital for sustaining biodiversity.

CONSERVATION STRATEGY 
Scientists from more than 100 countries  crafted a thorough Global Conservation Strategy for 
the responsible  utilization of wildlife and resources during the UN Conference on Biodiversity 
in 1992. This  strategy encompasses: 
i.Wildlife habitats require  thorough study for protection, examining  critical  areas like  feeding, 
breeding, nursery, and resting grounds. 
ii.Steps should be taken to safeguard these habitats, including the development of new protected 
areas tailored to animals' needs. 
iii.Accurate  census  data  on  wild  animals  in  specific  areas  must  be  maintained,  with  counts 
conducted at least once every five years. 
iv.International  agreements  need  establishment  to  protect  the  habitats  of  migratory  species 
covering large areas. 
v.Strict  enforcement  of laws  is  necessary  to  prevent  international  wildlife  trade and pleasure 
hunting. 
vi.Preservation of wild varieties essential for genetic engineering and biotechnology work should 
be prioritized. 


 
 
vii.National parks and wildlife reserve areas should be established to conserve the natural habitats 
of wildlife. 
viii.Efforts should be made to educate people about the use and importance of wildlife.  
 
10.2.2 IUCN RED LIST 
 
Figure 10.2.2 Categories of IUCN Red list 
The  IUCN  Red  List  is  a  comprehensive  inventory  of  the  global  conservation  status  and 
extinction risk  of biological  species. It was established in 1964 by the International Union for 
Conservation  of  Nature (IUCN). The  primary  purpose  of the  IUCN Red List  is to provide 
information about the conservation status of species and to draw attention to the magnitude and 
importance  of threatened biodiversity.  The  IUCN Red List  categories  are  intended to be  an 
easily  understood system  for classifying  species  at high  risk  of global  extinction. It divides 
species  into nine categories:  Not Evaluated, Data Deficient, Least Concern, Near Threatened, 
Vulnerable,  Endangered, Critically  Endangered, Extinct in  the Wild  and Extinct.  The  eight 
categories  of  wildlife  species  are: Extinct,  Extinct  in  the  Wild,  Critically   Endangered, 
Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern, Data Deficient, and Not Evaluated. 
The list is updated periodically. (For latest updates visiting  its official site is recommended) 
 
1. Extinct: Species that no longer exist. 
2. Extinct in the Wild:  Species that only exist in captivity or human-managed situations. 
3. Critically  Endangered: Species  that are facing an extremely  high risk  of extinction in 
the wild. 
4. Endangered: Species that are facing a very high risk of extinction in the wild. 
5. Vulnerable: Species that are facing a high risk of extinction in the wild. 
6. Near Threatened: Species that are close to being classified as Vulnerable. 
7. Least Concern: Species that are not currently at risk of extinction. 
8. Data Deficient: Species for which there is insufficient information to make an assessment  
of their risk of extinction. 
9. Not  Evaluated:  Used for  species  that have  not yet  been  assessed  by  the  IUCN This 
category does not indicate that a species  is not at risk  of extinction, but simply  that the 
species has not yet been studied enough to assign it to a category. 
 
  


 
 
The objective of designing the red list is: 
i.to raise awareness about the level of risk to biodiversity. 
ii.to document and recognize species highly susceptible to extinction. 
iii.to develop strategies for the conservation of endangered species. 
iv.to  disseminate  information  on  global  agreements,  such  as  the  Convention  on  Biological 
Diversity  and Convention on  International Trade  in Endangered Species  of Wild  Fauna and 
Flora (CITES) with  awareness  campaigns  conducted  by  the  government  and  concerned 
agencies. 
What is IUCN? 
The  International  Union for the  Conservation of Nature and 
Natural     Resources     (IUCN),    founded    in     1948    and 
headquartered   in   Morges,   Switzerland,   is   a   prominent 
international  institution dedicated to the  sustainable  use  and 
conservation  of  biodiversity.   Renamed  IUCN-The  World 
Conservation  Union  in  1990,  it  boasts  over  70  member 
organizations,  encompassing  both  governmental  and  nongovernmental  agencies.  With  a 
panel   comprising   more   than  5,000   experts,  the  IUCN  collaborates   with  influential 
institutions  like   the World  Bank,  Asian   Development  Bank,  UNESCO,  and  others. 
Recognized for its unique position, the IUCN serves as a key provider of technical advice on 
biodiversity issues.
