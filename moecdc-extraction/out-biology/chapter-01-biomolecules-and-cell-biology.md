1.1.1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION 
Think  about the incredible  variety of living  things, from tiny microorganisms  to the complex 
structures of plants and animals.  What ties them all  together at the tiniest level?  Well,  it's all 
about  molecules!   Cells  and  organisms   are  made  of  different  kinds  of  molecules  called 
biomolecules.  These special molecules  are like the building blocks of life, coming in different 
sizes from simple  ones like carbon dioxide and water to big ones like proteins. As the smaller 
molecules are soluble and easily transported, they frequently enter the general chemical activity 
of cells  known as metabolism.  Larger  molecules  tend to be used for structural purposes  and 
some  can  be  described  as  "informational"   molecules,   concerned  with  carrying   genetic 
information (DNA and RNA) and the expression of that information (proteins). The collection 
of the biomolecules in the cell is called cellular  pool. 
Biomolecules  of cellular pool can be classified into two types: 
1. Organic  Compounds:  These  are  the  compounds,  basically,  composed  of carbon  in 
combination  with  other  elements  like  H, O,  N,  S,  etc.  For  example,  carbohydrates, 
proteins, fats, nucleic acids, hormones and vitamins. 
2. Inorganic  Compounds: These  are  the compounds found in  the bulk  of physical  and 
non-living  universe  like  elements,  non-metals  and  their  compounds.  For  example, 
water, minerals,  salts, and ions. 
 
 
Do you know? 
Living cells  are primarily  made up of C, H, N, O, P, and S, which make up about 99% of 
their mass. The remaining 1% consists of elements like Ca, Fe, Zn, Na, K, Cl with water (H 
and O) making up around 70% of a cell's mass. 
Cellular  pool obtains chemicals  from external environment through membranous  covering  of 
cell  organelles   and  plasma   membranes.  Based  on  molecular   weight  and  solubility,   the 
biomolecules  are classified into two types:

3. Polysaccharides 
Polysaccharides are complex molecules composed of ten to thousands monosaccharides. They 
are also known as glycan. Despite their long size, polysaccharides do not function as reducing 
sugars. They are  not water  soluble  and  lack  a  sweet  taste. For  example,  starch,  cellulose, 
glycogen, and dextrin. Starch serves as a food storage in plants, but animals store it in the form 
of  glycogen,  and cellulose is  a  structural  component  of plant  cell  wall. Thus,  they  can  be 
classified into two functional types: 
a. Structural  polysaccharides  (cellulose  in  plant  cell  wall;  chitin  in  fungal  cell  wall; 
mannon in yeast cell wall; peptidoglycan in bacterial cell wall) 
b. Nutrient polysaccharides (starch in plant and bacterial cell, glycogen in animal  cell) 
 
Based on their composition, they can also be classified into: 
a) Homopolysaccharides/homoglycans: They   are   polymers   of  only   one   type   of 
monosaccharide. For example, glucan (made of glucose), fructan (made of fructose). 
b) Heteropolysaccharides/heteroglycans:    They    are   polymers    of   many   types   of 
monosaccharide. For example, arabanogalactans.

Functions of Carbohydrates 
Carbohydrates are essential for various functions in our body: 
1. Approximately 50% of our total energy comes from breaking down carbohydrates. 
2. Monosaccharides  like ribose  and deoxyribose are structural elements in nucleic  acids. 
Polysaccharides like cellulose  and hemicellulose  form the plant cell wall. 
3. Starch serves as a storage polysaccharide in plants, while glycogen (animal  starch) is a 
primary  reserve food stored in the liver and muscles. 
4. Carbohydrates support the synthesis of fats and amino acids. 
5. Glucose  plays  a  crucial  role  in  preventing  the  incomplete  oxidation of  fats and  the 
formation of ketone bodies in the blood.

1.1.4 PROTEINS 
Proteins (G. proteios = of the first rank) are long chains of a large number of chemically bonded 
amino acids. They are the most complex and varied macromolecules  that form more than 50% 
of the cell's dry weight. Enzymes, basically  proteins, control all body reactions. 
Around 20 different amino acids in a  specific sequence  form  distinct protein whose specific 
sequence is determined by gene. 
 
CATEGORIES OF PROTEINS 
A. Based on chemical nature and solubility,  proteins are divided into three categories: 
a) Simple  proteins 
b) Conjugated proteins 
c) Derived proteins 
a) Simple proteins: They are made up of only amino acids. For example, albumins,  globulins, 
histones, protamines,  etc. 
b)  Conjugated  proteins:  They  contain  simple  proteins  associated  with  some  non-protein 
components. These additional components are known as prosthetic groups. Some specific types 
of conjugated proteins are: 
1. Glycoproteins:  These  result from the combination  of protein  and glucose,  as seen  in 
substances like mucin of saliva. 
2. Phosphoproteins: They are formed by the combination of amino acids and phosphate. 
For example, caesin of milk and ovovitellin of eggs. 
3. Lipoproteins:  These are the combination of amino acids and lipids. They are found in 
various  proteins  such  as  those  in  the brain,  serum,  and the chemical  composition  of 
biological  membranes. For example, High density lipoprotein (HDL). 
4. Nucleoprotein: They are formed by the combination of amino acids and nucleic acids. 
They are located in the chromosomes  of cells. For example, histone protein. 
5. Chromoprotein:  They  are  formed  by  the  combination  of  amino  acids  and  colored 
pigments  containing  elements  like  Fe,  Cu, Mg,  or  Cobalt. For  example,  hemoglobin 
and the retina of the eye. 
 
c)  Derived  proteins: These  proteins  are  formed  by  the  partial  hydrolysis  of  simple  and 
conjugated proteins. For example, peptones and proteoses. 
 
B. Based on the shape, proteins are categorized into two types: 
1. Fibrous proteins show twisted structure, forming long fiber-like  arrangements.  They 
are strong,  elastic  and insoluble  in  water, and are  exclusively  found in  animals.  For 
example, keratin, elastin, myosin, collagen, etc. 
2. Globular proteins are roughly spherical,  water-soluble  and are predominantly  found 
in  plants, such as  albumin  and globulin. For  example,  egg albumin,  serum,  glutelins, 
etc.

STRUCTURE OF PROTEIN 
Proteins are, basically,  polymers  of numerous  amino acid molecules.  Proteins exhibit various 
configurations or structures based on which it can have following four levels: 
1. Primary  structure 
3. Tertiary  structure 
2. Secondary structure 
4. Quaternary structure 
Do You Know? 
The sequence of a protein is determined by the DNA of the gene responsible  for the coding 
of the protein.  A change  in DNA sequence  brings  change  in  the sequence  of amino  acids 
altering not only its structure but also its function. 
      
For  example,  in  sickle  cell  anemia,  one  of the polypeptide chains  that makes  hemoglobin 
suffers a slight alteration in amino acid sequence. The glutamic acid normally  located as the 
sixth amino acid of the hemoglobin β chain is replaced by valine. 
 


 
 
 
 
Figure 1.1.13 Primary, Secondary, Tertiary and Quaternary structures of protein 
 
1. Primary structure 
Arrangement of amino acids in a polypeptide chain is the primary  structure of a protein. It is a 
linear  arrangement  of amino acids and has only  peptide bonds. Insulin is the smallest protein 
which shows primary  structure. 
 
2. Secondary structure 
Secondary  structure  of  protein  is  a  regularly  repeated  polypeptide  chain  resulting  three 
dimensional shapes. In this type, the polypeptide chain bends and folds due to molecular force 
and gives a special  shape to the protein. Between carbonyl  oxygen, there is a hydrogen atom. 
The  force  originated  from  the  hydrogen  bond twists  the  polypeptide  chain  into  its  helical 
structure.  This  is  called  alpha - helix.  Such  proteins  are  found in  hair,  wool and in  fibrous 
substances. 
 
3. Tertiary structure 
Tertiary protein structure describes more compact arrangement in which helical and non-helical 
parts of a polypeptide chain fold back on themselves. This type of structure is characteristic of 
globular proteins, where the folding is facilitated by the non-helical regions. 
In a tertiary protein, the polypeptide chain is held together by bonds like ionic bonds, hydrogen 
bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bonds. 
 
  


 
 
4. Quaternary structure 
In the proteins  which are  made of two or more  polypeptide chains,  the quaternary  structure 
describes  how these chains  are  arranged relative  to each other and the interactions  that hold 
this arrangement together. For example, ribonuclease,  haemoglobin,  and immunoglobulins. 
Do You Know? 
Why does the transparent fluid in an egg turn white when boiled? 
Egg whites have proteins called albumins  with a specific 3D shape due to bonds between 
amino  acids.  Heating  breaks  these bonds  and reveals  hydrophobic  amino  acids.  These 
amino acids stick together, forming a network that gives the egg white opaque structure. 
Each  protein  has  a  unique  shape.  If the  environment's  temperature,  pH,  or  chemicals 
change, the protein's  shape can be affected, causing it to lose its structure and become a 
jumbled  string  of amino  acids,  called  denaturation. Denatured proteins  often lose their 
function. 
 
Functions of Proteins 
1. Structural  proteins  are  crucial  for  building  and  repairing  body  tissues,  supporting 
growth. 
2. Proteins also serve as energy sources. They provide 5.65 kcal of energy per gram, with 
a physiological  fuel value of 4.0 kcal per gram. 
3. Nearly  2000 different kinds of enzymes  are known to act as  enzymes  or biocatalysts 
which regulate life processes 
4. Insulin is a hormone (protein) that regulates glucose level in the body. 
5. Antibodies or  immuno-globins  are  proteins  that develop  immunity/protect  the  body 
against foreign bodies. 
6. Thrombin   and  fibrinogen   are  proteins  that  prevent  bleeding  by  clotting  blood.

CLASSIFICATION OF LIPIDS 
Lipids are grouped into three categories: 
1. Simple lipids 
2. Complex or conjugated lipids 
3. Derived lipids 
 
  


 
 
1. Simple Lipids 
They are esters of fatty acids and alcohol. 
 
They are broadly categorized into the following types: 
i. Neutral fats 
ii. Oils 
iii. Waxes 
 
i.  Neutral  fats:  They  are  triesters  of  fatty acids  and  glycerol.  Hence,  it  is  also  known  as 
glycerides or  triglycerides. Each molecule  of glycerol  can react with three molecules  of fatty 
acids.  Depending  upon  the  number  of  fatty acids  attached, they  are  called  monoglyceride, 
diglyceride or tri-glyceride. It can be found in solid state in room temperature.

a. Monoglyceride:  One molecule of glycerol + one molecule  of fatty acid. 
b. Diglyceride:  One molecule of glycerol + two molecules of fatty acids. 
c. Triglyceride:  One molecule of glycerol + three molecules  of fatty acids.

ii. Oils: Oils  are  fats rich  in  unsaturated fatty acids  (with more  than one  double bond). 
Plant-based oils,  such as  olive  oil  and sunflower  oil,  are  liquid  at room  temperature, 
usually clear or semi-transparent. Unsaturated fatty acids can combine with oxygen and 
other chemicals  and turn into solid saturated state called Vanaspati ghee and margarine. 
This process is called hydrogenation.  


 
 
iii. Waxes: Waxes are esters of long chain saturated fatty acids (with single bond) and long 
chain  alcohol  except  glycerol.  Waxes  are  chemically  inert.  They  are  protective  in 
functions and form water insoluble coating on hair and skin in animals  and on stems, 
leaves, and fruits of plants. The important types of waxes are as follows: 
1. Plant wax:  This  type of lipid forms  a coating on plant parts to reduce the rate of 
transpiration and prevent. 
2. Bee's wax: It is secreted by abdominal glands of worker honeybees. 
3. Paraffin Wax: Paraffin wax is a petroleum-derived wax composed of long-chain 
saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes).  It is widely used in candles, cosmetics,  and food 
coatings. 
4. Lanolin (wool  fat): It is obtained from sheep’s wool. It is secreted by sebaceous 
gland and get deposited onto the wool fibers.

2. Complex lipids 
These lipids are complex as they form by the combination of lipids with either carbohydrates, 
phosphates, or proteins. 
a. Glycolipids (Glycolipids = lipids + carbohydrate): They are formed by the combination 
of lipids with carbohydrate molecules. They are key components of cell membrane and 
white matter in nerve tissue. For example, cerebrosides and gangloside.  
b. Phospholipids (Phospholipids = lipids + phosphate): These lipids include a phosphate

A. Deoxy-Ribonucleic Acid (DNA) 
DNA is  a  molecule  made  of two nucleotide-chains  which  coil  around each other  forming  a 
double helix. It present in almost all cells (at nucleus, mitochondria, and plastids) except some 
viruses.  It  carries  genetic  instructions  essential  for  growth,  development,  functioning  and

bases: A, T, G or C. 
 
Figure 1.1.23 DNA Structure (Watson and Crick Model) 
 
Characteristics  of DNA: 
− DNA has two parallel chains of polynucleotides running as a right-handed double helix. 
− The  strands are spirally  coiled  that run in  opposite directions (one  in  3' - 5' direction 
and another in 5' - 3'). 
− They have sugar-phosphate chains outside and purines and pyrimidine bases inside. 
− These  two strands are  held together by weak hydrogen bonds between purine  (A and 
G) and pyrimidine bases (T and C). 
− The  distance between strands is  2 nanometer  (nm).  There  are  10 base  pairs  in  a turn 
(3.4 nm), with a gap of 0.34 nm between pairs. 
− DNA has 28% adenine, 24% guanine, 20% cytosine, and 28% thymine. 
− Nucleotides in a helix connect with phospho-diester bonds. 
 
Functions of DNA 
− Being  a genetic  material,  it carries  genetic information  in  the form  of a  sequence  of 
nitrogenous bases. 
− DNA copies genetic information to pass it to cells of the next generation. 


 
 
− Crossing over in DNA causes variation in off springs. 
− Alteration in components of DNA causes mutation, an inheritable genetic change. 
− Transcription  of DNA synthesizes RNAs for different cell functions. 
− It regulates cellular  metabolism through RNA and protein synthesis (Central Dogma). 
 
B. Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) 
RNA is a single stranded chain of ribonucleotides synthesized in the nucleus but found in the 
nucleolus,  cytoplasm,  and  on  ribosome  membranes.  Generally,  it  is  single  stranded  and 
functions as a hereditary material in some viruses and helps in protein synthesis. 
Structural Details: 
1. RNA is a macromolecule  smaller  than DNA. 
2. It is a single-stranded polynucleotide chain. 
3. It is composed of nucleotides linked together by 3'-5' phosphodiester bonds. 
4. Each  ribonucleotide  consists  of  a  ribose  sugar,  a  nitrogen  base  (Adenine,  Guanine, 
Cytosine, or Uracil)  and a phosphate. 
Types of RNA: 
1. Messenger RNA (mRNA): It is  the most abundant type of RNA. It translates genetic 
information from DNA to ribosomes for protein synthesis. 
2. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): It is a stable RNA linked with ribosomes. 
3. Transfer  RNA (tRNA): It acts  as  an  adapter,  carrying  amino  acids  to  the  protein 
synthesis site. 
 
Functions of RNA: 
1. Messenger   RNA  (mRNA)  carries   coded  information   to  synthesize   polypeptide 
chains/proteins  and enzymes. 
2. RNA forms compact genes for genetic engineering via reverse transcription. 
3. Transfer  RNA  (tRNA)  transfers  specific  amino  acids  to  the  ribosome  for  protein 
synthesis. 
4. RNA possesses a cap region for the attachment of ribosome. 
5. RNA acts as a genetic material in some viruses.

1.1.8 ENZYMES 
Have you ever thought about how your body breaks down the food you eat? 
What role do you think enzymes play in this process? 
Enzymes are special  proteins  that speeds up chemical  reactions  in living  things without itself 
undergoing any change. Thus, they are also known as biocatalysts. Based on the site of action, 
enzymes can be of two types: 
a) Exoenzymes: The enzyme like enzymes of digestive juices that function outside 
living cell are called exoenzymes. 
b) Endoenzymes:  These  are  the  enzymes  that function  inside  living  cells.  For 
example enzymes found inside mitochondria (in Kreb's cycle). 
Substrate is  the biochemical  over  which  enzymes  act. However,  if there  are  more  than one 
biochemical  participating in the reaction, then they are called reactants. 
Do You Know? 
The  term 'enzyme'  (G. enzymos = leavened) was coined by German  physiologist  Wilhelm 
Kuhne (1878). Buchner (1903) isolated the first enzyme. Sumner (1926) first discovered the 
protein  nature  of  enzymes;  he  purified  and crystalized  Urease  enzyme  for  which  he  was 
honored with the Nobel prize (1946). 
 
CHARACTERISTICS OF ENZYMES 
1. Enzymes  are  usually  globular  proteins,  and  they  can  include  inorganic  or  organic 
substances for their activity. 
2. They are hydrophilic and form hydrosol in free-state. 
3. They are highly specific in their action, catalyzing only a single reaction, but different 
enzymes can act on the same substrate to produce different products. 
4. All  enzyme-catalyzed  reactions  are  theoretically  reversible,   depending  on  energy 
requirements,  reaction availability,  end product concentration, and pH. 
5. They are highly efficient, so a small  amount can catalyze a large amount of substrate. 
6. Enzymes don't start chemical reactions but speed them up, not altering the equilibrium 
but by lowering the activation energy. 
7. They are separately coded by genes as they are proteins. 
8. Heavy metals and high-energy radiation can inactivate or denature protein structure. 
 
Biological  Importance of Enzymes: 
1. Enzymes mediate almost all chemical  reactions in living organisms. 
2. They can function as a biocatalyst at biological  temperatures. 


 
 
3. They are pH-specific so it functions at required pH at different parts of body. 
4. Reactions which are mediated by enzymes do not require harsh treatment. 
5. Enzymes  force  living  organisms  to  live  in  optimum  environment  as  enzymes  are 
functional only at specific conditions. 
6. Enzyme  formation  is  controlled  by  separate   regulatory  genes,  allowing   precise 
regulation of enzyme activity.

Water is the most common inorganic  substance in cells,  forming a significant part of all living 
organisms,  whether on land or in water. It constitutes 60%-70% of body weight and occupies 
75%-90%  of the protoplasm.  While  it doesn't provide energy, water is crucial  for sustaining 
life.

Biological  Functions of Water

7. It eliminates  waste products in solution  form, primarily  as urine  and to a lesser  extent 
as sweat, maintaining homeostasis.

All living  beings ever  existed  or existing  are basically  composed  of a collection  of  special 
compounds called  biomolecules  packaged in  a structure  termed  a cell. The cell  is the basic 
building block of life, responsible  for all necessary life  processes. It is the smallest  unit that

on  its  own  and  carries  out  vital  functions  like  respiration,  growth,  and  excretion.  In

organisms, a single cell manages all life activities,  making it the fundamental unit of life. The 
study of structure and function of a cell and its components is called Cytology, coined by Oskar

lignin in plantae. However, cell wall in fungi is composed of fungal cellulose or chitin. 
v. Its locomotary organ - Flagella - consists of (9 + 2) fibrillar  arrangement. 
vi. The  cells  exhibit  cytoplasmic  streaming  and carry  out photosynthesis  in  membrane-
enclosed chloroplasts. 
vii. Its  cytoplasm  comprises   ribosomes   of 80s  type (60s  +  40s),  and  chloroplast   and 
mitochondria consist of ribosomes of 70s type (30s + 50s). 
Examples of eukaryotic cells comprise  a wide range of organisms, including plants, animals, 
fungi, and protists.

1.2.3 STRUCTURE OF EUKARYOTIC CELL 
Shapes and sizes 
The shape of cell is influenced  by factors such as function,  age, cell wall, external  pressure, 
location,  tension  and viscosity.  It may have different  shapes like  irregular,  cylindrical,  oval, 
plate-like,  tubular, branched, etc. The size of eukaryotic cells can generally range from 1 μm - 
1 mm  long. The smallest  cell is mycoplasma  (0.3 μm) and the largest  cell is an ostrich  egg 
(around 15 cm). 
 
A typical  eukaryotic animal  cell is composed of a jelly-like,  translucent  protoplasm  enclosed 
by a plasma membrane. In plant cells, the plasma membrane  is further surrounded by a rigid 
and porous cell wall made of cellulose. The fluid between the plasma membrane and nuclear 
envelope is called cytoplasm. 
 
Parts of Eukaryotic Cells

1. CELL WALL 
The cell wall is the outermost  protective  layer, providing rigidity,  and mechanical  support to 
cells. It is present in plant cells, bacteria, cyanobacteria, fungi, and certain protists.  However, 
the cell wall may be absent in certain lower plants and in cells involved in reproduction, such 
as plant gametes  and zoospores. Notably, the cell wall is entirely  absent in animal cells. The 
thickness of the cell wall may vary (0.1 - 10 μm). The discovery of the cell wall is attributed to 
Robert Hooke (1665). 
 
The complex structure of the plant cell wall is divided into following different  layers and 
structures: 
       
i. Middle Lamella: It is a thin layer between adjacent plant cell walls (formed during cell 
division) which is composed of pectates of calcium and magnesium. Its pectin layer is 
a hydrophilic  colloidal  substance that absorbs water and acts as a cementing  material 
between cells. 


 
 
 
Fig: 1.2.4 Different layers in plant cell wall 
 
ii. Primary Cell Wall:  It is a thin, elastic and permeable membrane  basically composed 
of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectic compounds. It is formed by protoplasm on either 
side of the middle lamella  in a growing cell. Most leaves, fruits, cortex, and pith cells 
contain only the primary cell wall and middle lamella. 
iii. Secondary Cell Wall: It is a thick, rigid, and inelastic protective membrane composed 
of  three  concentric  layers  of  microfibrils,  chemically  formed  of  lignin,  cellulose, 
hemicellulose,  and pectin of thickness 5-10 μm that lays down on the primary cell wall 
after its growth is complete. It is found in mature  and specialized cells  like tracheids, 
vessels,  collenchymatous  cells,  and  sclerenchymatous  cells.  Additional  chemical 
substances like cutin, suberin, waxes, mucilage, and silica are deposited on the wall. 
iv. Tertiary Cell Wall: It is a very thin protective  membrane composed of cellulose  and 
xylan. It is found beneath the secondary  cell wall, particularly  in xylem  tracheids  of 
Gymnosperms and provides strength to the cell wall. 
v. Plasmodesmata: It is a small aperture between the primary cell wall and middle lamella 
that is connected to the endoplasmic  reticulum.  It permits  the circulation  of fluid and 
passage of solutes between cells forming bridges. 
 
Functions of Cell Wall: 
The functions of cell wall are: 
a. It provides rigid walls, offering mechanical strength to cells and the plant. 
b. It protects  the inner cell  contents from  mechanical  injuries  and prevents  the entry  of 
germs. 
c. Its  permeability  allows  exchange  of  essential  molecules  between  the  cell  and  its 
surroundings. 
d. It maintains the shape of cells, preventing undue expansion. 
e. The cell wall of root hairs aids in the absorption of water and minerals from the soil. 
f. Glycerolipid  like  Suberin  (at  root)  and cutin  (at  cuticle)  deposited  on the  cell  wall 
prevent the loss of water from evaporation. 
g. It  prevents  cell bursting  by maintaining  a  balance  between  intracellular  osmotic 
pressure and the environment. 
h. It is involved in various enzymatic activities.

3. PLASMA MEMBRANE 
All living cells are enveloped by a thin, transparent and selectively permeable membrane called 
plasma membrane. It is an elastic, regenerative living structure. The term "cell membrane" was 
coined by Cramer and Nagelli (1855), but it was referred to as plasmalemma  by Plowe (1931). 
Notably,  the  plasma  membrane  and all  the  membranes  that  cover  different  cell  organelles 
within the cell share the same ultrastructure. It separates the cytoplasm  from the surrounding 
cellular  environment. Collectively,  these  membranes  are  referred  to  as biomembranes.  This 
emphasizes  the uniformity  in the structural  composition  of cell membranes,  both at the cell's 
outer boundary and within its internal organelles. 
Plasma  membranes  are  chiefly  composed  of  lipids  and  proteins  (i.e.  lipoproteinous).  The 
membrane  contains  three  different  classes  of  proteins,  viz. structural  proteins,  enzymes 
(functional proteins), and carrier proteins. 
Structural  proteins  are a major  component  of the  cell  membrane.  Enzymes  form  the  major 
component of many membranes and are catalytic proteins. Carrier proteins transport substances 
across  the  membrane  against  the  concentration  gradient.  Lipid  constitutes  20-40%  of  the 
membrane. 
 
To explain  the structure  of plasma  membranes  different  models have been developed. Most 
convincing two models are described below:  
 
Structural models of plasma membrane 
a. Sandwich Model: This model was proposed by Danielli and H. Davson (1935) which 
is based on the physical and chemical properties of plasma membrane.  
 
Fig: 1.2.5 Sandwich Model of cell membrane 
 
Plasma membrane is basically a lipoproteinous trilaminar  membrane distinctly divided 
into outer and inner denser protein monolayer and a middle phospholipid bilayer. It is 
called  so  because  of  the  presence  of  the  phospholipid  bilayer  between  two  protein 
monolayers.  Here, protein molecules  are globular  that bear polar and non-polar side-
chains and provide stability. Each phospholipid molecule is polar with hydrophilic and 
hydrophobic ends. Heads (glycerols) are directed in opposite directions, associated with 
proteins. Tails (fatty acids) of two layers face each other which is held together by weak 
Van der Waals force. It also bears microscopic pores of size 7 – 10 Angstrom (Å). 
However,  this  model  doesn't  explain  functional  specificity  and  variability  in 
biomembranes  nor  accounts  for  phenomena  like  active  transport  and movement  of 
water-soluble substances. 


 
 
 
b. Fluid-Mosaic Model: According to this model, protein is not found in a uniform layer 
but found in a mosaic pattern like icebergs in a sea. This model was proposed by Singer 
and Nicholson (1972).  
The  plasma membrane  is  composed of  lipoproteins in trilaminar  arrangement.  It 
consists of a fluidly phospholipid bilayer, exhibiting two types of movements: transition 
and  flip  flop.  Within  this  bilayer,  there  are  globular  proteins,  which  are  further 
categorized  as  extrinsic  (peripheral)  or  intrinsic  (integral).  Extrinsic  proteins  are 
positioned outside the bilayer, while intrinsic  proteins are embedded with it. They are 
present in fluidly phospholipids bilayer  either in partially  or in completely  suspended 
forms.  They form  channels  for  the  passage  of  water, ions  and  other  water  soluble 
solutes. The phospholipid molecule  is polar, with a hydrophobic tail  and hydrophilic 
head of two fatty acid chains. So it is an amphipathic molecule. This amphipathic nature 
contributes  to the formation of a water resistant  barrier, with head and tail in opposite 
directions.  
  
Fig: 1.2.6 Fluid mosaic model of plasma membrane 
 
Functions of Plasma membrane 
Following are the functions of plasma membrane:  
a. Plasma membrane provides shape and protects the cell from mechanical injury.  
b. It  helps in transportation  of materials  by different processes like  diffusion,  osmosis, 
active transportation,  endocytosis (taking in large solid or liquid materials),  exocytosis 
(expelling large sized materials  from the cell), etc.  
a. It  also  helps  in cellular  locomotion  through  pseudopodia  movement  (involving 
extension  and folding  of  the  plasma  membrane  like  in amoeba,  etc.)  or  undulatory 
movement (wave-like undulations of the plasma membrane like in fibroblast cells). 
b. The antigens present in cell membranes help in separation of blood groups.  
c. Microvilli  is  its  modified  form  that  helps  in  absorption  of  digested  materials in  the 
intestine.  
d. It serves as a site where different stimuli,  like hormones and antigens, can be detected 
and recognized.

4. PROTOPLASM 
Protoplasm  is the  living  colloidal  mass  of a cell,  encompassing  various  cellular  organelles. 
According to Huxley, it  is ‘a  physical  basis of life’.  It is  thick,  translucent  fluid  containing 
lipids, carbohydrates, inorganic salts, and nucleic acids. The term ‘protoplasm’  was coined by 
Purkinje (1837). It comprises cytoplasm and nucleus. 
Protoplasm = Cytoplasm + Nucleus 
 
5. CYTOPLASM 
Cytoplasm is a jelly-like,  semi-fluid component of protoplasm that fills the space between the 
cell  membrane  and  the  nuclear  membrane.  It  comprises  cytosol,  cell  organelles,  and  cell 
inclusions. The cytoplasm  maintains the cell’s shape, stores necessary substances required by 


 
 
organelles,  and  facilitates  essential  reactions  within  the  cell.  In  plants,  it  is  crucial  for 
photosynthesis. 
 
6. CELL ORGANELLE 
The subcellular  living membrane bound components having specific biological  functions  are

7. MITOCHONDRIA 
Mitochondria (G. mito = thread; chondrion = granule) are granular or filamentous  cytoplasmic 
structures  also known as "the powerhouse of the cell" as they give energy in the form of ATP 
during aerobic respiration  to carry out basic activities  of a cell. It was discovered by Kolliker 
(1880 AD) in the voluntary  muscles  of insects.  The term  mitochondria  was given by Benda 
(1897). 
Mitochondria  are absent  in prokaryotes  but present  in all eukaryotic  cells  except for mature 
mammalian  red  blood  cells  and sieve  tubes  in  vascular  plants.  In  prokaryotes,  respiratory 
enzymes are on the plasma membrane instead of mitochondria. Mitochondria, shaped variably 
from rod-like to globular, change based on cell conditions. Sizes range from 1 μm in yeast to 
20-40 μm in Xenopus. They are the largest organelles in animal cells and second-largest in plant 
cells, ranking third in overall cell structures after the nucleus and chloroplast.  Mitochondrial 
number  varies,  being  more  abundant  in  growing,  dividing,  and  metabolically  active  cells. 
Positioned strategically  in metabolically  active areas, mitochondria supply immediate ATP for 
cellular activities.

which is called outer chamber. The inner chamber, or matrix, contains granules, ribosomes, and 
mitochondrial  DNA. The enzymes  for the Kreb's cycle are located  in the  matrix.  The outer 
membrane is smooth, while the inner membrane folds into cristae, increasing the surface area 
for  cell  respiration.  The  inter-cristae  space  within  the  folds  is  continuous  with  the 
intermembrane  space. On the side of the inner membrane facing the matrix, numerous small 
tennis-racket-shaped  particles,  referred  to as elementary particles,  F

are present. Each elementary  particle comprises a head, stalk, and base. These particles play a 
crucial role in oxidation and phosphorylation  reactions.

the synthesis  of ATP. Consequently, these particles  are termed ATP particles  that serve as the 


 
 
functional  units  of  mitochondria.  While  the  chemical  composition  of  mitochondria  varies 
among animal and plant cells, they generally contain protein (65-70%), lipids (25-30%), RNA 
(0.5%), and a small amount of DNA.

Functions of mitochondria

8. PLASTIDS 
Plastids are cellular organelles in plants and certain protozoans, like Euglena. The term plastid 
was coined by Haeckel (1866).  Plastids  are absent  in bacteria,  blue-green  algae, fungi,  and 
animals.  In bacteria, chromatophore  is  present  instead  of  plastid. Additionally,  they are  not 
found  in the gametes  and  zoospores  of  plants.  Plastids  are  classified  as  chloroplast  (green 
coloured), chromoplast (coloured other than green) and leucoplast (colourless).  
 
a. Chloroplast 
Chloroplasts  (G. choloro = green, plastos = formed), the most prevalent  plastids,  are crucial 
for plants as they facilitate  photosynthesis, the process responsible for manufacturing complex 
organic  food.  It  was  discovered  by  Antonie  Van  Leeuwenhoek  (1679)  and  the  term 
"chloroplast"  was introduced by Schimper  (1883). These green plastids  contain  the pigment 
chlorophyll.  They  are  widespread  in  photosynthetic  cells  of  plants  and  some  protists like 
Euglena, chloroplasts are absent in plant parts not exposed to light. 


 
 
 
Chloroplasts  exhibit  diverse  shapes,  commonly  biconvex  or plano-convex  in  higher  plants. 
Various shapes include filamentous,  saucer-shaped, ovoid, discoid, spheroid, star-like, girdle-
shaped, spiral ribbon-like, reticulate, or cup-shaped, depending on the plant cell. The size may 
vary from 2-3 μm in thickness and 5-10 μm in diameter and may be one (Chlamydomonas) to 
many (an intermodal cell of Chara) in number.

Structurally,  chloroplast  is  composed  of  three  main  components:  envelope,  stroma  and 
thylakoid. It is bounded by an envelope made of two membranes, outer and inner with an inter-
membrane space. The inner membrane encloses a dense, colorless, granular ground substance 
called stroma  or matrix. It is rich in proteins  (over 50%), 70s ribosomes,  circular  and naked 
DNA molecules  (0.5%), mRNA, tRNA molecules, water, minerals, and enzymes. The stroma 
is the site of the dark reaction of photosynthesis.  Within the stroma, structures  like grana and 
intergrana  connecting  membranes  are  embedded.  Each  granum  consists  of  disc-shaped 
membranous  sacs  called  thylakoids  piled  upon  each  other.  Grana  are  interconnected  by  a 
network  of  membranous  tubules  known  as  intergrana  or  stroma  lamellae.  The  thylakoid 
membrane contains all the enzymatic  components required for photosynthesis,  serving as the 
site for light reactions. 
 
Similar  to  mitochondria,  chloroplasts  are  semi-autonomous  organelles  with  the  ability  to 
synthesize  some  proteins  independently.  However, for  certain  proteins,  they rely on nuclear 
DNA and cytoplasmic ribosomes.

Chromoplasts (G. chromo = colour, plastos = formed) are colored plastids found in the non-
green  parts  of  plants such  as  flowers  and  fruits.  They  may  develop  from  chloroplasts  by

c. Leucoplasts: 
Leucoplasts are colorless plastids found in storage organs where light is limited. They may be 
rod-like,  spherical,  or oval,  enclosed  by double-layered  membranes,  and contain  a granular 
matrix.  Leucoplasts  transform  into  chloroplasts  with a thylakoid  structure  when exposed  to

a. Amyloplasts: They are found in potato tubers and grains of wheat and rice, synthesizing

c. Proteinoplasts or  Aleuronoplasts:  They  are  protein-storing  leucoplasts  mainly

Function of Leucoplasts: 
Leucoplasts primarily  serve to store various reserve food materials  like carbohydrates,  lipids,

9. ENDOPLASMIC RETICULUM (ER) 
The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a network of interconnected cisternae, tubules and vesicles 
present  throughout  the cytoplasm,  especially  in the  endoplasm.  It  resembles  a factory  with 
winding  pathways  and  chambers  where  proteins  and  lipids  are  synthesized,  processed, 
packaged and transported.  It is found only in eukaryotic  cells, excluding  germinal  cells  and 


 
 
mature mammalian  red blood cells. Its occurrence and development depend on the metabolic 
state  and differentiation  stage  of cells,  like  it  is less  developed  in spermatocytes,  and well-
developed  in  fully  differentiated  and  metabolically  active  cells,  like  pancreas  and  liver. 
However, it is absent in embryonic cells, 
ER was discovered by Garnier (1897) but its structural  detail was reported by Porter, Claude 
and Fullam (1945), while the term 'Endoplasmic Reticulum' was coined by Porter (1953). 
 
Types of Endoplasmic Reticulum: 
The ER is classified into two types based on the presence or absence of ribosomes: 
a. Agranular or Smooth  Endoplasmic  Reticulum  (SER): SER is  located  near  cell 
membrane and lacks ribosomes on its surface. RER after losing ribosomes forms SER. 
It mainly  comprises  tubules. It  is primarily  found in cells like  adipocytes,  brown fat 
cells,  interstitial  cells  (testes),  cells  of  corpus  luteum  (ovaries),  sebaceous  cells, 
adrenocortical  cells, muscle cells. It synthesizes and stores lipids. 
b. Granular or Rough Endoplasmic  Reticulum (RER): RER is located  near nucleus 
and possess ribosomes  on its surface. RER formed from nuclear membrane. It mainly 
comprises cisternae. It is primarily found in protein forming cells like pancreatic acinal 
cells, goblet cells, plasma cells, Nissl's granules of nerve cells. It transports proteins. 
 
Structure: 
The  endoplasmic  reticulum  (ER)  comprises  three  main  structures:  cisternae,  vesicles,  and 
tubules. 
1. Cisternae: They are flattened,  sac-like, unbranched tubules located near the nucleus. 
They  are interconnected,  and  often  found  in parallel  bundles/stacks  in cells like 
pancreas, notochord which have synthetic roles. 
2. Vesicles:  They  are oval, membrane-bound  vacuolar  structures  scattered  in  the 
cytoplasm, especially abundant in pancreatic cells. 
3. Tubules:  They  are  wider, branched and  dynamic structures  that  form  the  reticular 
system along with cisternae  and vesicles. It is associated with membrane movements, 
fission and fusion between membranes within the cell.

Functions of Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): 
1. SER synthesizes  lipids (Phospholipids,  cholesterol,  etc.) and phospholipids,  and RER 
synthesizes proteins.  
2. ER  acts  as  an  intercellular  circulatory  system  that  transports  material  by  osmosis, 
diffusion, and active transport. 
3. It functions as the cytoskeleton providing mechanical support to the cell. 
4. ER membrane forms primary lysosome and nuclear envelope. 
5. ER contributes to cell plate formation in plant cells during cytokinesis. 
6. ER contains many enzymes to carry out many synthetic  and metabolic  activities  such 
as glycogenolysis, detoxification,  and sterol metabolism. 
7. It facilitates  transfer of information  from nucleus to cell organelles. 
8. It stores synthetic products like glycogen. 
9. It helps in detoxification  of xenobiotics. 
 
10. GOLGI COMPLEX 
The Golgi complex is a membrane-bound,  cup-shaped cell  organelle found near the nucleus 
that  processes,  packages,  and  transports  proteins  and  lipids  in  eukaryotic  cells.  It  was 
discovered by Camillo Golgi in nerve cells of cats and barn owls (1898). It is referred to as the 
'traffic  police' of the cell by Darnell (1986) because it helps in sorting  the cell's proteins  and 
membrane constituents, directing them to their destinations.  It is also known as Lipochondria, 
Idiosome, or Dalton complex. It is present only in eukaryotic cells except in mature mammalian 
red blood cells, antherozoids of bryophytes and pteridophytes, and sieve tube cells. Depending 
on the cell type, functional state, and maturity of a cell, its shape and form may vary; therefore, 
it is also known as a Pleomorphic  organelle. It is larger  in mature,  functional,  and secretory 
cells  like  nerve  cells,  pancreatic  cells, liver  cells,  and germinal  cells; and  smaller  in  non-
secretory cells like muscle cells. In plant cells, the scattered subunits of the Golgi apparatus are 
known as Dictyosomes. 
 
 
Fig: 1.2.11 Ultra Structure of Golgi complex 
 
The Golgi body consists of four smooth membranous  compartments:  cisternae,  vacuoles, and 
vesicles and tubules. 
1. Cisternae  or  flattened  sac: Cisternae  are  double-layered,  flat,  curved  fluid-filled 
parallel sacs held in stacks. In each stack, cisternae are separated by a space which may 


 
 
have fibrils. Each stack comprises 5-6 cisternae in animal cell and 20 or more cisternae 
in plant cell. 
It is also known as saccules and are fenestrated and curved. The large concave face that 
faces cell  membrane is known as trans/maturing  face and a smaller  convex face that 
faces nucleus is known as cis/forming  face.  
2. Tubules: The  fenestrated  cisternae  give  rise  to  a  tubule,  which  is  a  network  of 
associated vesicles towards the periphery and maturing face of the apparatus. 
3. Vesicles: Vesicles  are  small  sacs attached  to the tips  of tubules.  They are associated 
with cisternae, present along the convex face. They are of two types, Smooth vesicles 
and coated vesicles. 
4. Golgian Vacuoles: These are enlarged parts of cisternae formed from the concave face 
of the  Golgi apparatus.  These have glandular  substances  which contain  hydrolyzing 
enzymes. 
Do You Know? 
In plant cells, Golgi apparatus is made of a number of separate and scattered units 
known  as  dictyosomes.   However,  in  animal   cells   it  is   made  of  a  single 
compact/loosely  stacked complex known as Golgi apparatus or Golgi complex. 
 
Functions: 
1. Golgi bodies transport and chemically modify cell  materials  such as proteins,  lipids, 
and sterols. 
2. It stores, condenses, and packages materials within the cell. 
3. It is involved in cell secretion;  vesicles containing  secretory  molecules  pinch off and 
discharge contents to the cell's exterior. 
4. They  participate  in  cell  plate  formation  during  cytokinesis  in  plant  cells;  vesicles 
separate from the main body to form a new cell plate. 
5. Golgi bodies contribute to the formation of primary lysosomes. 
6. It plays a role in the synthesis of hormones in endocrine cells, e.g., thyroxine in thyroid 
gland cells. 
7. They  participate  in  the  transformation  of  membranes  and  recycling  of  the  plasma 
membrane. 
8. It contributes to the biosynthesis of glycoproteins. 
 
11. LYSOSOMES 
Lysosomes  (Gr. Lyso - digestive, soma - body) are  microscopic,  membrane-bound  vesicles 
within the cytoplasm crucial for intracellular  digestion. These pleomorphic structures  contain 
up to 40 types  of hydrolytic  enzymes,  mostly  active  at pH 5, and digest  several  biological 
materials  (forming autophagosome), and, sometimes, own cell organelles causing death of the 
cell (autolysis).  Therefore, they are also known as "suicidal bag". Lysosomes were discovered

12. RIBOSOMES 
Ribosomes  are tiny, compact,  round and granular  organelles  crucial  for protein  synthesis.  It 
was discovered by Claude (1943). Ribosomes are present in both prokaryotic  (70s type) and 
eukaryotic cells  (80s type), except in mature  sperm and red blood cells. In prokaryotic  cells, 
they are often free in the cytoplasm. Eukaryotic cells host cytoplasmic ribosomes in two forms: 
free and bound (on RER and outer nuclear  membrane). Organellar  ribosomes are also found 
inside certain cell organelles like mitochondria and plastids. The number of ribosomes depends 
on the cell's RNA content, with higher numbers in plasma cells, liver cells, meristematic  cells, 
cancer cells, and endocrine cells. For example, E. coli has about 20,000-30,000 ribosomes. 
 
Structure: 
Ribosomes, oblate spheroids, consist of two subunits—one larger subunit (50s or 60s) and one 
smaller subunit (30s or 40s), joining only during protein synthesis. The larger subunit is dome-
shaped, attached to the ER, and has various  structural  features.  The smaller  subunit is oval-
shaped and fits  like a cap. Ribosomes are composed of approximately  45% rRNA and 55% 
proteins. When a number of ribosomes attach each other like beads in a strand of mRNA, it is 
called polyribosome. Depending on sedimentation coefficient, ribosomes are of two types:  


 
 
1. 70s type: It consists  of two sub-units, large 50s subunit and a small  30s subunit. It is 
smaller  in  size  and  can  be  found  in  bacteria,  cyanobacteria,  mitochondria,  and 
chloroplasts.  
2. 80s type: It consists of two sub-unit, large 60s subunit and small 40s subunit. It is larger 
in size and is present in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells in fungi, protists, plants and 
animals.

Based on cellular composition, RNA is of three types: 
a) Ribosomal RN (rRNA): 50 - 80% 
b) Messenger RNA (mRNA) 5 - 10% 
c) Transfer RNA (tRNA) 10 - 20% 
Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): Ribosomal RNA is the factory where proteins are synthesized.  In 
eukaryotic  cells,  the  60s  subunit  contains  around  35 different  proteins  and  three  different 
rRNAs. It  is  considered  essential  for  mRNA to  bind  with  ribosomes  and  carryout  protein 
synthesis. It can also act as an enzyme (ribozyme). 
Messenger  RNA  (mRNA): Messenger  RNA  is  a  linear  molecule  synthesized  inside  the 
nucleus  by transcription  from  DNA. Its  sequence  is  complementary  to  the  template  DNA 
strand.  Messenger  RNA carries  genetic  information  from  nucleus  (chromosomal  DNA) to 
cytoplasm for protein synthesis.  
As mRNA reaches cytoplasm it combines with ribosomes to form polyribosomes or polysomes. 
Each polysome  contains  several  ribosomes  (normally  five)  which form  a complex  with m-
RNA. 
Transfer RNA (tRNA): Transfer  RNA (soluble  RNA) molecule is relatively  small molecule 
comprising  71-80 nucleotides. There are more than 20 species of tRNA corresponding  to 20 
amino acids present in protein structure. It is synthesized in nucleus from DNA template strand.

Functions: 
1. Ribosomes act as protein factories,  being essential sites for protein synthesis. 
2. Proteins  synthesized  by  ribosomes  contribute  to  cellular  structure  or  act  as  enzymes, 
regulating cellular functions. 
3. Free ribosomes produce intracellular  enzymes, while bound ribosomes on RER synthesize 
enzymes for extracellular  use. 
4. Ribosomes temporarily  store proteins and rRNA, contributing to protein synthesis. 
 
13. CILIA AND FLAGELLA 
The cilia (L. cili = eye-lash) and flagella (L. flagellum = little whip) are microscopic, contractile 
and hair-like or thread-like motile structures that originate inside the cell from the basal bodies 
but are present outside the cell. They aid in movement, locomotion,  feeding, and circulation. 
Englemann (1868) first reported the presence of flagellum. 
Occurrence 
Cilia are present in all protozoans of the class ciliate, flame cells of flat worms, in some larval 
forms e.g. Bipinnaria larva of starfish, in some body structures e.g. wind pipe, fallopian tubes, 
etc., ciliated epithelium of the metazoa, etc. Flagella are found in all the protozoans of the class 
flagellate,  choanocyte  cells  of sponges, gastrodermal  cells  of coelenterates,  spermatozoa  of 
metazoa and lower plants, zoospores of algae, gamete cells, etc. 
 
Ultrastructure 
Both flagella  and cilia have similar  structures:  basal body, rootlets,  basal plate, and shaft. In 
fact, term cilium  is used when it is shorter than a cell (5 - 20 μm), while the longer extension 
is called flagellum (100 - 200 μm). 
A  cross-section  of  either  organelle  reveals  a  pair  of  central  filaments  surrounded  by  nine 
peripheral  filaments,  forming  a 9 + 2 arrangement.  This bundle of filaments  is known as an 
axoneme or axial filament. It is encased in a double-layered protective coat, 90 angstroms thick, 
which is  continuous  with  the  plasma  membrane.  Each peripheral  microtubule  is  a doublet, 
consisting of two subtubules, 'A' and 'B'. The 'A' microtubule has pairs of dynein arms, which 
can hydrolyze ATP. The central filaments are connected to the 'A' microtubules of the peripheral 


 
 
filaments  by radial  spokes. The  movement  of  cilia  and flagella  is  powered by ATP, which 
diffuses from the cell cytoplasm. 
 
Chemically, the central tubules are made of dynein protein, while the peripheral  microtubules 
are made of tubulin protein. Dynein acts as an ATPase enzyme, breaking down ATP to release 
energy for the beating of cilia and flagella.

Functions 
1. Cilia  or  flagella  bring  locomotion  in  organisms  by  their  power  strokes  and  return 
strokes. 
2. Cilia  of Paramecium and flagella  of collar  cells  of  sponges  set up  current  in  water 
allowing nutrition-and  respiratory gas enter inside the body. 
3. Ciliary movements in the respiratory tract help to eliminate solid wastes out of the body. 
4. Cilia in kidney-nephrons cause movement of nephric filtrate. 
5. Cilia push eggs out of the oviduct in amphibians and mammals. 
6. Cilia help larval forms to disperse. 
 
14. NUCLEUS 
Nucleus (L., nucleus = kernel)  is a double membrane  bound protoplasmic  body comprising 
genetic  information  which  regulates  all  the  cellular  activities  and  carries  the  hereditary 
information  of the cell. It is the largest cell organelle embedded in the cytoplasm. It was first 
discovered by Robert Brown (1833) in plant cells. 
 
A true  nucleus  (Eukaryon)  is found in all  the  eukaryotes  except mature  mammalian  RBCs, 
sieve tube cells of phloem, tracheids and vessels of xylem. The prokaryotes have an incipient 
nucleus called nucleoid or prokaryon or genophore (the nucleus without nuclear membrane and 
nucleolus). Its shape and size varies. Generally, its shape depends upon the shape of the cell. 
However, it  could  be  spherical,  cuboidal,  ellipsoidal,  discoidal  or  even  irregular.  Its  size 
depends  upon  nueleo-cytoplasmic  index  and  the  number  of  chromosomes  present  in  the 
nucleus. In young cell, nucleus occupies 25% of cell volume while in mature cell, it occupies 
about 10% of cell volume. 
 
  


 
 
Structure 
The nucleus is made of the following four components: 
a. Nuclear membrane (karyotheca) 
b. Nuclear sap (nucleoplasm) 
c. Chromatin fibres and 
d. Nucleolus 
 
Fig: 1.2.15 Structure of nucleus (during interphase) 
 
a.  Nuclear  membrane: Nuclear  membrane is the  outermost  layer  of  the  nucleus.  It  was 
discovered by Erclab (1845). It consists of two membranes  with a fluid-filled  space between 
them called the perinuclear space. The outer membrane is connected to the rough endoplasmic 
reticulum.  Pores  in  the  nuclear  envelope  enable  the  exchange  of  substances  between  the 
nucleus  and the cytoplasm.  This structure  performs  several  functions,  including  controlling 
interactions between the nucleus and cytoplasm, permitting the passage of small molecules and 
ions, facilitating  the uptake of large molecules through pinocytosis and phagocytosis, allowing 
the movement of ribosomal  subunits, mRNA, tRNA, and proteins through nuclear pores, and 
maintaining the shape of the nucleus. 
 
b. Nucleoplasm (Nuclear sap or karyolymph) 
The space between nuclear  envelop and nucleolus  is filled  with a transparent,  homogenous, 
semi-fluid,  gel-like, colloidal and slightly acidophilic ground substance called nucleoplasm. It 
constitutes  water,  nucleotides,  sugars,  minerals  (Mn,  Mg),  ribosomes, enzymes,  lipids, 
proteins,  mRNA, tRNA, etc. which are essential  for the synthesis  and functioning  of DNA, 
RNA, nucleoproteins and spindle fibres. 
c. The chromatin fibres (Nuclear reticulum) 
The  nucleoplasm  comprises  thread-like,  coiled  and  much  elongated  structures  known  as 
chromatin  fibres  (G. chroma =  colour).  Such chromatin  fibres  are  observable  only  during 
interphase stage of cell division (mitosis  and meiosis). When it becomes thick and ribbon-like 
in structure, it is called chromosomes. Chromatins are differentiated  into two types: 
i. Heterochromatin:  The condensed and darkly  stained section  of the chromatin  is 
known  as  heterochromatin.  It  occurs  around  the  nucleolus.  Perhaps,  it  is 
metabolically  and genetically inactive since it comprises relatively small amount of 
the DNA and large amount of the RNA. 
ii. Euchromatin: It is true and light stained chromatin  located at the diffused region 
of the chromatin.  It is metabolically  and genetically  active.  It  contains  relatively 
large amount of DNA. 
 
  


 
 
d. The Nucleolus 
The nucleus contains one or more prominent,  large, spherical  and acidophilic  dense granules 
known  as  the  nucleoli  (sing.  nucleolus).  Nucleolus,  also  called  plasmosome,  is  generally 
associated with the nucleolar organizer region (NOR) of the nucleolar chromosome. Nucleolus 
is chemically composed of RNA and non-histone acidic proteins. 
Nucleolus is the site of synthesis for ribosomal subunits. Additionally, it synthesizes and stores 
rRNA. It helps to form spindle fibers during cell division. It gets the ribosomal  proteins from 
the cytoplasm that combines with rRNAs and ribosomal proteins to form ribosomal subunits. 
 
Functions of nucleus 
1. It controls all cellular activities. 
2. It regulates the synthesis of structural  proteins. 
3. It controls cellular functions by controlling the synthesis of enzymatic proteins. 
4. It comprises genetic information to carryout reproduction and development. 
5. It helps to synthesize ribosomes. 
6. It brings genetic variations bringing organic evolution. 
7. It regulates cellular differentiation. 
 
15. CHROMOSOMES 
Chromosomes  (L. chrom = coloured, soma = body) are thread-like  nuclear component  with 
special organization,  individuality  and function. E. Strasburger (1875) discovered thread-like 
structures  which  appeared  during  cell  division  and  W. Waldeyer  (1888)  coined  the  term 
'chromosome'  to describe  it that became intensely  coloured after staining.  Chromosomes  are 
typically  present in the nucleus and its shape changes from phase to phase in the continuous 
process of cell growth/division. They are composed of DNA and proteins (histones) with small 
amount of chromosomal RNA. Chromosomes carry genes, which are the units of heredity that 
encode instructions  for the synthesis of proteins  and determine  various traits. The number of 
chromosome is constant for a particular species. However, based on the number of chromsomes 
present in a cell, cells can be classified into two types: 
a. Haploid 
b. Diploid 
Haploid  cell  consists  of  one  set  of  chromosome  which  is  essential  to  carryout  sexual 
reproduction. Diploid cell consists of a pair of chromosomes. In paired sets, one set is obtained 
from mother, which is called maternal  chromosome  and the other set is obtained from father 
and it is called paternal chromosome. In diploid cells of human, there are two distinct types of 
chromosomses: 
a. Autosomes (regulate cellular  activity) 
b. Sex chromosomes  (determine gender) 
The  electron  microscopic  structure  of  each  chromosome  comprises  the  chromonema, 
centromere, secondary constrictions,  nucleolar organisers, telomeres and satellites.

a. Chromatid 
During metaphase stage  during mitosis,  each  chromosome comprises two  identical

c. Chromomere 
Chromomeres  are regions of tightly folded DNA that appear like a cluster of bead of

The structure  and function  of the centromere  is different  from  that of the rest  of the

The constriction  near one end of chromosome that induces the formation  of nucleolus

In addition  to the  primary  constriction  or centromere,  the arms  of the chromosomes

Sometimes  chromosomes  possess  round,  elongated,  knob-like  extensions  called

The tips of chromosomes located at the terminal end after Secondary Constriction II are

16. CELL INCLUSIONS 
Due  to  metabolic  activities  in  the  cell,  several  non-living  substances  are  produced.  The 
metabolically inactive non-living substances such as proteins, amino compounds, starch grains, 
essential oils, latex, mineral crystals, cellulose, inulin, sugars, fats, oils, pigment, enzymes, etc. 


 
 
present  in the cytoplasm  of the cell  are known as cell  inclusions  or deutoplasmic  bodies  or 
ergastic bodies. 
 
Cell inclusions can be divided into three categories: 
1. Reserve materials 
2. Secretory materials 
3. Excretory materials 
1. Reserve materials 
The metabolic products stored in plant cells form the reserve food of the plant. They are either 
nitrogenous or non-nitrogenous and may remain in solid form or liquid form. 
a. Nitrogenous  reserve food  materials: Besides  carbon,  hydrogen and  oxygen, these 
materials  also possess some amount of nitrogen.  Protein granules are the solid forms 
and amino acids are the liquid form of nitrogenous reserve food materials. 
b. Non-nitrogenous  reserve  food  materials:  These  reserve  food  materials  contain 
carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. The proportion of hydrogen and oxygen is found to be 
like in water (i.e. 2:1 in H
2
O). They may or may not be soluble in water. They are the 
products of photosynthesis  e. g. starch, cellulose, sugars, inulin, glycogens, etc. 
 
2. Secretory materials 
Some important  chemical compounds which may not be related with nutrition are synthesized 
during plant  metabolism.  Such materials  are called  secretory  materials.  They may be of the 
following types: 
a. Plant pigments:  Phycocyanin, phycoerythrin,  carotenes, fucoxanthin, flavoxanthin, 
lutein,  chlorophyll-a,  chlorophyll-b,  chlorophyll-c,  etc.  are some  colour  producing 
substances that are secreted. These are also known as plant pigments. 
b. Enzymes: These water soluble biocatalysts are the nitrogenous secretory products.  
c. Nectar: It is secreted by the glands known as nectaries.  
 
3. Excretory materials 
Excretory materials are the by-products obtained during plant metabolism  which are of useless 
to plants. Such excretory products are given below: 
a. Alkaloids: These excretory compounds are composed of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and 
nitrogen and occur in association with organic acids in various plant parts. For example, 
Quinine, nicotine, morphine, atropine, daturine, etc. 
b. Organic acids: These are the outcome of various chemical  processes in living  cells. 
For example, citric acid, tartaric acid, oxalic acid, malic acid, etc. 
c. Gums: These are the products obtained after the decomposition of cellulose (cell wall) 
which is soluble in water. They swell up in water forming a viscous mass. 
d. Resins: Resins are solid, brittle, non-volatile complex substances found in various parts 
of plants. 
e. Tannins:  Tannins  are  non-nitrogenous,  complex  compounds,  commonly  found  in 
unripe fruits. It is actually found dissolved in the cell sap of parenchymatous cells. 
f. Essential oils:  The essential  oils are found in fruits,  flowers, and glandular  leaves of 
plants such as lemon, Eucalyptus, etc. 
g. Latex: Latex is an emulsion of matrix  in watery fluid. Starch grains, sugars, proteins, 
enzymes and alkaloids are dissolved in this fluid. 
h. Mineral crystals: Mineral crystals are present in parenchymatous cells of several plants 
in the form of silica crystals, calcium carbonate crystals, calcium oxalate crystals, etc.

1.3.1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION 
Cells  typically  go  through  a  series  of  activities,  including  growth,  essential  functions  for 
survival, and division to generate new cells. In fact, the growth and development of every living 
organism  depends  on the  increment  in the  number  or size  of its  cells.  Cell  division  is  that 
fundamental  process in which a parent/mother  cell divides  into two or more daughter  cells, 
ensuring growth, repair, and reproduction  in living organisms. And a series of distinct  events 
takes place in the cell leading to its division producing two daughter cells. It is crucial for the 
maintenance, growth, and repair of multicellular  organisms. 
 
Primitive  protozoans and prokaryotes divide by a simple method of cell division that involves 
copying the  cell's  chromosomes,  separating  the copied  DNA, and dividing  the parent  cell's 
cytoplasm. But in complex eukaryotic cells, division occurs involving intricate distinct phages, 
either  maintaining  the  number of chromosomes  or reducing  their  number  in daughter  cells. 
General growth and development  of multicellular  organisms  require  a diploid cell  to divide 
into  two diploid  daughter  cells  which  are  genetically  identical.  On  the  other  hand,  sexual 
reproduction in developed organisms requires a diploid cell to divide into four haploid daughter 
cells. 
In unicellular  organisms,  cell division  is the means of reproduction  by which the mother cell 
produces two or more cells. In multicellular  organisms also, new individuals  develop from a 
single cell, the zygote, by cell division. It also forms the basis of replacement of old and worn-
out cells in the process of repair and regeneration. Cell division is thus central to the life of the 
cell and is essential for the perpetuation of the species. 
 
The cell division is a process by which a mature cell divides to form new cells which resemble 
the mother cell in a number of characters. The division of the cell is achieved by two integral 
activities: 
1. the division of the nucleus i.e. karyokinesis 
2. the division of the cytoplasm i.e. cytokinesis

1.3.2 CELL CYCLE 
All cells are reproduced by dividing the pre-existing  cell. This process gives continuity to our 
life. The entire sequence that takes place during cell growth and cell division are collectively 
called cell cycle.  
Basically, a cell cycle consists of the following three main cycles: 
1. Chromosome  cycle: In this process,  a DNA molecule  is replicated  into  two identical 
daughter DNA molecules. 
2. Cytoplasmic cycle: In this process, cell growth and cytoplasm division takes place one 
after another. 
3. Centrosome  cycle:  In  this  process,  the  centrosome  is  duplicated  and  divided  and 
separated into two poles of the cell. 
The above-mentioned cycles can be accumulated into the following broad two phases: 
 
1. Interphase (L. inter = between; phasis = stage) 
It is the stage between the end of one cell division and the beginning of the next cell division. 
This  stage  is  also  known  as  resting  stage.  The  cell  appear  to  be  inactive;  however, it  is 
metabolically  very active  synthesizing  a number of essential  biomolecules  required to carry 
out the next cell division. 
Based on synthetic activities,  interphase can be divided into three sub-phases which are 
briefly featured below: 
Gap-one (G
1

During  the  gap-one  (G

enlargement of the cell, driven by an active synthesis of RNA, proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, 
and amino acids for histone, as well as the formation  of energy-rich compounds necessary for 
DNA synthesis.  This  robust  biosynthetic  process  results  in  an  increase  in  the  number  of 
nucleotides and organelles, but not in the DNA itself. The cell's metabolic activity  and growth 
are  sustained  by  this  process,  enabling  it  to  meet  the  demands  of  cellular  function  and 
replication. 
 
Synthetic phase or S-phase: 
During the synthetic phase (S-phase), DNA molecules replicate, and most histone proteins and 
subunits  of  kinetochores  are  synthesized.  This  phase  ensures  the  accurate  duplication  and

integrity and stability.

1.3.3 TYPES OF CELL DIVISION 
The cells may divide by any of the following three methods: 
1. Direct cell division or amitosis 
2. Indirect cell division or mitosis 
3. Reductional cell division or meiosis

3. Mitosis  ensures genetic stability  by maintaining  a consistent  number of chromosomes

4. Mitosis contributes to cell repair, regeneration, and heal wounds. 
5. Mitosis controls cell size by inducing the division of overgrown cells. 
6. Mitosis guarantees qualitatively  and quantitatively genetic consistency between mother 
and daughter cells. 
7. Uncontrolled mitosis  causes cancer.

stages:  Leptotene,  Zygotene,  Pachytene, 
Diplotene, and Diakinesis. 
Metaphase Chromosomes are double-stranded. 
Chromosome pairs are in a four-part state in 
Metaphase I. 
Anaphase 
Each  chromosome’s  two  chromatids  move  to 
opposite spindle poles. 
Each  pair’s two homologous chromosomes 
separate and move to opposite spindle poles. 
Telophase Always happens. The first Telophase may not occur. 
Cytokinesis 
The  division  of cell  contents  to  form two new 
cells begins in Anaphase or Telophase. The cell divides exactly in half. 
Interkinesis Not applicable. 
A rest period between Meiosis I and Meiosis 
II. 
Significance 
Daughter  cells  are  genetically  identical  to  the 
mother cell. The DNA amount is the same as in 
the mother cell. 
Daughter cells are genetically  different from 
the mother  cells.  The DNA amount  is half 
that of the mother cell.
