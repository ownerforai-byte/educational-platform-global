8.1.1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION  
The organisms shown in the picture above are Euglena, Paramecium, Fuligo and Plasmodium

belong to the kingdom protista. 
8.1.2 OUTLINE CLASSIFICATION OF PROTISTA 
Kingdom protista (Gr; protistos,  first of all) was proposed by Ernst Haeckel in 1866. Later, an 
American taxonomist  Robert Harding Whittaker  broadly divided all the living  beings of this 
universe into five kingdoms in 1969 basically on the basis of phylogenetic relationship  among 
them.

Protistans consist of microscopic, single-celled and primitive eukaryotic organisms. This group 
includes diverse living organisms that are plant-like, such as golden algae and diatoms, animals 
like such as Paramecium and Amoeba. Some of them are both plants and animals like such as 
Euglena and Astasia and some of them are fungi like such as Fuligo, Protostelium, etc. 
CHARACTERISTICS FEATURES OF KINGDOM PROTISTA 
Organisms belonging to the protista have following features: 
1. They are usually  aquatic and some of them are also found in moist soil. 
2. They  are simple,  microscopic  and unicellular  eukaryotes. Some  protist like Volvox is 
colonial. 
3. They have protoplasmic  or acellular  level of body organization. 
4. Cell  body is  externally  covered by cell  membrane  or  cell  wall  and some  of them  are 
without any outer covering. 
5. They have either single nucleus (monomorphic)  or two nuclei (dimorphic condition) in 
Paramecium or many nuclei (coenocytic condition) in Opalina. 
6. Their  locomotion takes place  with the help of pseudopodia, flagella and cilia.  In some 
forms, there may not be locomotory organs. 
7. Mode  of  nutrition  is  different among  them.  They  show  holophytic  or  holozoic  or 
mixotrophic or saprozoic or parasitic mode of nutrition. 
8. Digestion of food particles takes place inside the cell (intracellular  digestion). 
9. Respiration and excretion take place through general body surface. 
10. Reproduction takes place asexually  by binary fission, multiple  fission, cyst formation, 
sporulation etc. Sexual reproduction takes place by conjugation and fusion of gametes 
(syngamy). 
Some protistans are planktons. Planktons are those creatures  which are free swimming on 
the surface of water. They are autotrophic  plant like, called phytoplankton (Diatoms)  and 
heterotrophic animal like, called zooplankton (paramecium).

3. Group C: Protozoan protists 
i. They are aquatic and terrestrial both. Most of them are parasitic. Some of them

ii. They are simplest,  primitive, acellular and microscopic animalcules. 
iii. They are solitary or colonial having protoplasmic  body organization. 
iv. Body is covered by cell membrane,  pellicle  and shells. 
v. They have varied body shape such as spherical,  oval, elongated and flattened.

vii. Number and shape of nucleus also vary among protozoans.

xii. Reproduction takes place by asexually and sexually both. Asexual reproduction

Conclusion 
 
A. External structure (Morphology) 
a. Shape and size 
Paramecium caudatum has slipper shaped or spindle shaped cell body so it is commonly called 
the ‘Slipper Animalcule’.  Its body is asymmetrical due to the presence of obliquely situated 
oral groove. Body is elongated and blunt at the anterior end, wider just below the middle region 
and somewhat pointed at the posterior  end. Its ventral  side is flat and marks the oral surface 
and dorsal side is called the aboral surface. 
It is a microscopic  organism and visible to the naked eye as a whitish or greyish spot. Among 
the  several  species, Paramecium caudatum is  the  largest  measuring  about  170μ-290  μ  in 
length. 


 
 
 
Figure 8.1.6 Paramecium caudatum 
b. Pellicle: 
Body of Paramecium is externally  covered by a thin, double layered, elastic  and gelatinous 
protective  membrane  called  pellicle.  Elastic  nature  of  pellicle  facilitates  the  organism  to 
squeeze or pass through narrow places. Under high power microscope, the pellicle consists of 
regular series  of hexagonal shaped depressions  and from the centre of each hexagonal shape 
emerges  out a cilium.  The anterior  and posterior  margin  of the hexagonal  depressions  have 
opening for the discharge of trichocyst. 
 
Figure 8.1.7 Surface view of small area of pellicle of Paramecium 
 
 
  


 
 
c. Cilia: 
The whole body of Paramecium is covered by numerous tiny hair like projections called cilia. 
Cilia are uniform sized and arranged in longitudinal  rows except at the posterior  end, where 
they are the long tuft, forming caudal tuft. Each cilium arises from a spherical basal granule or 
kinetosome lying just below the pellicle in ectoplasm. 
Cilia  are use by the organism  for  locomotion,  food capturing  and also as sensory receptors 
which respond to external stimuli. Caudal tuft helps to change the direction while swimming.  
 
B. Internal structure 
a. Cytoplasm 
   Cytoplasm of Paramecium is clearly differentiated  into two regions. 
i. Ectoplasm: It is a thin, peripheral, clear and dense region of cytoplasm which includes 
trichocyst, basal bodies and fibrils. 
ii. Endoplasm: It is  the large  central, granular  and semifluid region  which contains cell 
components  such  as  nucleus,  contractile  vacuole,   food  vacuoles,   mitochondria, 
golgibodies, ribosomes  etc. 
 
b. Trichocyst: 
Trichocysts  are small,  peculiar,  rod-like or oval shaped organelles  found in ectoplasm 
beneath  the  pellicle  alternating  with  basal  bodies.  They  lie  right  angle  to  the  body 
surface  and open  by  small  pores  on  the  ridges  of  hexagonal  areas  of  pellicle.  Each 
trichocyst consists of an elongated shaft and a terminal pointed tip, called spike covered 
by  a  cap  like  structure.  Trichocyst  is  filled  with  a  refractive  dense  fluid  having  a 
swelling  substance.  Discharge  of  trichocyst  is  triggered  by  external  stimuli  such as 
touch, chemical, temperature, electric current etc. 
Trichocysts are discharged as long, sticky threads in response to stimuli. At the moment 
of  stimulation,  the  ectoplasm  quickly  contracts,  separating  the  cap  and  spike  of 
trichocyst.  Consequently, water enters the sac  of trichocyst  and substance in  the sac 
swells by absorbing water to form a filamentous spike for discharging out. A discharged 
trichocyst  has  an  opaque  spike  like  the  inverted nail  with  a  cap,  attached to a  long 
striated shaft. Shaft can’t be seen in undischarged trichocyst. 
Function: Trichocysts  help to attach on solid object or substratum. They  also help  in 
offence and defence in some ciliates.

c. Nucleus: 
Paramecium caudatum possess   two  types  of  nuclei   and  this  is   called  nuclear 
dimorphism. Larger, bean shaped nucleus is called macronucleus or meganucleus. Its 
function is  to regulate the metabolic  activities  of its cell.  It is  also  called somatic or 
vegetative nucleus. Similarly,  the small  and spherical shaped nucleus lying close or at 
the  concavity  of  macronucleus  is called micronucleus.  Micronucleus  controls  the 
reproductive activity of Paramecium and so it is also called generative nucleus. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
d. Contractile vacuole: 
Unlike Amoeba, there are two large liquid filled contractile vacuoles in Paramecium, 
each fixed at the anterior and posterior  position closer to aboral surface in endoplasm. 
Contractile vacuoles open outside through permanent pore in pellicle  on aboral surface. 
Sometimes, they may disappear temporarily. Each contractile vacuole is surrounded by 
about six to ten long, narrow tubular structures called radiating canals or radial canals. 
The radial canals communicate  with the interior part of body from where they take up 
excess water and pour into the central vacuole. When the vacuole becomes fully turgid 
then  it  contracts  and discharges  its  content  through  permanent  pore  in  the  pellicle. 
Posterior  contractile  vacuole  contracts  more  frequently  than  the  anterior  contractile 
vacuole. Since, more water enters this vacuole as it is nearer to the cytopharynx.  
Function of contractile vacuole is osmoregulation i.e. maintaining water and salt in the 
body.

e. Food vacuole: 
Food vacuoles  are also  called  gastrioles  which are  roughly  spherical,  non-contractile 
structures  with variable  numbers  and size.  They  are  found embedded in  endoplasm. 
Food vacuoles  contain ingested food particles  such as bacteria, small  amount of fluid 
along  with digestive  enzymes bounded  by  a  thin  membrane.  They  are  seen,  moving 
with the streaming cytoplasm (cyclosis)  in the clockwise direction throughout the body. 
During the course of movement, food particles are digested, absorbed and assimilated. 
f. Oral groove: 
Oral groove is a large oblique depression on the ventro-lateral side of the body, making 
the body of Paramecium asymmetrical  shaped. Oral  groove  posteriorly  leads into  a 
short conical funnel shaped depression called vestibule. Vestibule leads posteriorly into 
cytopharynx  through  a  fixed  oval  shaped  opening  called  cytostome  (cell  mouth). 
Cytopharynx runs obliquely downward and form new food vacuole at its proximal end. 
Several rows of cilia are found lining the oral apparatus and beating action of these cilia 
help  in collecting and  passing down  food  particles.  The  food  particles  are  finally 
trapped in food vacuole. 
 
Figure 8.1.10 (a) Food particles with water current into the oral groove (b) Cyclosis 
 
g. Cytopyge (cell anus):  
At the time of egestion, a minute opening on the pellicle on ventral surface is visible, 
just behind the cytostome  called  cytopyge. Cytopyge  is  also  called  anal  spot  or 
cytoproct. This is used for expelling out undigested food particles.

Reproduction 
Reproduction is the life process in which living beings produce offspring of their own kind for 
the continuation of their generation. 
In Paramecium,  asexual  reproduction  takes  place  by  transverse  binary  fission  and  sexual 
reproduction takes place by various methods such as conjugation, endomixis, autogamy etc. 


 
 
 
Transverse binary fission 
This type of reproduction  takes place during favourable condition  at the availability  of food, 
suitable temperature etc. Paramecium caudatum divides 2-3 times a day by this process. Entire 
process completes in about 1-2 hours. In transverse binary fission, Paramecium divides in such 
a way that plane of division  is right angle to the axis of the body. In this process, division  of 
nucleus (karyokinesis) is followed by the division of cytoplasm (cytokinesis). Following events 
occur for the completion of transverse binary fission: 
 
Figure 8.1.11 Transverse binary fission in Paramecium 
 
i. Paramecium stops feeding and its oral groove or buccal structure also disappears. 
ii. At that time,  micronucleus  divides by mitosis  to form two daughter nuclei  and each 
move towards the opposite end. 
iii. Simultaneously,  macronucleus  becomes elongated  and  constricted  at  the  middle  to 
divide by amitosis. Daughter macronuclei  also move to the opposite pole. 
iv. Now, two oral grooves start to form, one at the anterior half and another at the posterior 
half on the ventral side.  
v. Each half develops a new contractile vacuole at the required position. Buccal structures 
also start to reappear.  
vi. Meanwhile,  a  transverse  constriction  or  furrow  appears  at  the  middle  region  which 
deepens and finally cytoplasm is also divided to form two daughter Paramecia. 
vii. Anterior daughter Paramecium is called proter and posterior one is called opisthe. 
Daughter Paramecia produced by this  process are called clone because they are genetically 
similar.  After many generations,  the rate  of binary fission  becomes slow and may stops. So, 
they should be rejuvenated frequently by conjugation. 
 
Conjugation 
It is the simplest method of sexual reproduction  in which temporary union of two Paramecia 
of  same  species  but  different  strain  takes  place  to exchange  their  genetic  materials. It is  a 
significant  process  that  should occur  frequently  between  transverse  binary  fissions because 
after many generations  (about 300 asexual generations), Paramecium is unable to reproduce 


 
 
asexually. Paramecia undergoing conjugation are morphologically  similar but physiologically 
different, called pre-conjugants. Factors which induce conjugation are: 
i. Insufficient supply of food or starvation. 
ii. Being genetically and physiologically  weak and small due to continued binary fission. 
iii. Sudden darkness or fall in temperature in some species. 
iv. A specific protein present in cilia  of mating individual. 
 
Events that occur in conjugation 
1. Two Paramecium caudatum of different strain or mating type come in contact ventrally 
and unite by their oral grooves. 
2. Buccal structures disappear and feeding is also stopped. 
3. At  the  point  of  contact,  pellicle  and  ectoplasm  also  dissolve  forming  protoplasmic 
bridge between them and Paramecia at this moment are called conjugants. 
4. The  macronucleus degenerates into  pieces  and absorbed  by  cytoplasm.  Meanwhile, 
micronucleus   grows  first  and  divides  by  meiosis  to  yield  four  haploid  daughter 
micronuclei. 
5. Out of four daughter micronuclei,  three get degenerated and remaining  one divides by 
mitosis to produce two unequal sized pronuclei or gametic nuclei. 
6. Among two pronuclei,  smaller  sized and active nucleus behaves as migratory  or male 
gametic  nucleus  whereas  larger  sized  and passive  nucleus  behaves  as  stationary  or 
female gametic nucleus. 
7. Each migratory or male gametic nucleus moves from one conjugant to another through 
protoplasmic  bridge and fuses with stationary or female gametic nucleus. 
8. Now, zygote nucleus or synkaryon is formed. This phenomenon of complete fusion of 
two nuclei from different individual is also called amphimixis. 
9. After 12- 48 hours of union of two individuals, they become separated and now called 
ex-conjugants. 
10. In each ex-conjugant, zygote nucleus divides thrice by mitosis to produce eight nuclei. 
Out of them,  four nuclei  become  enlarged  to  form  macronuclei  and remaining  four 
become micronuclei. 
11. Among four micronuclei,  three micronuclei  get degenerated. 
12. Now, remaining  micronucleus  of each ex-conjugant divides mitotically  and transverse 
binary  fission in Paramecium occurs  to produce two daughter Paramecia,  each  with 
two macronuclei and one micronucleus. 
13. The daughter Paramecium, so produced, again divides by binary fission to produce two 
Paramecia, each with one micronucleus and one macronucleus. 
14. In this  way, altogether  eight daughter Paramecia are  produced at the end which  are 
genetically different.

Significance of conjugation 
i. Conjugation brings nuclear re-arrangement or re-organization  which is essential for the 
continuation of binary fission. 
ii. Conjugation  is  the means  of rejuvenation  maintaining  vitality  and genetic  vigour  in 
offsprings. 
iii. It brings about the replacement of macro or meganucleus  with the material from zygote 
nucleus. 


 
 
iv. Conjugation brings variation in offsprings. 
v. In conjugation, Paramecium multiplies  significantly, as eight offsprings are  produced 
at the end.

8.1.4 Plasmodium vivax 
Let’s discuss the given questions. 
i. What do you know about malaria?  
ii. Why is malaria  a significant health problem globally? 
iii. What do you think about the strategies to prevent malaria? 
Plasmodium is a protozoan parasite that causes dreadful disease called malaria in human beings 
and other  animals.  There  are  more  than  fifty known species  of Plasmodium, causing  such 
disease in man and other animals like lizards, birds and other mammals. So, they are commonly 
called malarial fever parasites. Among them, four species of Plasmodium namely Plasmodium 
vivax, Plasmodium malariae, Plasmodium ovale, Plasmodium falciparum are most common to 
human beings causing different types of malaria. 
Classification 
Kingdom :  Protista 
Phylum :  Protozoa 
Class  : Sporozoa 
Genus  : Plasmodium 
Species : vivax 
Common name: Malarial parasite 
 
Habit and habitat: 
Plasmodium vivax is distributed in the temperate or warmer region of the world such as Nepal, 
India, and Sri Lanka, Bangladesh etc. It is an intracellular blood parasite in human, thriving in 
RBC and liver  cells  but it  is  extracellular  in female Anopheles mosquito  living  in gut  and 
salivary  glands. Female Anopheles mosquito  is  also called vector since it  is the carrier  of 
Plasmodium parasite from malaria infected person to healthy person. Plasmodium vivax causes 
tertian malaria in human which is characterized  by a 48-hour cycle between the first  malaria 
fever and subsequent recurrence of chills and fever. 
Do you know? 
In the past, it was believed that the cause of malaria  is bad air or foul gases of marshy areas 
and hence  the name  malaria  (Gr;  mala=bad;  aria=air)  was  given.  Later in  1880,  Charles 
Laveran  reported for the first  time  that blood of  malarial  patient contains Plasmodium as 
parasites. But it was in mystery for a long time that how these parasites were transmitted to 
human beings. Then, Patrick Mason in 1894 suggested that there is role of mosquitoes in the 
transmission  of this disease from person to person.

Life cycle 
An organism  which harbors  parasites  is  known as host. Plasmodium vivax is the digenetic 
parasite; completing  its life cycle in two hosts: Female Anopheles mosquito and human. In 
this case, humans suffer from malaria but mosquitoes are not harmed.  
Female Anopheles mosquito  is considered  primary host as sexual phases of life  cycle of the 
parasites  are  passed  in  it  whereas  human  is  referred  secondary  host  in  which Plasmodium 
completes its asexual cycle.  
The female Anopheles mosquito is also called a vector as it transmits  malarial  parasites  from 
one host to another. 
1. Asexual life cycle of Plasmodium vivax in human 
a. Infection or inoculation  of sporozoites 
The  infective  female Anopheles mosquito  carries  infectious stage  of Plasmodium  vivax 
called sporozoite  in  its salivary  gland. When it bites a  healthy person  for sucking  blood, 
large numbers of sporozoites are inoculated along with saliva. It pours saliva at the time of 
blood sucking because it contains anticoagulants which prevent the clotting of blood so that 
it is  able to suck blood continuously.  Structure of a sporozoite under electron microscopy 
is given below: 
 
Figure 8.1.15 Structure of sporozoite 
i. Sporozoite is  a microscopic,  unnucleated, and sickle  shaped structure with somewhat 
tapering ends. It measures about 12μ length and 1μ width. 
ii. It is externally covered by a thin, firm and elastic membrane called pellicle. 
iii. It  shows  wriggling  movement  because  of  the presence  of longitudinally  arranged 
contractile microtubules. 
iv. Paired  organelles  which  secrete  lytic  juice,  open  into apical  cap  at the anterior  end. 
Lytic juice helps in the penetration of targeted cells of host. 
v. Similarly,  convoluted tubules of unknown function, a centrally placed large prominent 
nucleus and a mitochondrion are also found embedded in the cytoplasm.

b. Schizogony in liver 
After the sporozoites are inoculated into the human blood by mosquito, they disappear from 
blood stream after half an hour and enter hepatic or liver cells.  Sporozoites penetrate liver 
cells  with  the  help  of  lytic  enzymes  secreted  by  paired  organelles and  multiply  there 
asexually by  a  process  called  schizogony. The  daughter cells  so  formed  are  known  as 
merozoites. Liver schizogony can be discussed in following headings: 
i. Pre-erythrocytic scizogony (First schizogony in liver cells) 
After the  entry  of sporozoite  in liver cell, it grows  in  size  and shape,  becomes 
spherical  to form  schizont.  Schizont  divides  by schizogony (multiple fission)  to 
form thousands  of  unnucleated  cells  called  cryptomerozoites.  Now, liver  cells 
rupture and cryptomerozoites are released into liver  sinusoids. 
 
 
Figure 8.1.16 Schizogony in liver 
ii. Exo-erythrocytic schizogony (Second schizogony in liver  cells) 
From  liver  sinusoids,  Cryptomerozoites  enter fresh  liver  cells;  grow into circular 
schizonts which feed on the liver content. They undergo series  of schizogony there 
and finally produce thousands of metacryptomerozoites which are  released by the 
bursting of the liver cells. Metacryptomerozoites are of two types: smaller and more 
numerous micro-metacryptomerozoites enter  blood and attack RBCs. Larger and 
fewer macro-metacryptomerozoites again attack fresh liver cells.  
 
Do you know? 
The time  period between initial  sporozoite infection and first appearance of parasites in 
blood is called pre-patent period. It is about 8 days in Plasmodium vivax. 
 
 
c. Schizogony in RBC or erythrocytic schizogony 
After entering  the  blood  stream,  micro-metacryptomerozoites invade  RBCs  and  pass 
through several stages that are discussed below: 
i. Micrometacryptomerozoites  feed  nutrients  from  RBC;  and  get vacuolated  and 
rounded to become young trophozoite. 


 
 
ii. As the trophozoite grows, a central large non-contractile vacuole pushes the nucleus 
and cytoplasm towards the peiphery. This  stage seems to be a ring with a gem like 
form  called signet  ring  stage.  Digestive  enzymes of  the  parasite break  down 
haemoglobin  into protein and hematin. Protein is used as  food by trophozoite and 
hematin  forms  a  toxic  malarial  pigment  called haemozoin which  is  yellowish 
brown coloured fine granules.  
iii. The signet ring later develops into amoeboid stage having pseudopodial processes. 
Small  red eosinophilic  granules  appear  in  cytoplasm  of RBCs called schuffner’s 
dots and RBC becomes enlarged and pale yellow at this time. 
iv. Amoeboid stage after active feeding changes into an enlarged and rounded structure 
called schizont. It undergoes schizogony in which nucleus divides mitotically into 
12- 24 nuclei and each remaining in the periphery and surrounded by cytoplasm to 
form oval shaped merozoites.  The peripheral  arrangement of the merozoites  with 
central mass  of unused cytoplasm,  and haemozoin  resembles  like  the arrangement  
of petals in rose and hence this stage is named as Rosette stage. 
The red  blood  cells  rupture  to release  large  number  of  merozoites  in  blood  plasma.  These 
merozoites,  again, attack  fresh  RBC and finally  repeat  the cycle  in RBC. One Erythrocytic 
schizogony cycle completes in 48 hours.  
d) Post erythrocytic  schizogony 
Sometimes,  some merozoites  may again attack liver cells and undergo schizogony  which is 
referred as post-erythrocytic  schizogony. 
 
Figure 8.1.17 Schizogony in RBC 
Do you know? 
Release  of haemozoin  toxin  and merozoites  to the  blood  plasma  by  the  rupture  of  RBC 
causes malaria  fever in which the patient suffers from chills, shivering, high temperature and 


 
 
convulsion  with  profuse  sweating.  It comes  at  the  interval  of  every  48  hours  i.e  at  the 
completion  of one  erythrocytic  cycle  which  releases  merozoites  and haemozoin  in  blood 
plasma. 
 
Incubation period is the time  period between the entry of pathogen and appearance  of first 
symptom. For Plasmodium vivax it is 14 days in average. 
 
 
e. Formation of gametocytes 
After the many generations of schizogony in RBCs, some merozoites  slowly increase in size 
producing  large  amount  of  haemozoin  and  transform  into  specific  structures  called 
gametocytes. Gametocytes are of two types: - macrogametocytes  and microgametocytes. 
Macrogametocytes  are called female gametocytes which are less numerous, larger with small 
compact nucleus and food ladened cytoplasm.  
Microgametocytes  are called male gametocytes which are more numerous, smaller with clear 
cytoplasm and large central nucleus. 
 
These gametocytes  do not  develop  further  but  remain  inactive  in human  blood  for  several 
weeks.  They  get  either  degenerated  in  human  blood  or  sucked  up  with  blood  by  female 
Anopheles mosquito to continue their sexual cycle. 
2. Sexual phase of Plasmodium vivax in female Anopheles mosquito 
a. Sucking up blood by the mosquito  
Female Anopheles mosquito sucks blood of malaria infected person with gametocytes and other 
phases  of this  parasite  in  RBCs. In the alimentary  canal  of mosquito,  Gametocytes  remain 
undigested because they have developed adaptation against digestive enzymes of the mosquito. 
b. Gametogony (Development of gametes) 
Gametogony is the  process of development  of male  and female  gametes  from  gametocytes. 
Microgametocytes  give rise to the formation of male gametes or microgametes. In this process, 
microgametocytes  in  the  stomach  of  mosquito  undergo  ex-flagellation process,  which  is 
favoured by the sudden fall in temperature  as the microgametocytes  are transferred from warm 
blooded human to cold-blooded  mosquito.  During this process, nucleus of microgametocyte 
divides by mitosis and produce about 6-8 haploid nuclei which lie in the periphery of cytoplasm 
and then cytoplasm along with each nucleus is projected out in the form of flagellar structure. 
Each projection separates as a mature and motile male gamete. 
Similarly,  macrogametocyte  undergoes some structural  re-organization  and grows into a non-
motile gamete called female gamete or ovum.

c. Fertilization  and ookinete formation: 
The  macrogamete or  female  gamete develops  a  small  protuberance  or  outgrowth  called 
receptive  cone which is to receive micro-gamete or male gamete at the time  of fertilization. 
Many microgametes  approach the female  gamete but  only one of them  is  able to  enter  the 
macrogamete  or ovum  by  penetrating  the  receptive  cone. Fertilization  takes  place  to  form 
zygote and this process is also called syngamy. 
 
Zygote remains rounded and motionless for about 24 hours and then gets elongated to become 
a motile  worm like (vermiform)  structure  called ookinete or vermicule  which measure  about 
15μ-20μ in length and 3 μ in breadth. 
d. Encystment and sporogony 
Ookinete penetrates the wall of stomach of mosquito and it remains attached beneath the outer 
epithelial  layer  of  stomach.  Then,  it  becomes  spherical  and  undergoes  encystment  by 
developing a thin, elastic,  membranous cyst wall. The cyst wall is secreted  by ookinete itself 
and with some tissues of stomach of mosquito. The encysted ookinete is now referred as oocyst. 
About 50 or more oocysts can be seen on the stomach of an infected mosquito. 
Sporogony is the process of asexual  multiplication  to form sporozoites  in the oocyst. In this 
process,  the  nucleus  first  divides  by meiosis  and,  subsequently, by  mitosis  to  form  several 
haploid daughter nuclei. Meanwhile, cytoplasm  also develops many large vacuoles making it 
sponge like appearance. Numerous cytoplasmic masses are also formed that take up individual 
nuclei. Then, each cytoplasmic mass along with a daughter nucleus and vacuoles give rise to a 
slender, spindle shaped haploid cell  called a sporozoite. A mature  oocyst may contain  about 
10,000 sporozoites.  Each sporozoite is a sickle shaped structure  which is pointed at the ends 
and  broad  at  the  middle  bearing  a  large  prominent  nucleus  with  other  cell  organelles  in 
cytoplasm.  By the  rupture  of mature  oocyst, sporozoites  are  released  into  haemocoelom  or 
body cavity of the Anopheles mosquito, from where they reach the salivary gland and enter the 
duct of hypopharynx to be discharged with saliva while biting a healthy human. Then, another 
asexual phase of life cycle continues in human.

8.1.5 Concept of Plasmodium falciparum 
Plasmodium  falciparum is  worldwide  in  distribution.  It  is  mainly  distributed  in  tropical, 
subtropical and warmer regions of the world. It causes malignant tertian malaria or pernicious 
malaria  fever  almost  continuously  or  at  the  interval  of  about  2  days.  It  causes  a  serious 
condition  in  human  called Blackwater  fever which  is  characterized  by  the  complete 
destruction  of  RBCs and haematuria  (discharging  the  characteristic  black  or dark  coloured 
urine). It is most dangerous species of Plasmodium that causes cerebral malaria. Death rate is 
higher in the infection of P. falciparum. 
Incubation period for this disease is about 12 days in average. Pre-patent period is about 5 days 
due to  the  absence  of  exo-erythocytic  schizogony. In the  signet  ring  stage,  a small  ring  is 
situated at the edge of RBC with two chromatin dots. Trophozoite is small as compared to that 
of Plasmodium vivax and sometimes  there are two trophozoites in one RBC. Schizont is very 
small measuring 5 μ diameter. Haemozoin is black coloured and RBCs are not enlarged but get 
shrinked  and greenish  coloured. No schuffners dots are seen but maure’s dots are found in 
RBC. Schizonts  in  RBC form  8-36 merozoites  which are not  seen in  peripheral  blood and 
gametocytes are crescentic shaped.

8.1.6 Economic importance of Plasmodium 
Plasmodium vivax has no any harmful effect inside the body of Anopheles but it is pathogenic 
to  human  being  causing a  disease, Benign  tertian  malaria.  The symptoms in  the  case  of 
P.vivax appear after 14 days of initial infection. This time is utilized by the parasite to establish 
malarial  symptoms. For this, large number of parasites continues their life cycle in the RBCs. 
When all  the schizonts  in RBC burst  and made free,  merozoites  along with haemozoin  are 
released in to the plasma inducing fever to the person because haemozoin is a toxin. Each attack 
of fever consists of three stages: 
i. Cold stage: In this stage, patient feels much cold with shivering and chattering teeth. 
ii. Hot  stage:  As the cold stage or  chill  subsides, the body temperature  increases up  to 
106°F with a terrible headache. 
iii. Sweating stage:  Patient sweats profusely in this stage due to lowering of temperature. 
Temperature  becomes normal until the next attack of fever (in 48 hours). 
Other symptoms  of malaria  are anaemia, haemolysis,  splenomegaly  (enlargement  of spleen) 
etc. 
Control measures of mosquito 
1. Adult mosquitoes can be destructed by various methods like manual killing, using traps, 
fumigation, spraying insecticides etc. 
2. Swampy areas, marshes, stagnant water such as pools ponds ditches should be removed 
from human dwelling areas as they are the breeding places of mosquito. 
3. Open drains should be closed. 
4. Tin  cans,  bucket, unused tyre,  barrels  etc. should be  removed  because  they  serve  as 
breeding places for mosquito. 
5. Bushes and shrubs should be cleared off. 
6. Destructing the breeding places, such as  water reservoir  of mosquito by spraying  oil, 
kerosene, and other chemicals. 
7. Biological  control  of  mosquito  can  be  done  by the  help  of predator  fishes  like 
Gambusia, trout, stickle back in ponds, pools, ditches and other water reservoirs.

8.2.1 SOME TERMS USED IN BIOLOGY 
1. Level  of  organization:   Depending  upon  the  number  and  degree  of  complexity   or 
specialization  of  cells  present  in  an  animal,  following  body organizations are  found in 
animals: 
i. Cellular  level of body organization:  In primitive  multicellular  organisms  like sponges, 
there is  no co-ordination among  the cells  and cells  are  not organized to form tissues. 
Individual cell performs its own function. This is cellular  level of body organization. 
ii. Tissue level of body organization: In coeleneterates, certain cells are organized together 
to form tissues which is called tissue level  of body organization. Example:  Nerve cells 
forming nerve net in Hydra. 
iii. Organ-system    level    of    body    organization:    In    multicellular     animals     from 
platyhelminthes  to  chordates,  tissues  are  aggregated  into  organs  and organs  are  co-
ordinated or  associated to form a particular  system performing  specific  function such 
as digestion, circulation, respiration  etc. 
 
2. Body plan: body plan in animals  can be divided into following types: 
i. Cell  aggregate  plan:  In  this,  body  consists  of  aggregation  of  many  cells  where 
individual cell functions independently. There is no tissue formation in this type of body 
plan. It is only found in porifera phylum.

ii. Blind sac plan: Body contains a single cavity which blindly ends at the posterior side. 
There  is  a  single  opening  at the anterior  end. It is  found in  coelenterates  and some 
platyhelminthes.

4. Body cavity (coelom) 
The large fluid filled space between gut (alimentary canal) wall and body wall is called coelom. 
On the basis of presence or absence of coelom in the body, animals are of following types: 
i. Acoelomate:  Some  animals  like  sponges,  coelenterates,  and  platyhelminthes  donot 
have coelom and hence they are called acoelomate animals. 
ii. Pseudocoelomate:  Coelom  derived from  blastocoel  of the  embryonic  stage is  called 
pseudocoelom. This  type of coelom  is  enclosed by ectoderm and endoderm. Animals 
in Aschehelminthes only are acoelomate. 
iii. Eucoelomate or coelomate:  This  is  the true coelom  which is enclosed by mesodermal 
cells from both sides. It is of following types: 
a. Schizocoel: Coelom formed by splitting of mesoderm layer in the embryonic stage. 
It is found in annelida, arthropoda, mollusca and higher chordate. 
b. Enterocoel:  In ehinoderms and hemichordate, mesodermal  pouches  develop from 
larval  archenteron and then fuse to form coelom. It is called enterocoel. Example: 
Echinoderms and protochordates. 
c. Haemocoel:  It is  the modified form of coelom  in  arthropods, mollusca  and some 
annelids in which coelom is greatly reduced and filled with blood.

5. Segmentation:  
Segmentation  is  the division  of body  of an  animal  into  linear  series  of similar  parts  or 
sections. 
i. Unsegmented  body:  Animals  such  as  sponges,  coelenterates,  nemathelminthes, 
mollusca,  echinoderms have no segmentation of their body. 
ii. Metameric  segmentation:  It is  the division  of body in  specific  pattern, into many 
linear series  or segments where all segments are interdependent, work together and 
of same stage of maturation. Example: annelida, arthropoda and chordata. 
iii. Psedosegmentation: Body with many independent and false segments where growth 
of body takes place by a type of budding or strobilization  and all  segments are not 
in the same stage of maturation. Example: tapeworms

8.2.2 PHYLUM PORIFERA 
The name porifera (L; porus-pore; ferro-to bear) was coined by Robert grant. The evidence of 
their animal nature was given by John Ellis in 1765 in which he found water currents. Porifera 
are commonly  called  sponges. Branch of  biology which  deals with  the study  of porifera  is 
called parazoology.

General characters 
1. Porifera  are  all  aquatic  animals,  which are  mostly  found in marine  water and few of 
them are found in freshwater. 
2. They  are  primitive  and  first  multicellular  organisms  having  cellular  grade  of  body 
organization. 
3. Adult body is sessile and sedentary, found attached on substratum. 
4. Body shape is vase or cylinder like, tubular, cushion like, branched tube like. 
5. Their  body is either asymmetrical  or radially symmetrical. 
6. They  are  diploblastic  i.e.  developed from two germ  layers  in  embryonic  stage. Their 
body wall  consists  of  outer  pinacoderm  (dermal  epithelium)  and  inner  choanoderm 
(gastral  epithelium)   with  non-cellular  gelationous  mesenchyme  layer  in  between. 
Mesenchyme is composed of skeletal elements and amoeboid cells. 
7. Body is perforated by many pores where smaller  pores are called ostia and larger pores 
are called oscula. Water enters the body through ostia and exits through osculum.  This 


 
 
phenomenon  is  called  water canal  system.  Its advantages for  sponges  are  to  help  in 
feeding, respiration and excretion. 
8. Internal  hollow  cavity  of body is  called  spongocoel  which  is  lined  by  choanocytes. 
Choanocytes are flagellated collar cells. 
9. Their  skeleton  is  formed of  either  spongin  fibres  or  silicious  spicules  or  calcareous 
spicules. 
10. They  have no alimentary  canal  and digestion takes place inside  the cell  (intracellular 
digestion). 
11. Respiratory,  excretory and nervous  system are absent. Respiration  and excretion take 
place through general body surface. 
12. Nervous and sensory cells  are not differentiated. Individual cell responds to stimuli. 
13. They are hermaphrodite but there always occur cross fertilization. 
14. They reproduce asexually by budding, gemmule formation and regeneration. 
15. Sexual reproduction takes place by the fusion of sperms  and ovum and fertilization is 
internal. There  is holoblastic  cleavage of zygote and development is indirect with free 
swimming  ciliated larva called parenchymula  or amphiblastula  larva. The larva finally 
settles on a substratum and undergoes metamorphosis  to spend sedentary mode of life. 
Unique   features: primitive    multicellular    animals,    cellular    grade   of   body 
organization, numerous pores in the body, presence of water canal system, adult form 
fixed or attached on substratum, internal  hollow  cavity is  spongoecoel,  skeleton  in 
the  form  of  calcareous  or  silicious  spicules  or  spongin  fibres,  parenchymula  or 
amphiblastula  larva  
 
Classification 
Based on the types of skeleton found, following are the classes of porifera: 
Class 1: Calcarea (L; calcareous-limy)  or calcispongiae 
i. They  have  calcareous  (made from  calcium  carbonate) spicules  as  skeleton which  are 
monaxon, tetraxon and triradiate. 
ii. Body shape is vase like or cylindrical. 
iii. They are found in shallow marine water. 
iv. They are smaller  sized and dull coloured sponges. 
v. They are solitary or colonial. 
Example: Sycon (vase sponge), Grantia, leucosolenia, Clathrina 
Class 2:  Hexactinellida  (Gr;  hex- six;  actin-ray)  or hyalospongiae  (Gr;  hyalos- glassy; 
spongos - sponge) 
i. They have six rayed silicious  (made from silica)  spicules as skeleton which are triaxon. 
ii. They are found in deep marine water. 
iii. Body shape is like cup and vase. 
iv. They are moderate sized and commonly called glass rope sponges. 
v. Most of them are solitary. 


 
 
Example: Euplectella (venus flower basket), Hyalonema (glass rope sponge), Pheronema 
(bowl sponge), Farnera etc. 
Class 3: Demospongiae (Gr; demos-frame; sponges- sponge) 
i. They  have skeleton in  the form of sponging fibres or silicious  spicules  with sponging 
fibre or no skeleton. Spicules are never six rayed. 
ii. They are mostly found in marine  water. Few of them are found in fresh water such as 
Spongila, Ephydatia. 
iii. Body shape is vase or cup or cushion like. 
iv. They are more numerous and mostly large sized sponges. 
v. They are solitary or colonial. 
Example: Euspongia (bath sponge), Chalina (mermaids’ sponge), Phyllospongia (leaf sponge), 
Cliona (boring sponge), etc. 
       
8.2.3 PHYLUM COELENTERATA 
Leukart created  the phylum  coelenterata  (Gr; koilos-cavity;  enteron-intestine)  and Hatschek 
named them as cnidarians. They are commonly  called cnidarians  or stinging  animals as they 
specialized stinging cells or nematocyst (Cnidoblast or nematoblast)  in their body. 
                         
Hydra (freshwater polyp)      Aurelia (jelly fish)  Gorgonia (sea fan) 
Figure 8.2.11 some coelenterates 
General characters 
1. They are all aquatic, mostly found in marine water; few of them are found in fresh water 
such as Hydra. 
2. Level  of body organization  is  cell-tissue  grade because  some  cells  are  aggregated to 
form tissue and there is no formation of organs and system. 
3. They  are  either solitary  or colonial  and sedentary; found attached on  substratum like 
rocks stone and hard objects. Some of them are free swimming. 
4. They are diploblastic i.e developed from two germ  layers. Their  body wall consists of 
outer ectodermal layer and inner endodermal layer of cells with gelationous mesogloea 
in between. Composition of mesogloea  varies which may be either acellular  or cellular 
with connective tissue. 
5. They are acoelomate animals. 
6. The shape of the body is hollow tube-like, umbrella- like or large bell-like. 
7. The mouth and margin of the bell are surrounded by slender and short tentacles. 
8. Tentacles help in capturing and ingestion of food and also in locomotion in some forms. 
Stinging cells  or cnidoblasts help in paralyzing  prey and also in defence. 


 
 
9. They exhibit unique phenomenon of polymorphism  in which main forms or zooids are 
polyps  and medusa.  Polyp  is  the asexual  zooid  found attached on  substratum  while 
medusa is free swimming having role in sexual reproduction. 
10. Exoskeleton is made up of either chitin (in perisarc)  or cacium carbonate (in corals). 
11. There  is  a  internal  hollow  cavity  inside  the  body  called  gastrovascular  cavity  or 
coelenteron which is lined by gastrodermis. This cavity opens anteriorly by an opening 
called mouth and ends blindly at the posterior side. 
12. They are carnivorous  animal.  Digestion is both extracellular and intracellular. 
13. Respiratory, circulatory, excretory systems are absent. Respiration takes place through 
general body surface. 
14. They have radial or biradial symmetry about a longitudinal oral-aboral  axis. 
15. They  have primitive  and diffused type of nervous system in which nerve cells  form a 
network called nerve net on either side of mesogloea in epidermis and gastrodermis but 
no central nervous system in them. 
16. Sensory  organs  are  ocelli  (light  receptor);  statocyst (maintaining  orientation)  in  free 
swimming  forms. 
17. They are hermaphrodite or unisexual. Reproduction takes place asexually  and sexually 
both. 
18. Asexual reproduction takes place by budding, fragmentation and regeneration. 
19. Sexual reproduction occurs by fusion of gametes. Fertilization is external. Development 
is indirect with a free-swimming ciliated planula larva. 
Unique features 
First  tissue  graded  animals,  diploblastic,  presence  of  nematocysts  (stinging  cells), 
internal  hollow  cavity  is  coelenteron,  incomplete  alimentary  canal,  polymorphism, 
carnivorous,  coral forming animals,  larva is planula  
 
 
Classification 
Phylum coelenterata  is classified  into three classes on the basis of composition  of mesogloea 
and body forms. 
Class 1: Hydrozoa 
i. Most of them are found in marine water and some of them in freshwater. 
ii. They  occur  as  either  polyp  form  (Hydra)  or  both  polyp  and medusa  form  (Obelia). 
Mostly, they exhibit alternation of generation. 
iii. There is non-cellular  mesogloea  in between ectoderm and endoderm. 
iv. Few of them contribute to the formation of coral reef. 
v. They are colonial  or solitary and free swimming or sessile  forms. 
Example: Hydra (freshwater polyp), Obelia (sea fur), Physalia (portages man of war), Porpita, 
Tubularia etc. 
Class 2: Scyphozoa 
i. They are all solitary forms and found exclusively in marine water. 


 
 
ii. Polyp stage is absent or reduced and medusa stage is dominant. Some of them exhibit 
alternation of generation. 
iii. Medusae are large bell or umbrella  shaped and free swimming with marginal  tentacles. 
iv. Gelatinous mesogloea  contains amoeboid cells. 
v. They don’t form corals. 
Example: Aurelia (jelly fish), Pericolpa, Lucernaria, Rhizostoma 
Class 3: Anthozoa 
i. They are both solitary or colonial  forms and all found in marine water. 
ii. There  is  only polyp  stage but no medusa stage and hence alternation  of generation is 
absent. 
iii. Mesogloea is well developed that contains amoeboid cells and fibres. 
iv. Gastrovascular cavity is further divided by septa or mesentaries. 
v. They have major role in the formation of coral reef. 
Example: Tubipora (organ pipe coral), Alcyonium (soft coral), heliopora (blue coral), Gorgonia 
(Sea fan), Metridium (sea anemone) etc.

General characters 
1. They are mostly parasitic, living inside the organs such as liver,  intestine, blood etc. of

places.  
2. Their  body is  bilaterally  symmetrical  and  dorso-ventrally  flattened. Ventral  surface

3. They have organ-system grade of body organization. 
4. Body is vermiform (worm like), elongated ribbon or leaf like. 
5. They are microscopic  or small  to moderate sized and some are long ribbon measuring 
13 metre length in average. 
6. They are first triploblastic  animals i.e. body is developed from three germ layers. 
7. Body is covered with syncytial and ciliated epidermis in free living forms but parasitic 
forms have cuticle as outer layer and without epidermis. 


 
 
8. Muscular layer of mesodermal origin lies below epidermis that consists of longitudinal,

9. Parasitic forms have spines, hooks, suckers, adhesive secretions for attachment on host. 
10. These animals  lack coelom (acoelomate)  and spaces between the organs are filled with 
mesodermal parenchymatous tissue. 
11. Digestive  system  is  incomplete  and branched  without anus.  In tapeworms,  digestive

12. Respiratory, circulatory and skeletal systems are absent. 
13. Excretion takes place through special structures called protonephridia or flame cells. 
14. Nervous  system is  primitive  consisting  of a pair  of cerebral  ganglia  and longitudinal

appearance. 
15. Sense  organs  are  simple  eye  spots or  photoreceptor  in  free  living  forms  but usually 
absent or reduced in parasitic forms. 
16. They  are  mostly  hermaphrodite  and very  few of them  are  unisexual.  Fertilization  is 
internal which may be self or cross. 
17. Parthenogenesis and polyembryony commonly occurs in parasitic forms. 
18. Reproduction occurs by sexually and asexually both. Asexual reproduction takes place 
by regeneration and sexual by fusion of gametes. 
19. Development is direct and indirect both. Indirect development is found in endoparasites 
with many larval forms. 
Unique features 
Dorsoventrally  flat  body,  mostly  endoparasites,  presence  of  hooks  and  suckers  in 
parasitic  forms,  first  triploblastic  animals,  excretion  by  flame  cells,  alimentary  canal 
incomplete or absent, mostly hermaphrodite 
 
Classification  
Phylum platyhelminthes  is divided into three classes on the basis of their habitat and digestive 
tract. 
Class 1: Turbellaria 
i. They are mostly free living, found in freshwater, marine water and moist land. 
ii. Body  is  unsegmented,  flat,  and  covered  with  ciliated  cellular  layer  or  syncytial 
epidermis. Cuticle is absent. 
iii. Suckers are absent. 
iv. Alimentary canal is incomplete with eversible  pharynx.. 
v. Touch receptor, chemoreceptor,  and photoreceptor are commonly  found in free living 
forms. 
vi. Mostly hermaphrodite. Reproduction occurs by sexual and asexual method. They have 
remarkable  power of regeneration.  
Example: Planaria, Bipalium 
  


 
 
Class 2: Trematoda 
i. They are commonly  called flukes, found as ectoparsites and endoparasites in the body 
animals. 
ii. Their  body shape is usually leaf like and dorso-ventrally flattened. 
iii. Outer covering of body wall is cuticle. 
iv. Digestive tract is incomplete  with branched intestine. 
v. Suckers are usually present to attach with host. 
vi. They are mostly hermaphrodite. 
vii. Development is direct in ectoparasites and indirect in endoparasites. 
Example: Fasciola (Liver fluke), Schistosoma (blood fluke), Dipolozoan, Gyrodactylus etc. 
Class 3: Cestoda (kestos-girdle;  eidos-form) 
i. They  are  commonly  called  tapeworms  found  as  endoparasites  in  the  intestine  of

ii. Body is pseudosegmented, elongated, flat and ribbon like. 
iii. Body segments  are called  proglottids which are immature  anteriorly,  matured at  the 
middle and gravid posteriorly. Each mature proglottid is hermaphrodite. 
iv. Head or scolex is provided with hooks and suckers. 
v. Alimentary canal and sense organs are absent. 
vi. Complicated life cycle involving two vertebrate hosts and embryo is with hooks. 
Example: Taenia (tape  worm), Echinococcus (dog tapeworm), Hymenolopis (dwarf 
tape worm) etc.

General characters 
1. They  are  found in  aquatic  and terrestrial  habitat  and also  as  parasites  of  plants  and 
animals.  Some of them are free living forms. 
2. They are bilaterally  symmetrical, triploblastic,  unsegmented, thread like worms having 
organ system grade of body organization. 
3. Body is slender, vermiform and usually cylindrical  in outline with indistinct head. 
4. They are pseudocoelomate i.e. coelom is derived from blastocoel. 
5. Body wall  consists of outermost protective cuticle  layer,  syncytial  epidermis  without 
cilia  and a layer of longitudinal muscle. Cuticle layer sheds off periodically. 


 
 
6. Alimentary canal is straight and complete with mouth, pharynx, intestine and anus. 
7. There are no circulatory and respiratory organs. Transport of materials  occurs with the 
help  of pseudocoelomic  fluid and respiration  occurs  through general  body surface by 
diffusion in free living forms. Parasitic forms respire  anaerobically. 
8. Excretion occurs through a system of canals  or tubes; renette cells  and protonephridia 
in some cases. 
9. Nervous system consists of ganglionated nerve rings around pharynx and a number of 
longitudinal nerve cords. 
10. The sensory organs are in the form of papillae, eye spots, bristles  and pits. 
11. They  are  mostly  unisexual  with  sexual  dimorphism  and  having  relatively  simple 
reproductive organs. Males are usually smaller.  
12. They  do not reproduce asexually  but sexual reproduction takes place  by the fusion of 
sperms and ovum. 
13. Fertilization is internal. Eggs are shelled and life cycle is either simple  or complicated, 
usually with no larval forms. 
Unique features 
Body is  thread like,  usually  cylindrical  in  outline,  pseudocoelomate,  first  animals 
with complete alimentary  canal, excretion through renette cells  (H-shaped excretory 
canal), distinct sexual dimorphism,  No asexual reproduction

iii. There is the presence of paired phasmids in the caudal region. 
iv. Only anterior testis is found. 
v. There is well developed excretory system with renette cells. 
vi. Presence of glandular tail. 
Example: Ascaris (round  worm), Wuchereria (filarial  worm), Loa  loa (eye  worm),

vi. Presence of non-glandular tail.

8. Locomotary  organs  are  segmentally  repeated chitinous  bristles  called setae  or lateral

Metamericlly    segmented   body,   schizocoelomate    and   some   haemocoelomate,

ii. Distinct head with eyes, palps and tentacles.

iv. Clitellum is absent. 
v. Sexes are separate. 
vi. Fertilization is external, and presence of trochophore larva.

v. Hermaphrodite and fertilization is external that takes place in cocoon.

 Example: Pheretima (Earthworm), tubifex (sludge worm), Lumbricus, Megascolex etc.

iii. No parapodia or setae. 
iv. Sexes are usually separate and larval form is called trochophore. 
v. Prostomium bears 2 or 3 tentacles.

8.2.7 PHYLUM ARTHROPODA 
Artheopoda (Gr; arthros-jointed;  podo-foot)  is the largest  phylum of animal  kingdom which 
consists  of more than 800000 species  found in all  types of habitat. These animals  comprise 
about 80% of all known animals. 
General characters 
1. They  are  the  most  diversified  animals  inhabiting  the  land,  water  and  air.  They  are 
mostly free living and some of them are parasites of both plants and animals. 
2. Body is bilaterally symmetrical,  triploblastic  and metamerically  segmented. 
3. Body segments  bear  jointed appendages  which are  variously  modified such  as  jaws, 
antenna, gills  etc. Legs are adapted for walking, swimming,  food capturing, tearing of 
food, defense etc. 
4. They have organ system level of body organization. 
5. Their  coelom is true but reduced and filled with blood called haemocoelom. 
6. Body is externally covered by thick chitionous and tough layer  of cuticle which act as 
exoskeleton,  secreted  by  underlying  epidermis.  Cuticle  cast  off periodically  during 
growth and development and this process is called moulting or ecdysis. 
7. Body is distinctly divided into three regions-head, thorax and abdomen. However, head 
and thorax is fused to form cephalothorax in some cases such as prawn, spider. 
8. Muscular  layer  is  not continuous  but lie  as  separate  bundles  below epidermis  which 
allows rapid contraction during movements. 
9. Respiratory  organs are  booklungs  and trachea in  terrestrial  forms  and gills  in  aquatic 
forms. Some of them also respire  through general body surface. 
10. Digestive  system  is  complete  with  mouth  and anus  at  the  opposite  ends.  Mouth  is 
surrounded by a number of appendages called mouth parts which are adapted variously 
for bitting, chewing, sucking etc. 
11. Excretion takes place  through malpighian  tubules or coelomoducts or green glands or 
coxal glands. 
12. They  have  open  type  of circulatory  system  which  consists  of  dorsally  placed  heart, 
arteries and blood sinuses or lacunae. There are no veins and capillaries. 
13. Nervous system is typically annelidan type that consists of dorsal brain with nerve ring 
and ganglionated ventral nerve cord with lateral nerves. 
14. Sensory organs are simple eyes, compound eyes, antenna, hairs, statocyst, and auditory 
organs.  These  organs perceive  stimuli  like  light, chemical,  touch, sound etc. and also 
help in maintaining body posture during locomotion.  
15. Compound eye is composed of many visual  units or lenses called ommatidia and such 
eye can detect very slowly moving object. 
16. They are usually unisexual and well-marked sexual dimorphism is found in most cases. 
17. Fertilization  is mostly  internal. Development is  direct and indirect both. Parental care 
is also found in some arthropods. 
18. Parthenogenesis occurs in some cases. 
  


 
 
 
Unique features 
Most   diversified,  Presence   of  jointed   appendages,  metamerically    segmented, 
haemocoelomate,  shedding  off  outer  cuticle  frequently,  body  divided  into  head, 
thorax  and  abdomen  or  cephalothorax  and  abdomen,  open  circulatory  system, 
compound eye, larval form in the most. 
 
Classification 
Classification  of phylum  arthropoda  is controversial  and complicated  which is divided  into 
seven subphyla but the true arthropods belong to the three sub-phyla namely trilobitomorpha, 
chelicerata  and  mandibulata.  Among  them,  trilobitomorpha  is not  mentioned  here  as  it 
comprises extinct arthropods.

Class 1: Merostomata (Meros-thigh, stoma mouth)

iii. Head is five segmented and each segment bears biramous (two branched) appendages.

 Example: Palaemon (prawn), Cancer (crab), lobster, shrimp, copepod, water flea etc.

ii. Body is divisible into head and trunk. Trunk is many segmented and each segment bear

iii. They have a pair of antennae, three pair of jaws and more than 11 pair of legs. 
iv. Breathing  occurs  through  trachea.  Trachea  opens  outside through small  pores  called

v. They excrete through malpighian  tubules.

v. There is three pair of walking legs arising  laterally from thorax and wings are two pair

vi. Respiration occurs by branched tracheae which open externally through spiracles.

viii. Sexes are separate, fertilization is internal and development is direct and indirect both.

The term ‘mollusca’  was first applied by Aristotle to cuttle fish and is derived from latin word 
‘mollis’ which  means  soft  body.  Phylum  mollusca  is  the  second  largest  phylum  which 
comprises more than 100000 species. Among them 80000 species are gastropods.

General characters 
1. They are mostly found in aquatic habitats, especially marine water, a few are freshwater 
forms and some  are terrestrial.  Most of the members  of the phylum  mollusca  are  free 
living and there are some parasitic forms (some gastropods). 
2. Body is  soft, unsegmented (except monoplacophora), and bilaterally symmetrical  and 
some are unsymmetrical.  
3. Body is usually protected by exoskeleton in the form of calcareous shell. Skeleton may 
be internal or absent. 
4. Their  body can be divided into head, foot, mantle and visceral mass. 
5. Head  is  distinct  with  mouth,  eyes,  tentacles,  and  other  sensory  organs  except  in 
pelecypoda and scaphopoda. 
6. Ventral surface consists of flat or plough like muscular foot which is variously modified 
for creeping, burrowing and swimming in water. 
7. Mantle or pallium  is a thick glandular fold of body wall on the dorsal surface. 
8. Visceral mass consists of vital organs in compact form within the shell. 
9. Alimentary  canal  is  simple  with anteriorly  situated mouth and posterior  anus.  But in 
gastropods, scaphopods and cephalopods, intestine become U-shaped bringing the anus 
in anterior position. Radula or rasping organ in buccal cavity is usually present. 
10. Circulatory system is open type (exception cephalopods) in which heart is with one or 
two auricles and one ventricle. Blood contains copper based respiratory pigment called 
haemocyanin. 
11. Respiratory organs consist of numerous gills (branchiae) or ctenidium in aquatic forms, 
pulmonary sac or lungs in terrestrial  forms. 
12. Excretion occurs through a pair of metanephridia or kidneys. 
13. Nervous   system   comprises    paired   ganglia   (cerebral,    pleural,    pedal,   visceral), 
connectives and nerves. 
14. Sense organs are eyes, statocyst, osphradium (detect water chemistry) and tentacles. 
15. They are usually  dioecious and some are hermaphrodite too. 
16. Fertilization is internal or external. Development is either direct or indirect with larva. 
Unique features 
Soft bodied animals, calcareous  exoskeleton in the form of shell or internal skeleton 
or  absent,  body  divisible  into  head,  mantle,  visceral  mass,  ventral  foot,  open 
circulatory  system,  respiratory  pigment  haemocyanin,  respiration  through  gills  or 
ctenidia in   aquatic   and   through   lungs   in   terrestrial   forms,   osphradium   as 
chemoreceptor

mantle, gills, radula etc. 
Class 1: Monoplacophora (Gr; monos-one; placo-plate) 
i. All marine  forms. 
ii. Body is bilaterally symmetrical,  internally  segmented and with dome shaped mantle.

iv. Head is without eyes and tentacles. 
v. Sexes are separate. 
Example: Neopilina 
Class 2: Amphineura (Gr; Amphi-both; neuron-nerve)

ii. Their body is dorso-ventrally flattened with shell and foot. Some of them are worm like

iii. Shell, if present, consist of 8 plates. 
iv. They have small  head without eyes and tentacles. 
v. Radula, mantle and gills are present. 
Example: Chiton, Neomenia 
Class 3: Scaphopoda (Gr; scapha-boat; podos-foot) 
i. They are exclusively  marine forms. 
ii. Body is  bilaterally  symmetrical,  enclosed  within  tusk-shaped  shell  opening  at  both

iii. Head is vestigial or not distinct, eyes and gills are absent. 
iv. Mouth is with filamentous tentacles and presence of radula. 
v. Foot is conical used for digging. 
Example: Dentalium (Elephant tusk shell), Cadulus

ii. Body is unsegmented and asymmetrical. 
iii. Shell is present or absent or in reduced form. Shell is univalved and spirally coiled. 
iv. Head is distinct with eye tentacles and mouth. 
v. Muscular foot is broad and flat on ventral side. 
vi. Buccal cavity is with radula and anus is anteriorly situated.  
vii. Respiration by ctenidia (gills)  or pulmonary sac or through general body surface. 
Example: Pila (apple snail), Helix (garden snail), Limax (slug) etc.

ii. Body is bilaterally symmetrical,  laterally  compressed with bivalve shell.

iv. Foot is hatchet or plough shaped. 
v. Some of them form pearl. 
Example: Unio (freshwater mussel), Pecten, Pearl oyster etc. 
Class 6: Cephalopoda (Gr; kephale-head; podos-foot) 
i. They are extremely found in marine water. 
ii. Body is elongated and bilaterally symmetrical  and divisible into head and trunk. 
iii. Shell is external, internal or absent. 
iv. Head bears eyes, mouth, tentacles and radula. 
v. Suckers are found in arms or tentacles encircling  mouth. 
vi. Sexes are separate. 
Example: Sepia (cuttle fish), Loligo (squid), Octopus (devil fish), Nautilus etc.  
8.2.9 PHYLUM ECHINODERMATA (Gr; echinos-hedgehog or spiny, derma-skin) 
The term echinodermata literally means spiny skinned and was first introduced by klein in 1734 
for “sea  urchins”.  Because of their  radial  symmetry,  they were kept with  coelenterates  in a 
single group ‘radiata’ for a long time. Later, they were recognized as distinct group by leukart 
in 1847. All the animals of phylum echinodermata form a peculiar group of invertebrates, living 
at the bottom of sea by creeping slowly, though some can swim.

General characters 
1. They  are  exclusively  found in  marine  water, mostly  in  sea  bottom and hence  called 
‘benthic invertebrates. 
2. The  shape  of the body varies  among the species,  which are  cylindrical  or  elongated, 
globular, star like, discoidal, or flower like. 
3. They are triploblastic  and have organ grade of body organization. 
4. Adults  have   pentamerous   radially   symmetrical   body  and  larvae   are   bilaterally 
symmetrical. 
5. They are enterocoelomate animals. 
6. They have no definite head. Body surface is divided into oral and aboral surface where 
oral surface is marked by five symmetrically  radiating areas called ambulacra  and five 
alternating interambulacra. 
7. Body  wall  consists  of  outermost  epidermis,  dermis,  muscular  layer  and  innermost  
epithelial  layer (ciliated cuboidal). 
8. Many  of them  bear  pincer  like  pedicellaria  with  spine  on  body surface;  pedicellaria 
clear body by removing debris and other things which are attached on the body. 


 
 
9. Endoskeleton consists of calcareous ossicles  or plates of mesodermal origin in dermis. 
10. Water vascular  system or  ambulacral  system of coelomic  origin,  consisting  of canals 
filled with water-like fluid connected with tube feet and madreporite. 
11. Tube feet are the locomotory organ which consists of ampulla, podium and sucker. 
12. Alimentary  canal  is  either straight  or coiled and mouth is  present on the oral  surface 
and anus usually on the aboral surface. 
13. Circulatory  system is  open type which includes haemal  and perihaemal  systems  with 
sinuses but without heart. A true blood vascular system is lacking. 
14. Excretory system is entirely  absent. However, nitrogenous  wastes are expelled out by 
papulae and tube feet in some forms. 
15. Nervous  system  is  primitive  or  poorly  developed with a  circum-oral  ring  and radial 
nerves. Brain is absent. 
16. Sexes  are  separate  with  few  exceptions.  Reproduction  occurs  both  sexually  and 
asexually. Asexual reproduction takes place by regeneration. 
17. Fertilization is external. Development is indirect with larval forms.  
18. Larvae are free swimming and bilaterally symmetrical, get metamorphosed into radially 
symmetrical  adult. Some echinoderms are viviparous. 
Unique features 
All marine animals,  skin with calcareous spine, enterocoelomate, presence of water vascular 
system,  some  with  pincer  like  pedicellaria  on  body  surface,  locomotion  by  tube  feet, 
excretory system lacking, absence of brain and heart, larva bilaterally  symmetrical  but adult 
radially symmetrical,  well-marked regeneration power

8.2.10 PHYLUM: CHORDATA (Gr; chorde-string  or cord; ata-bearing) 
Phylum Chordata is the last major group of kingdom animalia  which was created by Balfour 
in 1880. The term ‘chordata’  is derived from two Greek words ‘chorde’ means string  or cord 
and ‘ata’ means bearing. It indicates that these animals have a common characteristic  which is 
a stiff supporting rod like structure on the back called notochord. (Gr; noton-back; L; chorda-
cord).  Notochord  is  found  in  all  the  member  of  this  phylum  at  some  stage  of  their  life.  
Following are the major characters of phylum chordata: 
1. Presence  of  notochord: An  elongated,  flexible  and  supporting  rod  like  structure 
present in mid dorsal line from head to tail. It lies beneath the nerve cord and above the 
alimentary canal. It persists throughout the life in lower chordates (protochordates) but 
embryonic  notochord is replaced by vertebral  column in the adult of higher  chordates 
(vertebrates)  


 
 
2. Dorsal  tubular  nerve  cord: A  longitudinal  hollow  nerve  cord of  ectodermal  origin 
extends lengthwise of the body above notochord. In vertebrates, its anterior region form 
brain  which  is  protected by  cranium  and  posterior  part  form  spinal  cord  which  is 
protected by vertebral column. 
3. Presence of pharyngeal gill  slits: Series  of paired gill slits present on the lateral  wall 
of pharynx at some stages of development in all  chordates. In primitive  chordates and 
lower aquatic vertebrates, gill slits or gill clefts remain throughout the life for breathing 
but disappear in the adult of higher vertebrates.  
 
Figure 8.2.18 A chordate’s body plan 
Other general characters: 
4. They are aquatic, terrestrial and aerial. Mostly free living and very few are parasitic. 
5. They are triploblastic,  metamerically  segmented and bilaterally symmetrical. 
6. They have schizocoel or true coelom formed by splitting of mesoderm in the embryonic 
stage. Enterocoelom is found in proto chordates. 
7. Exoskeleton is  often present and well developed and endoskeleton is cartilaginous or 
bony in majority  of them 
8. There is complete alimentary canal with digestive glands. 
9. Circulatory  system  is  closed type, consists  of ventrally  located heart and all  types of 
blood vessels. 
10. Some of them have post anal tail beyond anus. 
11. Respiratory system is well developed consisting of gills or lung or moist skin. 
12. They have kidneys for excretion. 
13. They have well developed nervous system 
14. Majority  of  them  have  well  developed sensory  organs  such  as  eye,  auditory  organ, 
olfactory organs, taste receptors etc. 
15. Sexes  are  separate.  Mostly  direct  development  and  some  of  them  have  indirect 
development. 


 
 
 
Figure 8.2.19 classification of chordata 
Phylum  Chordata  consists  of  four  sub-phyla  which  are  Hemichordata,  Urochordata, 
Cephalochordata  and  Vertebrata.  First  three  sub-phyla  are  primitive  chordates  which  are 
exclusively  marine,  relatively  small  and  without  vertebral  column  and  also  called 
protochordates. They also lack cranium and hence placed in group acraniata. Last sub-phylum 
vertebrata  consist of animals  which are more advanced and specific over these protochorates 
and hence called Euchordates. Their notochord is replaced  by vertebral  column in adult and 
have cranium and hence placed in the group craniata. 
1. Hemichordata  (Gr; hemi-half; chorde-cord): 
i. They  are exclusively  marine,  solitary  or colonial  and mostly  tubicolous (living  inside 
self-constructed tube). 
ii. Body is soft and worm like. 
iii. Notochord is present at the anterior region of the body. 
iv. Their  body is divided into three regions- proboscis, collar  and trunk 
Examples: Balanoglossus(tongue worm or acorn worm), Saccoglossus etc. 
2. Urochordata (Gr;oura-a tail; chorde-cord) or Tunicata  
i. They  are  also  exclusively  marine  found  at  all  depth  and  commonly  called  as

4. Vertebrata (L; vertebratus-jointed)   
A vertebrate  can be defined  as an animal  of phylum  chordata  having bony or cartilaginous 
endoskeleton that consists of cranium enclosing brain and a vertebral column enclosing a nerve 
cord. 
General characters: 
1. Lower vertebrates are aquatic and higher vertebrates are terrestrial. 
2. Body is usually divisible into head, neck, trunk and post-anal tail. 
3. Their  exoskeleton consists of protective scales, feather, hair found in skin. Nail, claws, 
hoof, horn etc. are also found. 
4. Skin  or  integument is  differentiated into epidermis  and dermis  along  with cutaneous 
glands. 
5. Notochord in the early embryonic  stage is replaced by cartilaginous or bony vertebral 
column in adult. 
6. Endoskeleton is either cartilaginous  or bony where muscles  are attached. 
7. They have well developed organ systems.

Division  1.  Agnatha  (Gr;  a-not;  gnatha-jaw): Division  agnatha  comprises  jawless

Characters 
i. They are exclusively aquatic forms, mostly found in marine water. Mostly parasitic 
or scavengerous.

v. Mouth is circular  and suctorial.

viii. Sexes are separate or united, gonad single and fertilization is external.

Super class 1: Pisces (L; piscis-fish) 
It includes  all the fishes  which are essentially  aquatic having 3 main characters  such as body 
streamlined  and covered with scales, respiration  by gills, paired fins for locomotion.  
Characters: 
1. They are always aquatic, freshwater or marine form. 
2. Body is  streamlined or spindle shaped and divisible  into head, trunk and tail. Neck is 
absent. 
3. Locomotion takes place by paired pectoral and pelvic  fins; unpaired median dorsal fin 
and tail fin. Muscular tail acts as propeller  and rudder. 
4. Body is externally covered by waterproof dermal scales with some exceptions. 
5. Their  endoskeleton is either bony or cartilaginous. 
6. Respiration  mainly  takes  place  through  gills  and  gills  are  naked  or  covered  by

10. Kidneys are mesonephric and excretion is ammonotelic  or ureotelic.

etc.  
12. Sexes  are separate and fertilization  is  external  or  internal.  Female  is  oviparous  (eggs 
large and yolky) or ovoviviparous  and development is usually direct. 
Unique features 
Exclusively aquatic, gill breather, body covered by waterproof scales, locomotion by 
fins, heart two chambered, poikilothermic, cranial  nerves 10 pairs

i. They are bipedal, feathery and warm blooded (homeothermic) animals  which maintain

xiii. Sexes  are  separate  and sexual  dimorphism  is  well  marked.  Female  has  a  single  and

care and territorial behaviour.

The animal  shown  in the  diagram  is earthworm belonging  to the  phylum  annelida  and  its 
scientific  name is Pheretima posthuma. Earthworms are found under the soil in moist burrow. 
They have not developed any special respiratory organs like lungs, gills or other structures and 
hence they respire through moist skin. They maintain skin moist with the help of mucous glands 
present in their skin. Such type of respiration  is called cutaneous respiration. They are free 
living and omnivorous animals. They feed on soil rich in decaying organic matter, dried leaves, 
insect  larva,  protozoans,  arthropod  exoskeleton  etc.  Nearer  to  the  anterior  end,  their  body 
contains a ring or belt like conspicious glandular tissue called clitellum which forms cocoons. 
Earthworms  are  useful  in  agriculture  as  they  help  to  increase  the  fertility  of  soil  by  their 
movements and excreta, hence considered friend of farmers.

Earthworms are terrestrial,  living in burrows and moist  soil rich in decaying organic matters.

Body wall of Earthworm 
Body wall of earthworm is thin, soft, elastic and moist which is composed of four layers. These 
layers  are  cuticle,  epidermis,  a  well-developed  muscular  layer  and a  thin  parietal  layer  of 
coelomic epithelium from outside. 
1. Cuticle: 
Cuticle is a thin, elastic, non-living and finely striated layer which forms the external protective 
covering  of  the  body.  It  is  composed  of  two  layer  of  collagen  fibres  mixed  with  a 
polysaccharide and small amount of gelatin. It is secreted by underlying epidermis. Cuticle also 
consists of numerous pores through which epidermal glands and nephridia open out. 
 


 
 
 
Figure 8.3.6 T.S of body wall of earthworm 
2. Epidermis: 
This is a single layer of epithelial  cells lying just below cuticle resting on basement membrane. 
The constituent  cells  are of different  types: supporting cells,  gland cells,  basal cells, sensory 
receptor cells.  
• Supporting cells form the major bulk of epidermis and secrete cuticle.  
• Gland cells comprise mucous cell and albumen cell which secrete mucus and albumen 
respectively. These secretions make skin moist, slippery and also help in cementing the 
wall of burrows.  
• Basal  cells are  small  and conical  shaped present  between gland  cell  and supporting 
cells at the lower end. These cells replace worn out gland cells and supporting cells and 
hence called replacing cells.  
• Sensory cells are  also  found in  clusters  with hair  like  processes  at the outer end and 
they are photo, chemo and touch receptors. 
3. Muscular layer: 
Muscular layer lies just below the epidermis.  It consists of outer thin layer of circular  muscle 
fibres (around the body) and inner thick layer of longitudinal muscle fibre (along the length of 
body). 
• Circular  muscle  layer  is  thin  and  contains  scattered granules  of  porphyrin  pigment 
which imparts brown colour to the skin.  
• Longitudinal muscle  layer  is thicker and lies  in the form of parallel  bundles separated 
by connective tissue and strengthened by collagen fibres.  
• There  is  another  thin  strip  of  circular  muscle  lying  below  the  layer  of  longitudinal 
muscles.  
Circular muscles and longitudinal  muscles are opposite in function i.e. contraction of one 
cause relaxation of other and vice versa.

It is a thin and single layer of flattened squamous epithelial  cells that forms the outer envelope 
of coelom.

PHYSIOLOGY OF DIGESTION 
The process of conversion of complex food particles into simpler absorbable or diffusible forms 
is  digestion. This  process is  carried  out  by the organs  and glands  in the  digestive  tract.  It 
involves both mechanical and chemical processes. Mechanical process is simply the conversion 
of larger food particles  into smaller  forms so that enzymes thoroughly act on it. Whereas the 
chemical process is the chemical conversion of complex organic compounds such as different 
types of carbohydrates, protein, fat into glucose, galactose, amino acid, fatty acid and glycerol 
etc. as end products. Physiology of digestion can be discussed under following heads: 
1. Food: 
Earthworm is omnivorous and detrivorous. It feeds on soil rich in decaying organic matter such 
as decayed leaves, nematodes, protozoan, insect’s egg, larva and other micro-organisms. It can 
directly feed on leaves, grasses, algae etc. 
2. Feeding: 


 
 
At the time of feeding, buccal cavity is protruded or everted out and the soil with food is drawn 
into the mouth by sucking and pumping action of the pharynx. It is aided by the muscles around 
the pharynx. 
3. Digestion: 
Ingested foods into the buccal cavity do not undergo digestion. Now, the food is  passed into 
the pharynx which receives secretion of salivary  glands. Salivary secretion contains mucus and 
proteolytic  enzymes  which  lubricate  food  as  well  as  digest  proteins  into  proteoses  and 
peptones. Now, food passes  into  gizzard through  oesophagus  by  peristalsis.  Gizzard acts  as 
grinder because of its contractile muscular wall and cuticle lined lumen. It grinds or pulverizes 
the larger  food masses into the fine powdered form. Food now reaches into the stomach where 
further digestion of protein  by proteolytic  enzymes  occurs.  Semi  digested food, now passes 
into the intestine and gets digested completely. Such food is subjected to the action of various 
enzymes secreted by intestine and intestinal caeca. 
i. Amylase:  This  enzyme acts upon the carbohydrates to convert them into simple  sugar 
like glucose, fructose etc. 
ii. Protease: It hydrolyses proteins or proteoses and peptones into amino acid. 
iii. Lipase: It digests fats and converts into fatty acid and glycerol. 
iv. Chitinase: It digests chitin found in exoskeleton of arthropods. 
v. Cellulase:  This enzyme digests cellulose. 
4. Absorption and egestion 
Nutrients from digested food are absorbed by the intestinal villi, particularly by the typhlosole. 
After absorption, they are passed into blood capillaries  in the intestinal wall and transported to 
the various parts of the body. Hence, intestine is the site for both the digestion and absorption 
of food. Undigested food materials and soil are discharged out through the anus in the form of 
small  rounded pellets of mud called worm casting.

expelled out through anus. Septal nephridia have ciliated  nephrostome. Continous beating of 
cilia  extracts wastes from the coelomic fluid which is later  poured into alimentary  canal and 
eliminated  with faeces.

SENSE ORGANS IN EARTHWORM 
Sense organs are simple and well developed in earthworm which consists of single cell or group 
of ectodermal cells. They are of following types: 
i. Epidermal receptors: 
They are  scattered throughout the epidermis layer of body wall and more abundant in 
lateral side and ventral surface. The outer margin of these receptor cells consists of hair 
like  processes  and inner  margin  is with nerve fibre. These  receptors  respond to touch 
and chemical stimuli. 
ii. Buccal receptors:  


 
 
These  receptors  are found in the buccal cavity which  gives the sensation of taste and 
smell. 
iii. Photo receptors: 
These receptors are found only in the dorsal surface and more abundant in prostomium 
and  peristomium.  They  are  absent  in  clitellum.  Their  number  is  gradually  reduced 
towards posterior side. Such receptors help the animal to judge intensity of light falling 
on the body. 
 
8.3.7 REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM 
Reproduction is the life  process in which living  beings produce offspring of their own kinds 
for the continuation  of their generation. Earthworm is a hermaphrodite  (monoecious)  animal 
bearing  both  male  and  female  reproductive  organs  in  the  same  body. But  self-fertilization 
doesn’t  occur because  they are  protandrous animal.  Protandry  is the  condition  where male 
reproductive organs mature earlier than the female reproductive organs. So, cross fertilization 
takes place by the process of copulation between two sexually mature worms which is followed 
by cocoon formation.

8.3.8 COPULATION AND COCOON FORMATION 
Copulation: 
In earthworms, copulation generally takes place during rainy season in the month from July to 
October at  night time which  lasts  for about an  hour. At  this  time, two  sexually mature 
earthworms attach themselves by their ventral surfaces with their anterior end facing opposite  


 
 
 
Figure 8.3.22 coplaton in earthworm 
direction. In this condition, Male genital opening of one worm lies opposite to spermathecal 
pore of another worm. The area of male genital opening of each worm is raised into an elevated 
structure  called the papilla  which act  as  the  copulatory  organ  and  is  inserted  through 
spermathecal  pore of another worm for the transfer  of sperm. In this way, mutual transfer  of 
sperms and prostatic  fluid between copulating worms takes place and they become separate. 
Sperms  are  received  and  collected  in  spermathecae  and  maintained  live  until  female 
reproductive organ matures. 
Cocoon formation: 
Cocoon formation  takes place after  the process of copulation,  when ovaries  become mature. 
The clitellar  gland consists  of  mucous secreting  gland, cocoon forming  gland and albumen 
secreting gland. After the ovaries become mature, clitellum secrete a gluey or sticky substance 
which gets hardened on exposure of air to form a circular  band or girdle around it. When the 
membranous  girdle  is  formed, earthworm withdraws  itself  backwards  from  the  girdle  and 
several unfertilized ova are laid into the girdle through the female genital opening. As the girdle 
passes over the spermathecal pores, sperms from spermathecae are also shed off into the girdle. 
The girdle  is finally  thrown off from the anterior  end and both ends of girdle or cocoon are 
closed due to its elastic wall. In this way, a light-yellow  coloured, oval or rounded cocoon is 
formed which contains fertilized  eggs along with nutritive  albuminous  substance secreted by 
albuminous gland. Many cocoons are formed in succession after each mating. 
 
Figure 8.3.23 Cocoon formation in earthworm 
Fertilization  is external and takes place in cocoon when it is deposited on land. Among many 
fertilized  eggs in a cocoon, only one fertilized  egg develops into new youngone. Cleavage of 
zygote is  holoblastic,  unequal  and  development is direct. A young earthworm  thus  formed 
escapes from cocoon in about 15-20 days. 
 
8.3.9 ECONOMIC IMPORTANCE OF EARTHWORM 


 
 
Beneficial 
1. Earthworms are food for many  animals  like  birds, lizards  frogs snakes and aquarium 
fishes. Similarly,  they are used as fishing baits by fisherman or anglers. 
2. Earthworms  make  soil  porous,  loose  and fertile  by  their  continuous  movement  and

which is beneficial for farmers. 
3. Nowadays,  earthworms  are  widely  used  in  vermi-composting  i.e.  used  to  prepare

Harmful 
1. Many burrows formed by earthworm  causes seepage  and loss of water from irrigated 
land resulting reduced crop yield. 
2. Loose soil formed by earthworm is easily eroded by water from sloping land. 
3. Earthworm come out from the burrow on the earth surface with several  parasites  like 
Amoebotania, gapeworm, nematodes etc. and germs like  bacteria viruses  in their body 
which may infect other animals. 
4. Some  species  of  earthworm  damage  the  roots  of  cultivated  plants  such  as  paddy, 
betelvine etc.

8.4.2 HABITAT AND HABIT  
Rana tigrina is commonly found in water or on damp soil. It lives most of the time in moist 
land rich in green vegetation near fresh water or found swimming in pond, ditches, rivers lakes, 
streams. It frequently visits water bodies to keep its skin moist for cutaneous respiration. It can 
escape from enemies  by immediately  diving into water. While living  near aquatic habitat,  it 
can easily access food since plenty of insects, worms, slugs, small  frogs etc. are found there. 
Moreover, it needs water for reproductive activities, and all its embryonic stages including the 
larval stage are passed in water. 
 
Figure 8.4.1 Frog near water 
Rana tigrina is a non-poisonous,  solitary,  harmless  and normally  silent  animal  without  any 
armatures.  It  is a free living,  predaceous and cannibalistic  animal.  Generally,  its presence is 
only detected after it is being disturbed on the banks of ponds, streams, pools, or in the paddy 
field. Frog is active at the day time and hence called diurnal animal. In breeding season, males 
are gathered in shallow water in paddy field or ponds and croak. 
i. Locomotion:  
In frog, locomotion takes place  by leaping  on the land and swimming  in  the water. It 
has strong and long hind limbs  which are contracted in ‘Z’ shaped manner and short 
forelimbs  in upright position during the resting posture. When leaping, it extends hind 
limbs  and leaves the land quickly; throwing body up into the air and body is supported 
by fore limbs at the time of landing. A frog may leap about 1.5 meter at a jump. While 
swimming,  hind limbs provide powerful backward thrust with their broad webbed toes 
spreading apart for propelling  forward.  Left and right fore limbs  also support and are 
moved alternately while swimming. 


 
 
 
Figure 8.4.2 Stages of body posture while leaping on land 
ii. Feeding: 
Adult frog is carnivorous  and its food chiefly consists of living insects, worms, slugs, 
small  fishes, tadpole etc.  It  quickly  shots  out  its  protrusible  and  sticky  tongue  for 
capturing prey and whole food is, then swallowed without chewing. It prefers to hunt 
live prey and discards motionless or dead prey as food. Frog is a glutton and hence feed 
excess;  becomes  fatty, accumulates  reserve  food material  such  as  glycogen  and fat 
before going to hibernation. 
 
Figure 8.4.3 process of feeding in frog 
 
iii. Hibernation  and aestivation: 
Frogs are poikilothermic  or cold blooded animals  and their body temperature changes 
according to the temperature of surrounding environment. Their body temperature may 
fall abruptly in winters which may threaten life. To protect from such a harsh situation, 
they remain hidden or dormant inside the moist burrow or under soft damp bottom mud. 
This  phenomenon is  called hibernation or ‘winter sleep’.  This  duration lasts for about 
3  months.  At the  time  of  hibernation,  nostrils  and  mouth  remain  closed  and  stops 
feeding,  excretion,   become   metabolically   inactive, breathe   only by   cutaneous 
respiration and survive on reserve food such as glycogen and fat stored in their body. 
During summer also, they remain inactive or become dormant under the mud to escape 
from  extreme  high  temperature  of  the  environment,  which  is  called  aestivation. 
Aestivation lasts for about 15 days.  During this period, they occasionally  come out in 
search of food. Aestivation is commonly called summer sleep. 
iv. Croaking: 
It is the characteristic sound produced by male frogs during breeding season, also called 
sex  call  or  mating call. It is  heard louder  and produced only  by male  frogs to attract 
female frogs for mating purpose.  Males have a pair  of bluish skin  patches or folds on 
the throat region, called vocal sacs which are used to croak. 
 


 
 
v. Breeding 
Rainy season (July  to September) is breeding season for frog in which males gather in 
shallow water and start producing croaking sound to attract female frog. While mating, 
male mounts on the back of female and grasps the female firmly with the help of nuptial 
pad or copulatory pad developed on its first or index finger of fore limbs. This sexual 
embrace is also called false copulation or amplexus.  Hence, there is no true copulation 
or  mating in  frogs.  Meanwhile, female  frog gets stimulated and starts  laying  eggs  in 
water through cloaca. Mass of eggs intermingled with gelatin like material on the water 
surface is called spawn. Male discharges seminal  fluid or milt containing sperms  over 
the cluster of eggs or spawn. Thus, fertilization is external, taking place in water, which 
in  turn  forms  zygotes.  Then,  zygotes  develop  into  free  swimming  and  completely 
aquatic tadpole within about 15 days.  Tadpole after few weeks gets metamorphosed 
into amphibious  adult. 
 
Figure 8.4.4 A male frog croaking by inflating vocal sacs 
vi. Camouflage: 
Frogs can change their body colour according to the surrounding where they are living 
so  that they are not easily  noticed by  enemies.  This  is  called camouflage. Change  in 
colour occurs due to the dispersion of amoeboid pigmented cells called chromotophores 
found in dermis of skin.  
vii. Ways of protection from enemies 
Enemies of frog are snakes, human beings, birds such as crane, hawk, eagle, crow etc. 
Similarly,  small  frogs  are also eaten up by larger  frog. Human beings also kill  large 
number of frogs for dissection  in laboratory to study their anatomy. Ways adopted by 
frogs to protect themselves from enemies are: 
a. They can change its body colour according to the surrounding so that enemies can’t 
detect it easily. 
b. Frog suddenly ejects urine, when tried to catch. 
c. They  have  slippery  skin  because  of the presence  of mucous  glands due to which 
they can’t be easily caught by enemies. 
d. They have habit of living  near fresh water sources and quickly dive into the water

2. Division of the body 
The  body of an  adult frog is  divisible  into head and trunk with limbs.  Neck and tail  are

i. Head: 
The head is flat and roughly triangular in outline, with a short and less blunt anterior snout. 
A wide transverse opening is situated at the anterior end of the snout, called mouth. There 
are two small  openings  lying dorsally above the mouth called external nostrils  or external 
nares which serve in breathing. A pair  of spherical  and bulging  eyes, each situated at the

is present which receives sound waves but external ear is absent in frog. In male frogs, two 
bluish and wrinkled vocal  sacs are present on the undersurface of throat, used to produce 
croaking sounds in the breeding season for attracting female frog. 
ii. Trunk: 
Trunk is a flat and oval structure which is joined directly  with the head. During resting or 
squatting condition, posterior  side of trunk, in the middle is slightly  raised forming hump 
which is also called  sacral  prominence.  There is a small  circular opening called  vent or 
cloacal  aperture  at  the  posterior  end of  trunk between  hind  limbs.  This  aperture  is  the 
common for discharging out faeces, urine and gametes.  
There  are  two  pairs  of  limbs  arising  laterally  from the trunk.  Anterior  pair  is called 
forelimbs, which arise just behind the head. Each forelimb consists of proximal upper arm 
(brachium), fore arm (antebrachium),  wrist (carpus) and hand (manus) bearing 4 fingers or 
digits where thumb is externally invisible or rudimentary. Fingers of forelimbs  are without 
fold of skin or web between them. In the case of male frog, base of index or first finger is 
thickened to form nuptial pad or amplexuary pad or copulatory pad to hold the female frog 
firmly  during amplex or copulation. Posterior  pair is hind limbs which are comparatively 
much longer and powerful than fore  limbs.  Each hind limb  consists of proximal thigh, 
middle shank, a long ankle or tarsus and large foot bearing five fingers with a fold of skin 
in between, called webbed toes used in swimming. Undersurface of digits of forelimb and 
hind limb contains small and round pads called sub-articular pads which are shock absorber 
and also aid in gripping and climbing surfaces.

species are distinguished by observing their external features. Sexual dimorphism is relatively 
more distinct in the breeding season in Rana tigrina. In copulating frogs, upper one is male and 
the lower one is female.

8.4.4 DIGESTIVE SYSTEM OF FROG 
Let’s discuss given questions. 
i. What is the digestive system? 
ii. What are the end products of carbohydrate, protein and fat digestion? 
iii. What is the feeding habit of frog? 
 
Digestion is the process of conversion of complex food particles  into simpler forms. For this, 
various  organs are co-ordinating  or  working  together called  digestive  organs  forming  a 
complex  system  called  digestive  system.  Mainly  two  processes  involve  in  the  process  of 
digestion which are mechanical and chemical. Mechanical process is the conversion of larger 
food particles  into smaller or fine state whereas chemical pocess is the conversion of complex 
organic compound in food into simpler forms. Digestive system in frog can be discussed under 
two headings: 
A. ALIMENTARY CANAL 
In frog, alimentary  canal is a complete, long and coiled tube of varying diameter extending 
from  mouth  to  cloaca.  Alimentary  canal includes the  mouth,  buccal  cavity,  pharynx, 
oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine and cloaca. 
 
Figure 8.4.7 Mouth open to show the parts of buccal cavity 
1. Mouth: The alimentary canal starts with a wide opening called mouth. It is a transverse 
gape  bounded by  upper  and  lower  jaws  covered  by  immovable  lips.  Upper  jaw  is 
immovable  but lower  jaw is  partially  movable  which can be  moved up and down for 
closing and opening mouth respectively. 
 
2. Bucco-pharyngeal  Cavity: Mouth posteriorly  leads into a  wide and spacious  cavity 
called oral cavity or buccal cavity. In frog, due to the absence of neck, its buccal cavity 
and  pharynx  are  not  distinct  and  hence  called  bucco-pharyngeal  cavity.  Bucco-
pharyngeal  cavity  serves  as  the  common  passage  of food and air.  Posterior  part  of 
buccal cavity is often called as pharynx. The bucco-pharyngeal cavity is internally lined 
by ciliated columnar epithelium with mucus secreting cells or goblet cells. The function 
of mucus is to lubricate  the food particles  for swallowing  easily. There  are no salivary 
glands. Structures present in bucco-pharyngeal  cavity are:  


 
 
 
i. Teeth:  
The upper  jaw is  provided with a row of closely  set,  small,  conical  and backwardly 
pointed teeth over the premaxillae and maxillae bones. Teeth on premaxilla and maxilla 
are called premaxillary  and maxillary  teeth respectively. The lower jaw does not bear 
teeth. Besides, there are two more patches of teeth, each patch lying on the either side 
of the median line of the roof of the buccal cavity called vomerine teeth.   
 
  
Figure 8.4.8 (a) The upper jaw showing teeth (b) A maxillary tooth in V.S. 
They are found on the vomer bone. Teeth in frog  are not used for chewing purpose 
instead they are used to hold the prey inside the buccal cavity after the food is captured 
by tongue. The teeth are homodont i.e. similar shaped. They are curved backwards and 
directly attached to the jaw bones called acrodont condition. Old and worn out teeth are 
being continuously  shed off during the life span and replaced by new ones and hence 
called  polyphyodont.  Each tooth  is conical  shaped  consisting  of mainly  two parts: - 
base  and  crown.  The  broad  bony  base  is  attached  to  the  jaw  bone.  The  crown  is 
primarily composed of dentine and traversed by numerous fine branching canals called 
canaliculi.  Tip of the crown is  coated with a very  hard and shining  white substance 
called enamel (secreted  by ameloblast  cell). The internal cavity of tooth is called pulp 
cavity  which  is  filled  with  a  highly  vascular  and nutritive  tissue,  odontoblast  cells 
(dentine secreting cells) and nerves.  
ii. Internal Nostrils:  
The  buccal  cavity in  its roof near vomerine  teeth contains  paired openings  called 
internal or posterior nostrils which communicate  with external nostrils. 
iii. Tongue:  
Tongue is large,  thick, fleshy and protrusible structure lying on the floor of buccal 
cavity. It is attached anteriorly  to the inner  border of lower jaw;  free and notched 
(bifid) posteriorly.  Its upper surface bears taste buds in the form of small  papillae. 
Secretion  of tubular  lingual  glands and intermaxillary  gland inside  buccal  cavity 
make  the tongue adhesive. Such a  sticky tongue can  be thrown out and retracted 
suddenly by frog to capture live prey. 
iv. Bulgings of Orbits:  
The  roof of buccal  cavity just behind the vomerine  teeth contains two large,  soft, 
oval and cushion like  areas  the bulgings  of orbits and eyeballs.  While  swallowing 
food, eyes are depressed, pushing the food backward.  


 
 
v. Eustachean openings:  
Openings  of Eustachian tube into  the buccal  cavity,  each situated at the postero-
lateral side of roof of bucco-pharyngeal cavity. They communicate with the middle 
ear. 
vi. Vocal sacs opening: 
In male frog, there are two openings of vocal sacs, each present in the angle of two 
jaws on the floor of bucco-pharyngeal  cavity. Vocal sacs amplify the sound at the 
time of croaking.  
vii. Glottis and gullet: 
Glottis  is  a  median   longitudinal  slit   like   aperture  in  the  posterior   side  of 
buccopharyngeal cavity, just behind the tongue. It opens while breathing so that air 
is passed to lungs and closes at the time of swallowing food. 
Similarly, Bucco-pharyngeal  cavity posteriorly  leads into the oesophagus through 
a transverse opening called gullet.  
viii. Fossae and prelingual elevations 
Fossae are three in number  which are  minute pits or depressions at the apical  part 
of upper jaw, just below the maxillary  teeth. Front end of lower jaw contains three 
small  elevations, called prelingual  elevations.  
Prelingual  elevations fit into fossae to close the mouth tightly.

3. Oesophagus: 
Buccopharyngeal cavity leads behind into a short, and stretchable muscular tube called 
oesophagus. Its inner lining  is thrown into a large number of longitudinal folds for the 
sufficient distension of the oesophagus  during the passage of the ingested food to the 
stomach. It abruptly leads into the stomach as there is no distinct demarcation between 
the oesophagus and stomach. Its inner wall also contains peptic glands and goblet cells. 


 
 
 
Figure 8.4.10 Alimentary Canal of Frog 
4. Stomach: 
Stomach is a J-shaped muscular  sac like  structure situated in the left side of the body 
cavity or the coelom and attached to dorsal body wall by a mesentery called mesogaster. 
It is  divided into two regions – the anterior  large and wider region  is  called cardiac 
stomach and the posterior short narrower region is called pyloric stomach. The internal 
lining (mucous epithelium) of the stomach is thrown into numerous longitudinal folds 
which  may  allow  the  expansion  of  the  stomach  whenever  required.  Its  mucous 
epithelium also possesses   multicellular   gastric  glands  which  secrete  pepsinogen 
enzyme,  and unicellular  oxyntic  cells  that secrete  hydrochloric  acid. Stomach  also 
contains  goblet  cells  which  secrete  mucus  for  lubricating  food. The  pyloric  end  of 
stomach  is  constricted externally and  its  opening  into  small  intestine  is internally 
guarded by a muscular  ring  or flap like sphincter  or pyloric  valve. This  valve  checks 
reverse flow of food from the intestine into the stomach. Function of stomach is to store 
food for few hours and also digest the food. 
5. Intestine:  It consists of two parts- 
a. Small Intestine: The small intestine is the long-convoluted tube of 30 cm length 
in the form of several loops, supported by a fan-like membrane,  the mesentery. 
Small intestine is the main site of digestion and absorption of food. 
Small intestine consists of duodenum and ileum.  
i. Duodenum 
The anterior part of the small  intestine which curves upwards to form a 
U shaped with the stomach is called the duodenum. The internal mucous 
lining  of  duodenum  is  provided  with  low  transverse  folds.  In  the 
duodenum,  opens  a  common  hepato-pancreatic  duct  from  liver  and 
pancreas, draining the bile and pancreatic juice respectively. 
ii. Ileum 
The  remaining  part  of small intestine continues as  a long,  narrow  and 
much twisted tubular part called  ileum. The  internal  mucous layer of 


 
 
ileum forms a number of longitudinal folds. True villi, glands and crypts 
are absent.  
b. Large intestine:   The  ileum  opens into a  short and dilated tube of about 4 cm 
called  large  intestine  or  rectum  which  leads behind into cloacal  chamber. Its 
inner layer is with goblet cells and forms numerous low longitudinal folds. This 
part absorbs water and stores faeces. 
6. Cloacal chamber and cloaca: Rectum is followed by a small terminal sac-like structure, 
called cloacal  chamber. This sac also receives  the opening of ureters. Cloacal chamber 
opens exteriorly through a vent or cloacal opening situated at the posterior most end of 
the body.

B. DIGESTIVE GLANDS 
In addition  to the gastric  glands and the intestinal  glands, two large digestive  glands namely 
liver and pancreas are also found in frog. 
1. Liver:  
Like other vertebrates,  liver in frog, is the largest gland situated in the anterior  body cavity 
close to the heart  and lungs.  It is reddish  brown coloured and lobulated  gland consisting of 
three lobes: - the right, median and the left lobe. The left  lobe is again subdivided  into two 
lobes. A spherical, thin-walled, large and greenish sac called gall bladder is present ventrally 
between the right  and left lobe  of liver.  It serves  as a reservoir  for the bile juice constantly 
secreted by the hepatic cells. The bile passes into the gall bladder through hepatic ducts. Cystic 
duct from gall bladder  and some hepatic ducts from  liver get united to form a common bile 
duct which passes through the pancreas and opens into the duodenum. The common bile duct 
receives the fine ducts from the pancreas while passing on its way to open into duodenum. Bile 
lacks digestive enzymes.

Histologically,  frog’s liver is composed of numerous lobules or hepatic acini and these lobules 
are separated from each other by the presence of connective tissue containing bile capillaries, 
hepatic  ducts, blood  capillaries and  sinusoids.  Each  lobule  or hepatic  acinus  consists of 
numerous polyhedral,  glandular hepatic  cells; each containing a nucleus and cytoplasm  with 
protein granules, droplets  of fats, glycogen and often black or dark brown pigment  granules. 
Hepatic cells secrete bile juice which is poured into canalicule. These canaliculi unite to form 
bile ductule which again unite, re-unite and finally form bile or hepatic ducts. The liver receives 
blood from the hepatic artery and the hepatic portal vein.  
Functions  
The liver performs the following important functions: 
i. It  secretes green  coloured alkaline  bile  juice  containing  bile  salts,  bile  pigments, 
cholesterol, water etc. Bile juice  reduces acidity of food, provides suitable medium for 
the  activation  of pancreatic  enzymes  and  emulsifies fats.  Conversion  of  larger  fat 
globules into smaller  forms by the action of bile juice is called emulsification. 
ii. Liver is  an important organ of metabolism  of carbohydrate, protein and fats. Glucose 
absorbed from the wall of alimentary  canal, if excess, is converted to glycogen by the 
process  of glycogenesis  in  liver.  Similarly,  glycogen  may be  converted to glucose,  if 
required,    by    the    process    of    glycogenolysis.     Formation    of    new    glucose 
(gluconeogenesis)  from non-carbohydrate compounds such as amino acid and glycerol 
also takes place in liver. 
iii. Liver  performs  the  function of de-amination  and detoxification. As  the  amino  acids 
can’t be stored in the body of animals, metabolism of amino acid produces ammonia as 
by-product, called deamination. Ammonia  is  highly  toxic, reacts with carbon dioxide 
in liver  to convert into less toxic urea by the process of detoxification. 
iv. It also removes  some other excretory products such as bilirubin  and biliverdin formed 
by  break  down of  old  RBCs, which  are  discharged  with  bile  into  the intestine  and 
expelled with the faeces.  
v. In an embryo,  RBCs are  formed in  liver but in  an  adult old and worn out RBCs are 
destroyed in liver. 
vi. Kupffer cells are the phagocytic cells which destroy harmful microbes such as bacteria. 
vii. It stores copper and iron and fat-soluble vitamins. 
viii. Fibrinogen and prothrombin are synthesized in liver which are essential blood clotting 
factors. 
ix.  Liver also secretes heparin which prevents clotting of blood in blood vessels. 


 
 
2. Pancreas: 
It is an irregularly branched, flat and cream coloured gland of exocrine and endocrine nature, 
which is held in position by mesentery between the stomach and duodenum. It is traversed by 
the common bile duct into which the ducts from pancreas also open to from a hepatopancreatic 
duct. 
Histologically, exocrine part of pancreas consists of many pancreatic lobules or acini embedded 
in connective  tissues. Connective tissue  also contains pancreatic  ducts, blood vessels, lymph

cavity  or  lumen.  Secretory  cells  are  with  a  large  nucleus and filled  with  non-granular 
cytoplasm. These cells secrete pancreatic juice containing digestive enzymes which is poured 
into central lumen or ductule. Acini unite with each other through their ductules, which in turn 
unite to form the larger ducts and finally form the pancreatic ducts. The pancreatic ducts open 
into the bile duct from liver to form hepatopancreatic duct.

C. PHYSIOLOGY OF DIGESTION 
1. Process of capturing food and ingestion:  
Frog is a carnivorous;  especially  insectivorous animal  that feeds on insects, earthworms, 
spiders, snails,  fishes  and smaller  frogs  which are  captured  with the  help of  sticky  and 
protractible  tongue. Mode of catching prey is remarkable. While feeding, the frog sits at a 
suitable  place frequented by insects. When any prey comes close to it, it opens its mouth 
and suddenly  flicks  out its  sticky  tongue  and strikes  the  prey. The prey soon comes  in 
contact with the tongue and gets attached.  Now, the tongue is withdrawn into the buccal 
cavity.  
Once the prey is captured and brought into the buccal cavity, it is seized and killed with the 
help of hook-like and pointed maxilliary  and vomerine  teeth. Teeth do not chew the food 
but food is moistened by mucus secreted by goblet cells found in the inner lining of buccal 
cavity. From the buccal cavity food is pushed back by the beating of cilia on the inner wall 
of  buccal  cavity  and  with  the  help  of  inbulging  of  eyeball.  Then, contraction  of  the 
pharyngeal wall and by peristalsis in oesophagus, food gets passed through oesophagus, 
down into the stomach.  
2. Digestion of Food:  
Food contains  complex  organic  substances  which  are  not  of  immediate  use  as they  are 
insoluble and can’t diffuse or absorbed through the inner lining of the alimentary canal. Such 
complex foodstuffs  are subjected  to the physical and chemical  changes to transform  them 
into soluble  and absorbable  form. The physical  change or mastication  of food is brought 
about by the peristaltic  movements of the alimentary  canal, while the chemical changes are 
brought about by the enzymes or biological catalysts. Enzymes are complex proteins having 
specific  property.  In the digestive  tract,  several glands are present which produce various 
enzymes and they always act at the optimum body temperature.  Proteolytic enzymes digest 
proteins, amylolytic enzymes digests carbohydrates and lipolytic  enzymes digest fats.  
i. Digestion in buccal cavity and oesophagus:  
There is neither mechanical nor chemical digestion in the buccal cavity as the buccal cavity 
is  devoid  of digestive  glands.  From  the  buccal  cavity, the  prey  is  directly  pushed  into 
oesophagus where it undergoes physical changes to some extent due to constant peristaltic 
movement  of  its  wall.  The  inner  lining  of oesophagus  secretes mucus  and  an inactive 
pepsinogen  enzyme.  No digestion  occurs in  oesophagus. Mucus simply  makes the  food 
moist or slippery to pass down easily.  
ii. Gastric Digestion:  
Food reaches  the stomach and stays  there for  about 2-3 hours;  its  wall shows a series  of 
peristaltic  movements which  brought  about  the  churning  of  food and  mix the  food 
thoroughly  with  gastric  juice  secreted  by  the  gastric  glands; situated  in  the inner  layer 
(internal lining) of stomach. Here, secretion of gastric juice is under the influence of gastrin 


 
 
hormone which is produced by the stomach wall as soon as the food reaches to stomach. 
Gastric  glands  consist  of  multicellular  peptic  glands  secreting a  pro-enzyme  called 
pepsinogen  and  unicellular  oxyntic  cells  secreting  hydrochloric  acid.  The  gastric  juice 
secreted by the gastric glands contains a large amount of water, inactive pepsinogen enzyme 
and  hydrochloric  acid.  Inactive  pepsinogen  changes  into  active  pepsin, if mixed  with 
hydrochloric  acid (HCl). Pepsinogen secreted  by oesophagus also becomes active by HCl 
in  stomach.  Hydrochloric acid  prevents  the  bacterial  decomposition  and  dissolves  the 
inorganic  salts  as  well as  makes  the food soft.  The pepsin acts  on proteins  of food  and 
changes them into peptones and proteoses.  
 
   Pepsinogen (inactive) + HCl → pepsin (Active) 
   Pepsin + Proteins → Peptones + Proteoses  
Now, Food takes  the form of a thick  creamy acidic  fluid  called  chyme. Peristalsis  in the 
stomach wall forces the chyme to pass in small amount at a time through the pyloric opening 
into the duodenum. To conclude, three functions of stomach are: Storage of food, mechanical 
mixing and chemical modification  of food. 
iii. Intestinal  Digestion:  
As the acidic chyme enters duodenum, wall of duodenum is also stimulated and secretes two 
hormones namely secretin  and cholecystokinin  which diffuse into the blood and reach the 
pancreas and liver respectively. Then, secretin stimulates  the pancreas to secrete pancreatic 
juice and cholecystokinin  activates the gall bladder to release the bile juice.  
Bile  and  pancreatic  juices  are  poured  into  the  duodenum  through  the  common 
hepatopancreatic  duct. Meanwhile, the intestinal  mucosa layer also secretes  the intestinal 
juice  called  succus  entericus  under  the  influence  of  enterocrinin  hormone. All  the three 
substances get mixed up with the food in the intestine.  
Bile contains no digestive enzymes. It is alkaline because it contains certain inorganic salts 
like  sodium  bicarbonate,  sodium  glycocholate  and  sodium  taurocholate  which  not  only 
neutralise the acidity of the chyme but also activates pancreatic juice as the pancreatic juice 
acts only in  alkaline  medium.  Bile juice mechanically breaks down larger  fat into small 
globules, called emulsification.  
Pancreatic  Juice  contains powerful  enzymes such  as  amylase (amylopsin),  trypsinogen, 
chymotrypsinogen  and lipase (steapsin). All of them act in alkaline  medium and hydrolyse 
carbohydrates, proteins and fats. Trypsinogen is an inactive enzyme or pro-enzyme and gets

3. Absorption of nutients:  
Absorption is that process by which the digested food is passed into blood stream. It occurs 
either by active  transport  or by passive transport. Absorption is mainly carried  out in the 
duodenum and ileum  as their inner layer is very much suited i.e. modified  as absorptive 
surface or villi  (singular;  villus).  Each villus is richly supplied with blood capillaries  and 
lymph  vessel  or lacteal.  Water, mineral  salts  and other  nutrients  are  passively  absorbed 
through the mucosa. The amino acids, glucose and fructose are absorbed by the diffusion 
through mucosa and passed into the blood capillaries and finally reach the liver through the 
hepatic portal vein. The glycerol and fatty acids pass into lymph vessels called lacteals. The 
glycerol can be absorbed easily as it is soluble in water, whereas the fatty acids cannot be 
absorbed as they are insoluble in water. So, before absorption the fatty acids mix with bile 
salts to make themselves water soluble substance called micelles.  The micelles  now enter 
intestinal mucosa cell where they are converted to chylomicrons which enter to lymph ducts 
or lacteals.

4. Assimilation:  
Assimilation  is the process in which absorbed nutrients are utilized by the body. Nutrients 
are utilized by body as per need such as synthesizing enzymes, amino acid, repairing cells 
and tissues, stored as reserve food material for future use, running metabolic  activities etc. 
Similarly, Glucose is transported by the blood into the cells and tissues where glucose burns 
with oxygen or oxidizes to release energy, carbon dioxide and water. 
5. Egestion of undigested Food:  
After the completion of digestion and absorption in small intestine, undigested food enters 
rectum  by peristalsis  for storage and formation  of faeces. Undigested food particles,  old 
epithelial cells, leucocytes, bile pigments and large number of bacteria forms faecal matters 
which are discharged through the cloacal aperture.

To keep a cell  alive,  it  requires  some  essential  substances  such  as regular  supply  of water,

excess water  etc. should be transported  to the excretory  organs for their  elimination.  Blood 
vascular system is the organ system that is responsible for the transportation of both useful and 
harmful substances to the required sites within the body. Specific organs such as heart, blood 
vessels and blood form blood vascular system or circulatory system in frog.

A. HEART 
1. External features 
The heart of a frog is a dark red colored and conical muscular organ with a broad anterior side 
and somewhat tapered posterior side. It is protected dorsally by vertebral  column and ventro-
laterally by the pectoral girdle. It lies mid-ventrally  in the anterior body cavity in between two 
lungs, just anterior to the liver. It is enclosed within a delicate, thin and transparent two layered 
sac called the pericardium. The outer layer is parietal  pericardium and inner layer is known as 
visceral pericardium. A space or cavity between these two layers is called the pericardial cavity 
which is filled with a pericardial fluid. The pericardial fluid maintains the heart moist, reduces 
friction and allows its free movements; also protect from the shocks, and mechanical injuries. 
 
Figure 8.4.15 External Structure of heart of frog 


 
 
When  the pericardium is  removed  and  observed  thoroughly,  the  heart consists  of  three 
chambers where two anterior chambers are called auricles or atria and a posterior pink coloured 
conical  chamber  is  called ventricle.  In  addition  to the  auricles  and  ventricle, frog  has  two 
additional  chambers known as sinus  venosus and  truncus  arteriosus. Sinus venosus  present 
dorsally and truncus arteriosus present ventrally.  
On the dorsal side, heart shows a thin walled, dark coloured and triangular chamber which is 
called sinus venosus. Sinus venosus is formed by the union of three caval veins in which two 
are anterior  precavals and one is posterior post caval. Sinus  venosus receives  impure  blood 
from various parts of body through precaval and postcaval veins to pour into right auricle. Two 
pulmonary veins from lungs also unite and open into the left auricle of the heart. 
On the ventral side, there is a tubular structure  arising from the anterior margin of ventricle 
on the right side called truncus arteriosus. It runs anteriorly and divides into two branches called 
right  aortic  trunk  and  left  aortic  trunk.  Each  aortic  trunk  further  gives  off  three  branches 
namely- carotid  arch, systemic  arch and pulmo-cutaneous  arch.  Right and Left  auricles  are 
separated externally  by a faint inter-auricular  groove and two auricles are separated  from the 
ventricle by a transverse groove called coronary sulcus.  
2.  Internal structure 
The internal  structure of heart can be observed and studied by removing its ventral  wall. It 
shows that it is a hollow conical  muscular  organ with two thin walled auricles  and a thick-
walled ventricle.  
 
Figure 8.4.16 Internal Structure of heart of frog 
Right  and left  auricles  are separated  from  each other  by a thin  and vertical inter  auricular 
septum. The right auricle is larger and receives blood from sinus venosus through a transverse 
oval shaped sinu-atrial  opening, guarded by valve formed  of two flaps  like structures. Sinu-
atrial  or sinu-auricular  opening is situated  medially  near interauricular  septum. The common 
opening of pulmonary veins is situated in the left auricle near the septum but it is without valve.  
Both the auricles open into a single ventricle by a common wide auriculo-ventricular aperture, 
which is guarded by valve made up of four flaps; one dorsal, one ventral  and two lateral  in 
position.  The free  edges  of these  flaps  project into  the  ventricle  and are  supported  by fine 
contractile  but  tough  thread-like  structures  known  as  chordate  tendineae. The  chordae 
tendineae regulate  the movement  of the auriculo-ventricular  valve to allow the blood to pass 


 
 
from the auricles into the ventricle but prevent the backward flow of blood from ventricle into 
the auricles. 
The wall of ventricle is thick hence highly muscular and spongy and its inner surface contains 
numerous ridges and strands called the columnae carneae. The spaces between these strands 
are called fissures. Ventricle on the right side opens into truncus arteriosus on the ventral side. 
A row of three semilunar  valves is present at the opening where ventricle  opens into truncus 
arteriosus. These valves allow the flow of blood into the truncus arteriosus from the ventricle.  
Truncus  arteriosus  is  further  divisible  into a proximal or basal muscular  part called  conus 
arteriosus and the distal  thinwalled  bulbous  aretriosus. The inner  hollow cavities  inside  the 
conus arteriosus  and bulbous arteriosus  are called  pylangium and synangium respectively. A 
row of the three semilunar valves separates the pylangium from the synangium.The pylangium 
is further divided incompletely into two chambers by a vertical and spirally twisted valve called 
spiral valve. The right chamber is the cavum aorticum and the left chamber is called the cavum 
pulmocutaneum. The spiral valve is attached dorsally but its ventral edge is free. In the cavum 
pulmocutaneum  lies  a  common  opening  of  pulmocutaneous  arches.  This  opening  is  also 
guarded by a valve. The synangium bifurcates  into two branches as right aortic trunk and and 
left aortic trunk. Each aortic trunk divides into three arches, the pulmocutaneous, systemic and 
carotid arches.  
3. Working of Heart: 
Heart  of  frog  is  myogenic  (heart  beat  originates  from  the  heart muscles  itself). Rhythmic 
contraction and relaxation  of the heart is called heartbeat. Heart continuously beats during the 
life span which is under the nervous control to pump blood throughout the body. Contraction 
phase of ventricle is called systole and relaxation phase is called diastole. Wave of contraction 
originates  from the sinus venosus and spreads over its wall and auricles. The sinus  venosus 
contracts, whose contraction drives the mixed blood from the sinus venosus to the right auricle. 
The backward flow of the blood from right auricle to the sinus venosus is checked by the sinu-
auricular  valves. Meanwhile,  the  left  auricle also receives  oxygenated  blood  through 
pulmonary  veins  from  the  lungs.  When  the  two  auricles  are  full  of  blood,  they  contract 
simultaneously. The  blood  cannot  go  back  into  pulmonary  veins  as  the  openings  of  the 
pulmonary veins are small  and oblique which are closed automatically  by the contraction  of 
the  left  auricle.  Thus,  blood  from  both  auricles  is  pumped  into  the  single  ventricle 
through auriculoventricular  aperture. Now, wave of the contraction is passed over the wall of 
the ventricle. There are two views regarding how the ventricle pumps blood through the truncus 
arteriosus: 
a. Old view or conventional view 
According to this view given by Brucke 1858, the ventricle contains impure blood in the right 
side received from the right  auricle,  mixed  blood in middle region and pure or oxygenated 
blood in the left side from the left auricle. Whole Blood can’t get mixed thoroughly because of 
viscosity  of blood and presence of columnae carnea on the inner wall of ventricle. When the 
ventricle contracts, deoxygenated blood from its right side passes into cavum pulmocutaneum 
and is then carried to the lungs and skin for oxygenation. The spiral  valve doesnot allow the 
blood to pass into the cavum aorticum at this time. Then, opening of cavum pulmocutaneum is 
closed by spiral valve. Next, mixed blood is pumped into cavum aorticum which passes through 


 
 
systemic arches. Finally, oxygenated blood from the left side is passed through cavum aorticum 
into the carotid arches which flow to the head region. Thus, spiral valve in truncus arteriosus 
play major role in directing blood in different arches.  
b. Modern View 
According to the modern view, both oxygenated and deoxygenated bloods are mixed in the 
ventricle.  When the ventricle  contracts and the backward flow of blood into  auricles  are 
prevented by the auriculo-ventricular  valve,  the mixed  blood  of the ventricle  enters  the 
truncus arteriosus.  From the truncus arteriosus  mixed blood goes to three arches (carotid, 
systemic and pulmocutaneous) and reaches all the parts of the body. 
By this view, It is concluded that there is complete mixing of blood in ventricle, spiral valve is 
nonfunctional  and  blood  leaving  from  truncus  arteriosus  is  mixed blood which  circulates 
throughout the body.  
B. BLOOD VESSELS 
Blood vessels are tubes or channels of variable sizes forming the complex network in the body 
of frog. Their prime function is to transport blood among the various parts of the body. There 
is unidirectional  flow of blood in blood vessels. Blood vessels are of three types 
1. Arteries:  
They are muscular, elastic and thick-walled blood vessels. Arteries transport blood from heart 
to the various parts of the body. They have narrow lumen. There is more pressure of blood in 
arteries and hence they are without valves. To withstand such a great pressure, they have thick 
wall. They are deeply seated in the body and transport  oxygenated blood but in frog, arteries 
transport mixed blood. These blood vessels do not collapse easily after death because of their 
thicker  and rigid  wall. Arteries  divide into  smaller  branches  called  arterioles  and  arterioles 
divide into capillaries.

Histologically, wall of artery consists of outermost fibrous layer of collagen (tunica adventitia), 
middle thick muscular  layer and innermost  layer of squamous epithelial  cells (endothelium). 
Muscular layer is well developed in arteries.

2. Capillaries: 
Capillaries are fine thread like network of blood vessels extending all body tissues. Their wall 
is  extremely  thin  which  i.e  made  up  of  single  layer  of  squamous  epithelial  cell  called 
endothelium. There is no muscular and fibrous layer in the capillary wall. Capillaries are found 
abundantly  in muscles  and glands.  Because of their  thin wall  exchange of nutrients,  gases, 
hormones,  waste  occurs  in  between  blood  and  tissues  under  pressure.  Capillaries  form  a 
network around organ or tissue  between the arterioles and venules. But in some organs like 
liver,  spleen  and  red  bone  marrow,  irregular  blood  spaces  called  sinusoids  replace  blood 
capillaries. 
 
3. Veins: 
Veins are thin walled, inelastic  blood vessels  having wide lumen. They transport  blood from 
different  parts of the body to the heart. Comparatively,  there is less pressure  of blood on the 
wall of veins so they have valves at frequent intervals. Valves in the veins check reverse flow 
of blood. Veins are superficially  situated in the body. Generally, veins transport deoxygenated 
blood but in the case of frog, they carry mixed blood. These vessels collapse after cut or death 
easily because of their feebly developed muscular wall. Veins divides into fine branches called 
venules.

Histologically,  wall of vein consists of outermost fibrous layer of collagen (tunica adventitia), 
middle  thin  muscular  layer  and innermost  layer  of squamous epithelial  cells  (endothelium). 
Muscular layer is poorly developed in veins.

C. BLOOD 
Blood is a liquid connective tissue composed of different type of blood cells suspended freely 
in  a  clear  liquid  called  plasma. It  keeps circulating  in  closed  tubes  called  blood  vessels 
throughout the body. Generally, Blood pH is 7. Blood consists of mainly 2 parts: Plasma and 
Blood corpuscles. 
1. Plasma 
Plasma is non-living, clear, pale yellow, slightly alkaline watery portion comprising about 55% 
of the blood. Dissolved substances in the plasma are nutrients, mineral  salts, urea, hormones, 
proteins  and other  soluble  substances. Major  proteins  found in  plasma  are  serum  albumin, 
serum  globulin,  fibrinogen  and  Heparin.  Serum  albumen  has  role  in  maintaining  osmotic 
pressure and serum globulin forms enzymes and antibodies. Fibrinogen has role in clotting of 
blood in wound and injuries and heparin is an anticoagulant  which prevents blood clotting  in 
blood vessels. 
Functions of plasma: 
i. Plasma  transports nutrients, hormones,  oxygen and metabolic wastes like urea  carbon 
dioxide to the respective parts of the body. 
ii. Plasma proteins perform various functions such as maintaining blood osmotic pressure; 
neutralize damaged body protein, toxin and engulf germs. 
iii. Some plasma  proten like fibrinogen helps in clotting of blood during cut etc. 
2. Blood cells or blood corpuscles 
a. Red blood cells or Erythrocytes 
Red coloured cells (RBCs) are numerous (about 2.5-4 million  per cubic mm of blood), oval, 
flattened,  nucleated,  and  biconvex  in  shape.  RBCs  have  respiratory  pigment  called 
haemoglobin.  Hemoglobin  transports  oxygen to the tissues  in the form of oxy-haemoglobin 
and also imparts red colour to the blood.  They are formed in liver and speen and destroyed in 
the same organs. In frog, their life span is about 100 days.

Functions of RBC 
i. RBCs transport oxygen from lungs to tissues (in oxyhaemoglobin form) and carbon 
dioxide (in carbamino form) from tissues to lungs. 
ii. RBCs also have role in maintaining pH of blood. 
iii. They maintain viscosity of blood.

cell and bacteria through phagocytosis and also called phagocytes. Because of their changeable 
shape, they squeeze through capillary wall and show diapedesis.

8.4.5 RESPIRATORY SYSTEM 
Organs coordinated for the intake and supply of oxygen to the tissues and throwing out carbon 
dioxide from the body constitute respiratory system. The entire process of respiration consists 
of mechanical  and chemical processes such as inhaling oxygen and exhaling carbon dioxide, 
transport of gases and oxidation of food in the tissues to release energy.  
Tadpole or larva of frog lives completely in water and breathes through four pairs of external 
gills.  Such  respiration  is  called  branchial  respiration  whereas  Adult  Frog  spends  dual  or 
amphibious mode of life and performs respiration by the following ways: 
1. Cutaneous respiration  (through moist skin) 
2. Buccopharyngeal respiration  (through buccopharyngeal lining) 
3. Pulmonary respiration  (through lungs) 
 
1. Cutaneous respiration: This type of respiration occurs all the time whether the frog is on 
land or in water. When the frog is metabolically  less active and demand of oxygen is low 
or at the time of hibernation and aestivation; cutaneous respiration  takes place. 
Skin of frog is very thin, moist,  highly vascularized  and an efficient  organ for cutaneous 
respiration.  It  maintains  skin  moist  by  frequently  visiting  water  sources  and  secreting 
slippery mucus by mucous gland present in its skin. 
  
Figure 8.4.24 Cutaneous respiration in frog 
 
During gaseous exchange through skin,  oxygen first  dissolves  in the thin layer  of water 
covering the skin and diffuses  through the epidermis  into the blood capillaries  present  in 
the dermis.  Then, oxygen is carried  by the blood in the  form of oxyhaemoglobin  to  the 
tissues  and oxy-haemoglobin  dissociates  into  oxygen and haemoglobin  near the tissues. 
Oxygen diffuses  into  the  cell.  Oxygen is  used  for  oxidation  of  glucose  in  the  cell  and 
carbon-dioxide is the waste product produced due to this process which is also carried by 
blood to the skin surface and then gets diffused out. 
2. Bucco-pharyngeal respiration: 
This type of respiration  takes place through the lining  (internal layer) of bucco-pharyngeal 
cavity when the frog is resting on land or partially immersed in water. The  inner lining  of 


 
 
bucco-pharyngeal  cavity  is moist  and richly  supplied  with  blood  capillaries. Hence,  it 
serves as the respiratory surface for exchange of gases. Bucco-pharyngeal respiration takes 
place with the help two sets of muscles  called sternohyal  muscle  and petrohyal  muscles. 
Sternohyal  muscles   are  those  muscles,  connected  with  sternum  and  hyoid  whereas 
petrohyal muscles are connected with squamosal and hyoid bone. Contraction of sternohyal 
muscles  lowers  the floor of buccal  cavity and contraction of petrohyal  muscle  raises  the 
floor  of  buccal  cavity.  Process  of inspiration and expiration in  buccal  respiration  is 
discussed below. 
i. Inspiration 
• At first, sternohyal muscle contracts and floor of buccal cavity is lowered. 
• Then,  volume  of  buccal  cavity  increases  and  air  pressure in  the  buccal  cavity 
decreases. 
• Air from outside, rushes into the cavity through nostrils. 
• Gullet,  glottis and mouth  remain  closed  during this  process  and no  air  passes into 
lungs. 
• Fresh air or oxygen diffuses through mucous layer (inner  layer) of buccal cavity and 
passed into blood capillaries 
 
 
Figure 8.4.25 Inspiration in bucco pharyngeal respiration 
 
• Meanwhile, CO2 diffuses into the buccopharyngeal cavity from blood capillaries. 
ii. Expiration 
• Sternohyal muscle  relaxes,  petrohyal  muscle  contracts and floor of buccal  cavity is 
raised. 
• Consequently, volume  of buccal  cavity  decreases  and air  pressure  increases  in  the 
buccal cavity. 
• Then air rich in carbondioxide, from buccal cavity escapes out through nostrils.   
 
  


 
 
3. Pulmonary respiration 
This type respiration  takes place through the lungs on land, in which atmospheric  oxygen is 
inhaled through nostrils.  It occurs when the frog is more active and demand of oxygen is very 
high. Lungs are primary organs for pulmonary respiration. The route through which air enters 
or  exits  from  the  lungs  is  called  respiratory  tract  or  path  which  consists  of  nostrils, 
buccopharyngeal cavity, laryngotracheal  chamber, bronchi and lungs. 
 
Figure 8.4.26 Pulmonary respiratory organs 
a. Nostrils: 
External  nostrils  are the paired  openings  situated  dorsolaterally,  a  little  above  the 
mouth.  They  open  into  olfactory  chamber  of skull  and  then lead  into  buccal  cavity 
through a pair of openings called internal nostrils. Each internal nostril is present on the 
either side of patches of vomerine teeth.  
b. Buccopharyngeal cavity: 
Internal nostrils open into a wide cavity which serves as a common passage of food and 
air  called  buccopharyngeal  cavity.  Buccopharyngeal  cavity  posteriorly  leads  in  to 
laryngotracheal  chamber through an opening called glottis.  
c. Laryngotracheal chamber 
Laryngotracheal chamber  is a small  thin walled chamber  which performs  the function 
of larynx and trachea. Its wall is supported by cartilages that consist of paired crescentic 
arytenoid  cartilages  and  a  single  ring  like  cricoid  cartilage. The  inner  or  mucous 
membrane  of laryngotracheal  chamber  is thrown into a pair of elastic  horizontal folds 
called vocal cords. These  vocal cords are stretched and relaxed by muscles  connected 
with the cartilages. Such muscles  can change the tension of cord and hence pitch of the 
sound too. When  air  from  lungs  is  forcefully  passed out between the vocal  cords  of 
laryngotracheal chamber, vibration vocal cords results the production of sound. Hence, 
laryngotracheal  chamber  is  also  called voice box. At the time  of croaking, vocal sacs 
amplify the sound in male frog. Laryngotracheal chamber is connected to lungs of either 
side by a short tubular bronchus. 


 
 
 
Figure 8.4.27 Laryngotracheal chamber in frog 
d. Lungs: 
Lungs  are  paired,  pinkish,  oval,  thin walled  and  highly  elastic  sacs  like  structures 
situated inside the anterior body cavity on either  side of the heart. They are the chief 
respiratory  organs  and  also  help  in  buoyancy  in  water. They  are  supplied  with 
pulmonary arteries and pulmonary veins.  
Histologically,  the wall of lung consists of three layers: outer peritoneum, middle layer 
of connective  tissue and inner layer of epithelial  cells. Peritoneum consists of simple 
squamous epithelium, connective tissue contains unstriated muscle fibre, blood vessels, 
nerves etc. and inner layer of epithelial  cells. The inner surface of lung is divided into 
a series  of small  chambers called alveoli  by trabeculae  or septa. The inner surface  of 
alveoli  is lined by ciliated  columnar  epithelium  having mucus secreting  goblet  cells. 
Septa are highly vascularised for gaseous exchange. There is no division of bronchi into 
bronchioles in lung and there are few alveoli in lungs of frog. Hence, lungs of frog are 
poorly developed and less efficient.

Mechanism of pulmonary ventilation or breathing 
Pulmonary  respiration  takes  place  in  between  buccal  respiration. Two sets  of  muscles  i.e. 
sternohyal  and  petrohyal  present  in  buccopharyngeal  cavity  have main role  in  pulmonary 
respiration. Sternohyal muscles are connected with the sternum and hyoid bone present in the 
lower jaw and their contraction  lower floor of buccal cavity. Similarly, petrohyal muscles are 


 
 
connected with hyoid and squamosal of skull and their  contraction  raise the floor  of buccal 
cavity. Process of pulmonary ventilation can be discussed under following points:  
1. Inspiration: 
• Glottis and mouth are closed but nostrils remain open. 
• Sternohyal muscle  contracts causing the lowering of floor of buccal cavity. 
• Volume of buccal cavity increases and air pressure  inside the cavity decreases. 
• Air rushes into the buccal cavity through the nostrils from outside. 
• Now,  mentomeckalian  bone  of  lower  jaw  raises  premaxillae   upward  to  close 
nostrils. 
• Then, Contraction of petrohyal muscle takes place raising the floor of buccal cavity. 
• As a  result, volume  of bucco  pharyngeal  cavity  decreases and air  pressure  in  the 
cavity increases. 
• Glottis open and air rushes to lungs. (mouth and gullet are closed in this time) 
• When lungs  are  filled with air,  then glottis is  closed and gaseous exchange  takes 
place in lungs for some time and buccal respiration also take place at that time. 
 
Figure 8.4.29 Inspiration in pulmonary respiration 
2. Expiration 
• First of all, contraction of lungs takes place, aided by abdominal muscles. 
• As a result, lung volume decreases and air pressure  in the lung increases. 
• External nostrils are closed. 
• Sternohyal muscles  contract and volume of buccal cavity increases. 
• Consequently, air pressure decreases in the buccal cavity and air from lungs moves 
to buccal cavity by opening glottis. 
• Then, contraction of petrohyal muscles takes place. 
• Volume of buccal cavity decreases and air pressure increases  inside it. 
• Glottis is closed and air moves out by opening nostrils. 
  


 
 
Physiology of respiration 
In frog, entire process of respiration can be discussed under the following headings: 
1. Breathing or external respiration: It is the process of taking in oxygen and throwing out 
carbon  dioxide. As discussed  earlier, this  process  occurs in  frog through  moist  skin, 
buccopharyngeal cavity and lungs.

tissues. One molecule  of haemoglobin  transports four molecule  of oxygen. Formation

with oxygen to release energy, carbon dioxide and water.

1. MALE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM 
a) Testes: 
Testes are  paired, oval, yellowish  coloured  structures.  Each testis  is attached  on the antero-
ventral surface of the kidney with the help of a double fold of peritoneum called mesorchium.  
Histologically, each testis contains hundreds of microscopic convoluted tubular units called the 
semeniferous  tubules. The wall of semeniferous  tubule is lined by germinal epithelial  cells or 
primordial  germ cells  which later  transform  into sperms  by the process of spermatogenesis. 
The  space  between  the  semeniferous  tubules  is  filled  with  the  connective  tissue,  blood 
capillaries,  nerve fibers and interstitial  cells. The interstitial  cells secrete a male sex hormone 
called testosterone, which produces the secondary sexual characters in breeding season such as 
development  of vocal sacs, nuptial  pads etc. The sperms  are arranged in the form  of sperm 
bundles and nourished by nurse cells. A mature sperm is elongated microscopic cell with three 
distinct parts:  head, middle piece and tail.  A conical cytoplasmic  structure  present on top of 
the head is  called  acrosome  which is  used for the penetration  of  the egg membrane during 
fertilization as it contains  a chemical  called sperm  lysine. In the middle  piece, mitochondria 
and two centrioles  are present. Tail helps in movement  of sperm. Some yellowish finger like 
outgrowths are found attached nearer to the anterior region of each testis which are fat-bodies, 
used up at the time of hibernation.

b) Vasa efferentia: 
There are 10-12 very fine  tubes emerging  from  the testis  to  the kidney. The vasa efferentia 
traverse through the mesorchium and open into Bidder’s canal along the inner margin of each

Each kidney gives rise to a white muscular  tube called urinogenital  duct or ureter at its outer

2. FEMALE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM 
1. Ovaries: 
They are paired, irregular  shaped,  blackish  white, lobulated  structures,  each attached  to  the 
dorsal wall of abdomen, and kidney with the help of thin membrane known as mesovarium. 
Ovaries  become enlarged  during  breeding  season and female’s  belly become  bulging.   The 
ovaries have no internal connections with the kidneys. During the breeding season, the wall of 
the ovarian follicles ruptures to release the ova into the coelom which is called ovulation. 


 
 
Histologically,  ovary is a thin walled sac that contains many lobules. Each lobule consists  of 
outer layer of theca externa and inner layer of theca interna with connective tissue in between. 
Connective  tissue  contains  blood  vessels,  nerves  etc. Theca  externa  is internally lined  by 
germinal epithelial cells at certain places which undergo oogenesis to form ovum. The ovum is 
surrounded by single layer of follicular cells, which in turn enveloped by the extension of theca 
interna.  
Each  unfertilized  ovum  is  blackish  white  large  cell  and  is  surrounded  by  vitelline or  egg 
membrane. Upper pole of ovum is called  animal  pole and lower pole is called vegetal  pole. 
Ovum is  furthermore surrounded  by  albumin  which protects the  egg from  desiccation  and 
external infections. Egg of frog is mesolecithal  i.e. containing  moderate  amount of yolk and 
telolecithal  i.e. yolk concentrated towards vegetal pole.

These are paired, white, long, much convoluted and internally glandular and ciliated tubes lying
