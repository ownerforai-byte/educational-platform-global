4.1.1 CONCEPT OF ECOLOGY 
All the things of the world are classified  into two major groups namely living and non-living 
things. The living things includes all types of life and is known as biotic component while non-
living things includes soil, water, air, light, etc. and is known as abiotic components.  
The inter-relationship  between biotic and abiotic components as well as the relationship among 
the individuals of the biotic component is called ecology.  
The word ecology (Greek; oikos-household, logos-study)  was coined by Hanns Reiter (1885). 
Literally ecology means the study of organism at home. The ‘home’ includes the entire physical 
environment as well as other organisms that are there. Thus, ecology is the branch of Biology 
that studies the interrelationships  between organisms and their environment.  
Ecology is divided into two groups: 
1. Autoecology: It is a study of relationship  between individual species or its population 
and its environment.  
 
2. Synecology:  It is a study of relationship  between the individual of different species  or 
its  population  and  its   environment.  It  is   concerned  with  the  structure,  nature, 
development and causes of distribution of population of different species.

factors or environmental factors. These factors either singly or in combination  influence or 
determine the presence or absence, vigour or weakness and relative success or failure of various 
plant communities  in a particular  habitat. Ecological factors are classified into two groups:

slope,  steepness  etc.  The  variation  in  these  factors  causes  the  variation  in  climatic

c. Edaphic factors:  
It concerned with soil factors. It includes texture, organism, temperature, pH, air, water 
etc of soil.  
 
2. Biotic factors:  
The living things of the environment are known as biotic factors. The biotic factor includes all 
types  of  life  forms  and  categorized  in  three  groups  on  the  basis  of  food  relationship  i.e. 
producer, consumer and decomposer.  
a. Producers – The  green  plant  in  the  ecosystem  are  called  producers.  In  a  terrestrial 
ecosystem,  main   producers  are  herbaceous   and  woody  plants  while   in  aquatic 


 
 
ecosystem the main producers are various species like phytoplankton, algae and higher 
plants.  
b. Consumers – All animals  are called as consumers  because they depend on plants for 
their food. They are heterotrophs. They are categorized into following groups: 
i. Primary  consumer: These  organisms  fully depend upon the producer for food. e.g. 
rat, rabbit, dear etc.  
ii. Secondary consumer: These organisms depend upon the primary consumer for food. 
e.g. insects, frog, small fishes etc.  
iii. Tertiary consumer: These organisms depend upon the secondary consumer for food. 
e.g. tiger, lion, snake, big fish etc.  
c. Decomposers – They obtain their food from dead and decaying body of organism. They 
convert the complex organic matter of dead and decaying matter into simple  inorganic 
compounds. It includes bacteria and fungi.  
4.1.3 SPECIES INTERACTION 
The living organisms of an ecosystem form a biotic community. All the organism of a particular 
habitat influences each other directly or indirectly. Interaction is a necessity for any living being 
to survive. They can be either of the same species (intraspecific  interactions),  or of different 
species (interspecific  interactions).  
1. Positive Interaction 
It is the type of interaction in which one or both partners are benefited and no one is harmed.  
a. Mutualism (Symbiosis): It is the biotic interaction between two organisms of different 
species  where both partners are benefited. The relationship is obligatory (i.e.  they can 
not  survive   independently).  Example: Rhizobium bacteria  and  leguminous   plant, 
mycorrhiza  etc.  
b. Commensalism:  It is the biotic interaction between two organisms  of different species 
where  one partner  is  benefitted and other is  neither  benefited nor  harmed.  Example: 
epiphytes, Sucker fish and shark etc.  
c. Protocooperation:  It  is  the  biotic  interaction  between  two  organisms  of  different 
species  where  both  partners  are  benefited  but  the  relationship  is  not  obligatory. 
Example: Flower and insect, birds and cattle etc.  
d. Colonization: It is the grouping of individuals of same species for better protection and 
food. Example: Volvox, Nostoc etc.  
e. Social  Organization:  It is  the grouping  of individuals of same  species  that show the 
division of labour. Example: honey bee, wasp, ants etc.  
2. Negative interaction:  
It is the type of interaction in which one partner is benefited and other is harmed.  
a. Parasitism:  It is the interaction in which one organism,  the parasite is benefited while 
the  other  organism,  the  host,  is  harmed.  Example:  mosquito  and  human,  leech  and 
cattle, etc.  


 
 
b. Predation: It is the interaction in which one organism kills and feeds on the other. Here 
one partner is benefited and other is harmed. Example:  tiger and deer, frog and insect 
etc.  
c. Competition: It is the interaction between similar  or different species for food, support, 
shelter,  space  etc. Here  either  of the organisms  is  harmed. Example:  Lichens,  herbs, 
birds etc.  
 
4.1.4 CONCEPT OF ECOSYSTEM 
In nature an organism does not exist in isolation,  it is associated with organisms of its 
own and of different  kinds as well as with the physical environment. If the organism is plant, 
it depends for growth on the aerial environment  for light, temperature and carbon dioxide, on 
the soil environment for inorganic nutrients and water. If it is an animal it depends on the plants 
for food and shelter. If it is a microorganism  it depend on the dead organic matter from plants 
and animals  for its food, and releases  the nutrients  tied in the organic matter  for the reuse of 
plants. Thus, there are continuous interactions among the organisms and between the organisms 
and the habitat  resulting  into an integrated,  functional  unit. This functional  unit is known as 
ecosystem.  It is the structural  and functional  unit of ecology. Thus, ecosystem is defined as 
‘the structural  and functional  unit of ecology where the living organisms  interact  with 
each other and the  surrounding environment’.  The term  ecosystem  was coined by A.G. 
Tansley (1935). Ecosystem varies greatly in size from a small pond to a large forest or a sea. 
The  ecosystem  is  divided  into  two  basic  categories:  Aquatic  Ecosystem  and  Terrestrial 
Ecosystem.  Forest,  grassland  etc.  are  terrestrial  ecosystem  while  pond, lake,  river  etc.  are 
aquatic ecosystem.  
STRUCTURAL AND FUNCTIONAL ASPECT OF POND ECOSYSTEM 
Ponds are fresh water ecosystem. It is a small reservoir which may or may not dry up during 
dry season. It is self-sustaining  and self-regulating.  
Pond ecosystem consists of structural and functional components.  
Structural components 
Structural  components  are  categorized  into  two main  components:  Abiotic  component  and 
Biotic component. 
A. Abiotic component: Abiotic components of pond ecosystem are non-living things of pond 
and consist of water, dissolved minerals, oxygen, carbon dioxide, solar energy etc.  
B. Biotic component: Biotic components of pond ecosystem are living beings of pond and are 
categorized into following groups: 
1. Producers:  The main  producers in pond ecosystem are algae and other aquatic plants. 
The green plants can prepare their own food by the process of photosynthesis. In pond 
ecosystem, the producers are phytoplanktons which are minute microscopic  floating or 
suspended plants  like Volvox,  Spirogyra,  Anabaena,  Ulothrix, etc. and larger  plants 
known as  macrophytes  which  may  be  floating, suspended or  submerged  like Chara, 
Hydrilla, Marsilea,  Eichornia, Lemna etc.  


 
 
2. Consumers: The consumers  in pond ecosystem are  categorized as primary,  secondary 
and tertiary consumers. The primary consumers like tadpole, small fishes, etc. consume 
green  plants  and  algae  as  their  food.  They  are  herbivorous  of  pond  ecosystem. 
Secondary consumers  like  frog, big  fishes, crabs  etc. consume  primary  consumers  as 
their  food.  These  secondary  consumers  are  consumed by  tertiary  consumers.  The 
tertiary consumers  are water birds, snake, crocodile etc.  
3. Decomposers: When  aquatic  plants  and animals  die, a  large  number  of bacteria  and 
fungi attack their dead bodies and convert the complex organic substances into simpler 
inorganic compounds and elements. These  micro-organisms  are called decomposers.  
Functional components

The aquatic  green plants of the pond ecosystem  capture the solar  energy and convert it into 
chemical energy of food and store in their body through photosynthesis. This process is called

ecosystem derive the energy by consuming plants or plants parts. These primary consumers are

ecosystem  contains  several  food  chains  of  each  of the  above types  which  are  inter-linked 
forming a food web.

photosynthesis,  move from organism to organism through food chain and ultimately  from all 
trophic  levels,  the  decomposers  break  down the  complex  organic  compounds  and  release 
nutrients  back  to  atmosphere,  water  and  soil  again  in  a  cyclic  manner.  This  is  known  as 
biogeochemical cycling.

4.1.5 TROPHIC LEVEL 
The trophic  level of an ecosystem refers  to the position  that an organism  occupies in a food 
chain or food web. It indicates  the organism’s position  in the transfer of energy and nutrients 
within  the  ecosystem.  All  green plants  and photosynthetic  bacteria prepare  their  own food 
through  photosynthesis.  So,  producers  belong  to  first  tropic  level.  Similarly,  primary 
consumers, secondary consumers and tertiary consumers form second, third and fourth trophic 
level respectively.

4.1.7 ECOLOGICAL PYRAMID 
At each step in the food chain a considerable fraction of the potential energy is lost as heat. As 
a result, organisms in each trophic level pass on lesser energy to the next trophic level than they 
actually receive. This limits  the number of steps in any food chain. Longer the food chain the 
lesser energy is available for final members. Because of this tapering off of available energy in 
the  food  chain  a  pyramid  is  formed  is  known  as  ecological  pyramid.  It  represents  the 
relationship  of organisms of different trophic levels. This relationship is expressed in terms of 
number, biomass or energy. Ecological pyramid is defined as the graphical representation 
of different  parameters like number, biomass or energy at different  trophic level in the 
form of pyramid. In ecological pyramid, producer level forms the base and successive levels 
make up the apex. 
 
There are three types of ecological pyramid in an ecosystem:  
1. Pyramid of number 
2. Pyramid of biomass 
3. Pyramid of energy 
 
1. Pyramid of number  
 It shows the relationship  between producers,  herbivores  and carnivores  at successive 
trophic  levels  in  terms  of their  number. The graphical  representation  of  the number  of 
organisms at different tropic level in an ecosystem is called pyramid of number.  


 
 
 
Figure: 4.1.7 Pyramid of Number in forest and pond ecosystem 
 
The number of producers are found in large number than the consumers.  So, the pyramid  of 
numbers is usually upright except for some situations like that of the detritus food chain, where 
many organisms feed on one dead plant or animal.

 The graphical representation of the biomass of organisms at different tropic levels 
in an ecosystem is called pyramid of biomass. In grassland and forest ecosystem the biomass 
of producers is maximum and decreases gradually in successive trophic level. So, the pyramid

so biomass is least. The biomass of consumers increases gradually in successive trophic level.

4.1.10 CONCEPT OF SUCCESSION 
Succession is like a natural makeover  that happens in nature over time.  Imagine you have a 
bare plot of land. At first, it might seem empty, but over time, different plants and animals come 
in and make it their  home. This process of change from bare land to a thriving  ecosystem is 
called succession. It helps the ecosystems grow and develop over time.  
 


 
 
Succession is defined as ‘The gradual replacement of one type of biological community by 
the other community in a particular area over a period of time leading to the establishment 
of a stable and mature community, known as the climax community.’  The first  biological 
community  that  develops  in  a  bare  area  is  called pioneer  community while  the  final 
community which is more or less stable is called climax community.  
 
Succession happens because nature is always changing. Succession generally occurs due to the 
development  of bare area  (by erosion,  deforestation,  fire,  volcanic eruption  etc.),  biological 
interactions  (competition and reaction, predation, aggregation, etc.) and climate change.  
 
Types of Succession 
There are two main types of succession: 
1. Primary  Succession:  A  succession  that occurs  in  an  area  where  there  was  no life 
before, such as bare rock, volcanic lava, etc. The pioneer species include is lichens and 
mosses.  
2. Secondary Succession:  A succession  that occurs  in an area  where soil  already exists 
or the area where there is previous life but has been disturbed by landslide, fire etc. The 
pioneer species of the succession is either mosses or grasses.  
 
Process of Succession 
The various steps involved in succession are given below:  
1. Nudation: It is the process  of formation of bare area by natural causes (like landslide, 
volcanic  eruption, flood etc.) or human  causes (like  deforestation, development work, 
forest fire, etc.).  
2. Invasion:  It is  the  successful  establishment  of  a  species  in  a  bare  area.  It includes 
migration  or dispersal  of seeds, germination  and formation of adult plants and finally 
increases  in number of individuals.  
3. Competition   and  Coaction:   The   increase   in  number   of  individuals   starts  the 
competition  for resources  like  sunlight,  water and nutrition. Individuals of  a  species 
affect each other’s life in various ways is called coaction.  
4. Reaction:  It  is  the  modification  of  environment  through  the  influence  of  living 
organisms. Due to reaction, environmental condition changes so existing community is 
replaced by another new community. The  replacement  process is  continuous until  the 
development of climax community.  
5. Stabilization:   It  is   the  stage  in  which   a  community   becomes   stable  for  that 
environmental condition for longer time.

4.2.1 CONCEPT OF ADAPTATION 
There  are  millions  of  species  on  Earth.  They  can  be  found  in  a  wide  range  of  natural 
environments.  The environment  is  constantly  changing. This changing  environment  ensures 
that the species that are capable of adapting survive and an advantage over organisms that do 
not  adapt.  The competition  caused  by  adaptations  is  critical  to  the  evolution  process.  The 
changes in the structure of organisms aid in their survival in an environment.  
 
Ecological adaptation refers to the process by which organisms adjust to their environment 
in order to survive and reproduce successfully.  This  adaptation  can occur through  various 
mechanisms,  including  physiological,  behavioral,  and morphological  changes. The ultimate 
aim of all adaptations  is to make the individual  fit to obtain food and space for its  survival, 
opportunities for its reproduction and rearing of young ones.  
 
Plant  life,  like animals,  is dependent  on a variety  of basic survival  needs. Light, air, water, 
nutrients,  soil  and a proper climate  are all  required  for  growth. However, not every  habitat 
provides  the  necessary  needs. As a  result,  to  live  in  such  conditions,  plants  have evolved 
specific physiological, behavioral, and morphological adaptation.

4.2.2 HYDROPHYTES 
Plants which grow in wet places or grow either partially or completely submerged in water are 
commonly referred to as hydrophytes or aquatic plants. Examples: Hydrilla, Pistia, Eichornia, 
Lemna, etc.  
     
Hydrilla   Pistia     Eichornia 
Fig: 4.2.1 Hydrophytes 
 
According to their distribution in a fresh water body, there are five categories of 
hydrophytes:  
a. Free-floating  
These  are  the aquatic  plant that float freely on water surface. They  remain  in  contact 
with water and air, nut not soil. Example: Pistia,  Eichornia, Lemna, etc.  
 
b. Rooted with floating leaves 
These are the aquatic plants whose roots are fixed in mud but leaves have long petioles 
which keep floating on the water surface. Rest of the plant body is submerged in water. 
Example: Marsilea,  Trapa, etc.  
 
c. Submerged floating 
These are the aquatic plants that remain in contact only with water. They are not rooted 
in the mud and being completely submerged in water. Example: Najas, Ceratophyllum, 
etc.  
 
d. Rooted submerged 
These  are  the hydrophytes that remain  completely  submerged  in water, but rooted in 
the soil. Example: Hydrilla, Chara, etc.  
 
e. Rooted emergent 
These hydrophytes grow in shallow waters and require excess of water but their shoots 
are  partly  or  completely  exposed to air.  The  root system  is  completely  under  water, 
fixed in soil. Example: Sagittaria,  Ranunculus, Typha, etc.  
 
Adaptation of hydrophytes 
Different parts of various hydrophytes shows following specialized features:  
a. Roots: Roots may  be completely  absent or are poorly  developed. Root hairs  and root 
caps  are usually  absent. However, in  free-floating plants the root hairs  and root caps 


 
 
develop prominently.  In Eichornia, root caps are replaced by ‘root pockets’. Roots of 
hydrophytes are generally fibrous, short, unbranched or poorly branched.  
b. Stems:  In free-floating  forms,  stem  is  slender,  horizontally  floating,  short, thick  and 
spongy, while in submerged, stem is long, slender, spongy and flexible.  
c. Leaves: The leaves are generally  thin, small  and translucent in submerged hydrophytes 
while large, flat and with waxy coat on entire surface in floating hydrophytes.  
d. Flowers and reproduction: The  flowers are not common  in submerged form. They  are 
usually  produced on  the  surface  of water  or  above  it.  Pollination  and dispersal  are 
performed by water, insects and birds. Vegetative reproduction is common.  
e. Anatomical Adaptation: Aerencyma  in submerged leaves and stem is well developed. 
Mechanical  tissue and vascular  tissues are  poorly  developed. The cuticle  is  absent or 
poorly developed. Stomata are completely absent in submerged leaves while present on 
the upper surface of floating forms.  
f. Physiological  adaptation: The aquatic plants exhibit a low compensation point and low 
osmotic  concentration  of  cell  sap.  Nutrients  are  absorbed  by  the  submerged  plants 
through the general  plant surface. The  gases  are exchange  from the water though the 
surface cells  (epidermis).  Transpiration  is  absent in submerged hydrophytes however, 
emergent and free-floating plants have excessive rate of transpiration.  
 
4.2.3 XEROPHYTES 
Xerophytes are plants that have adapted to survive in dry or arid environments  where water 
availability  is limited.  Xerophytes are characteristic  plants of desert and semi-desert  regions, 
yet they  can grow in mesophytic  conditions  where water available  is in  sufficient  quantity. 
Example: Opuntia, Aloe, Begonia, Agave, etc.  
 
           
Opuntia     Aloe 
Fig: 4.2.3 Xerophytic plant 
 
According to their adaptations to arid or dry environments, there are three categories of 
xerophytes:  
a. Ephemerals 
They are xerophytic plants that complete their life cycle in short period of time when 
sufficient moisture  is  available.  They  are  also  called ‘drought evaders’  or  ‘drough 
escapers’.  Since  these plants avoid dry seasons.  Example: Solanum  xanthocarpum, 
Tephrosia purpurea, etc.  
  


 
 
b. Succulent 
They  have specialized  tissues  for water storage that can store large  amounts of water 
during  periods  of  rainfall  or  high  humidity.  In  these  plants,  the  succulence,  i.e. 
development  of water storing  parenchymatous  tissues  may  occur  in  roots,  stems  or 
leaves.  The  rest  of  the plant  body  other  than succulent  organ  is  generally  reduced. 
Example: Aloe, Asparagus, Begonia, Agave, etc.  
c. True xerophytes or drought resistants 
The plants which have the ability to maintain growth under critical dry conditions and 
high  temperature  are  true xerophytes.  The  plants  are  woody trees,  shrubs  or  herbs. 
Example: Acacia nelotica, Nerium, etc.  
 
Adaptation of Xerophytes: 
The xerophytic plants show following adaptations:  
a. Root: They  have  well  developed root  system  that can  penetrate deep into the soil  to 
access  moisture.  Root hairs  are  concentrated in  abundance near  the tips  of  growing 
rootlets.  
b. Leaves: The  leaves, if present, are modified to minimize  the rate of transpiration. The 
modification in leaves may vary from species to species.  There are small sized, rolling 
and folding of leaf blades or  delicate leaves  which  are  shed under conditions of less 
supply  of water. The  leaves  surfaces  are  generally  shining  and glazed to reflect light 
and heat.  
c. Stems:  The  stem  of  some  xerophytes  are  hard  and woody  while  some  plants  have 
succulent type of stem. In some it may modified into thorns.  
d. Flower and reproduction: The flower is generally  reduced. It generally develop during 
high moisture  availability,  such as after rainfall.  In some xerophytes the flowers have 
fleshy  structure  that  can  store  water.  Pollination  takes  place  by  wind  or  gravity. 
Vegetative propagation is more common.  
e. Anatomical adaptation: The leaves have heary cuticular and epidermal layer and waxy 
coating  on  its  surface  to  reduce  the  transpiration.  The  stomata  are  sunken  type. 
Conducting and mechanical tissues are well developed.

Ozone molecules are constantly formed and destroyed at any given time. Ozone layer depletion 
is the thinning of ozone layer of earth’s atmosphere.  
 
The main cause of ozone layer depletion is release of certain human made chemicals which are 
known as ozone layer depleting substances  (ODS). This includes  chlorofluoro  carbon (CFC) 
which  is  used  in  refrigerator,  air  conditioner  and  aerosol  propellants.  Halons  used  in  fire

bromides and chlorides are released. Those compounds act as a catalyst in a chain reaction that 
destroys ozone. 
A single chlorine atom can destroy over 100,000 ozone molecules before it is removed from the 
stratosphere.

4. It can influence atmospheric circulation pattern and contribute to climate change.

burdens on societies and nations

Ozone layer depletion can be controlled by following ways: 
1. By reducing the use of CFCs. 
2. By increasing the use of alternative source of energy instead of fossil fuels.

Causes  
Air emission:  The main cause of acid rain is the emission  of gases containing  sulfur dioxide

) and nitrogen oxides (NO

). These gases are emitted  by burning of fossil  fuel like coal 
and oil in power plants, factories and vehicles. These gases travel in atmosphere and react with

Acid rain has several adverse effects on  
1. Surface water: lower pH of surface water can damage fishes and other aquatic life. 
2. Soils:  Soil  chemistry is  altered  by acid  rain,  resulting  to  the death  of  existing 
microorganisms.  This  changes the chemical and biological  properties of soil. 
3. Forest and Plants: Acid rain  can damage the leaves  of plants and hinders growth. It 
can degrade the essential nutrients in soil making plants difficult to survive.  
4. Human health effects: Exposure of acid rain to human can cause irritation to skin and 
eyes, dental damage and erosion, cause lung disease like asthma and bronchitis etc. 
5. Others: Acid rain  can  damage quality of monuments, building,  statues etc. specially

renewable source of energy helps in achieving less emission. 
2. Liming lakes and ponds: Acidity of ponds and lakes can be neutralized by treating with 
lime or lime stone to minimize  the impact on aquatic life after acid rain.  
3. Awareness: Being responsible citizen we should be aware of harmful effect of acid rain 
and unethical wastes containing oxides of nitrogen and sulfur from industries. 
 
4.3.4 BIOLOGICAL INVASION  
The process of introduction, establishment  and spread of species to a new environment where 
it is not native is called biological invasion. Those organisms which live naturally in a particular 
area and have been present there for long time playing important  role in local ecosystem  are 
known as native species. Those organisms   which have been introduced to new environment 
are called exotic or alien species. Exotic species may or may not cause the problems to local 
ecosystem. Those exotic species that start to spread causing problems for the local environment 
are known as Invasive alien species.  
 
Invasive  species possess  certain better  features  which are superior to native  species become 
more competent in that environment. Some feature are given below 
1. Fast growth and reproduction: Invasive species often have high reproductive rate and 
allows them to colonize rapidly in new areas. 
2. Adaptive  to  wide  range  of  environmental  conditions:  Invasive  species  often  can 
tolerate  and sustain  in  various  environment.  That  allows  them  to spread  in  various 
environment that may not be suitable to other native species. 
3. Few natural predators and diseases: Invasive species often lack predators and diseases 
in the new environment that allows them to spread unchecked. 
4. Aggressive competitors: Invasive species are aggressive competitors for resources like 
food, water and space. This can reduce population of native species. 
5. Efficient dispersal:  Invasive  species  maintain efficient mechanism  to  disperse  their 
propagules to new environment. For example they produce seeds with wings or hooks 
which can be easily  carried out by wind or animals. 
 


 
 
The main reason behind the spread of invasive species is believed to be human activity often 
unintentional. Some key causes are  
1. Globalization  and  increase   trade:  With  increase  in  trade  and  travel  in  the 
interconnected world, opportunities of movement species  or its propagules  have 
been increased. For example this can occur to ballistic  water, contaminated goods, 
vehicles etc.  
2. Accidental  release:  Sometimes  due  to  careless  or  lack  of  awareness  invasive 
species  are  released  in  the new  environment.  For  example  pet  trade, escape  of 
animals  from farm, introducing new plants for cultivation etc.  
3. Land use  change:  Human  activities  that modify natural  habitat  can  make  them 
more susceptible to invasion.  
4. Climate change:  Change in climatic  condition of the area  can allow new species 
to enter that can diestrum established ecosystem and make vulnerable to invasion 
 
Impact of invasive species 
Although introduction of invasive species increases the number of species in that area instantly 
but causes many negative impacts in that area. Some of the negative impacts are as follows: 
1. Disturb  ecosystem:  Invasive  species  can  disturb  balance  in  existing  ecosystem  by 
many ways. They may result in loss of native species, can become new predator in the 
ecosystem,  may  carry  new  diseases,  can  alter  microbial  community  and  physical 
structure of the habitat which becomes unsuitable to native species. 
2. Economic damage: They have significant negative impact on economy. They reduce 
agriculture  yield,  investment  on  controlling   invasive  species   increase  the  cost, 
decrease scenery of lakes and ponds, damage infrastructures etc. 
3. Health:  Invasive  species  can  introduce,  transmit  and  cause  new  diseases.  Some 
invasive species  can cause allergic  reactions. 
4. Loss of biodiversity:  The  overall  impact of the consequences  is loss  of biodiversity. 
The  loss  of biodiversity  have severe  impact on  disturbing natural  process  that keep 
ecosystem healthy and functioning.  
 
Management of invasive species  
1. Prevention:  The  best  way  to  deal  with  invasive  species  is  to  prevent  them  to 
introduce  in  the  new  environment.  This  can  done  by  proper  inspecting  cargo, 
regulating import of live plants and animals etc.  
2. Early  detection  and  rapid  control:  Early  detection  may  make  it  possible  to 
eradicate it completely before proper establishment. 
3. Control measures 
a) Mechanical  control: This  includes physically  removal  of invasive species  
b) Chemical control: These include using herbicides or pesticides to kill invasive 
species. However, this  method should be  used cautiously  to avoid  harming 
native species and environment. 
c) Biological  control:  This includes  introduction  of  natural  enemies  of  the 
invasive species  such as parasite or predators.   


 
 
4. Public education and awareness: Educating and awaking public may help in early 
detection and prevention  of spread of invasive  species.  This  also  include  avoid 
introducing  invasive   species   and  reporting   sighting  of  invasive   species   to 
authorities.
