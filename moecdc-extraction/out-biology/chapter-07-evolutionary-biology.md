7.1.1 ORIGIN OFEARTH AND PRIMITIVE ATMOSPHERE 
Scientists  believe  that our  planet,  Earth, came  into existence  approximately  four and a  half 
billion  years ago. During its formation, the entire solar system emerged from the condensation 
of  cosmic  dust  and  gases  (According  to Dust  and  cloud  hypothesis).  At  its  birth,  it  was 
incredibly  hot that everything  existed as  gases,  making  it impossible  for any  form of life  to 
exist. 
Due to the extreme  temperature, elements existed as individual atoms. Intense heat broke  the 
bonds apart quickly and atoms couldn't bond together. These elements included carbon, silicon, 
oxygen, sulphur, chlorine,  phosphorus, sodium, calcium, potassium, and iron. 
Over time,  Earth gradually  cooled down, taking  about 100 million  years  to transition  into a 
molten core. During this cooling process, the elements began to separate based on their density, 
forming distinct layers within the Earth: 
i. The heaviest elements, like  iron (Fe), nickel (Ni), zinc (Zn), and sodium (Na), sank to 
the very centre, forming the Earth's core. 
ii. Lighter elements, such as aluminium  (Al), silicon (Si), phosphorus (P), and sulphur (S), 
flowed to create the mantle and crust of Earth. 
iii. The  lightest elements,  like  helium  (He), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), and 
carbon (C), remained closer  to the surface forming atmosphere. 
 
7.1.2 ORIGIN OF LIFE 
The  origin  and history  of life  on  Earth have  led to various  theories  over  time.  Some  of the 
theories are discussed below: 
1. Theory  of  special  creation: It  suggests  that  all  living  beings,  including  plants  and 
animals,  were created by a supernatural power or God. 
2. Cosmozoic theory (Interplanetary/Panspermia):  The theory was proposed by Richter 
(1865)  and it suggests that life  might  have  arrived  on the Earth  via meteorites from 
another planet in the form of spores, also known as theory of Panspermia.

This  is  the  modern  and widely  accepted  explanation  for  how  life  began.  Russian  scientist 
Alexander Ivanovich Oparin proposed it in 1923. It was further supported by J. B. S. Haldane 
in  1929.  They  believed  that life  spontaneously  started from  nonliving  substances  in  Earth's 
ancient oceans around 3.3 billion years ago. 
This theory can be studied under following headings: 
A. Chemogeny 
B. Biogeny

B. BIOGENY 
This phase involves the formation of complex, self-reproducing biological  molecules  from 
complex organic molecules  in three distinct phases. 
1.Formation  of  Nucleic  Acids:  It  is  believed  that  sugars,  phosphates,  purines,  and 
pyrimidines  combined to create nucleotides. These nucleotides then joined together to 
form  nucleic  acids  like  DNA and RNA. This  event  marked  a significant  step in  the 
origin of life since nucleic acids could replicate themselves, indicating the beginning of 
life. 
2.Formation  of  Coacervates:  Nucleic  acids  and  other  macromolecules  bounded by 
water in  the primitive  ocean formed colloidal  structures known as coacervates.  These 
coacervates consisted of nucleic acids and enzymatic proteins, called nucleo-proteins, 
with the ability to self-duplicate. They acted as intermediaries  between molecules  and 
living organisms. 
3.Formation of Primary Organisms:  Coacervates absorbed organic materials  from the 
oceanic soup and started replicating. They grew in size and number, creating anaerobic 
chemoheterotrophs, proto-cells, or the first cells. Oparin referred to them as protobionts 
or ebionts.  These  first cells  eventually  gave rise  to prokaryotic  cells  such as bacteria, 
cyanobacteria, and blue-green algae.

7.1.5 Miller-Urey EXPERIMENT 
In  1953, Stanley  L.  Miller and  Professor Harold  C.  Urey conducted  an  experiment  that 
supported Alexander  Oparin's  biochemical  concept  for  the  origin  of  life.  They  recreated 
conditions resembling  early Earth in their laboratory. 


 
 
 
Figure: 7.1.5 Miller-Urey Experiment 
Experiment 
i. The apparatus was constructed using glass tubes and a flask, as shown in the figure. The 
apparatus consisted of two glass chambers: a water chamber and a gas chamber. 
ii. The water in the flask was heated to generate steam, facilitating the circulation of water 
vapour within the chambers. 
iii. The  gas  chamber  contained a mixture  of gases—hydrogen, methane, and ammonia  in 
the proportion of 2:2:1. 
iv. Energy was introduced into the closed chamber through heating, electric discharge from 
electrodes, and UV radiation. 
v. A condenser was used to cool the resulting mixture. The entire setup was operational for 
a week. 
 
Observation 
The products condensed and gathered within a designated chamber. 
Result 
The  obtained products  contained  numerous  organic  compounds  like  amino  acids  (glycine, 
alanine, aspartic acid, etc.), aldehydes, simple sugars, ribose, etc. 
Conclusion 
The  chemical  reactions  utilized the  energy  from  electric  sparks  and led to  the formation  of 
amino acids and other organic compounds. This supports Oparin’s idea about the origin of life.

7.2.1 CONCEPT OF ORGANIC EVOLUTIONf.2 Evidences of Evolution 
Evolution (L. evolvere = unrolling/unfolding)  is a gradual and orderly change from one state 
to another. Charles Darwin defined it as "descent with modification". It's important to note that 
evolution isn't about changes happening to one individual during its lifetime;  rather, it's about 
how entire groups of organisms change over many generations. 
Simple  organisms  in the past evolved to present-day complex  organisms  adapting to Earth's 
changing  environment. Evolution  can also be defined as  a slow, continuous, and irreversible 
journey  from  primitive  life  forms  to  today's  advanced  organisms.  The  father  of  organic 
evolution is Charles Darwin. The term ‘evolution’ was coined by Herbert spencer. 
PATTERN OF EVOLUTION 
a) Microevolution involves  small  scale  changes  in  gene frequencies  in a population  by 
factors like natural selection, genetic drift, etc. For example, Peppered moths evolving 
darker coloration due to pollution. 
b) Macroevolution involves  large  evolutionary  patterns  leading  the  formation  of new 
species  by phenomena  like  adaptive radiation, speciation,  etc. For example,  Darwin's 
finches evolving different beak shapes for varied diets. 
c) Progressive  evolution shows  an  evolutionary  trend  in  organisms  from  simple  to 
complex. For example,  Whales evolving from land-dwelling mammals. 
d) Retrogressive  evolution involves  a  reversal  evolutionary  trend  in  organisms  from 
complex  to simple.  For instance, evolution  of small  sized reptiles  from giant reptiles. 
For example, Transition  from giant dinosaurs to small lizards. 
e) Convergent  evolution involves  evolution  of  similar  characters  in  organisms  from 
different  ancestors.  For  example,  the  development  of  fusiform  type  of  body  by 
Palaemon (arthropod), shark (fish), and a dolphin (mammal). 
f) Divergent  evolution occurs  when  a  common  ancestor  gets  diversified  to  different 
forms.  It  is  also  known  as  adaptive  radiation.  For  example,  African cichlid fish 
diversifying jaw shapes due to varied diets. 
g) Parallel  evolution refers to the process  in  which two related species,  independently, 
evolve  similar  characters  over  the long  time  after they  diverged from  their  common 
ancestor.  For  example,  Marsupial  mammals   in  Australia  and  placental  mammals 
evolved independently but in parallel  lines  due to adaptation to their similar  ways of 
life.

7.2.2 EVIDENCES OF ORGANIC EVOLUTION 
Evidence  supporting  the  theory  of  organic  evolution  comes  from  diverse  fields  such  as 
morphology,  paleontology,  geology,  embryology,  biogeography,  biochemistry,  and genetics. 
Collectively, these lines of evidence offer a compelling  narrative of the interconnectedness and 
gradual change within the biological  world: 
i. Evidences from morphology, comparative anatomy and vestigial organs 
ii. Evidences from palaeontology 
iii. Evidences from embryology 
iv. Evidences from biochemistry and comparative physiology 
 
1. EVIDENCES FROM MORPHOLOGY, COMPARATIVE ANATOMY AND 
VESTIGIAL ORGANS: 
A  comparative  analysis  of  morphological  and  anatomical  structures  in  different groups  of 
animals  provides important evidence in favour of organic evolution. 
Diverse   animal   groups   present   distinct  categories   of  organs—homologous,   analogous, 
vestigial, and atavism providing evidential support for evolutionary processes. 
 
a. Homologous  Organs: Homologous  organs  are  the organs  which have similar  origin 
and design but different functions. For example, the forelimbs of a whale (flippers), bat 
(wings),  bird (wings),  horse,  and man.  Despite  their different functions—swimming, 
flying, running, leaping, and grasping—the forelimbs share a common structure, known 
as  the pentadactyl pattern, featuring similar  bones  like  humerus,  radio-ulna,  carpals, 
metacarpals,  and phalanges.  Additionally, the arrangement  of muscles,  heart, nerves, 
and  blood  vessels  follows  a  similar  pattern.  The  presence  of  homologous  organs 
suggests a shared evolutionary ancestry. 
 
Figure: 7.2.1 Homologous organs 
b. Analogous  Organs: Analogous  organs,   despite  different  origins,   serve   similar 
functions.  For  example,  the  wings  of  a  butterfly  and  bird. Wings  of  butterfly  are 
membranous but wings  of birds  have feathers on arm  bones.  Despite these structural 
differences, both facilitate flight. Similarly,  fins of fish and flippers of whales; sting of 
honey  bees  and  sting  of  scorpions   are  analogous  organs.  This   highlights   their 
adaptability, even with different evolutionary backgrounds.

c. Vestigial Organs: Vestigial organs, non-functional and practically useless, were likely

The  appendix,  once  a  functional  caecum  aiding  in  cellulose   digestion,  indicates

of  organic   evolution,   implying   a  transformation   from  herbivorous   ancestors   to

2. EVIDENCES FROM PALAEONTOLOGY: 
Palaeontology,  the  study  of  fossils,  focuses  on  the  examination  of  fossils,  which  are  the 
remains,  impressions,  or entire organisms  of plants and animals  from past geological  periods. 
These artifacts provide direct evidence of evolution. Fossilization is the process through which 
fossils  are  formed.  The  fossil  record  indicates  a  gradual  evolution  from  simpler  to  more 
complex forms. 
Branches of Paleontology: 
Palaeontology can be divided into two primary branches: 
i. Palaeozoology: The study of animal fossils. 
ii. Palaeobotany: The study of plant fossils. 
 
Do You Know? 
The pioneer of palaeontology was Leonard da Vinci, but modern palaeontology was formally 
established by George Cuvier. 
 
Fossilization  Process: Fossilization  is the process by which the remains of organisms from the 
past become fossilized. It involves the burial of the entire organism or its parts in sand or mud. 
The ocean is an ideal environment for fossilization  due to the inhibitory effect of saltwater on 
decay. 
After an organism's  death, its soft tissues decompose, and the hard parts settle at the bottom, 
eventually  being  covered by sand. This  sedimentation continues  over time,  preserving  older 
fossils in deeper layers and more recent ones closer to the surface. 
Types of Fossils: 
Fossils are categorized based on the preserved parts of the organism: 


 
 
i. Intact Fossils:  Entire organisms  or parts preserved in original  condition, often in cold 
or dry regions. Example: wooly mammoth found in Siberia  
ii. Petrified Fossils: Preservation of hard parts like bones, teeth, and exoskeleton through 
mineralization. 
iii. Moulds/Casts Fossils: Impressions  left behind by organisms  as surrounding materials 
harden, creating exact replicas. Example: Trilobite  fossils 
iv. Compression  Fossils: Common  in  plant  fossils,  where  a  thin  carbon  film  remains, 
outlining the organism. 
v. Imprints or Impressions: Fossils  in the form of footprints, wing prints, skin prints, or 
impressions  of crawling creatures. Example: Dinosaur foot prints 
vi. Pseudofossils: Mineral  formations resembling  plant remains  but, are not true fossils. 
 
Significance of Paleontology: 
1. It helps in understanding the evolutionary history (phylogeny) of organisms. 
2. It provides the proof of existence of diverse groups of organisms at different time 
periods. 
3. While studying fossils from older to recent strata of rocks simple to complex forms of 
life are observed. 
4. Fossil records also indicate that complex life forms of today originated from simpler 
forms of the past.

3. EVIDENCES FROM EMBRYOLOGY 
Embryology  involves  studying the  embryonic  stages of animals  or  how animals  grow  from 
eggs to adults. This  field provides strong evidence supporting the theory of organic evolution, 
and here's how: 
a. Early Developmental  Similarities: In the beginning,  all  multicellular  animals  start as  a 
single  cell  known  as  a  zygote.  This  cell  progresses  into  morula,  blastula,  and gastrula 
stages, forming  three germinal  layers—ectoderm,  mesoderm,  and endoderm. These  early 
developmental stages share striking similarities  across various animals. 
b. Comparing Vertebrate Embryos: The embryos of fish, frogs, tortoises, pigeons, rabbits, 
and humans  resemble  each  other significantly  during  their  early  stages of development. 
Even organs like the kidney, heart, and arteries follow a similar  fundamental pattern in their 
development, indicating a common ancestry among vertebrates. 
c. Recapitulation  Theory  (Biogenetic  Law): According  to Ernst  Haeckel's  recapitulation 
theory, "every organism during its development repeats the evolutionary history of its race". 
Comparing  vertebrate  embryos  further emphasizes  this  idea. For  example,  a  tadpole  of 
frogs resembles  a fish-like  ancestor, and the larva  of Herdmania shows characteristics  of 
its chordate ancestry. These  traits include having  a notochord, a  well-developed nervous 
system,  and  a  tail.  However,  when Herdmania develops  into  adulthood,  it  loses  the 
notochord,  tail,  and  experiences  a  significant  reduction  in  its  nervous  system.  This 
transformation shows the larva's resemblance  to the ancestral characteristics.  
Thus,  Haeckel  concluded "Ontogeny  (developmental  history  of  an  organism)  recapitulates 
(repeats) its phylogeny (ancestral history)”. It means, an organism repeats its ancestral history 
during its development.

4. EVIDENCE FROM BIOCHEMISTRY AND COMPARATIVE PHYSIOLOGY 
The strongest evidence supporting the idea that all living organisms  share a common ancestor 
lies in the remarkable similarities  found in their biochemistry and physiological functions. 
a. Chemical  Composition  Resemblance: All  living  beings  share  a  common  chemical 
composition,  they are  essentially  composed of elements  like  Carbon  (C),  Hydrogen (H), 
Oxygen  (O),  and  Nitrogen  (N).  These   elements   combine   to  form   crucial   organic 
compounds like Carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. Protoplasm, the fundamental substance 
of all living beings, also shares the same composition. Chromosomes and genes, DNA and 
RNA  that store  genetic  information  are  coded in  the  arrangement  of  bases,  sugar  and 
phosphate molecules.  Therefore,  based on their  chemical  composition,  it's  reasonable  to 
infer that all living  organisms may have originated from a common ancestor. 
b. Serological (Precipitation)  Tests: Serum is the liquid component of blood separated after 
blood clotting. 
When antigens from one animal are injected into another, antibodies are produced, leading 
to  an  antigen-antibody  interaction  and  the  formation  of  a  white  precipitate  known  as 
precipitin.  This  reaction,  observed  in  serological  tests, helps  determine  the  relationship 
between different animals.  By comparing  the amount  of precipitate  formed with diluted 
antigens,  scientists  can  assess  the closeness  of  the  organisms.  This  technique  has  been 


 
 
crucial  in clarifying  phylogenetic  uncertainties, such as the classification  of the king  crab 
(Limulus),  where  antigens  from  various  arthropods  were  tested against Limulus serum, 
confirming  its placement  in  the Arachnida class.  Similarly,  comparative  serological  tests 
have resolved uncertainties among mammals,  such as the relationship between whales and 
fishes. Despite morphological  similarities,  serological  tests show that whale serum proteins 
are  most  like  those  of  even-toed  hoofed  mammals,  suggesting  their  evolution  from  a 
primitive   artiodactyla  stock.  Comparative  serology   among  primates,  including  man, 
anthropoid apes,  monkeys,  lemurs,  and dogs, revealed  an increasing  order of precipitate 
formation, indicating the closest relationship  between man and anthropoid apes. 
c. Enzymes and Hormones: Remarkable similarities  in digestive enzymes such as Trypsin, 
Lipase, amylase,  etc. and hormones  are observed in large groups  of animals.  This  further 
supports organic evolution.

Like  groups  of animals  and specific  types of creatures,  life  forms have  changed slowly  and 
gradually  over a long  time. Scientists  have come  up with different ideas to explain  how this 
happens. Let's explore the main ideas from Darwin and Lamarck about how life evolves. 
i. Lamarckism  (Theory  of inheritance of Acquired characters) 
ii. Darwinism (Theory  of Natural Selection) 
iii. Modern Synthetic Theory (Neo-Darwinism) 
7.3.2 LAMARCKISM (Theory of Inheritance of Acquired Characters) 
 
Jean Baptiste de Lamarck, a pioneering French biologist (1744 - 
1829), introduced a groundbreaking concept in 1809, known as the 
"Inheritance  of  Acquired  Characters."  His  theory  proposed  that 
organisms evolve gradually in form and structure over time, due to 
the   influence    of   the   environment.    His   book 'Philosophie 
Zoologique,'  proposed  a  foundational  concept  in  evolutionary 
biology. 
The foundation of Lamarckism rests on essential concepts, serving as the core ideas of this 
theory. Lamarckism  comprises  four fundamental postulates: 
i.Tendency to increase  in size:  According to Lamarck, Living organisms  and their component 
parts tend to develop and expand in size and structure over time. He believed that increase  in 
size of an organism is due to internal vital force and growth is delimited by life itself. 
ii.Direct  environmental  influence:  Any changes  in  the environment  might cause  a species  to 
require  alteration  in their  specific  organs  or  traits  for survival.  For example,  to combat new 
predators,  a  species  might  need  to  develop  distinct  characteristics,  initiating  evolutionary 
changes. 
iii.Use and Disuse of Organs:  As per the needs, a species  may undergo changes in character or 
organs  across  generations.  The  more  these  features  or  organs  are  utilized,  they  get  more 
developed. If an organ becomes unnecessary,  a species  gradually ceases to use it, leading to a 
decline in its function and structure. 
iv.Inheritance of Acquired character: New traits or changes acquired by any individuals during 
their lifetime are transmitted from one generation to the next, ensuring  the changes within the 
species.  This  transmission  guarantees  that  subsequent  generations  inherit  the  same  set  of 
changes, making them a consistent feature of the species. In this way, a new species  different 
from their original  ancestors emerges.

CRITICISMS OF LAMARCKISM 
Lamarck's theory faced criticism and lacked widespread support from scientists. 
i.Evidence does not always support the tendency of organisms to increase their size. There have 
been  some  cases  where  size  gets  reduced. For  example,  primitive  trees  are  larger  whereas 
shrubs, herbs, and grasses that emerged later are smaller. 
ii.It is clearly illogical  to accept that organisms can gain new organs just by their need or will. 
iii.It was observed that mutilations, like lost limbs or fingers, are not passed onto offspring. 
Weismann's  Tailless  Mice Experiment:  Scientist August Weismann  conducted a five-
generation   experiment   involving   68   white  mice,   surgically   cutting  their   tails. 
Surprisingly,  no  tailless  or  shorter-tailed  mice  were  born  in  subsequent  generations. 
Weismann's  experiment  provided strong evidence against  the inheritance  of acquired 
characteristics,  especially  if changes occurred in somatic  cells.  However, he proposed 
the theory of Continuity of germplasm, which implies that heritable traits get transferred 
through germ cells in the ovaries and testicles.

iv. Contrary to Lamarck's  idea of improved  organs through continuous use,  it was noted 
that organs, such as eyes, developed defects instead.

i.somatic cell (somatoplasm) 
ii.germinal  cells (germplasm)

7.3.3 DARWINISM (Theory of Natural Selection) 
Darwinism  is a theory of organic  evolution propounded by Charles  Darwin as an explanation 
of changes  in  living  beings  that is  driven mainly  by  natural  selection.  Darwin's  meticulous 
research  and observations,  primarily  conducted during his journey  aboard HMS Beagle from 
1831 to 1836, laid the foundation of this theory in  the field of modern evolutionary biology. 
Exploring  diverse  regions  like  the  Galápagos  Islands,  South  America,  and  Australia,  the 
systematic arrangement of flora, fauna, and geological formations, shaped his later discoveries. 
Darwinism is based on several facts and observations. The five fundamental facts are: 
a. Enormous fertility 
b. Struggle for existence 
c. Variation and heredity 
d. Natural selection 
e. Origin of species 
a) Enormous  fertility: All  living  things  have  an  extraordinary  ability  to reproduce  in 
geometric ratio. This type of reproductive potential can be observed in nature in various 
organisms: 
i. An oyster lays around 80 million  eggs in a year. 
ii. A starfish produces about a million  eggs every  season.  If all  of them survive  and 
continue to reproduce, a single species will fill all the sea after a few generations. 
iii. Paramecium  divides three times within 48 hours. 
iv. A salmon produces 28 million  eggs in a single breeding season. 
v. A slow breeding elephant couple, in 800 years, can give 29 million offsprings. 
b) Struggle for existence: Darwin observed three types of struggle for existence: 
i. Intraspecific struggle: This is the struggle between individuals of the same species for 
food, shelter,  breeding  place,  etc.  For  instance,  dogs compete  with  one  another  for 
flesh.  
ii. Interspecific  struggle:  This  is  the struggle  between individuals of different species. 
For  example,  both  leopards  and  wolves  hunt  rabbits.  Elephants,  rhinos  and  deer 
sometimes  wander  into  farmland  nearby  national  parks  for  food, which  can  cause 
problems  for farmers.  At the same time, people clear  forests to make space for farms, 
which affects the habitat of these animals.  This  competition for land and food shows 
how humans and wildlife struggle for survival. 
iii. Environmental  struggle:  This  is  the struggle  against  environmental  challenges  like 
floods, droughts, cold and other natural disasters.

c) Variations and heredity: Competition among organisms  has driven them to adapt to their 
surroundings, aiming  for better access to resources  and increased chances of survival.  This 


 
 
process brings variation which can be: useful, useless or harmful. Harmful variations cause 
individuals less capable in the struggle for survival,  while useful ones are quite significant 
that help the organism  fit the environment.  Useless  variations  are not significant from an 
evolutionary  point of view. Beneficial variations  are  passed down to the next generation, 
making them better suited to their environment. 
d) Natural selection  (Survival of the Fittest): The organisms  which are provided with useful 
or favorable variation would survive because they are fit to face environmental  challenges, 
whereas unfits get destroyed. The organisms which sort out useful variations are said to be 
the fittest. But it was unknown how only useful variations  are sorted out by the organisms 
for  which  Darwin  said  that  these  variations  are  sorted  out  by  nature.  This  is  natural 
selection. Herbert Spencer first used the phrase 'survival of the fittest' for this phenomenon. 
e)  Origin  of species  (Speciation): The  useful variations  acquired by  organisms  as  result  of 
natural selection are passed down to offsprings that become prominent over the generations. 
Improvements  in  the  organisms  in  successive  generations  gradually  diverged from  their 
parents, resulting in the emergence of more adapted and better organized organisms  which 
are identified as a new species. 
EXAMPLES OF NATURAL SELECTION 
a) Evolution of Giraffe 
Originally,  there were giraffes with both short and long necks. With the scarcity of leaves 
on the lower branches of trees, they were forced to reach the higher branches favouring the 
survival  of the long necked giraffes. This  phased out the giraffes with short necks as  they 
could not reach higher  leaves. Thus, the giraffes with longer necks were selected by nature 
that survived and reproduced to the present day giraffes.

b. Resistance of mosquito to pesticides: Mosquitoes make people sick by spreading diseases 
like  malaria. So, an  insecticide  called  DDT  (banned  now)  was  used  to  kill  mosquitoes 
effectively. But now, it is not effective because some mosquitoes have developed a special 
ability  to survive  the DDT. This  shows how nature picks  the mosquitoes that can survive 
and pass their abilities  onto their offspring. 
c. Industrial  melanism: In  Britain, peppered moths  (Biston  betularia) in  industrial  areas

colored moths. 
CRITICISM OF DARWINISM 
Darwin's  theory of natural  selection  was  widely accepted, given  its  logical  basis  and strong 
support. However, some objections were later raised: 
i. Darwinism  lacked  the  knowledge  of  heredity  and  could  not  explain  how  variation 
occurred. 
ii. It could not justify the inheritance of small variations which are not useful in organisms. 
iii. It could not explain over specialization of some organs like elephant’s tusk and antlers 
of deer. 
iv. Darwin's theory explained survival of the fittest but not how the fittest initially emerged. 
v. It didn't account for the presence of vestigial organs. 
vi. The distinction between somatic and germinal variations was not addressed. 
 
MODERN SYNTHETIC THEORY (NEO-DARWINISM) 
Modern Synthetic Theory is a modified form of Darwin’s theory of natural selection which is 
often  called   Neo-darwinism.   The   fundamental  concept   of  this  theory   is   based  upon 
Dobzhansky’s  book ’Genetics  and the Origin  of Species’.  A number  of biologists  such  as 
Fischer,  Huxley, Ernst Mayr, Haldane, Sewall Wright, Hugo de Vries,  etc. contributed to the 
development of this theory. G. L. Stebbins (1971) discussed this theory in his book ‘Process of 
Organic Evolution’ encompassing five fundamental processes like gene mutation, chromosome 
mutation, genetic recombination,  natural selection, and reproductive isolation. 
Modern Synthetic Theory can be described under following points: 
i. Mutation 
ii. Genetic recombination 
iii. Gene migration 
iv. Genetic drift 
v. Reproductive isolation 
vi. Natural selection 
These points are briefly explained below: 
i.  Mutation:  Mutation  refers  to  sudden  inheritable  changes  in  genetic  composition  of 
organisms.  Mutations lead to variations  within a population, playing  a crucial  role in driving 
evolution.  They  are  considered a  source  of organic  evolution  because  they can  replace  old 
genes with new ones, resulting in changes and contributing to the evolutionary process. 
Dutch botanist Hugo de Vries first proposed this idea after studying Evening primrose  plants 
(Oenothera spp.) in 1901. He suggested that new species form due to sudden changes caused 
by mutations. There are two main types of mutations: 
a. Chromosomal mutation:  This  type of mutation involves  alteration in the structure or 
the number of chromosomes.  For example, a part of a chromosome  may break off and 
reattach  to  another  chromosome  (translocation),  or  an  entire  chromosome  may  be 
duplicated or lost (duplication or deletion). 


 
 
b. Gene Mutation:  Gene mutations affect the DNA sequence within a gene, altering  the 
genetic information. Gene mutations can be of several types, such as substitutions (one 
base is replaced by another), insertions  (extra base pairs  are added), or deletions (base 
pairs are removed). 
ii. Genetic Recombination: Genetic recombination involves the reshuffling and recombination 
of  genes  among  different  organisms,  creating  new  genetic  constitution  from  existing 
genotypes. This  process  primarily  occurs  in sexually  reproducing organisms  by following 
ways: 
i. crossing over in meiosis 
ii. independent assortment of genes during gamete formation 
iii. random fusion of gametes in fertilization 
Genetic recombination is a major source of observable genetic variation within a population. 
This  phenomenon  adds new  alleles  and  their  combination  to  the  gene  pool,  playing  a 
significant role in the evolution and formation of new species. 
 
iii.  Gene Migration:  Migration is the process of movement of individuals from one place 
to another. When such migrated individuals breed within a new population, new alleles 
or genes are added in the local gene pool of the host population. It leads to evolution. 
 
iv.  Genetic Drift (Sewall Wright Effect): Genetic drift is the change in gene frequencies 
in a population  randomly by chance,  not due to natural selection.  This  happens  in all 
populations;  however it's most noticeable in small,  isolated groups. Sewell Wright, an 
American geneticist, describes this process as a significant force in evolution. 
Bottlenecks and founder effects are two examples of Genetic drift. 
a. Bottleneck  effect is  a  genetic  phenomenon  that  occurs  when  a  large  population  is 
drastically  reduced in  size  due to a  catastrophic  event,  such  as  a  natural  disaster  or 
disease outbreak. As a result it can cause a substantial loss of genetic diversity in small 
populations, affecting  their  ability  to  adapt  to  environmental  changes.  Later  when 
population expands, gene frequencies distinctly differ from the one before catastrophe. 
b. The  founder effect is  a  genetic  phenomenon  that occurs  when  a  new  population  is 
established by a small  number of individuals from a larger  population. These founding 
individuals carry limited genetic diversity. As a result, the new population formed from 
the separated individuals will  have  different gene frequencies  from that of the parent 
population. For example, in Galápagos Islands, a small number of finches arrived from 
the mainland and established a new population. Due to the limited genetic diversity of 
the founding individuals, the new finch populations on different islands evolved distinct 
beak shapes and behaviors adapted to their specific island habitats.

v.  Reproductive  Isolation: Isolation  is  the separation  of organisms  from their  original 
population  that prevents  interbreeding  between different groups.  Isolation  can  result 
from  various  factors:  physical  barriers   (geographical,  ecological,   etc.)  and  biotic 
barriers  (physiological, behavioural,  genetic, etc.). Dobzhansky (1930) introduced the 
term ‘isolating mechanism’ to define the phenomena by which two related populations 
residing in a particular  area remain  distinct. There are two main types of isolation,  viz. 
geographical  isolation and reproductive isolation. 
Geographical  Isolation  occurs  when a  population  is  divided into separate  groups  by 
geographical   barriers   like   mountain,   rivers,   glacier,   ocean,   forests,  desert,  etc., 
preventing interbreeding and leading to the formation of new species over time. It could 
be due to independent evolution that accumulated in the gene pools. Thus, new species 
have been formed by geographical isolation. 
Reproductive  isolation  is  the  separation  that  prevents  interbreeding  between  the 
populations  of different species.  It ensures  the maintenance of species'  characteristics 
and potentially gives rise to new species. Reproductive isolating mechanisms  are of two 
types:  Premating  isolation  and  Postmating  isolation.  Premating  isolation  prevents 
interspecies  mating  and fertilization while postmating isolation  prevents reproduction 
in interspecies  offspring. 
 


 
 
vi.  Natural  Selection: This  process  actually  means ‘differential reproduction’ in which 
some members of a population produce a large number of progeny while others produce 
less  or  none.  The  organisms  which  are  physically,  physiologically  and behaviorally 
better adapted to the surrounding survive and reproduce. The better adapted organisms 
transfer their successful characters to the upcoming generation. Those who are not well 
adapted fail to reproduce and die in the process of natural selection. In this way, natural 
selection leads to the change in allele or gene frequencies.

7.4.5 EVOLUTION OF MODERN MAN 
The palentological evidence suggests that man originated in Central Asia, China, Java and India 
(Shivalik  Hills).  An established  theory suggests Dryopithecus to be one of the oldest fossils 
that later evolved into apes and man. The origin and evolution of man can be understood in the 
following major  headings: 
 


 
 
 
Figure: 7.4.4 Timeline of evolution of man (with cranial capacity) 
1. Dryopithecus or Proconsul 
The fossil of Dryopithecus africanus, that lived 15 million  years ago, was discovered from 
Miocene rocks of Lake of Victoria, Africa and Europe by Leakey. It is regarded as a common 
ancestor of man and apes.  
 
Its characteristic  features are listed  below: 
i. They were hairy and looked more like an ape but walked like a gorilla and chimpanzee. 
ii. They were arboreal that fed on leaves and soft fruits. 
iii. They had round forehead like modern man but canine teeth were like apes. 
iv. They had forelimbs that were weaker and shorter than hind limbs. 
v. The  characteristics  shown by the fossils  indicates that they are not the direct ancestor 
of humans.

2. Ramapithecus  
It is believed that in the late Miocene epoch, Dryopithecus gave rise to Ramapithecus which 
was on the direct line of human evolution. Thus, it appeared about 14 - 15 million  years ago. 
Its fossil was discovered from Pliocene rocks of Shivalik Hills (India) by Edward Lewis and 
from Africa by Leaky. Its teeth were found in Dang valley, now preserved in the Natural 
History Museum,  Kathmandu. Ramapithecus is  assumed to be in  the direct line  of human 
evolution according to fossil records.  
 
Some of its characteristic  features are listed  below: 
i. They were hairy and looked more like a man but walked like gorillas  and chimpanzees. 
ii. They  had  small  canine  teeth  but  large  molar  teeth.  It  suggests,  like  modern  man, 
Ramapithecus ate hard nuts and seeds.

3. Australopithecus (First ape man). 
Australopithecus evolved as the first human-like  form that existed around 2 million  years 
ago. The fossil of Australopithecus (African Ape man) was discovered by Raymond Dart in 
South Africa from the Pleistocene rock. Australopithecus was of two species: A. africanus 
and A. robustus. 
 
    Some of the characteristic A. africanus are listed  below: 
i. They had erect posture more like a man and walked upright without chin 
ii. They had an ape-like brain (500 cc) and man-like  teeth with brow ridges projected over 
the eyes. 
iii. Evidence suggests them to have hunting life and used stones, sticks, bones as hunting 
weapons. 
iv. Their  canine teeth were smaller  than that of apes 
v. They were omnivorous but mainly fed on fruits. 
 
It is believed that Australopithecus africanus later gave rise to Homo habilis.

6. Neanderthal Man (Homo sapiens neanderthalensis) 
They are considered a primitive form of modern man that thrived in Europe, Asia and North 
America between 1,00,000 - 40,000 years ago. Fossils  of Neanderthal man were recovered 
from Neanderthal Valley (Germany)  from the late Pleistocene epoch by Carl Fuhlott. Later 
many other fossils were excavated in various parts of the world by different palaeontologists. 
It is  considered  that H.  sapiens  neanderthalensis did not  evolve  into H.  sapiens, which 
implies  they might have vanished due to extreme cold weather. 
 
Some of its characteristic  features are listed  below: 
i. They were like modern men with tallness of around 5 feet and faces projecting slightly 
forward. 
ii. They walked upright and had low brows, receding jaws, and high domed heads. 
iii. Their  cranial capacity was 1400 c.c. like a modern man. 
iv. Neanderthals were cave dwellers, skilled hunters (all time predators) and were adapted 
to a cold environment and harsh climate. They had fashioned the skin into clothing. 
v. They were omnivorous and cannibals. 
vi. They used to light fire in natural caves at night for heat and light. 
vii. It is  believed  that they buried their  dead with flowers and tools. And they may have 
religion.

7. Cro-Magnon man (Homo sapiens fossilis). 
They are considered the early form of modern humans that thrived around 34,000 years ago 
in the Holocene epoch. They were more developed than Neanderthal man. Their fossils were 
first discovered in 1868 from Cro-Magnon rocks of France by Mac Gregor. Thus, they are 
called Cro- Magnon man. Thus, it is regarded as the most recent ancestor of modern man. It 
became extinct about 10,000-11,000 years ago. 
Some of its characteristic  features are listed  below: 
i. They were similar  to modern men in terms of size and appearance.

wall.  
vii. They  used to live in families  and were good at making  tools and even ornaments,  not 
only of stones and bones, but also of elephant tusks. Its tools included spears, bows and 
arrows. 
viii. Their  cranial  capacity  was  1600  c.c.  which  is  more  than modern  man.  Thus,  it  was 
believed that they were more intelligent and cultured than the man of today. 
ix. They were unaware of agriculture, domestication and socialization by then.

8. The Living Modern Man (Homo sapiens sapiens) 
Further evolution of man after Cro-Magnon was of culture rather than of anatomy. During

and hunting type to crop raising type in around 10,000 years with which human settlements 
started.

morphological  evolution are distinctly observed in modern man: 
i. Modern men have slight raises in the skull cap. 
ii. Their  skull bones are thinner. 
iii. There is slight reduction in cranial capacity which is about 1450 cc. 
iv. They developed four curves in the vertebral column.
