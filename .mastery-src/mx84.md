## 1. Topic Introduction — Angle Bisector Worked Example (the a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ Method at Full Speed)

This canonical runs the **complete bisector computation on one concrete pair**: the lines x + 2y − 5 = 0 and 4x + 2y + 9 = 0, from normalisation through the acute/obtuse verdict to the finished equations. It is the worked-example face of the rule canonical.

%%%
## 2. Theorem Mirror (as used in the example)

- **Standardise:** both constants made positive — L₁: −x − 2y + 5 = 0 (a₁ = −1, b₁ = −2), L₂: 4x + 2y + 9 = 0 (a₂ = 4, b₂ = 2).
- **Normalisers:** √5 and √20 = 2√5.
- **Rule:** a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ = (−1)(4) + (−2)(2) = **−8 < 0** ⟹ the (+) bisector is **acute**, the (−) bisector obtuse.
- **Bisectors:** (+) 6x + 6y − 1 = 0 (acute); (−) 2x − 2y + 19 = 0 (obtuse); slopes −1 and +1 ⟹ perpendicular ✓.

%%%
## 3. Deep Conceptual — Reading the Example as a Chain of Licences

- Each step consumes a theorem: the standardisation licenses the rule, the normalisers convert equations into distances, the dot-product verdict assigns the labels, and the perpendicularity closes the audit. The example is not "arithmetic that happens to work" — it is four named theorems in sequence.
- **The unequal normalisers are the interesting part:** √5 vs 2√5 means the bisector equations carry a factor 2 — 2(−x−2y+5) = ±(4x+2y+9). Forgetting the factor is the classic error; the fix is to multiply through by the lcm of the normalisers before expanding.
- **Verification layers:** slopes multiply to −1 (perpendicularity), and substituting a point of one bisector into both normalised forms gives equal absolute distances (the definition itself). Two independent checks, one computation.

%%%
## 4. Deep Conceptual — Why the Verdict (−8 < 0) Was Predictable

- The normals (−1, −2) and (4, 2) point in *opposing* half-plane directions (dot product negative) — the same-side wedge (the (+) equation) is the narrow one. Geometrically: two normals pointing away from each other sandwich the acute wedge between the lines.
- **The constant-sign convention's role:** with c₁ = 5, c₂ = 9 both positive, the origin side is the (+) side of both lines; the (+) bisector therefore passes through the origin's wedge — and here that wedge is acute. Every label in the final answer is traceable to one dot product computed under one convention.

%%%
## 5. Worked Example Types

- **Type A — full pipeline:** standardise → normalise → rule → both bisectors → checks.
- **Type B — factor discipline:** unequal normalisers, lcm multiplication.
- **Type C — definition check:** equidistance verified at a bisector point.
- **Type D — classification narration:** the −8 verdict explained as normal geometry.

## 6. Common Confusions

**"Drop the factor 2":** unequal normalisers must be cleared before expanding. **"The labels are arbitrary":** they follow from the dot product under the convention — traceable, not chosen.

@@practiceQuestions@@
**NEB-style (6m): Find the equations of the bisectors of the angles between x + 2y − 5 = 0 and 4x + 2y + 9 = 0, and label the acute one.** — **Standardise:** L₁ ⟹ −x − 2y + 5 = 0 (c = 5 > 0); L₂ already has c = 9 > 0. **Normalisers:** √5 and √(16+4) = √20 = 2√5. **Bisector skeleton:** (−x−2y+5)/√5 = ±(4x+2y+9)/(2√5) ⟹ multiply by 2√5: **2(−x−2y+5) = ±(4x+2y+9)**. **(+):** −2x−4y+10 = 4x+2y+9 ⟹ 6x+6y−1 = 0. **(−):** −2x−4y+10 = −4x−2y−9 ⟹ 2x−2y+19 = 0. **Rule:** a₁a₂+b₁b₂ = −8 < 0 ⟹ (+) = 6x+6y−1 = 0 is **acute**. **Check:** slopes −1 and +1 ⟹ perpendicular ✓. **Deep point:** the lcm multiplication (by 2√5) is where the example's difficulty lives — every coefficient in the final answers is integer precisely because it was done; expanding with the raw fractions invites both arithmetic errors and dropped factors.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): Verify that the point (1/6, 0) lies on the acute bisector 6x + 6y − 1 = 0 and is equidistant from the two lines.** — **On the bisector:** 6(1/6) + 0 − 1 = 0 ✓. **Distance to L₁ (x+2y−5=0):** |1/6 + 0 − 5|/√5 = |−29/6|/√5 = 29/(6√5). **Distance to L₂ (4x+2y+9=0):** |4/6 + 0 + 9|/√20 = |29/3 + 9|... compute: 4(1/6) = 2/3; 2/3 + 9 = 29/3 ⟹ (29/3)/(2√5) = 29/(6√5) ✓ — **equal distances**, as the bisector definition demands. **Deep point:** the definition check is the strongest verification — perpendicularity could hold for other perpendicular pairs, but equidistance is the *defining* property; a point on the line with equal distances certifies the bisector from first principles.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): Without computing the bisectors, determine which of the (+)/(−) bisectors of x + 2y − 5 = 0 and 4x + 2y + 9 = 0 is acute, and justify.** — **Standardise:** L₁ ⟹ −x−2y+5 = 0 (a₁ = −1, b₁ = −2); L₂: a₂ = 4, b₂ = 2. **Dot product:** a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ = −4 − 4 = **−8 < 0**. **Verdict:** under the constants-positive convention, the (+) bisector — the same-signed-distance equation — is the **acute** one. **Geometric reading:** the normals (−1, −2) and (4, 2) have opposing senses (negative dot product), so the wedge where a point lies on the same side of both lines is the narrow one. **Deep point:** the classification is one multiplication and one comparison — the full bisector equations are not needed for the verdict; the rule exists precisely to make this label cheap.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): The bisectors of two lines are 6x + 6y − 1 = 0 and 2x − 2y + 19 = 0. Find the angle between the bisectors and comment on what it verifies.** — **Slopes:** m₁ = −1, m₂ = 1. **Angle:** tan θ = |(m₂ − m₁)/(1 + m₁m₂)| = |2/(1 − 1)| = 2/0 ⟹ θ = **90°**. **Comment:** the bisectors of any pair of intersecting lines are perpendicular — this is not a special property of these lines but an identity (the normalised coefficients' cross terms cancel in the dot product). **Consistency:** the original lines' angle is bisected into two equal halves by each bisector, and the bisectors' own right angle is the envelope of that bisection. **Deep point:** when a problem *gives* you the bisectors, the perpendicularity check is the fastest sanity test — a non-right-angle pair cannot be the bisectors of any two lines, so the check can falsify an answer in one line of arithmetic.

Every step consumes a named theorem; the example is the licence chain, not the arithmetic.
