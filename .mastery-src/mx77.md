## 1. Topic Introduction — The Companion Doorway to the Definite Integral

This canonical is the **short-form doorway** to the definite integral: Riemann sums, the Fundamental Theorem, and the evaluation rules in one page. The parent canonical carries the full treatment; this companion mirrors the theorems, adds the deep readings, and drills four heavy-mark problems.

%%%
## 2. Theorem Mirror

- **Definition:** ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx = lim Σf(xᵢ*)Δx — the limit of Riemann sums as the partition refines (n → ∞).
- **FTC Part 1:** d/dx[∫ₐˣf(t)dt] = f(x) — differentiation undoes accumulation.
- **FTC Part 2:** ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx = F(b) − F(a) for any anti-derivative F — evaluation needs no limit, only the endpoints.
- **Properties:** linearity; additivity ∫ₐᵇ + ∫ᵦᶜ = ∫ₐᶜ; reversal ∫ₐᵇ = −∫ᵦₐ; same-value ∫ₐᵇ = ∫ₐᵇ; the constant c·(b − a).

%%%
## 3. Deep Conceptual — Riemann Sums vs FTC: Two Doors, One Room

- The Riemann definition is **local and constructive**: chop, sample, sum, refine — it defines what the integral *is* and works even when no anti-derivative exists (∫e^{−x²}dx has no elementary form yet a perfectly good definite value). The FTC is **global and computational**: it converts accumulation into endpoint arithmetic when F is available.
- **The two doors answer different questions:** "does the area have a value?" is Riemann's question (existence); "what is it, cheaply?" is FTC's question (evaluation). Exam problems asking for a limit of sums are testing door one; standard evaluations test door two.
- **The accumulation function A(x) = ∫ₐˣf(t)dt is the bridge:** its derivative is f (Part 1), and its value at b is the definite integral (Part 2) — one object, two theorems, the subject's crown jewel.

%%%
## 4. Deep Conceptual — The Dummy Variable and the Boundary Terms

- ∫ₐᵇf(t)dt is a **number**; the t is a dummy — rename it freely. But ∫ₐˣf(t)dt is a **function of x** (the upper limit is live). Confusing the two is the classic Part 1 error.
- **FTC Part 1 with variable limits:** d/dx[∫ₐ^{g(x)}f(t)dt] = f(g(x))·g′(x) — the chain rule rides on top of Part 1; and for a variable *lower* limit, the reversal property supplies the minus sign.
- **The constant's geometry:** ∫ₐᵇc dx = c(b − a) — a rectangle's area; every integral property has a picture, and the picture is the fastest verification.

%%%
## 5. Worked Example Types

- **Type A — FTC evaluation:** anti-derivative, endpoints, subtract.
- **Type B — Riemann limit:** the right-endpoint sum for x² or x, evaluated as n → ∞.
- **Type C — accumulation functions:** d/dx of an integral with variable or function limits.
- **Type D — properties arithmetic:** additivity, reversal, and constant rules combining known pieces.

%%%
## 6. Common Confusions

**"The definite integral needs + C":** no — endpoints subtract the constant away. **"∫ₐˣf(t)dt and ∫f(x)dx are the same kind of thing":** one is a function, the other a family.

@@practiceQuestions@@
**NEB-style (5m): Evaluate ∫₁³(2x + 1)dx and interpret the answer geometrically.** — **FTC Part 2:** F(x) = x² + x ⟹ F(3) − F(1) = 12 − 2 = **10**. **Geometry:** the region under y = 2x + 1 from 1 to 3 is a trapezoid with parallel sides f(1) = 3 and f(3) = 7, width 2 ⟹ area = ½(3 + 7)(2) = 10 ✓. **Deep point:** the trapezoid check is the picture verifying the theorem — for linear integrands the Riemann limit is finite geometry, and the agreement is the FTC made visible.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): Evaluate lim_{n→∞} Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (i/n)²·(1/n) by recognising it as a definite integral.** — **Recognise the anatomy:** Δx = 1/n ⟹ b − a = 1 with a = 0; xᵢ = i/n ⟹ right endpoints of [0, 1]; f(xᵢ) = (i/n)² = xᵢ². **Identify:** the sum is the right-endpoint Riemann sum of f(x) = x² on [0, 1]. **Evaluate:** lim = ∫₀¹x²dx = [x³/3]₀¹ = **1/3**. **Cross-check:** the closed form Σi² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 gives the sum = (1/n³)·n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 → 2/6 = 1/3 ✓. **Deep point:** the Riemann door and the algebraic door agree — the sum's closed form is the long route, the FTC the short one; recognising Δx and the sample points is the entire translation skill.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): Find d/dx[∫₁^{x²} e^{t}dt] and d/dx[∫ₓ⁵ cos(t²)dt].** — **First (upper limit is a function):** FTC Part 1 + chain rule: f(g(x))·g′(x) = e^{x²}·2x = **2x·e^{x²}**. **Second (lower limit is live):** reversal first: ∫ₓ⁵cos(t²)dt = −∫₅ˣcos(t²)dt ⟹ derivative = **−cos(x²)**. **Verification of the first by Part 2:** ∫₁^{x²}e^t dt = e^{x²} − e ⟹ derivative = 2x·e^{x²} ✓. **Deep point:** the live limit is the whole game — a function in the upper limit contributes the chain-rule factor, a function in the lower limit contributes a minus; cos(t²) has no elementary anti-derivative, yet Part 1 differentiates its accumulation anyway — the theorem outruns the table.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): Given ∫₁⁴f(x)dx = 5 and ∫₄⁶f(x)dx = −2, find ∫₆¹f(x)dx and ∫₁⁶f(x)dx, citing the properties.** — **Additivity:** ∫₁⁶f = ∫₁⁴f + ∫₄⁶f = 5 + (−2) = **3**. **Reversal:** ∫₆¹f = −∫₁⁶f = **−3**. **Consistency picture:** the signed area from 1 to 4 is +5, from 4 to 6 is −2 (the curve dips below the axis); the total 3 and its reversed −3 are the ledger's two directions. **Deep point:** signed area is the bookkeeping — additivity concatenates journeys, reversal walks them backwards; every property is a statement about walking the x-axis, which is why the pictures verify them instantly.
@@summary@@
The definite integral lives behind two doors: Riemann's limit defines it (chop, sample, sum, refine — valid even without anti-derivatives) and the Fundamental Theorem computes it (F(b) − F(a) — valid whenever F exists). The accumulation function is the bridge, the dummy variable distinguishes numbers from functions, and live limits bring the chain rule and the reversal minus. Linearity, additivity and reversal are the properties, each with a picture that verifies it.
