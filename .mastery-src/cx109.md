## 1. Topic Introduction — The Three Attackers

Once fission has made an electron-hungry or electron-rich species, reactions are a matter of matching attacker to target. **Electrophiles** love electrons and attack electron-rich sites; **nucleophiles** love nuclei (positive centres) and donate electron pairs; **free radicals** carry unpaired electrons and strip atoms from bonds. Every organic reaction is a conversation between one of these three and the substrate.

%%%
## 2. Fundamental Concepts

### 2.1 Electrophiles (E⁺)

- **Definition:** electron-loving species — **electron-deficient**, accept an electron pair; attack **electron-rich** sites (double bonds, lone pairs).
- **Charged:** H⁺, NO₂⁺ (nitronium), CH₃⁺ (carbocation), Br⁺, Cl⁺.
- **Neutral:** BF₃, AlCl₃, SO₃ — incomplete octets or polarised atoms make them acceptors.

### 2.2 Nucleophiles (Nu⁻)

- **Definition:** nucleus-loving species — **electron-rich**, donate an electron pair; attack **electron-deficient** centres.
- **Charged:** OH⁻, CN⁻, Cl⁻, CH₃O⁻, H⁻.
- **Neutral:** H₂O, NH₃, ROH, RNH₂ — lone pairs make them donors.
- Nucleophile = Lewis base in action; electrophile = Lewis acid in action.

### 2.3 Free radicals

- **Definition:** neutral species with an **unpaired electron** (written X·), formed by **homolytic fission**.
- Paramagnetic; highly reactive; drive **chain reactions**: initiation (Cl₂ → 2Cl·), propagation (Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl; CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·), termination (Cl· + Cl· → Cl₂).
- Attack by **abstracting** atoms or adding to multiple bonds — the third reagent class alongside ions.

## 4. Detailed Explanation

### 4.1 The matching rule

- Electrophiles seek **π bonds and lone pairs**; nucleophiles seek **δ+ atoms** (carbonyl carbon, alkyl halide carbon); radicals seek **any bond they can split into two radicals**. Predicting an organic reaction starts by identifying which of the three is present.

### 4.2 Charged vs neutral members

- Both families have charged and neutral members: charge is not the criterion — **electron deficiency or richness** is. BF₃ is neutral yet a strong electrophile (empty p orbital); NH₃ is neutral yet a good nucleophile (lone pair).

### 4.3 Radicals vs the ionic pair

- Radicals are made by **homolysis** (light/heat), ions by **heterolysis**. Radicals are neutral but have 7-electron hunger; electrophiles/nucleophiles carry charge or lone pairs. Conditions select the family: UV light → radical chemistry; polar solvent + catalyst → ionic chemistry.

## 6. Common Confusions

**Neutral ≠ inactive:** BF₃ and AlCl₃ are neutral electrophiles; H₂O and NH₃ are neutral nucleophiles — judge by electrons, not charge.
%%%
**Radicals are not anions:** Cl· has no charge; it differs from Cl⁻ by one electron, and that electron changes everything.
%%%
**Electrophile/nucleophile are roles, not fixed species:** H₂O can be a nucleophile (attacking a carbocation) or a weak electrophile-source (donating H⁺) — context assigns the role.

## 7. Exam Application

**NEB-style (2m): Define electrophile with two examples.** — An **electrophile** is an **electron-deficient species that accepts an electron pair** and attacks **electron-rich** centres (π bonds, lone pairs) in organic reactions. Examples: the **nitronium ion NO₂⁺** (attacks the benzene π system in nitration) and the **proton H⁺**; neutral examples include **BF₃ and AlCl₃** (incomplete octets).
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Define nucleophile with two examples.** — A **nucleophile** is an **electron-rich species that donates an electron pair** to an **electron-deficient** centre (a "nucleus-loving" reagent). Examples: the **hydroxide ion OH⁻** and the **cyanide ion CN⁻**; neutral examples include **ammonia NH₃** and **water H₂O**, whose lone pairs make them donors.
%%%
**NEB-style (3m): What are free radicals? Describe the chain mechanism of chlorination of methane.** — **Free radicals** are **neutral species with an unpaired electron**, formed by **homolytic fission** of a covalent bond under heat or light; they are paramagnetic and highly reactive. **Chlorination of methane:** **Initiation** — sunlight splits chlorine homolytically: **Cl₂ → 2Cl·**. **Propagation** — Cl· abstracts H: **Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl**; then the methyl radical attacks chlorine: **CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·** (the radical is regenerated, so the chain continues). **Termination** — radicals combine: **Cl· + Cl· → Cl₂**, CH₃· + Cl· → CH₃Cl, or CH₃· + CH₃· → C₂H₆, ending the chain.

Describe the chain mechanism of chlorination of methane.** — **Free radicals** are **neutral species possessing an unpaired electron** (written X·), produced by **homolytic fission** of a covalent bond under **heat or ultraviolet light**; they are paramagnetic and extremely reactive. **Chlorination of methane proceeds by a radical chain:** **Initiation** — UV light splits chlorine homolytically: **Cl₂ → 2Cl·**. **Propagation** — the chlorine radical abstracts hydrogen: **Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl**; the methyl radical then attacks another chlorine molecule: **CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·** — regenerating Cl·, so the chain sustains itself and one photon produces many product molecules. **Termination** — two radicals combine, consuming the chain carriers: **Cl· + Cl· → Cl₂**, **CH₃· + Cl· → CH₃Cl**, or **CH₃· + CH₃· → C₂H₆**.
@@summary@@
Organic reagents come in three classes. **Electrophiles** are **electron-deficient** — charged (H⁺, NO₂⁺, CH₃⁺) or neutral (BF₃, AlCl₃, SO₃ with incomplete octets) — and attack **electron-rich** sites: π bonds and lone pairs. **Nucleophiles** are **electron-rich** — charged (OH⁻, CN⁻, Cl⁻) or neutral (H₂O, NH₃, ROH via lone pairs) — and donate a pair to **electron-poor** centres; electrophile and nucleophile are Lewis acid and Lewis base in action. **Free radicals** are **neutral species with an unpaired electron** from homolysis (Cl·, CH₃·), paramagnetic and chain-prone: initiation (**Cl₂ → 2Cl·**), propagation (**Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl; CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·**), termination (radical–radical combination). Charge is not the criterion — electron deficiency, richness or unpairedness is; match the attacker to the target and the reaction predicts itself.
