## 1. Topic Introduction — Batch versus Continuous Processing

Every chemical plant answers one architectural question first: **make the product in discrete batches, or run the process continuously without stopping?** Batch processing makes a defined quantity, cleans, and repeats; continuous processing feeds raw material and draws product in unbroken streams at steady state. The choice shapes the plant, the economics, and the control philosophy.

%%%
## 2. Theorem Mirror

- **Batch processing:** charge the reactor → process for a set time → discharge → clean → repeat. Output is in lots; composition varies during the batch. Suited to: small volumes, multiple products in one vessel, pharma, food, dyes, specialty chemicals.
- **Continuous processing:** feed enters and product leaves constantly; every point in the plant holds a steady composition and temperature (steady state). Suited to: large volumes, single product, petrochemicals, ammonia, sulphuric acid, cement.
- **Comparison axes:** capital cost (batch low, continuous high); labour (batch high, continuous low); flexibility (batch high — one vessel, many products; continuous low); consistency (continuous wins — steady state is uniform); scale economics (continuous wins — fixed costs spread over unbroken tonnage); control (batch: time-based recipe; continuous: instrument loop at steady state).

%%%
## 3. Deep Conceptual — Steady State Changes the Whole Control Problem

- In a batch reactor the operator's variable is *time* — the recipe says hold at 80 °C for 2 hours; every batch travels the same composition path from start to finish. In a continuous plant the variable is *space* — composition depends on position in the train, and the operator's job is to hold every point fixed while material flows through. **Batch control is a recipe; continuous control is an equilibrium.**
- **This is why continuous plants are instrument-dense:** hundreds of sensors and control loops hold temperature, pressure, flow and composition at their set-points; a drift is a *fault*, not a phase. The batch plant's equivalent information lives in the written recipe and the operator's log — the two philosophies store their knowledge in different places (instruments vs documents).

%%%
## 4. Deep Conceptual — The Choice Is the Economics Wearing a Flowsheet

- The fixed/variable cost structure decides: high fixed costs and enormous demand ⟹ continuous (ammonia runs 350+ days a year; every idle day is lost fixed-cost spread); low volumes, frequent changeover, validation demands ⟹ batch (a pharma vessel makes antibiotic today, ointment tomorrow, with documented cleaning between). **Semi-batch** (one reagent fed in gradually) is the hybrid that buys heat-removal safety and selectivity control — the standard answer to exothermic reactions.
- **The same reaction can appear in both worlds:** esterification for flavours (batch, kg) and for biodiesel (continuous, tonnes) — the chemistry is identical; the volume and the customer decide the architecture. This is the applied-chemistry lesson of the whole unit: *process choice is an economic variable*.

## 6. Common Confusions

**"Continuous is always more modern":** it is more *economic at scale* — for pharma, batch remains the right answer for validation and flexibility reasons. **"Batch plants have no instrumentation":** they have plenty — but their control variable is time, not steady-state position.

@@practiceQuestions@@
**NEB-style (6m): Distinguish batch and continuous processing, and state the type of industry each suits with two examples each.** — **Batch:** the vessel is charged with reactants, processed for a set time under a written recipe, discharged, cleaned, and reused for the next lot; composition changes with time during each batch. **Suits:** small volumes, multiple products sharing equipment, strict quality documentation — **pharmaceuticals** (antibiotics, ointments), **food and dyes**; also specialty chemicals. **Continuous:** raw material flows in and product flows out without interruption; the plant holds a steady state in which every point has constant composition and temperature. **Suits:** single products at enormous volume where capital must be amortised over unbroken tonnage — **ammonia synthesis** (Haber), **sulphuric acid** (Contact); also petrochemicals, cement. **Deep point:** the two modes differ in what is constant — batch holds the *vessel* constant and varies composition with time; continuous holds *composition* constant and varies position; the rest of the comparison (labour, capital, flexibility) follows from that single difference.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): Why does a continuous plant need hundreds of instruments while a batch plant relies largely on its written recipe? Relate your answer to the nature of steady state.** — **Batch:** the control variable is **time** — the recipe prescribes the path (charge, heat to 80 °C, hold 2 h, cool, discharge); each batch replays the same path, and the operator's records are the recipe and the log. **Continuous:** the control variables are the **set-points at every position** — flows, temperatures, pressures, compositions must each be held constant while material streams through; a steady state is a *condition at every point simultaneously*, and it cannot be prescribed in advance — it must be *measured and corrected continuously*. **Consequence:** hundreds of sensors and feedback loops (measurement → controller → valve) hold the plant at its design point; a drift is a fault to be diagnosed, not a phase to be waited out. **Deep point:** the two philosophies store their knowledge differently — batch knowledge lives in documents and human practice; continuous knowledge lives in the instrument network — which is also why continuous plants automate first and batch plants digitalise last.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): A pharmaceutical company manufactures five different products in the same building, each in lots of 200 kg with strict cleaning validation between lots. Which processing mode suits it, and why would continuous processing fail here?** — **Mode: batch.** **Reasons:** (1) **flexibility** — one vessel train serves five products, reconfigured by recipe and cleaning, which continuous dedicated lines cannot do economically at this volume; (2) **validation and traceability** — each lot is a discrete, documented entity with a clean-out record, exactly the regulatory structure pharma requires; continuous manufacture would blur lot identity and complicate batch-release testing; (3) **volume** — 200 kg lots cannot amortise a continuous line's capital. **Why continuous fails:** a continuous plant is dedicated to one product at high rate — five products would need five lines; changeover between products in a continuous train is slow, wasteful, and hard to validate. **Deep point:** the assignment argument runs from the *demand profile* (multi-product, small lots, regulation) to the architecture — the reverse of the ammonia case, where single-product enormity dictates continuous; both directions are the same fixed/variable cost logic.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): An exothermic nitration is unsafe when all reagents are charged at once. Explain how semi-batch operation solves the problem, and state one additional benefit.** — **Semi-batch operation:** charge the less reactive component (e.g. the substrate in acid) fully, then **feed the dangerous reagent (nitrating mixture) gradually** at a rate the cooling system can absorb. **Why it works:** the heat generation rate is now set by the *feed rate* — a valve the operator controls — rather than by the entire charge reacting at once; the inventory of unreacted hazardous reagent in the vessel is kept minimal, so even a cooling failure releases only the small resident amount (inherent safety by inventory minimisation). **Additional benefit:** concentration control improves **selectivity** — keeping one reagent dilute suppresses side reactions (over-nitration, oxidation), raising yield of the desired mono-product. **Deep point:** semi-batch is the standard engineered answer to the L³/L² heat problem from the scale-up lesson — it converts a thermodynamic risk into a flow-control decision, which is why exothermic industrial reactions are rarely run as true batch charges.
@@summary@@
Batch and continuous are the two architectures of chemical production: a recipe followed in time versus an equilibrium held in space. Batch wins flexibility, low capital, and validation-friendly lots (pharma, food, dyes); continuous wins scale economics, consistency and low labour (ammonia, acids, petrochemicals) — because high fixed costs demand unbroken tonnage. Steady state is what makes continuous control instrument-dense, and semi-batch feeding is the hybrid that tames exotherms by turning heat generation into a valve setting. The chemistry is the same in both; the economics chooses.
