## 1. Topic Introduction — Quick Lime, Bleaching Powder, Plaster of Paris, Magnesia, Epsom Salt

Three calcium compounds and two magnesium ones carry most of the unit's industrial chemistry: **CaO** (quick lime) — made by calcining limestone and slaked to Ca(OH)₂; **bleaching powder** Ca(OCl)Cl — chlorine absorbed in slaked lime; **plaster of Paris** CaSO₄·½H₂O — gypsum dehydrated at ~120 °C; **magnesia** MgO — from magnesite or magnesium chloride; **Epsom salt** MgSO₄·7H₂O — the heptahydrate laxative. Each has a preparation equation, a set of reactions and a use list that NEB asks in rotation.

%%%
## 2. Fundamental Concepts

### 2.1 Calcium oxide (quick lime)

- **Preparation:** calcination of limestone in a lime kiln ~1000 °C: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂↑ (reversible; CO₂ swept out).
- White amorphous solid, mp ~2600 °C; reacts violently with water (**slaking**): CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat — the slaked lime.
- With acids: CaO + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O. With CO₂ (slowly) back to carbonate. With SiO₂ at high T: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (flux chemistry).
- **Uses:** cement/lime mortar, steelmaking flux, CaC₂ (carbide), paper, water treatment, drying agent (does not dry ammonia — reacts; use for alcohol), self-heating food cans.

### 2.2 Bleaching powder (calcium oxychloride)

- **Preparation:** pass dry Cl₂ over **slaked lime**: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → Ca(OCl)Cl + H₂O (Hasenclever plant, rotating drums).
- Formula written CaOCl₂ or Ca(OCl)Cl — "available chlorine" ~35–38 %.
- **Reactions:** with dilute acid liberates Cl₂: Ca(OCl)Cl + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + Cl₂↑ (the "available chlorine" test); with CO₂ from air slowly decomposes (must be stored dry/sealed); with excess CO₂ + H₂O: Ca(OCl)Cl + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + Cl₂.
- **Uses:** bleaching cotton/linen (never wool/silk — protein fibres damaged by Cl₂/HOCl), disinfecting water and drains, making chloroform and CHCl₃ precursor chemistry, laboratory Cl₂ source.

### 2.3 Plaster of Paris

- **Preparation:** heat gypsum **CaSO₄·2H₂O at ~120 °C (393 K)**, not above (over-heating gives anhydrite, dead-burnt, which will not set): CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O.
- White powder; **setting**: mixed with water it re-hydrates and interlocks needle crystals, expanding slightly: CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O; sets in ~10–15 min, slight expansion → sharp moulds.
- **Uses:** plaster casts (fractured bones), moulds/statues, chalk boards, surgical plaster bandages, dentistry.

### 2.4 Magnesia (MgO) and Epsom salt (MgSO₄·7H₂O)

- **Magnesia:** white refractory powder, mp ~2850 °C; made by heating magnesite (MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂) or by boiling MgCl₂·6H₂O with lime: MgCl₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → Mg(OH)₂↓ + CaCl₂, then Mg(OH)₂ → MgO + H₂O. Refractory furnace linings (basic refractory for steel), milk of magnesia (suspended Mg(OH)₂ antacid), Sorel cement (MgO + MgCl₂ paste), source of magnesium.
- **Epsom salt:** MgSO₄·7H₂O — colourless efflorescent needles, very soluble, bitter; used as a purgative/laxative in medicine, bath soaks for sore muscles, fertiliser (Mg + S nutrient), and as a flocculant in dyeing/soap. Heating loses water stepwise to the anhydrous sulphate.

## 4. Detailed Explanation

### 4.1 Why gypsum must not be overheated

- At ~120 °C exactly half the water leaves, leaving hemihydrate with channels that re-accept water rapidly — the setting property. Above ~200 °C the remaining water is driven out entirely: anhydrite ("dead-burnt plaster") has no such channels, hydrates too slowly to set, and the plaster is useless. The temperature window is the whole craft.

### 4.2 Why bleaching powder bleaches

- In the presence of moisture/acid it releases **hypochlorous acid** and chlorine: Ca(OCl)Cl + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂; Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl; HOCl → HCl + [O] — the **nascent oxygen** oxidises the colouring matter of the fibre. This is also why wool and silk (protein fibres) are ruined: HOCl attacks the amide bonds.

### 4.3 Why quick lime heats water so fiercely

- Slaking, CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂, is strongly exothermic (ΔH ≈ −65 kJ/mol): enough heat to boil the local water and crack a lump of lime with steam. The same heat makes lime useful in self-heating ration cans.

## 6. Common Confusions

**Plaster of Paris is a hemihydrate, not a hydrate:** the formula is CaSO₄·½H₂O — half a mole of water per mole of salt; writing CaSO₄·H₂O loses the mark.
%%%
**Bleaching powder bleaches by oxygen, not by chlorine alone:** the active agent is HOCl → HCl + [O]; the chlorine is the carrier.
%%%
**Quick lime is not slaked lime:** CaO vs Ca(OH)₂ — slaking converts one to the other; bleaching powder needs the *slaked* lime.
%%%
**Milk of magnesia is a suspension, not a solution:** the antacid is Mg(OH)₂, which is almost insoluble — its mildness (weak base, low solubility) is exactly why it neutralises stomach acid without the causticity of NaOH.

## 7. Exam Application

**NEB-style (3m): How is bleaching powder prepared? Give its reaction with dilute sulphuric acid and two uses.** — Bleaching powder is prepared by passing **dry chlorine gas over slaked lime** (calcium hydroxide): Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → Ca(OCl)Cl + H₂O — industrially in the Hasenclever plant, where chlorine rises through rotating drums of slaked lime. With **dilute H₂SO₄** it liberates its "available chlorine": Ca(OCl)Cl + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + Cl₂↑ (about 35–38 % of its mass as Cl₂). **Uses:** bleaching cotton and linen in the textile industry (it releases hypochlorous acid, the true bleaching agent), and **disinfecting drinking water and drains**; it also serves as a laboratory source of chlorine.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): How is plaster of Paris prepared? Why must gypsum not be heated above about 200 °C?** — Plaster of Paris is prepared by **heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) at about 120 °C**: CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O — the hemihydrate. If the temperature exceeds roughly 200 °C the remaining half-water is also driven off, giving **anhydrite ("dead-burnt" gypsum)**, which has no channels to re-absorb water quickly: it hydrates far too slowly to set, and the plastering property is destroyed. The narrow temperature window is what preserves the setting power, CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): What happens when quick lime is (i) added to water, (ii) heated with coke in an electric furnace?** — **(i) Slaking:** quick lime reacts violently with water, evolving much heat, to give slaked lime: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (the lump cracks with steam; the product is used as mortar and for whitewash). **(ii) With coke in the electric furnace (~2000 °C):** calcium carbide forms: CaO + 3C → CaC₂ + CO↑ — the route to acetylene, since CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Write the molecular formulae and one use each of magnesia and Epsom salt.** — **Magnesia: MgO** (magnesium oxide), prepared from magnesite, MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂, or by precipitating Mg(OH)₂ from MgCl₂ solution with lime and heating it; use — **refractory furnace lining** (mp ~2850 °C, basic refractory for steelmaking) and as the antacid in milk of magnesia (the suspension of Mg(OH)₂). **Epsom salt: MgSO₄·7H₂O** (magnesium sulphate heptahydrate), colourless efflorescent needles; use — **purgative in medicine** and as a magnesium-plus-sulphur **fertiliser**.

@@importantConcepts@@
All three compounds are hydration stories: CaO's slaking is hydration releasing heat, plaster's setting is re-hydration interlocking crystals, and bleaching powder's decay is hydration-triggered hydrolysis — store all three away from water or vapour.
@@examples@@
***The hospital cast*** — a bandage soaked in plaster-of-Paris slurry is wrapped on the fracture; within minutes the hemihydrate drinks 1½ H₂O, grows interlocking gypsum needles, and the soft wrap becomes a rigid cast — setting chemistry holding a bone straight.
@@universalFacts@@
Cement clinker is mostly calcium silicates — every tonne of Portland cement begins with ~1.2 t of limestone calcined exactly as the quick-lime equation, making CaCO₃ decomposition the single largest industrial CO₂ source.

Write its reaction with dilute sulphuric acid and give two uses.** — Bleaching powder is made by passing **dry chlorine over slaked lime**: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → Ca(OCl)Cl + H₂O — industrially in the **Hasenclever plant**, where chlorine is fed upward through a series of rotating drums carrying the slaked lime against the gas flow. @@summary@@
Three calcium compounds, three hydration stories. **Quick lime (CaO)** is born from limestone calcination (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, ~1000 °C) and slakes violently (CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat), fluxes silica to CaSiO₃ in the furnace, and yields calcium carbide with coke at 2000 °C. **Magnesia (MgO)** comes from magnesite (MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂) or lime-precipitated Mg(OH)₂, melts at ~2850 °C as the basic refractory of steel furnaces, and floats as milk of magnesia; **Epsom salt (MgSO₄·7H₂O)** dissolves bitter and efflorescent, dosing patients and magnesium-hungry crops. **Bleaching powder (Ca(OCl)Cl)** is dry chlorine absorbed in slaked lime (Hasenclever drums), carries 35–38 % "available chlorine" liberated by dilute acid (Ca(OCl)Cl + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + Cl₂), bleaches through hypochlorous acid's nascent oxygen — never on wool or silk — and must be stored dry, since air's CO₂ and moisture slowly decompose it. **Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O)** is gypsum dehydrated at exactly ~120 °C — hotter gives dead-burnt anhydrite that never sets — and sets by re-hydration into interlocking gypsum crystals, expanding just enough to fill a surgeon's or sculptor's mould.
