## 1. Topic Introduction — The Borderline of Life

Viruses straddle the line between living and non-living: inside a host they replicate, evolve and respond; outside it they are inert crystals of protein and nucleic acid. They are the smallest obligate intracellular parasites known, and the bacteriophage that attacks bacteria became the workhorse of molecular biology. The syllabus covers virus structure, importance, and the T2/T4 bacteriophage.

%%%
## 2. General Characteristics of Viruses

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Size | 20–300 nm — pass through bacterial filters; visible only by electron microscopy |
| Nucleic acid | **Either DNA or RNA, never both**; single- or double-stranded |
| Structure | **Nucleocapsid** = nucleic acid core + **capsid** (protein coat of **capsomeres**); some have an **envelope** (lipid from host membrane with spikes) |
| Enzymes | Almost none — host cell supplies everything (some carry reverse transcriptase, neuraminidase, lysozyme) |
| Metabolism | None of their own — no ribosomes, no ATP generation |
| Reproduction | Only inside a living host cell; assembly-line style (parts made separately, then assembled) |
| Host range | Specific — animal, plant, bacterial (phage), fungal viruses |
| Crystallisation | Can be crystallised like chemicals (TMV crystals) — yet retain infectivity |

**Why "borderline living" (NEB 2024):** viruses show life only inside a host — they replicate, mutate and evolve there; outside, they are inert, non-metabolising, crystallisable particles. They fail most definitions of life (metabolism, independent reproduction, cellular organisation) yet possess heredity and evolution.

### 2.1 Shapes and examples

| Shape | Example | Disease |
|---|---|---|
| Helical (rod) | TMV (Tobacco mosaic virus) | Mosaic disease of tobacco |
| Icosahedral (polyhedral) | Adenovirus, poliovirus | Respiratory infection, polio |
| Enveloped sphere | Influenza virus, HIV | Flu, AIDS |
| Complex/head-tail | **T2/T4 bacteriophage** | Attacks *E. coli* |

## 3. Structure of T2/T4 Bacteriophage

The phage is a **complex (head-tail) virus** attacking *E. coli*:

| Part | Detail | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Head** | Icosahedral protein capsid (~95 nm) containing **double-stranded DNA** | Protects and stores the genome |
| **Collar/whiskers** | Disc under the head | Connects head and tail |
| **Sheath** | Contractile protein tube | Drives genome injection by contraction |
| **Base plate** | Hexagonal plate with pins | Anchors to host wall |
| **Tail fibres** | 6 long fibres | Recognise and bind host receptor sites |

### 3.1 Life cycles of the bacteriophage

**Lytic cycle (NEB 2024, 4m):**
1. **Adsorption:** tail fibres attach to specific receptors on the *E. coli* wall; base plate settles.
2. **Penetration:** sheath contracts; the tail core pierces the wall; phage DNA is injected into the host (the protein coat stays outside — "syringe" action).
3. **Biosynthesis:** phage DNA takes over the host machinery; host DNA is degraded; phage nucleic acids and capsid proteins are mass-produced.
4. **Maturation/assembly:** DNA heads, tails and fibres assemble into complete virions (~25 min post-infection).
5. **Lysis/release:** phage-encoded **lysozyme** ruptures the wall; ~100–200 new phages burst out and infect other cells.

**Lysogenic cycle:** phage DNA integrates into the host chromosome as a **prophage**; it replicates with the host for generations without killing it; stress (UV etc.) can induce it to enter the lytic cycle (temperate phages, e.g. λ phage).

## 4. Importance of Viruses

| Beneficial | Harmful |
|---|---|
| **Phage therapy** — bacteriophages kill drug-resistant bacteria | **Human diseases:** influenza, common cold, AIDS (HIV), hepatitis, polio, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) |
| **Molecular biology tools** — phages proved DNA is genetic material (Hershey–Chase); vectors in genetic engineering | **Plant diseases:** tobacco mosaic, leaf curl, yellowing of leaves — crop losses |
| **Biological control** of insect pests (baculoviruses) | **Animal diseases:** foot-and-mouth, rabies, bird flu |
| **Scientific models** — simplest self-replicating systems for studying heredity | **Bacteriophage lysis** spoils dairy fermentations and cultures |

## 5. Conceptual Relationships

- **Topic 18 → here:** bacteria are the smallest *cellular* life; viruses are acellular and depend on cells — including bacterial hosts.
- **Here → Topic 1 (cell):** the virus's lack of ribosomes and membranes defines the minimum requirements of cellular life by their absence.
- **Here → genetics:** Hershey–Chase's phage experiment established DNA as the genetic material; phage λ taught lysogeny and gene regulation.
- **Here → immunity (Class 12):** vaccines against viral diseases train the immune system without causing disease.

### 6.2 Model answers

**Q1 (3m) — Structure and reproduction of T2 phage + two uses.**
Answer: **Structure:** the T2 phage has an **icosahedral head** holding double-stranded DNA, a **collar**, a **contractile sheath**, a hexagonal **base plate** and six **tail fibres** that recognise the *E. coli* host. **Reproduction (lytic):** adsorption → penetration (DNA injected, sheath contracts) → biosynthesis of phage DNA and proteins using host machinery → maturation/assembly of new virions → lysis by lysozyme releasing 100–200 phages. **Uses:** (1) phage therapy against antibiotic-resistant bacteria; (2) vectors and tools in genetic engineering/molecular biology (Hershey–Chase proof of DNA as genetic material).

**Q2 (3m) — Why are viruses borderline living/non-living?**
Answer: **Living characters (inside host):** they reproduce, mutate and evolve, possess genetic material (DNA or RNA), and show host specificity. **Non-living characters (outside host):** no metabolism, no respiration, no ribosomes or enzymes of their own, can be **crystallised** like chemicals (TMV), and remain inert until infection. Because they display life only within a host cell, viruses are placed on the borderline between living and non-living.

**Q3 (4m) — Lytic cycle with diagram description.**
Answer: (1) **Adsorption** — tail fibres bind *E. coli* receptors; (2) **Penetration** — sheath contracts, DNA injected through the pierced wall; (3) **Biosynthesis** — host DNA degraded; phage DNA and capsid proteins synthesised on host ribosomes; (4) **Maturation** — heads, tails and fibres assemble into complete phages; (5) **Lysis** — lysozyme breaks the wall and ~100–200 virions are released to infect new cells. *(Draw: head with DNA, sheath contracting, host cell, intracellular assembly, burst release.)*

### 6.3 Common examination mistakes

1. Saying viruses contain both DNA and RNA — **only one, never both**.
2. Writing that the whole phage enters the cell — only the **DNA is injected**; the coat stays outside.
3. Omitting **lysozyme** in the lysis step.
4. Confusing lysogenic (prophage, no host death) with lytic (host lysis).
5. Forgetting that the capsid is made of **capsomeres**.
@@confusion@@
**Lytic vs lysogenic:** lytic kills the host immediately; lysogenic integrates as prophage and replicates with the host until induced to lyse.
%%%
**Capsid vs envelope:** capsid = protein coat made of capsomeres; envelope = host-derived lipid membrane with spikes (present in influenza, HIV; absent in phage).
%%%
**Virion vs virus:** virion = the complete infectious particle outside a cell; "virus" is the broader term.

@@summary@@
Viruses are the borderline of life: 20–300 nm particles of a single nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) wrapped in a capsid of capsomeres, sometimes within a host-derived envelope, with no metabolism, no ribosomes and no independent life — yet capable of replication, mutation and evolution inside a host. The T2 bacteriophage adds a contractile tail: head with dsDNA, collar, sheath, base plate and tail fibres that lock onto E. coli. Its lytic cycle — adsorption, penetration, biosynthesis, maturation, lysis — turns one cell into ~200 phages; its lysogenic cycle hides as a prophage. Viruses give medicine phage therapy and molecular biology its greatest tools, but also influenza, AIDS, polio and tobacco mosaic — heredity stripped to its barest form.
