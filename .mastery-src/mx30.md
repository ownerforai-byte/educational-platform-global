## 1. Topic Introduction — When Both Sides Must Agree

**Existence of a limit** is the theorem that merges the one-sided atoms: lim_{x→a} f(x) = L ⟺ LHL = RHL = L. This topic works the theorem in both directions — building limits from agreement and detecting DNE from disagreement.

%%%
## 2. Fundamental Concepts

### 2.1 The existence theorem

- **Theorem:** lim_{x→a} f(x) = L **if and only if** lim_{x→a⁻} f(x) = lim_{x→a⁺} f(x) = L (finite).
- Both directions matter: agreement ⟹ existence; existence ⟹ agreement. One infinite side, or unequal finite sides, ⟹ **DNE**.

### 2.2 Modes of non-existence

- **Jump:** LHL ≠ RHL, both finite (piecewise mismatch).
- **Split asymptote:** sides infinite with opposite signs (1/x at 0).
- **Unified blow-up:** both sides +∞ (1/x²) — still **no (finite) limit**; ∞ is behaviour, not a value.
- **Oscillation:** sin(1/x) near 0 never settles — sides exist in no sense; a genuinely wild DNE.

### 2.3 Working the theorem

- Protocol: compute LHL, compute RHL, compare. Equal (finite) ⟹ write the two-sided limit; unequal or infinite ⟹ DNE **with classification**.

### 2.4 Limits at infinity (companion reading)

- lim_{x→∞} f(x) = L describes far-field behaviour (horizontal asymptotes) — a different "approach" with the same spirit: the neighbourhood of infinity, not the point.

| **Horizontal asymptote** | lim_{x→±∞} f(x) = L | 1/x → 0 |

## 4. Detailed Explanation

### 4.1 Why agreement is the right criterion

- A two-sided approach visits *both* neighbourhoods; if the stories differ, no single L is being approached. Agreement is not a convention — it is what "approaching one value" means.

### 4.2 DNE has species

- "Does not exist" is a family: jumps, split asymptotes, unified blow-ups, oscillation. Classifying the species is the analytic content of a DNE answer.

### 4.3 Oscillation is the exotic case

- sin(1/x) takes every value in [−1, 1] infinitely often near 0: no sequence of x's settles it. One-sided machinery fails here — the function has no one-sided limits at all.

## 6. Common Confusions

**"= ∞" is not existence:** infinite behaviour is a description; a finite limit requires finite agreement.
%%%
**Both sides +∞ still DNE:** 1/x² has no two-sided limit — agreement must be to a *number*.
%%%
**Oscillation defeats side analysis:** some DNEs have no one-sided limits to compare.

%%%
## 7. Exam Application

**NEB-style (3m): Discuss the existence of lim_{x→0} f(x) for f(x) = { x + 2 if x < 0; x² if x > 0 }.** — **LHL:** first branch: 0 + 2 = **2**. **RHL:** second branch: 0² = **0**. **Comparison:** 2 ≠ 0 ⟹ **the two-sided limit does not exist** — a jump discontinuity at 0 (f is not defined at 0 at all, but the limit question is independent of that). The existence theorem in the negative direction.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Show that lim_{x→0} 1/x² does not exist even though both one-sided limits agree.** — LHL = RHL = **+∞** — agreement, but to an infinite "value". A limit must be a **finite number**: unbounded growth is not approach, it is escape. Hence **DNE**; the line y = 0... rather, x = 0 is a vertical asymptote with unified (+∞) behaviour on both sides.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→3} (x² − 9)/(x − 3).** — 0/0 ⟹ factor: (x − 3)(x + 3)/(x − 3) = x + 3 (x ≠ 3) ⟹ limit **6**. Both one-sided limits equal 6 — the existence theorem is satisfied silently by every clean computation; the algebra *produced* the agreement.

%%%
## 8. Deep Conceptual — Existence as a Two-Gate Theorem

- The existence theorem has **two gates in series**: Gate 1 — both one-sided limits must *exist* (finite, or both agreeing infinities by convention); Gate 2 — they must *agree*. Failing Gate 1 (oscillation, one-sided domain edge) and failing Gate 2 (jump) are different diseases with different cures and different graph pictures.
- **Why both gates are needed:** a function can pass Gate 2 trivially if you never check Gate 1 — e.g. claiming a limit exists because "both sides look fine" when one side oscillates. The gates force *evidence on each side independently* before any merge.
- **The merge is arithmetic, not averaging:** when LHL = RHL = 5, the limit is 5 *because that is the shared value* — not because 5 is the average of two candidates. When the sides disagree (3 and 7), no arithmetic (average 5, closer 3) rescues existence; disagreement is final.
%%%
## 9. Deep Conceptual — Disagreement as Information

- A failed existence test is not a dead end — it is a **measurement**. LHL = 3, RHL = 7 tells you the jump's exact size (4), which side owns which value, and where the graph's gap sits. In piecewise pricing, thresholds, and phase changes, the jump size is the meaningful quantity.
- **Existence is vocabulary, not virtue:** "the limit does not exist" is a complete, correct answer when the gates fail — the error is claiming existence without evidence, not the non-existence itself. Exams award the verdict only when both sides were computed.
- **Upgrades downstream:** the same two-gate structure reappears for derivatives (one-sided slopes must exist and agree — |x| fails Gate 2) and for continuity (value must match the merged limit). Calculus repeatedly asks: do the sides exist, and do they tell the same story?
%%%
## 10. Deep Conceptual — Existence Under Transformation

- Transformation preserves existence *when licensed*: factoring, conjugates, and standard-limit steering convert an indeterminate expression into an equivalent one **for x ≠ a**, so the limit's existence and value carry over. This is why you may cancel (x − 2) and conclude — the cancelled form agrees with the original everywhere except at the excluded point, and the limit ignores that point.
- **Illegal moves that fake existence:** dropping a factor without noting x ≠ a, averaging disagreeing sides, or applying a star outside its conditions (e.g. sin u/u with u in degrees). Each manufactures agreement where there is none.
- **The existence question precedes the value question:** first establish *that* the limit exists (via sides, squeeze, or theorem), *then* compute what it is. Reversing the order — computing a number and calling it the limit — is the root of most limit errors.

@@practiceQuestions@@
**NEB-style (3m): Discuss the existence of lim_{x→0} f(x) where f(x) = x + 2 for x < 0 and f(x) = x² for x > 0.** — **Left-hand limit:** the left neighbourhood uses the first branch: lim_{x→0⁻} (x + 2) = **2**. **Right-hand limit:** the second branch: lim_{x→0⁺} x² = **0**. **Existence theorem:** the two-sided limit exists iff LHL = RHL; here 2 ≠ 0 ⟹ **lim_{x→0} f(x) does not exist**. **Classification:** both one-sided limits are finite but unequal — a **jump discontinuity** at x = 0 (with jump size 2 − 0 = 2), even though f is not defined at 0 at all: the limit question is about the neighbourhoods, and the neighbourhoods tell different stories.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Show that lim_{x→0} 1/x² does not exist, even though the one-sided limits agree.** — **One-sided behaviour:** for x near 0 on either side, x² > 0 and small, so 1/x² is large positive: lim_{x→0⁻} 1/x² = lim_{x→0⁺} 1/x² = **+∞** — the sides *agree*. **Why that is not enough:** the existence theorem demands agreement to a **finite** value L; +∞ is not a number but a description of unbounded growth. Escape is not approach. **Verdict:** **lim_{x→0} 1/x² does not exist** — the species is a *unified blow-up*: x = 0 is a vertical asymptote approached to +∞ from both sides (contrast 1/x, whose sides split into −∞ and +∞). The moral: agreement must be to a number; infinity is never a limit value.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Evaluate lim_{x→3} (x² − 9)/(x − 3) and relate the computation to the existence theorem.** — **Substitution:** (9 − 9)/(3 − 3) = **0/0** ⟹ transform. **Factor:** x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3); cancelling (licensed for x ≠ 3) leaves x + 3. **Limit:** 3 + 3 = **6**. **Connection to the theorem:** the original expression is perfectly defined on both sides of 3, and the simplified form x + 3 is continuous, so lim_{x→3⁻} = lim_{x→3⁺} = 6 — the algebra **manufactured the agreement** the existence theorem requires. Every clean 0/0 resolution is the existence theorem operating silently: transform until both sides visibly agree, then read the common value.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = (x² − 16)/(x − 4) for x ≠ 4, f(4) = 9: compute LHL, RHL, the limit at 4 if it exists, f(4), and the continuity classification.** — **LHL:** for x → 4⁻, (x²−16)/(x−4) = x + 4 (factoring, licensed for x ≠ 4) ⟹ 4 + 4 = **8**. **RHL:** same simplified form ⟹ **8**. **Existence:** both sides exist and agree ⟹ **lim_{x→4} f(x) = 8**. **Value:** f(4) = **9**. **Classification:** limit exists, value differs ⟹ **removable discontinuity** — a single detached dot at (4, 9) above the hole at (4, 8); redefining f(4) = 8 repairs continuity. **Deep point:** the existence verdict came from the two-gate test (both sides computed independently, then compared), and only *after* existence did the value comparison classify the break — order of operations in the reasoning mirrors order of the theorems.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): For f(x) = x + 1 for x < 2, f(x) = 5 − x for x > 2, f(2) = 3: evaluate LHL, RHL, existence, f(2), and continuity.** — **LHL (x → 2⁻):** lim (x + 1) = **3**. **RHL (x → 2⁺):** lim (5 − x) = 5 − 2 = **3**. **Existence:** both exist and agree ⟹ **lim_{x→2} f(x) = 3**. **Value:** f(2) = **3**. **Continuity:** f(2) = 3 = limit 3, both parts exist ⟹ **f is continuous at 2** — the two line pieces meet at the point (2, 3) where the assigned value also sits. **Deep point:** this is the existence theorem and the three-part continuity test *both passing at one joint* — the pieces weld seamlessly and the value lands on the weld; contrast the case where the sides agree but f(2) is set elsewhere, which produces a removable break with the hole *occupied by a detached dot*.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): For f(x) = −x + 4 for x < 1, f(x) = x for x > 1, f(1) = 2: show that lim_{x→1} f(x) does not exist, and describe the graph at x = 1.** — **LHL:** lim_{x→1⁻} (−x + 4) = **3**. **RHL:** lim_{x→1⁺} x = **1**. **Existence verdict:** both one-sided limits exist but **3 ≠ 1** — Gate 2 fails ⟹ **the two-sided limit does not exist**. **Graph:** a **jump discontinuity** — the left branch arrives at height 3 (filled dot, since f(1) = 2 is assigned separately... note f(1) = 2 is neither 3 nor 1, so both dots at the joint are open and a detached point sits at (1, 2)). **Deep point:** the assigned value f(1) = 2 is a third, independent quantity — the jump is between 3 and 1 regardless of where the dot sits; a value assignment can never merge disagreeing sides, it can only relocate the dot.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): A student argues lim_{x→0} cos(1/x) exists because cos(1/x) is always between −1 and 1. Assess and correct.** — **The error:** boundedness is not convergence. The squeeze theorem needs the rails to **converge to a common value**; here the rails are the constants −1 and 1, which converge to *different* values (−1 ≠ 1), so the squeeze pins nothing — it only certifies boundedness. **Correct analysis:** as x → 0, 1/x → ∞ and cos(1/x) cycles through [−1, 1] infinitely fast, visiting 1, 0, −1 repeatedly with no settling trend ⟹ **lim_{x→0} cos(1/x) does not exist** (oscillatory failure at Gate 1). **Contrast:** x·cos(1/x) → 0 by squeeze, because the rails −|x| and |x| both → 0 — converging rails squeeze the middle. **Deep point:** the squeeze theorem's power is exactly the convergence of its rails; bounded oscillation inside non-converging rails proves only that the function stays in a band, and "stays in a band" is not "approaches a value."
@@summary@@
**Existence of a limit** is the merge rule: lim_{x→a} f(x) = L ⟺ **LHL = RHL = L**, finitely. Working it means computing both sides and comparing — agreement certifies existence (every clean 0/0 resolution, like (x²−9)/(x−3) → 6, manufactures that agreement silently), and disagreement certifies DNE **with a species**: **jump** (finite sides, unequal — x+2 vs x² give 2 vs 0), **split asymptote** (1/x: −∞ vs +∞), **unified blow-up** (1/x²: both +∞ — agreement to infinity is still non-existence, since escape is not approach), and the exotic **oscillation** (sin(1/x) at 0, where no one-sided limit even exists). Limits at infinity share the spirit at the far field: lim_{x→±∞} f(x) = L draws the horizontal asymptote. Both sides, then compare, then classify — the theorem turns one-sided bookkeeping into every verdict about existence.
