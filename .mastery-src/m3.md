## 1. Topic Introduction — Engineering Life

Biotechnology applies living systems (or their components) to make useful products. Microbiology and biotechnology merged when scientists learned to cut, paste and multiply DNA inside bacteria. This topic covers the impacts of biotechnology in the field of microbiology — from genetic engineering tools to industrial fermentation, medicine, agriculture and the environment.

%%%
## 2. Fundamental Concepts — Tools of Genetic Engineering

| Tool | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Restriction endonucleases** | Cut DNA at specific palindromic sequences ("molecular scissors") | EcoRI cuts GAATTC |
| **DNA ligase** | Joins DNA fragments ("molecular glue") | Seals insert into vector |
| **Vectors** | Carry foreign DNA into host cells | Plasmids, bacteriophages, cosmids |
| **Host organisms** | Multiply the engineered DNA | *E. coli*, yeast |
| **PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)** | Amplifies DNA in vitro exponentially | Thermus aquaticus Taq polymerase |
| **Gel electrophoresis** | Separates DNA fragments by size | DNA fingerprinting |
| **DNA probes / sequencing** | Detect and read DNA sequences | Human Genome Project |

**Recombinant DNA technology in five steps:** isolate the gene → cut gene and vector with the same restriction enzyme → ligate into the vector (recombinant DNA) → transform the host (*E. coli*) → select, clone and express the product.

## 3. Impacts of Biotechnology in Microbiology (field by field)

### 3.1 Medicine and health

| Product | Made by/using | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Human insulin (Humulin)** | *E. coli* carrying the human insulin gene (1982 — first rDNA drug) | Diabetes |
| Growth hormone, interferons, blood factors | Engineered bacteria/yeast | Dwarfism, antiviral, haemophilia |
| **Vaccines** | Hepatitis B vaccine from engineered yeast | Disease prevention |
| **Antibiotics** | Fermentation by *Streptomyces*, *Penicillium* | Infection control |
| Gene therapy & DNA vaccines | Vector-delivered genes | Emerging treatments |

### 3.2 Agriculture

| Application | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Bt crops** | *Bacillus thuringiensis* toxin gene in cotton, maize — insect resistance |
| **Golden rice** | β-carotene (vitamin A precursor) engineered into rice |
| Roundup-ready soybean | Herbicide tolerance |
| **Biofertilisers** | *Rhizobium*, *Azotobacter*, cyanobacteria cultures as inoculants |
| **Biopesticides** | Bt sprays, viral pesticides (baculovirus) |
| **Transgenic animals/plants** | Improved yield, shelf life (Flavr Savr tomato) |

### 3.3 Industry and food

| Application | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Fermentation industry** | Beer, wine, bread (yeast); vinegar, lactic acid, citric acid |
| **Enzyme production** | Detergent proteases/lipases, amylases from microbes |
| **Single-cell protein (SCP)** | *Spirulina*, yeast biomass as protein source |
| Dairy | Curd, cheese by *Lactobacillus*, *Streptococcus* |
| Biofuels | Ethanol from fermentation; biogas from methanogens |

### 3.4 Environment

| Application | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sewage treatment** | Aerobic and anaerobic microbial digestion of organic waste |
| **Bioremediation** | *Pseudomonas putida* ("superbug") degrades oil spills |
| **Biogas** | Methanogens produce methane from dung and sludge |
| Biosensors | Microbes detect pollutants |

## 4. Impacts — A Balanced View

| Positive impacts | Concerns |
|---|---|
| Cheap, pure human proteins (insulin without animal pancreas) | **Ethics:** tampering with life, "playing God" |
| Higher food yield, nutrition (golden rice) | **Safety:** allergens, gene flow to wild plants ("superweeds") |
| Cleaner environment (bioremediation, biofuels) | **Bioterrorism:** engineered pathogens |
| Rapid disease diagnosis (PCR, ELISA) | **Patent/ownership** of living organisms; cost access |
| Conservation (cryopreservation, tissue culture) | Antibiotic-resistance genes escaping |

## 6. Exam Orientation

### 6.2 NEB-style practice questions with model answers

**Q1 (3m) — What is recombinant DNA technology? List its steps.**
Answer: Recombinant DNA technology joins DNA from two different sources (typically a human gene and a bacterial plasmid) to create a construct expressed in a host. Steps: (1) isolate the target gene; (2) cut gene and vector with the **same restriction enzyme**; (3) join with **DNA ligase** to form recombinant DNA; (4) transform the host (*E. coli*); (5) select clones and express/harvest the product (e.g. human insulin).

**Q2 (2m) — Name two medically important products of biotechnology.**
Answer: (1) **Human insulin (Humulin)** — produced by engineered *E. coli* for diabetes; (2) **Hepatitis B vaccine** — produced by engineered yeast. (Also acceptable: growth hormone, interferon.)

**Q3 (3m) — Explain the impacts of biotechnology in agriculture.**
Answer: (1) **Bt crops** — the *Bacillus thuringiensis* toxin gene confers insect resistance in cotton and maize, cutting pesticide use; (2) **Golden rice** — β-carotene-engineered rice fights vitamin-A deficiency; (3) **biofertilisers and biopesticides** — *Rhizobium* cultures and Bt sprays replace chemicals; (4) herbicide-tolerant and longer-shelf-life transgenic crops raise yields.

**Q4 (2m) — What is bioremediation? Give an example.**
Answer: The use of microorganisms to degrade pollutants. Example: *Pseudomonas putida* ("superbug") cleans up oil spills; microbial sewage treatment and methanogen biogas digesters are related applications.

### 6.3 Common examination mistakes

1. Writing restriction enzymes "join DNA" — they **cut**; **ligase** joins.
2. Confusing PCR (amplification) with cloning (multiplication in hosts).
3. Saying insulin is extracted from pancreases in modern industry — it is **rDNA-produced** since 1982.
4. Omitting both benefits and concerns when asked for "impacts" — the word includes risks.

**Bt gene vs Bt spray:** same Bacillus thuringiensis toxin — engineered into crops (Bt cotton) or sprayed as biopesticide.

@@summary@@
Biotechnology reshaped microbiology by turning bacteria into factories. Restriction enzymes cut, DNA ligase glues, plasmids and phages ship, and E. coli grows — recombinant DNA in five steps. The impacts: medicine gains human insulin, hepatitis B vaccine, interferons and antibiotics; agriculture gains Bt cotton, golden rice and Rhizobium biofertilisers; industry gains fermentation products, enzymes and single-cell protein; the environment gains sewage treatment, biogas and oil-eating Pseudomonas. The same power raises concerns — allergens, superweeds, bioterrorism and the ethics of patenting life. Biotechnology's promise and peril are the same thing: life, deliberately rewritten.
