## 1. Topic Introduction — Kekulé's Structure: The Model That Almost Worked

Kekulé (1865) proposed benzene as a cyclic hexagon with alternating single and double bonds — the first correct *skeleton* in organic chemistry. The structure explains the formula C₆H₆, the monosubstitution uniqueness, and even the o/m/p isomer pattern — but it fails on bond lengths, hydrogenation heat, and substitution chemistry. Its failures are as instructive as its successes.

%%%
## 2. Theorem Mirror

- **The proposal:** a six-membered ring with three alternating C=C and C–C bonds; two equivalent structures related by "breathing" (Kekulé's own oscillation story).
- **What it explains:** C₆H₆ with tetravalent carbon; only one monosubstitution product (all six positions equivalent by symmetry); exactly three disubstitution patterns (ortho, meta, para).
- **What it fails:** measured C–C bond length in benzene is **139 pm** — between C–C (154 pm) and C=C (134 pm), not alternating 154/134; heat of hydrogenation 208 kJ/mol vs 360 expected for three isolated double bonds; benzene *substitutes* rather than adds, unlike alkenes.

%%%
## 3. Deep Conceptual — The Three Failures as Three Experiments

- **Bond lengths (X-ray/electron diffraction):** alternating bonds would give two distinct lengths; measurement shows one — 139 pm, the average weighted toward the double-bond character. A structural proposal that predicts two lengths and reality shows one has been falsified at the 10-pm level.
- **Hydrogenation calorimetry:** three isolated double bonds should release 360 kJ/mol; benzene releases 208 — the 152 kJ/mol deficit *is* the delocalisation, measured. The Kekulé structure overstates benzene's energy.
- **Chemical behaviour:** an alkene with three double bonds should decolourise bromine water instantly and add H₂ readily; benzene does neither without catalysts or forcing. Reactivity is the third, chemical witness against simple alternation.

%%%
## 4. Deep Conceptual — Why the Kekulé Structure Survives on Every Page

- Modern resonance theory *keeps* the two Kekulé forms as contributing structures: benzene is a resonance hybrid of the two, with equal weights — the real structure is neither, but both are bookkeeping entries toward it. The hexagon with a circle is the hybrid's picture; the two Kekulé forms are its algebra.
- **The o/m/p isomer count as a hidden success:** the ring model (any ring, not just Kekulé's alternating one) makes all six positions equivalent ⟹ one mono product; disubstitution gives exactly 2,3 (ortho), 1,3 (meta), 1,4 (para) — three patterns, matching experiment. Alkenyl chain models cannot reproduce this; the ring was right even when the bond alternation was wrong.

## 6. Common Confusions

**"Benzene alternates rapidly between two structures":** no — it is a single time-independent hybrid; the two Kekulé forms are mathematical components, not flickering states. **"The circle means six free electrons":** the circle is a drawing convention for full delocalisation over the ring, not a separate pool of electrons.

@@practiceQuestions@@
**NEB 2024 (2m): Draw the two Kekule structures of benzene. Why are all carbon-carbon bond lengths in benzene equal?** — **The two structures:** the same hexagon with the three double bonds placed on alternating edges — form A with double bonds at bonds 1-2, 3-4, 5-6; form B with them at 2-3, 4-6... equivalently at 2-3, 4-5, 6-1; the two are related by shifting every double bond one position around the ring. **Why equal lengths:** neither Kekulé form describes real benzene — the actual molecule is a *resonance hybrid* of the two with equal contributions; every C–C bond is a single bond in one form and a double bond in the other, so each has identical partial double-bond character ⟹ one uniform bond length, measured **139 pm** — between C–C (154 pm) and C=C (134 pm). **Deep point:** the equal-length fact is the experimental fingerprint of the equal weights — if the two forms contributed unequally, the lengths would split; symmetry forces equality, and the measurement confirms it.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): The measured C-C bond length in benzene is 139 pm. Explain why this single number refutes the Kekule alternating-bond structure and supports the resonance hybrid.** — **Kekulé's prediction:** alternating single (154 pm) and double (134 pm) bonds ⟹ two distinct lengths in the ring, alternating around it. **Measurement:** one length only, 139 pm. **Refutation:** a structure predicting two lengths cannot survive a measurement showing one — the alternating model is falsified. **Hybrid support:** in the resonance hybrid every bond carries identical partial double-bond character (bond order ≈ 1.5), so all six bonds must be identical; 139 pm sits between 154 and 134, exactly where bond order 1.5 places it (closer to the double-bond end, as the π contribution is strong). **Deep point:** structure determination in chemistry is this loop — propose, predict a measurable, measure, keep or kill; the 139 pm reading is the cleanest kill-and-replace in the history of the benzene problem.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): Benzene's heat of hydrogenation is 208 kJ/mol, while cyclohexene's is 120 kJ/mol. Show what the Kekule structure would predict and what the discrepancy means.** — **Kekulé prediction:** three independent double bonds ⟹ 3 × 120 = **360 kJ/mol**. **Observed:** **208 kJ/mol**. **Discrepancy:** 360 − 208 = **152 kJ/mol** — benzene releases 152 kJ/mol *less* than three isolated double bonds would, meaning its starting energy is already 152 kJ/mol lower: the π electrons are delocalised over the whole ring (the resonance stabilisation). **Chemical consequence:** hydrogenation (an addition) destroys the aromatic π system and must climb this energy barrier — which is why benzene resists addition and prefers substitution reactions that preserve the sextet. **Deep point:** the discrepancy is not an error to be explained away but the *measurement* of resonance energy — the Kekulé structure fails numerically in exactly the way that reveals the truth it could not contain.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): Using the ring structure of benzene, predict the number of (a) monosubstitution and (b) disubstitution products of benzene, naming the disubstitution patterns.** — **(a) One.** All six ring positions are equivalent by the sixfold symmetry of the regular hexagon — substituting any hydrogen gives the same compound (e.g. chlorobenzene, however the substitution site is drawn). **(b) Three.** With one substituent fixed at position 1, the second can occupy: position 2 or 6 (equivalent) ⟹ **ortho (1,2)**; position 3 or 5 (equivalent) ⟹ **meta (1,3)**; position 4 ⟹ **para (1,4)**. **Historical note:** these counts were known before any structure — the ring model was *deduced* partly from them; a chain or isolated-double-bond model predicts different (wrong) counts. **Deep point:** isomer counting is structural inference — the observed "one and three" is data that any correct benzene structure must reproduce; Kekulé's ring passed this test even though his bond alternation later failed, which is why the ring survived the theory's evolution.
@@summary@@
Kekulé's 1865 hexagon gave benzene its skeleton, its isomer counts, and its two contributing structures — and failed on three independent experiments: one bond length (139 pm) where it predicted two, 208 kJ/mol of hydrogenation where it predicted 360, and substitution where it predicted addition. Modern resonance theory keeps the two forms as equal contributors to a single hybrid, the circle-icon of every textbook. The model's failures are the roadmap: each one points directly at delocalisation.
