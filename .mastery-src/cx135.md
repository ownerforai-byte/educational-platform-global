## 1. Topic Introduction — The Haber Process: Feeding the World from Air

Ammonia is the gateway to every nitrogenous fertiliser. The Haber process combines **N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ + heat (ΔH ≈ −92 kJ/mol)** under high pressure, moderate temperature and an iron catalyst — the single most consequential equilibrium compromise in industry, fixing the nitrogen that feeds roughly half the world's population.

%%%
## 2. Theorem Mirror

- **Reaction:** N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ/mol; 4 mol gas → 2 mol gas.
- **Conditions:** pressure **200–300 atm**; temperature **450–500 °C**; catalyst **finely divided iron** promoted with K₂O/Al₂O₃ (or Mo) — the promoters increase surface activity and resist sintering.
- **Feed gases:** N₂ from fractional distillation of liquid air; H₂ from steam reforming of natural gas (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂, then water-gas shift CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ with the CO₂ absorbed off).
- **Flow sheet:** N₂ + H₂ purification → compression (multistage) → catalytic converter (heat exchangers recover reaction heat) → condenser (NH₃ liquefies, bp −33 °C) → **recycle of unreacted gases**.
- **Le Chatelier:** high pressure shifts toward fewer moles (NH₃ side); low temperature favours the exothermic product but slows the rate ⟹ the 450–500 °C compromise.

%%%
## 3. Deep Conceptual — The Compromise That Runs the World

- Thermodynamics and kinetics pull opposite ways: **low T maximises yield, high T maximises rate.** The 450–500 °C operating point is neither the yield optimum nor the rate optimum — it is the *profit* optimum: enough rate per pass to make the plant pay, with the yield shortfall recovered by recycling. The catalyst does not shift the equilibrium (it cannot) — it lets the compromise temperature be as low as it is.
- **Why the recycle is the process's second engine:** per-pass conversion is only ~15% at plant conditions; the unreacted 85% is condensed-free N₂/H₂ that is recompressed and returned. Overall conversion approaches ~97% — the recycle loop converts a poor single-pass equilibrium into an excellent overall yield, and it is the reason high pressure is worth paying for twice (better equilibrium *and* faster loop turnover).

%%%
## 4. Deep Conceptual — Why High Pressure Is a Three-Layer Answer

- **Equilibrium layer:** 4 mol gas → 2 mol gas; Le Chatelier ⟹ pressure favours the ammonia side, raising the per-pass mole fraction.
- **Rate layer:** high pressure concentrates the molecules — more collisions per second — so the iron catalyst works on a denser gas, accelerating the slow N₂ activation.
- **Separation layer:** high pressure raises NH₃'s dew point, so simple cooling liquefies the product cleanly — the condenser works because the pressure did. **The full answer to "why high pressure" is all three layers**, and examiners award the equilibrium one but love the other two.

## 6. Common Confusions

**"The catalyst increases yield":** it increases *rate* only — the equilibrium position is fixed by T and P; the catalyst's gift is a lower compromise temperature. **"Low temperature is best":** best for equilibrium, fatal for rate — the plant would produce almost nothing per day.

@@practiceQuestions@@
**NEB 2024 (5m): Describe the Haber process for ammonia manufacture. Why is high pressure used?** — **The process:** nitrogen (from fractional distillation of liquid air) and hydrogen (from steam reforming of natural gas: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂, followed by the shift reaction CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ with CO₂ removed) are purified (catalyst poisons removed), compressed to **200–300 atm**, and passed over a **finely divided iron catalyst (promoted with K₂O/Al₂O₃)** at **450–500 °C**: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ + 92 kJ. The exit gases are cooled in a condenser — ammonia (bp −33 °C) liquefies and is drawn off; the unreacted N₂/H₂ are recompressed and **recycled** to the converter, lifting the overall conversion from ~15% per pass to ~97%. **Why high pressure (three layers):** (1) **equilibrium** — the forward reaction reduces 4 mol of gas to 2 mol, so Le Chatelier's principle shifts the equilibrium toward ammonia as pressure rises; (2) **rate** — higher pressure packs more molecules into the converter volume, increasing collision frequency on the catalyst surface and speeding the slow N₂ activation; (3) **separation** — high pressure raises ammonia's dew point, so the condenser liquefies the product efficiently. **Deep point:** the three layers are independent — even if the equilibrium argument were weaker, rate and condensation would still justify the pressure; complete answers quote all three, and the recycle arrow in the flow sheet is what makes the moderate per-pass conversion acceptable.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): The Haber reaction is exothermic. Explain why the plant runs at 450-500 C rather than at a lower temperature, and what makes the chosen temperature defensible.** — **Thermodynamics says low:** the forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH = −92 kJ/mol), so low temperature shifts the equilibrium toward ammonia — more product per pass. **Kinetics says high:** reaction rate falls exponentially as temperature drops (Arrhenius); at very low T the N≡N triple bond's activation is so slow that yield-per-day approaches zero — a plant at 100 °C would have a beautiful equilibrium and no output. **The compromise:** 450–500 °C is hot enough for the iron catalyst to deliver a useful rate per pass, cool enough to keep a workable equilibrium; the yield shortfall is recovered by the recycle loop. **The catalyst's role:** it does not move the equilibrium (impossible) — it lowers the activation energy, allowing the compromise temperature to be *lower* than an uncatalysed process could afford. **Deep point:** "compromise conditions" is the exam phrase that unlocks every industrial-equilibrium question — the operating point is chosen where rate × equilibrium × recycle-economics is maximised, and saying "profit optimum" rather than "best yield" is the understanding mark.
%%%
**NEB-style (5m): Only about 15% of the entering gases convert to ammonia on each pass through the converter. Explain how the process nevertheless achieves ~97% overall conversion, and why the unreacted gases are not wasted.** — **The recycle loop:** after each pass, the condenser removes ammonia as liquid; the remaining gas — mostly unreacted N₂ and H₂ with only traces of NH₃ — is recompressed and fed back to the converter, mixing with fresh feed. **Arithmetic of looping:** each pass converts ~15% of what enters it; after n passes the cumulative conversion approaches 1 − (0.85)ⁿ — after ~25 passes the residual unconverted fraction falls below 2% ⟹ ~97–98% overall. **Why not wasted:** the unreacted gases are *reactants already paid for* — purged they would be an economic and environmental loss (and hydrogen vented is an energy loss); recycled they cost only recompression energy. **Design note:** a small purge stream removes the argon (from the nitrogen source) that would otherwise accumulate and dilute the loop. **Deep point:** the recycle loop is the industrial answer to an unfavourable single-pass equilibrium — it decouples *per-pass conversion* (set by the equilibrium at operating conditions) from *overall conversion* (set by how many loops you run), and that decoupling is what makes mildly-yielding equilibria commercially viable.
%%%
**NEB-style (6m): Write the reactions by which the hydrogen feed for the Haber process is obtained from natural gas, and state why both carbon oxides must be removed before the converter.** — **Steam reforming:** CH₄ + H₂O --(Ni, ~800–900 °C)--> CO + 3H₂ — the natural gas and steam pass over a nickel catalyst. **Water-gas shift:** CO + H₂O --(Fe/Cr catalyst, ~400 °C)--> CO₂ + H₂ — converts the toxic carbon monoxide into additional hydrogen; the CO₂ is absorbed off (e.g. by hot K₂CO₃ solution) leaving an H₂-rich stream. **Removal necessity:** (1) **CO is a catalyst poison** — it binds to the iron surface and blocks the N₂/H₂ activation sites (the same coordination that makes CO lethal to haemoglobin); (2) **CO₂ would re-enter the product** — any oxide passing through dilutes the synthesis gas and contaminates the ammonia stream. Purification precedes compression because poisons destroy the promoted-iron catalyst's activity irreversibly. **Deep point:** the feed-train chemistry is as examinable as the converter — the two equations plus the poisoning argument (CO on iron parallels CO on haemoglobin) turn a "describe the process" answer into a chemistry answer.
@@summary@@
The Haber process fixes atmospheric nitrogen by the compromise of all compromises: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ at 200–300 atm, 450–500 °C over promoted iron — hot enough to run, cool enough to yield, pressurised for three independent reasons (equilibrium's fewer moles, collision-dense rate, ammonia's raised dew point), and looped so that a 15% per-pass conversion becomes ~97% overall. The catalyst lowers the compromise temperature; the recycle pays the equilibrium's tax; and the feed train (reforming + shift + purification) is the quiet half of the flow sheet where the catalyst's life is protected.
