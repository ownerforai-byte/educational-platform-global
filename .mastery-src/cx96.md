## 1. Topic Introduction — The Chemistry of Carbon

**Organic chemistry** is the chemistry of carbon compounds — hydrocarbons and their derivatives. Carbon is the only element that builds chains, rings and branches of itself in endless variety, which is why organic compounds outnumber all the compounds of every other element combined. This opening topic sets the definition, the historical birth (Wöhler's urea), the sources, and the characteristic properties that make organic compounds a world of their own.

%%%
## 2. Fundamental Concepts

### 2.1 Definition and scope

- **Organic compounds:** compounds of **carbon** (almost always with hydrogen, and often with O, N, S, halogens, P).
- **Organic chemistry:** the branch of chemistry dealing with these compounds — their structure, properties, preparation and reactions.
- Exceptions kept in mind: carbonates (CO₃²⁻), bicarbonates, carbides, cyanides (CN⁻) and oxides of carbon (CO, CO₂) are studied under inorganic chemistry even though they contain carbon.

### 2.2 The death of the vital force theory

- **Berzelius (early 1800s):** organic compounds could be made only by living things under the influence of a **"vital force"** — hence they could never be synthesised in the laboratory.
- **Wöhler (1828):** heated ammonium cyanate, an *inorganic* salt, and obtained **urea**, an organic compound found in urine: **NH₄OCN → NH₂CONH₂ (urea)**. The vital force theory collapsed, and synthesis of organic compounds began.

### 2.3 Sources of organic compounds

- **Plants and animals** — carbohydrates, oils, proteins, dyes, medicines.
- **Coal** (coal tar → benzene, phenol, dyes), **petroleum and natural gas** — the great industrial feedstocks.
- **Laboratory/industrial synthesis** — from simple molecules upward.

### 2.4 Characteristic properties of organic compounds

- **Covalent** bonding; exist as discrete molecules.
- **Low melting and boiling points** (weak intermolecular forces); many are gases or volatile liquids.
- **Flammable** — burn to CO₂ and H₂O (carbon + hydrogen skeleton).
- **Non-conductors** of electricity (no ions).
- **Generally insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents** (like dissolves like).
- **Show isomerism** — same molecular formula, different compounds.
- **Reactions are slow** — molecular reactions need activation energy; often need catalysts.
- **Numbers:** several tens of millions known — more than all inorganic compounds together.

## 4. Detailed Explanation

### 4.1 Why carbon is unique

- Carbon sits at the middle of the second period: neither too small to bond with itself nor too large to form strong bonds. Its **four valence electrons** let it form four strong covalent bonds, and its atoms **link to each other** in chains, branches and rings — catenation. This twin gift (tetravalency + catenation) is the whole reason organic chemistry is a separate subject (developed in the next two topics).

### 4.2 Reading Wöhler's experiment correctly

- Ammonium cyanate and urea have the **same composition** (CH₄N₂O) but are entirely different compounds — an early, dramatic example of **isomerism**. One rearrangement of atoms destroyed a philosophical doctrine: the same elements obey the same chemistry inside and outside a living cell.

### 4.3 Organic versus inorganic — a first pass

- Organic compounds are **molecular** (covalent, low formula mass units), so they melt and boil low, burn, and dissolve in non-polar solvents. Inorganic compounds are frequently **ionic**, so they are crystalline solids with high melting points, conduct when molten/dissolved, and resist burning. The full comparison table is the next topic's business.

## 6. Common Confusions

**"Organic" does not mean "grown without chemicals":** the advertising sense and the chemistry sense are different. In chemistry, organic = carbon-based covalent compound, whether from a leaf or a factory.
%%%
**CO₂ and carbonates are not organic:** despite containing carbon, their chemistry is that of inorganic salts and oxides; the conventional boundary excludes them.
%%%
**Wöhler made urea from ammonium cyanate, not from ammonia and cyanic acid directly in a cell:** the point is inorganic → organic in one flask, ending vitalism.

## 7. Exam Application

**NEB-style (2m): Define organic chemistry. Why are carbonates and cyanides excluded from it despite containing carbon?** — **Organic chemistry** is the chemistry of the compounds of carbon — hydrocarbons and their derivatives — studied together because of their common covalent, molecular character. **Exclusion:** carbonates, bicarbonates, carbides, cyanides and the oxides of carbon (CO, CO₂) retain the ionic/salt-like chemistry of inorganic substances and lack the C–H framework and catenation characteristic of organic compounds, so by convention they are treated as inorganic.
%%%
**NEB-style (3m): Describe Wöhler's synthesis of urea and state its historical significance.** — In 1828 **Friedrich Wöhler** heated the inorganic salt **ammonium cyanate (NH₄OCN)** and obtained **urea (NH₂CONH₂)**, an organic compound excreted in urine: **NH₄OCN → NH₂CONH₂**. **Significance:** (i) it refuted Berzelius's **vital force theory**, which claimed organic compounds could only be made by living organisms; (ii) it proved that the same chemical laws govern compounds inside and outside living cells; (iii) it opened the era of synthetic organic chemistry — today most organic compounds are made, not extracted. The two compounds also have the same molecular formula (CH₄N₂O) but different structures — an early example of isomerism.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): State four characteristic properties of organic compounds.** — (i) They are **covalent molecular** compounds, so they have **low melting and boiling points**. (ii) They are **flammable**, burning to CO₂ and H₂O. (iii) They are **non-conductors of electricity** (no ions) and are generally **insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents**. (iv) They commonly show **isomerism** and their reactions are comparatively **slow**, often requiring catalysts.

@@summary@@
**Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds** — hydrocarbons and their derivatives, with carbonates, carbides, cyanides and the carbon oxides left to inorganic chemistry by convention. The subject was born when **Wöhler (1828)** converted the inorganic salt **ammonium cyanate (NH₄OCN) into urea (NH₂CONH₂)**, destroying **Berzelius's vital force theory** and proving one chemistry for the living and the non-living world — while accidentally demonstrating isomerism, since both substances are CH₄N₂O. Organic compounds come from plants, animals, coal, petroleum, natural gas and the laboratory, and they behave as a family: **covalent molecules** with **low melting and boiling points**, **flammable**, **non-conducting**, **soluble in organic solvents rather than water**, rich in **isomers**, and slow-reacting — tens of millions of them, all because carbon can bond to itself.
