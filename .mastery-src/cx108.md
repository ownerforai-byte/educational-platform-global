## 1. Topic Introduction — How Bonds Break and Why It Matters

Every organic reaction begins with a covalent bond breaking. **How** it breaks decides everything downstream: **homolytic fission** splits the pair equally, creating **free radicals**; **heterolytic fission** gives both electrons to one atom, creating **ions** (carbocation + anion). The mechanism — the step-by-step path of bond breaking and making — is written with these two moves and the arrows that show where electrons go.

%%%
## 2. Fundamental Concepts

### 2.1 What a reaction mechanism is

- A **mechanism** is the **step-by-step description** of a reaction: which bonds break, which form, in what order, through which intermediates, catalysed how.
- Electron movement is shown with **curly arrows**: full-head arrow = a **pair** of electrons moves; half-head (fishhook) = a **single electron** moves.

### 2.2 Homolytic fission (homolysis)

- The covalent pair **splits equally**: each fragment takes **one electron**.
- Products: **free radicals** — neutral species with an **unpaired electron**, written X·.
- **A–B → A· + B·** — favoured by **symmetrical (non-polar) bonds** and by **heat or light**.
- Example: **Cl₂ → 2Cl·** (sunlight) — the initiation step of chlorination of methane.

### 2.3 Heterolytic fission (heterolysis)

- The covalent pair goes **entirely to the more electronegative atom**.
- Products: **a cation and an anion** — **A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻** (B keeps both electrons).
- Favoured by **polar bonds** (large electronegativity difference) and polar solvents.
- Example: **(CH₃)₃C–Cl → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Cl⁻** in water — the carbocation of substitution reactions; HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻.

### 2.4 The intermediates

- **Free radical** (from homolysis): neutral, 7-electron carbon or halogen, unpaired electron, paramagnetic.
- **Carbocation** (heterolysis, carbon loses the pair): 6-electron carbon, positive, electron-deficient — an electrophile.
- **Carbanion** (heterolysis, carbon keeps the pair): negative, electron-rich — a nucleophile.

## 4. Detailed Explanation

### 4.1 Choosing between the two fissions

- The bond's own polarity decides: a nearly **non-polar** bond (Cl–Cl, C–C) splits evenly — homolysis under heat/light. A **polar** bond (C–Cl, H–Cl) splits to the electronegative partner — heterolysis. Solvent and conditions modulate: non-polar solvents + light favour radicals; polar solvents favour ions.

### 4.2 Why radicals and ions behave differently

- Radicals are **neutral but electron-hungry** (7-electron species) — they attack σ/π bonds in chain reactions (initiation → propagation → termination).
- Carbocations are **positively charged and 6-electron** — they seek electron pairs (electrophilic behaviour). Carbanions **offer** pairs (nucleophilic behaviour). The fission mode thus selects the reagent class — the next topic's business.

### 4.3 Reading mechanisms

- Every organic mechanism is a chain of these events: heterolysis creates an electrophile, a nucleophile attacks, a proton shifts, a radical abstracts H. Learn to ask at each step: *which bond breaks, and which way do the electrons go?*

## 6. Common Confusions

**Radicals are neutral, not negative:** the unpaired electron carries no charge — Cl· is neutral; Cl⁻ is the anion from heterolysis.
%%%
**The arrow shows electron movement, not atom movement:** in heterolysis the H⁺ "leaves" because its electrons left with the other atom — track electrons, not protons.
%%%
**Homolysis needs energy input:** light or heat is required to split a symmetrical bond — radicals do not appear spontaneously at room temperature.

## 7. Exam Application

**NEB-style (3m): What is homolytic fission? Explain with an example.** — **Homolytic fission (homolysis)** is the breaking of a covalent bond in which the **bonding electron pair is divided equally** between the two atoms, each taking **one electron**. The products are **free radicals** — neutral species carrying an unpaired electron, written with a dot: **A–B → A· + B·**. It is favoured by **non-polar (symmetrical) bonds** and by **heat or light**. Example: in sunlight, **Cl₂ → 2Cl·**; each chlorine radical has seven valence electrons with one unpaired, and these radicals initiate the **chain chlorination of methane** (Cl· + CH₄ → CH₃· + HCl).
%%%
**NEB-style (3m): What is heterolytic fission? Explain with an example.** — **Heterolytic fission (heterolysis)** is bond breaking in which the **entire bonding electron pair passes to the more electronegative atom**, producing a **cation and an anion**: **A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻**. It is favoured by **polar bonds** and **polar solvents**. Example: **tert-butyl chloride in water, (CH₃)₃C–Cl → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Cl⁻** — chlorine takes the pair, leaving a **carbocation**, a 6-electron positively charged carbon that behaves as an electrophile. Similarly **HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻** in water.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Distinguish between a free radical, a carbocation and a carbanion.** — A **free radical** is a **neutral** species with an **unpaired electron** (7 electrons at carbon, paramagnetic), formed by **homolytic fission** (Cl·, CH₃·). A **carbocation** is a **positively charged, 6-electron carbon** formed when carbon **loses** the bonding pair in heterolysis ((CH₃)₃C⁺) — electron-deficient and electrophilic. A **carbanion** is a **negatively charged carbon that has gained the bonding pair** (CH₃⁻) — electron-rich and nucleophilic.

@@importantConcepts@@
The mode of fission selects the chemistry: split evenly and you get radicals running chain reactions; split unevenly and you get ions that attract or donate pairs — every mechanism is built from these two moves.
@@examples@@
***One bond, two destinies*** — the C–Cl bond of tert-butyl chloride gives ions in water (heterolysis, substitution proceeds), while the Cl–Cl bond gives radicals in sunlight (homolysis, chlorination proceeds): the same chlorine, two different reaction worlds.
@@universalFacts@@
Radical chain reactions (initiation, propagation, termination) explain everything from methane chlorination to polymer manufacture and atmospheric ozone chemistry.

Explain with an example.** — **Heterolytic fission (heterolysis)** is bond breaking in which the **entire bonding electron pair is transferred to the more electronegative atom**, producing **a cation and an anion**: **A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻**. **NEB-style (2m): Distinguish between a free radical, a carbocation and a carbanion.** — A **free radical** is a **neutral** species with an **unpaired electron** (seven electrons on carbon; paramagnetic), produced by **homolytic fission** — e.g. Cl· or CH₃·. A **carbocation** is a **positively charged carbon with only six valence electrons**, formed when carbon **loses** the shared pair during heterolysis — e.g. (CH₃)₃C⁺; it is electron-deficient and attacks as an **electrophile**. A **carbanion** is a **negatively charged carbon carrying an octet including the borrowed pair**, formed when carbon **retains** the bonding pair in heterolysis — e.g. CH₃⁻; it is electron-rich and behaves as a **nucleophile**. Charge, electron count and fission origin distinguish all three.
@@summary@@
Every organic reaction starts with a bond breaking, and there are exactly two ways. **Homolytic fission** divides the shared pair **equally** — **A–B → A· + B·** — creating neutral **free radicals** with unpaired electrons; it favours non-polar bonds and needs **heat or light** (Cl₂ → 2Cl· in sunlight, starting the chain chlorination of methane). **Heterolytic fission** hands the **whole pair to the more electronegative atom** — **A–B → A⁺ + :B⁻** — creating ions; it favours polar bonds and polar solvents ((CH₃)₃C–Cl → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Cl⁻; HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻). The fission mode determines the intermediate — neutral 7-electron **radical**, positive 6-electron **carbocation** (electrophile), or electron-rich **carbanion** (nucleophile) — and thus selects the reagent class that drives the rest of the mechanism. Curly arrows (full head for pairs, fishhooks for single electrons) record every move.
