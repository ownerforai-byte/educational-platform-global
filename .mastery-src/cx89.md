## 1. Topic Introduction — Three Ladders, One Explanation

The alkaline earth metals carry three solubility ladders that all point different ways: **hydroxides** get *more* soluble down the group, **carbonates** get *less* soluble, and **sulphates** collapse to near-insoluble barium sulphate. One thermodynamic argument — lattice energy vs hydration energy — explains all three, and NEB asks for it nearly every year.

%%%
## 2. Fundamental Concepts

### 2.1 The two competing energies

- Dissolution pays two bills: breaking the crystal lattice (**lattice energy, ΔL**, always endothermic) and rewarding the ions with water (**hydration energy, ΔH, always exothermic**). Solubility follows the balance: hydration wins → soluble; lattice wins → insoluble.
- Down the group the M²⁺ ion grows (Be²⁺ 31 pm → Ba²⁺ 135 pm): its **hydration energy falls steeply** (Be²⁺ ≈ −2494 → Ba²⁺ ≈ −1305 kJ/mol), while **lattice energy falls only modestly** and at a rate that depends on the size of the anion.

### 2.2 The anion decides the direction

- **Small anion (OH⁻):** lattice energy falls *fast* as M²⁺ grows (the small anion was what made the lattice tight) — faster than hydration falls → **solubility increases** down: Be(OH)₂ (insoluble) → Ba(OH)₂ (quite soluble, ~56 g/L).
- **Large anion (SO₄²⁻, CO₃²⁻):** the big anion dominates the lattice, so lattice energy falls only slightly as M²⁺ grows — hydration falls faster → **solubility decreases** down: MgSO₄ soluble → BaSO₄ insoluble (2.4 mg/L); MgCO₃ sparingly → BaCO₃ almost insoluble.

### 2.3 The ladders in numbers

| Salt | Trend down IIA | Endpoints |
|---|---|---|
| M(OH)₂ | increases | Be(OH)₂ insoluble → Ba(OH)₂ soluble |
| MCO₃ | decreases | MgCO₃ ~0.1 g/L → BaCO₃ ~0.002 g/L |
| MSO₄ | decreases sharply | MgSO₄ soluble → BaSO₄ 2.4 mg/L |

## 3. Scientific Definitions

| Term | Scientific meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Lattice energy** | Energy to separate 1 mol of ionic solid to gaseous ions | ΔL of BaSO₄ large |
| **Hydration energy** | Energy released when gaseous ions are hydrated | Be²⁺ ≈ −2494 kJ/mol |
| **Solubility product (Ksp)** | Equilibrium constant of dissolution | Ksp(BaSO₄) ≈ 1×10⁻¹⁰ |
| **Basicity trend** | Basic character of hydroxides down the group | Be(OH)₂ amphoteric → Ba(OH)₂ strongly basic |

## 4. Detailed Explanation

### 4.1 Why the hydroxide ladder rises

- OH⁻ is small (radius ~140 pm effective). In Be(OH)₂ the tiny Be²⁺ packs against the small OH⁻ → very high lattice energy. As M²⁺ grows, the lattice loosens rapidly (big cation, small anion = weak packing), while the cation's hydration loss is more gradual in relative terms — net: dissolution becomes easier. Consequence: lime water exists (Ca(OH)₂ sparingly), barium water is a usable reagent, and Be(OH)₂ is amphoteric and insoluble.

### 4.2 Why the carbonate and sulphate ladders fall

- CO₃²⁻ and SO₄²⁻ are large, so the lattice is already "loose" with respect to cation size: growing M²⁺ barely lowers lattice energy. But hydration energy keeps falling fast — the growing cation holds water less tightly. Net: dissolution becomes harder. Consequences: the BaCl₂ test for sulphate (BaSO₄ precipitates completely), and hard-water carbonate scale (CaCO₃ precipitates on heating bicarbonate).

### 4.3 The flip side — thermal stability (preview of the next topic)

- The same size logic explains stability: small cations polarise CO₃²⁻ more (Fajans), so BeCO₃/MgCO₃ decompose easily while BaCO₃ needs ~1300 °C. Solubility and stability are two views of the same polarisation argument.

## 6. Common Confusions

**"Small anion → increasing solubility" is the rule, not "hydroxides are soluble":** Be(OH)₂ and Mg(OH)₂ are insoluble; only Ca(OH)₂ onward are usable in water — the ladder matters, not the label.
%%%
**BaSO₄ insolubility is not "covalency":** it is the lattice/hydration balance; quoting "covalent character" misses the thermodynamic argument NEB wants.
%%%
**Carbonates vs hydroxides directions are opposite:** memorise by anion size — small OH⁻ rises, big CO₃²⁻/SO₄²⁻ fall.

## 7. Exam Application

**NEB-style (3m): State and explain the general trend in the solubility of the hydroxides, carbonates and sulphates of the alkaline earth metals.** — **Hydroxides:** solubility **increases** down the group — Be(OH)₂ is insoluble, Mg(OH)₂ sparingly, Ca(OH)₂ sparingly (lime water), Sr(OH)₂ more, Ba(OH)₂ quite soluble. **Carbonates:** solubility **decreases** down — MgCO₃ is the most soluble of the four, BaCO₃ nearly insoluble. **Sulphates:** solubility **decreases sharply** down — MgSO₄ soluble, CaSO₄ sparingly, SrSO₄ very sparingly, BaSO₄ practically insoluble. **Explanation:** dissolution balances lattice energy (endothermic) against hydration energy (exothermic). Descending the group the M²⁺ ion grows, so its hydration energy falls rapidly. With the **small OH⁻ anion**, lattice energy also falls fast as the cation grows — faster than hydration — so solubility rises. With the **large CO₃²⁻ and SO₄²⁻ anions**, the lattice energy falls only slightly (the big anion dominates the lattice), while hydration keeps falling — so solubility drops, most catastrophically for BaSO₄.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Why is barium chloride solution used to test for sulphate ions, and why is MgSO₄ soluble while BaSO₄ is not?** — Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓ gives a dense white precipitate insoluble in acids — the definitive sulphate test. The solubility contrast follows the lattice/hydration balance: in MgSO₄ the small Mg²⁺ is strongly hydrated (≈ −1922 kJ/mol for Mg²⁺), paying the lattice bill, so the salt dissolves; in BaSO₄ the large Ba²⁺ is weakly hydrated (≈ −1305 kJ/mol) while the large SO₄²⁻ anion keeps the lattice energy high, so hydration cannot pay for the lattice — Ksp ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ and the salt precipitates almost completely.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Arrange the hydroxides of Be, Mg, Ca, Ba in order of increasing solubility and basicity.** — **Solubility:** Be(OH)₂ < Mg(OH)₂ < Ca(OH)₂ < Ba(OH)₂. **Basicity:** the same order — Be(OH)₂ is amphoteric (dissolves in acids *and* NaOH), Mg(OH)₂ is a weak base, Ca(OH)₂ a strong base (sparingly soluble), Ba(OH)₂ a strong base and appreciably soluble. Both trends follow the growing M²⁺: less polarising of O–H (more ionic, more basic hydroxide) and a looser lattice (more soluble).

@@keyPoints@@
Dissolution = lattice energy (endothermic) vs hydration energy (exothermic); down IIA hydration of M2+ falls fast (Be2+ -2494 -> Ba2+ -1305 kJ/mol). Hydroxides (small OH-): lattice falls faster -> solubility increases: Be(OH)2 insoluble < Mg(OH)2 < Ca(OH)2 (lime water) < Ba(OH)2 soluble; basicity same order, Be(OH)2 amphoteric. Carbonates (large anion): hydration falls faster -> solubility decreases: MgCO3 > CaCO3 > SrCO3 > BaCO3. Sulphates: same, sharply: MgSO4 soluble > CaSO4 sparingly > SrSO4 > BaSO4 2.4 mg/L (Ksp ~1e-10) -> BaCl2 test for SO4^2-, barium meal. Rule: small anion -> rising ladder; large anion -> falling ladder.
@@importantConcepts@@
One competition, three ladders: hydration energy is the engine that falls at a fixed rate down the group, and the anion's size decides whether lattice energy falls faster (hydroxides rise) or slower (carbonates and sulphates sink).
@@examples@@
***Lime water vs baryta water*** — Ca(OH)₂ gives only ~1.7 g/L in water (hence the faint cloudiness of limewater with CO₂), while Ba(OH)₂ dissolves ~56 g/L — the same hydroxide chemistry, three rungs apart on the ladder, one test-tube apart in the lab.
@@universalFacts@@
BaSO₄'s Ksp of about 10⁻¹⁰ is so small that a single drop of BaCl₂ in a litre of sulphate solution precipitates essentially every sulphate ion — quantitative gravimetric analysis rests on this ladder's bottom rung.
@@importantStatements@@
"The solubilities of the hydroxides of the alkaline earth metals increase, while those of their carbonates and sulphates decrease, with increasing atomic number of the metal."

**NEB-style (3m): State and explain the general trends in the solubility of the hydroxides, carbonates and sulphates of the alkaline earth metals.** — **Hydroxides, M(OH)₂:** solubility **increases down the group** — Be(OH)₂ insoluble, Mg(OH)₂ sparingly soluble, Ca(OH)₂ sparingly (lime water), Sr(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂ appreciably soluble. **Carbonates, MCO₃:** solubility **decreases down the group** — MgCO₃ sparingly soluble, CaCO₃ less, BaCO₃ practically insoluble. **Sulphates, MSO₄:** solubility **decreases sharply** — MgSO₄ soluble, CaSO₄ sparingly, SrSO₄ very sparingly, **BaSO₄ practically insoluble** (2.4 mg/L). **Explanation:** dissolving an ionic solid costs lattice energy (endothermic) and gains hydration energy (exothermic). Down the group the M²⁺ ion grows and its **hydration energy falls rapidly** (Be²⁺ ≈ −2494 → Ba²⁺ ≈ −1305 kJ/mol). With the **small OH⁻ anion**, the lattice energy also falls rapidly as the cation grows — faster than hydration energy — so dissolution gets easier and solubility rises. With the **large CO₃²⁻ and SO₄²⁻ anions**, the lattice energy falls only slightly (the big anion dominates the lattice), so the falling hydration energy wins and solubility falls — catastrophically for BaSO₄, whose Ksp ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ makes it the sulphate test reagent and the safe barium meal.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Why is MgSO₄ soluble in water but BaSO₄ practically insoluble?** — Dissolution must be paid for by the lattice energy and funded by hydration energy. **MgSO₄:** the small Mg²⁺ ion is very strongly hydrated (hydration enthalpy ≈ −1922 kJ/mol); this large exothermic hydration more than compensates the lattice energy, so the salt dissolves freely. **BaSO₄:** the large Ba²⁺ ion is only weakly hydrated (≈ −1305 kJ/mol), while the large SO₄²⁻ anion keeps the lattice energy high — the hydration "income" cannot cover the lattice "cost", the free energy of dissolution is positive, and the salt precipitates (Ksp ≈ 1×10⁻¹⁰, solubility 2.4 mg/L). This contrast is exactly why BaCl₂ is the standard reagent for testing sulphate ions: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓, a dense white precipitate insoluble in acids.
%%%
**NEB-style (2m): Arrange the hydroxides of Be, Mg, Ca and Ba in order of increasing solubility and increasing basic character, and account for both orders.** — **Solubility:** Be(OH)₂ < Mg(OH)₂ < Ca(OH)₂ < Ba(OH)₂. **Basic character:** the same order — Be(OH)₂ is **amphoteric** (reacts with acids and with NaOH, Be(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂BeO₂ + 2H₂O), Mg(OH)₂ is a weak base, Ca(OH)₂ strong, Ba(OH)₂ strong and appreciably soluble. **Reason:** as the M²⁺ ion grows down the group it polarises the O–H bond less, so the hydroxide becomes more ionic and more strongly basic; simultaneously the lattice of the small-anion hydroxide loosens faster than hydration energy falls, so solubility rises — both trends are the same ionic-size story.
@@summary@@
Three solubility ladders, one thermodynamic engine. Dissolution spends **lattice energy** and earns **hydration energy**; down Group IIA the growing M²⁺ loses hydration enthalpy fast (Be²⁺ −2494 → Ba²⁺ −1305 kJ/mol). With the **small OH⁻** anion, lattice energy collapses even faster, so the hydroxides **rise in solubility** — Be(OH)₂ insoluble and amphoteric, Mg(OH)₂ sparingly, Ca(OH)₂ lime water, Ba(OH)₂ a usable reagent — with basicity climbing the same ladder. With the **large CO₃²⁻ and SO₄²⁻** anions, lattice energy barely falls, so hydration's decline wins and solubility **sinks**: MgCO₃ → BaCO₃ almost insoluble, and MgSO₄ soluble all the way down to **BaSO₄ at 2.4 mg/L (Ksp ≈ 10⁻¹⁰)** — the contrast that makes BaCl₂ the sulphate test and insoluble BaSO₄ the safe barium meal.
